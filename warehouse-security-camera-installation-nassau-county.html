The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need Warehouse Security Camera Installation

Nassau is packed tight. The Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey puts 1,392,438 people on 284.5 square miles of land — about 4,894 residents per square mile, roughly a dozen times the density of Dutchess and enough that nearly every industrial street in the county has houses, schools or a shopping center within sight of its loading doors. Industrial land here was never laid out in big empty parcels. It was squeezed between rail lines, parkways, village boundaries and post-war housing, and most of it was built out decades ago. That shapes camera work in three ways: buildings are older than their tenants, sites are small enough that the front lot and the street are part of the picture, and almost every job involves someone else’s rules — a landlord’s, a village’s, a condominium board’s or a utility’s.

The commercial-real-estate numbers say the same thing in square feet. Cushman & Wakefield’s first-quarter 2026 MarketBeat counted 16,000,620 square feet of industrial space in Western Nassau, 6,861,182 in Central Nassau and 22,046,534 in Eastern Nassau, with vacancy between 3.6 and 5.5 percent and the county as a whole under 5 percent for the first time since late 2024. Density changes the design as well as the risk. Owners, tenants and who pays for what

In Nassau’s tight market the person who calls us is as often a tenant as an owner, and the split matters before a single camera goes up. Owners and park landlords usually want the exterior, the shared lot and the common doors on a system they control, because those views protect the building between tenants. Tenants want their own doors, stock and office on a system they own and can take with them. Both can share a building without conflict if the boundary is drawn on day one: which cameras belong to whom, who can see which views, and what stays when a lease ends. We put that boundary on the layout drawing so the landlord, the tenant and the next tenant all read the same answer.

Where one party pays for a camera that covers the other’s area — a landlord camera on a tenant’s overhead door, say — we can give the tenant a view-only login to that one camera without handing over the whole system. It is a small setting that prevents a lot of arguments after an incident.

Warehouse Security Camera Installation for Nassau County’s Architecture

Post-war one-story block and brick industrial

Concrete-block or brick shells with steel bar joists, a few overhead doors and a small office in front, common across Hicksville, Westbury, Inwood and Oceanside. Solid walls take anchors well; the hard part is getting new cable past decades of abandoned wiring in the joists.

Mid-century flex and industrial parks

Long buildings divided into units with a dock or drive-in door each, in parks like those around Plainview, Port Washington and the old Grumman corridor. Shared roofs, shared walls and a landlord who controls the exterior.

Upper-floor and mezzanine warehouse space

Industrial floors above ground level, reached by freight elevator or a shared dock below — Newmark’s Q2 2026 report recorded an auto-parts distributor taking 72,489 square feet on part of a second floor in Port Washington. Cameras follow goods through areas the tenant does not control.

New tilt-up on former industrial land

The rare new building, like the 36-foot-clear spec warehouse on former Grumman land in Bethpage or the Hicksville building on a former plant site: concrete panels, long dock walls, and site restrictions that can govern any digging.

Strip-center back rooms and supply houses

Distributors and trade counters along Old Country Road, Sunrise Highway, Hempstead Turnpike and Jericho Turnpike, with a sales floor in front and a stock room and loading door behind, sharing a rear service lane with neighbors.

South Shore waterfront buildings

Low-lying industrial, marine and storage buildings near the bays from Inwood to Freeport and Seaford, where tidal and storm flooding decide where electronics can live.

How we mount and cable in each

  • Block and brick walls: sleeve anchors into solid block faces or brick, never into hollow cores or mortar joints; cable enters through a sealed sleeve rather than running down the outside face.
  • Bar-joist roofs: cable supported on rated hangers from the joists, kept clear of sprinkler piping, gas lines and unit heaters, and labeled at both ends because the next tenant will need to know what it is.
  • Shared-roof flex buildings: no new roof penetrations without the landlord’s written approval; we prefer interior routes along demising walls and existing chases.
  • Tilt-up panels: anchors set into solid panel away from joints and embedded steel; conduit stubbed through the panel above the camera, sealed to the landlord’s detail.
  • Drop ceilings in offices and back rooms: tiles lifted to check for old cable, asbestos-era materials and air-return plenums before anything is drilled or pulled.
  • Waterfront buildings: recorder, switch and battery backup on the wall or a mezzanine above recorded high water, and every outdoor connection in a sealed box mounted high.

Rules that shape a Nassau warehouse camera job

  • State licensing. New York’s Department of State licenses businesses that install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems under General Business Law Article 6-D. AESS holds license #12000287431. Ask any bidder for theirs.
  • Electricians are licensed town by town. The Town of Hempstead Code (chapter 84) bars anyone from installing and wiring electrical apparatus in the town professionally without a master electrician’s license, and North Hempstead licenses master electricians through its Commissioner of Buildings. New circuits for a camera head end go to an electrician licensed where the building sits; low-voltage camera cabling runs from that circuit.
  • Home-improvement licensing usually does not apply. Nassau’s home-improvement law (Administrative Code §21-11, Local Law 6-1970) covers work on dwellings of not more than three families and expressly excludes burglar alarm and fire alarm systems. Warehouses are commercial work.
  • Alarm permits and false-alarm penalties. Nassau’s Administrative Code requires a permit for alarms that call the police, sets graduated penalties for false alarms, and gives new installations a short grace period only when a permit has been issued. Video that lets someone check an alarm before police roll is worth designing in.
  • Asbestos surveys before disturbing old materials. The state Labor Department’s Industrial Code Rule 56 requires an asbestos survey by a certified inspector before renovation or repair work and bars trades without an asbestos license from disturbing asbestos material. We plan routes in older buildings to avoid suspect ceilings, floor tile and mastic, or wait for the owner’s survey.
  • Employee privacy. New York Labor Law §203-c forbids video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and rooms designated for changing; we mask or omit those views on every job.

Warehouse Security Camera Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

Building on Old Industrial Ground

Our building sits on former plant land in Bethpage or Hicksville. Can you trench across the lot to a camera pole?

Possibly, but not until someone checks what rules came with the land. Parts of Nassau’s industrial ground carry cleanup history: the state Department of Environmental Conservation still oversees containment of the Navy-Grumman groundwater plume, which it describes as stretching about 4.3 miles south of the old Bethpage facility and about 900 feet deep, and Long Island Business News described the New South Road warehouse site in Hicksville as a once-tainted former plant property. Where contamination remains under a state-approved site management plan, DEC’s standard environmental notice says there shall be no disturbance or excavation that threatens the cleanup’s engineering controls without the agency’s approval, and the excavation plans we have read require advance notice to DEC — one requires at least 15 days.

So before we price a trench, we ask the landlord or owner whether the parcel has a site management plan, an environmental easement or a soil cap. If it does, the usual answer is to avoid digging entirely: mount the camera on the building with a longer lens, use an existing light pole the landlord approves, or carry the signal on a short wireless link to a pole that needs only a surface base. If digging is unavoidable, it follows the plan’s excavation procedures and the owner’s environmental consultant schedules it, not us.

The light poles in our lot belong to the landlord and were approved with the site plan. Can cameras go on them?

Often yes, with the landlord’s written permission, and it is usually the cheapest way to cover a Nassau front lot because the pole already has height and power nearby. Three things decide it. First, ownership: the pole, its base and its electrical feed are the landlord’s, so mounting anything needs their approval and sometimes their electrician. Second, what is inside: many lot poles carry only a lighting circuit switched by a photocell, which is off all day; a camera needs constant power, so we either run a new low-voltage cable to the pole through existing conduit or have an electrician add an unswitched feed. Third, local approval: some villages review changes to approved site plans, and the landlord will know whether adding equipment to a pole needs a filing.

When the poles are off limits, a camera high on the building corner with the right lens covers most small Nassau lots from the wall, and a second camera on the opposite corner closes the blind spot behind parked vans.

Our unit is in Inwood, right at the Queens line. Do New York City rules apply to the camera work?

No. Inwood is in Nassau County and the Town of Hempstead, so the city’s Department of Buildings and its low-voltage rules do not govern a building on Inip Drive or Sheridan Boulevard, however close the city line is. Electrical work falls under the Town of Hempstead, whose code requires a master electrician’s license for professional electrical installation, and police response comes from the Nassau County Police Department’s Fourth Precinct — the precinct that answered the January 2026 burglary on Sheridan Boulevard.

What crosses the line is the traffic. Inwood’s industrial blocks sit beside routes into Queens, so the vehicle that leaves your lot may be in the city within minutes. That makes the clip of the vehicle’s exit, with a clear view of its side and rear and an accurate time stamp, the most valuable footage you can hand the precinct; we aim the lot-exit camera to catch exactly that.

Tides, Floods and Where the Recorder Lives

Our Oceanside or Island Park building takes water on big tides. Where should the recorder and switches go?

Up, and away from the floor. In September 2026, News 12 reported that a nor’easter’s tides flooded tracks in Island Park badly enough that the railroad suspended the Long Beach Branch for more than a day while crews pumped millions of gallons of water. A recorder sitting on a desk or a rack bottom in a building like that is the first thing lost, along with every hour of footage on it. We mount the recorder, the PoE switch, the internet modem and the battery backup on a wall shelf or in a cabinet well above the highest water you have seen, or on a mezzanine if the building has one, and we run cable to it from above rather than along the floor.

Outdoor junction boxes go high on the wall, not at grade beside the door. And we turn on a copy of key cameras to off-site storage or a phone app, so that if the building does flood, the hours before and during the storm are already somewhere dry.

Can our cameras help document flood damage for an insurance claim?

They can show when water arrived, how high it rose against fixed objects and what was standing where — useful facts for an adjuster — but only if the system keeps recording and the footage survives. That means the head end sits above the water, battery backup carries it through the power cut that usually comes with a surge, and at least the interior views are copied off site as the storm approaches. Cameras that look along a wall with marked shelving or a painted line give a clearer picture of depth than a camera pointed at open floor.

We cannot tell you what your policy covers or what an adjuster will accept; that is between you and your carrier. What we can do is set the system up so the evidence exists, and show you how to export a time-stamped clip of the water coming in.

Vans, Keys and the Front Lot

Our delivery vans park overnight in the front lot and the keys are kept inside. What should cameras cover?

The path from the door to the keys and from the keys to the vans — because that is the sequence in the Inwood case. Nassau police reported that at 2:44 a.m. on January 30, 2026, someone forced the front sliding doors of a business on Sheridan Boulevard, took a set of keys and drove a van off the front lot. CBS New York, covering the 2024 Nassau–Suffolk theft task force, described crews that break into homes looking for key fobs. A van with its keys is the easiest vehicle on Long Island to steal.

We put one camera on the inside of the entry the thief is likeliest to force, one on wherever the keys live — a locked cabinet in a camera’s view, never a hook by the door — and views of each van space that show the driver’s door and the plate. A last camera watches the lot exit, so the clip ends with the vehicle leaving and the direction it turned. Pair that with an after-hours alert on the key-cabinet camera and you learn about the break-in while the van is still nearby.

Our box trucks sit along the back fence overnight. Can cameras stop catalytic converter and parts theft?

Cameras alone rarely stop it, but they shorten it and they identify the people doing it. CBS New York has reported catalytic converters cut from cars in commuter lots by the Syosset and Hicksville railroad stations, with MTA police working alongside Nassau County police. A truck parked tight to a back fence in the dark is the same opportunity with a bigger prize, because the thief is hidden from the street.

Our approach is to light and watch the side of the vehicles where a thief has to kneel or lie down. That usually means a camera on the building looking down the row of trucks rather than across their fronts, with infrared strong enough to show a person under a chassis, and an after-hours detection zone drawn along the row. Parking the trucks nose-in against the building wall, so the exhaust side faces the camera, does as much as any equipment.

Storefront Glass and Smash-and-Grab

Thieves have been smashing front glass at Nassau shops at night. What should the camera at our counter entrance capture?

Faces before the glass breaks, the vehicle outside, and the time. News 12’s December 2025 video from a Hicksville tech repair store showed three people breaking the front glass with a brick while a fourth waited in a car, and Nassau police announced arrests in April 2026 for burglaries at phone repair shops across four communities. In both, the useful footage was the approach and the getaway, not the minute inside.

For a supply house or wholesaler with a glass front, we place one camera inside looking out through the door at head height, set for the glare of streetlights and headlights, and one outside under the canopy covering the curb and the parking spaces in front. The interior counter camera then shows what was taken. Recording all three on the same recorder, with accurate time, lets a detective line up the people, the car and the loss in one export.

Railroads, Neighbors and Property Lines

The back of our lot faces the railroad right-of-way. Can a camera watch the fence line along the tracks?

Yes, as long as the camera stays on your side and is aimed at your property. Several Nassau industrial streets back onto railroad land, and a fence line along the tracks is a common way in after dark. We mount on your building or your own pole, aim along the inside of your fence, and mask out the track area beyond it in the camera settings so the system records your boundary rather than the railroad’s property.

Do not attach anything to a railroad fence or structure without the railroad’s written permission; it is not yours to drill. If trespass from the tracks is a recurring problem, the clip of someone crossing your fence is evidence for the MTA police as well as the county — the CBS station-lot thefts were worked by both — so we make sure the export shows the fence, the person and the time together.

Three businesses on our industrial street were hit the same night. Can we and our neighbors share camera views?

You can, and in Nassau’s tight industrial blocks it is one of the most effective things a street can do. Each business keeps its own recorder and its own footage, but cameras on two or three buildings can be aimed so that together they cover the street, the shared driveway and the corner where vehicles enter. After an incident, each owner exports their own clip and hands it to police, who can line the views up by time.

Some neighbors go further and give each other view-only access to a single street-facing camera. That works technically, but agree on it in writing first: who can see what, for how long footage is kept, and that nobody aims a shared camera into another business’s interior or a neighboring home. We set up separate logins with only the agreed camera on each.

Police Programs and Alarm Rules

Garden City police run a voluntary camera registry. Should a warehouse sign up, and does it give police access to our system?

The Village of Garden City Police Department’s Private Camera Registration Program lets an address be listed as one with cameras so detectives know where to ask after a crime; the department says the program is strictly voluntary and does not give it access to your camera system, and that detectives may ask to inspect video from a set period. The county police run a separate information-sharing network for residents and businesses called SPIN. Other departments in Nassau may or may not have a registry; ask the one that patrols your building.

For a warehouse, registering costs nothing and saves time after a crime on your street, because the detective comes to you instead of canvassing. What matters more is being able to answer when they arrive: someone on site who can find the right camera and export the right minutes. That is what the handover drill on every job is for.

Nassau charges for false alarms. Can camera video keep our alarm from costing us?

It can cut the false dispatches that cause the charges. Nassau’s Administrative Code requires a permit for alarms that are called into the police and sets graduated penalties for false alarms; the schedule we reviewed shows commercial penalties for non-permit holders in the $550–$750 range, and new installations get a short grace period only when a permit has been issued. The county police have said they are asked to respond to more than 125,000 false alarms a year.

Linking cameras to alarm zones means a triggered zone produces a short clip your monitoring center or your phone can check in seconds: a forklift battery charger’s fan shaking a sign is not a burglar. Getting the alarm permit, keeping your contact list current and fixing doors that rattle in the wind still matter more than any camera, and we will tell you which of those is causing your false alarms.

Nassau has more plate-reader cameras than any county in the state. Do we still need our own on the gate?

Usually, yes. The Investigative Post, citing the DeFlock project, reported in July 2026 that Nassau had 331 reported automated plate-reader cameras, the most of any New York county — and a long r/longisland thread shows how much residents argue about them. But those readers belong to police and other agencies, sit on public roads, and are searched for their purposes, not yours. They will not show who drove into your lot, which door they went to or what they loaded.

Your own cameras answer those questions. On most small Nassau lots we do not need a dedicated plate camera at all; a well-placed overview at the entrance, recording at full resolution, catches the vehicle, its color and often the plate. A dedicated plate camera earns its place where trucks queue at a gate or where the entrance is wide and fast.

Old Panels, Old Roofs, Old Ceilings

Our building dates from before 1974. Does running camera cable trigger the state’s asbestos rules?

It can. The state Department of Labor’s Industrial Code Rule 56 requires an asbestos survey by a certified inspector before renovation, remodeling or repair work, singles out buildings built before January 1, 1974, and says trades without an asbestos license may not disturb or remove asbestos material. In older Nassau industrial buildings, the materials that worry us are textured ceiling tiles, old floor tile and mastic, pipe insulation and some roofing.

Most camera jobs disturb very little: a few small holes and cable laid on existing supports. We still ask whether the owner has a survey, route cable to avoid suspect materials, and stop if we find something that looks like insulation or tile we cannot identify. If drilling a particular ceiling or wall would disturb suspect material, the owner’s licensed asbestos contractor handles that spot first, and we work around it.

Our electrical panel has no spare breakers. Can cameras still go in?

Usually without touching the panel at all. A modern camera system draws power for every camera through the network cable from a single PoE switch, so the whole system needs one outlet near the recorder, preferably on a circuit that is not shared with heaters, compressors or battery chargers. In many older Nassau buildings an existing office circuit can carry it.

If the only available outlets share a circuit with heavy equipment, or the recorder location has no outlet, a licensed electrician adds a dedicated circuit — which, in a full panel, may mean a tandem breaker or a small subpanel. We tell you which case you are in at the walk-through, and the electrician’s scope appears as its own line on our estimate rather than as a surprise.

Our landlord won’t allow any new holes in the roof. How does cable reach cameras on the far wall?

From the inside, which is how we prefer to do it anyway. In Nassau’s shared-roof flex buildings the roof membrane is often under a warranty that the landlord protects, and a single unauthorized penetration can void it. Cable runs along the underside of the roof deck or the joists to the inside face of the far wall, then passes through the wall at the camera location in a sealed sleeve. No roof work at all.

Where a wall is a shared demising wall with another tenant, we go along it rather than through it, and on tilt-up buildings we use the landlord’s approved penetration detail. If the only way to a corner is across a neighbor’s ceiling, a short wireless link between two buildings or two walls can carry the video instead of cable.

The order we place cameras in a small Nassau building

Most buildings we see in the county are small enough that the order of placement matters more than the camera count. We work through it the same way every time, and stop when the next camera would not answer a new question.

  • Every way in: overhead doors, walk doors, the storefront entrance and any roof hatch or fire door, each framed to show a person at the threshold.
  • Where the valuables wait: the key cabinet, the cash or deposit point, the cage or high-value shelf, and the will-call or pickup area.
  • The front lot and the exit: the van or truck row from the side where someone would kneel, and the street entrance so a vehicle leaving is on record.
  • The head end itself: a camera on the recorder location, so a thief who goes for the recorder is recorded doing it and the clip is already copied off site.
  • Movement inside: cross-aisles, the dock or loading area and the route from stock to door.
  • Everything else: individual aisles, mezzanines and the back fence, only where a past loss or a specific risk justifies them.

Done in that order, an eight-camera system in a 10,000-square-foot Nassau shop usually covers what matters, and a sixteen-camera system covers almost any single-tenant building up to the average size of warehouses now on the market in the county.

What AI Gets Wrong About Warehouse Security Camera Installation in Nassau County

We could not capture Google’s AI Overview or its related-question boxes from our research tools for this page, so instead we tested the claims that show up again and again in ranking cost guides, chatbot answers and national installer pages against what is actually true in Nassau.

“Long Island is full of new warehouses with cameras built in”

Long Island did set a leasing record — Cushman & Wakefield counted 1.7 million square feet of new industrial leasing in the second quarter of 2026, the most in the region’s history. But the new buildings are mostly further east. Newmark reported that Nassau delivered no new industrial space in five quarters, and Cushman wrote that development sites are increasingly scarce in Nassau County and Western Suffolk.

In Nassau the typical tenant moves into an older building with whatever the last occupant left behind: cameras of unknown age, a recorder nobody has a password for, or nothing at all. Planning for a retrofit, not a new build, is the starting point for an honest estimate here.

“Installation costs the same everywhere”

It does not, and our own price list shows why. AESS prices each area from a Brooklyn base with a published multiplier — Nassau is 1.15 times Brooklyn — because travel, parking, labor time and building conditions differ.

National guides quoting a single per-camera figure also leave out what drives Nassau costs most: retrofit cabling through occupied ceilings, an electrician licensed in your town for any new circuit, landlord paperwork, and occasionally a survey for old materials. Our estimates put those on separate lines so you can compare like with like.

“You need a home-improvement license to install cameras”

Not for a warehouse. Nassau’s home-improvement law applies to work on dwellings of not more than three families and expressly excludes burglar alarm and fire alarm systems.

What does apply is state licensing for businesses that install security and fire alarm systems under General Business Law Article 6-D — AESS holds license #12000287431 — and town-level electrician licensing for any new power circuit, such as the Town of Hempstead’s master electrician requirement.

“Wireless cameras are the easy answer for an older building”

Older Nassau buildings are exactly where Wi-Fi cameras struggle: block walls, metal racking, foil-faced insulation and steel roof decks all eat the signal, and a battery camera that sleeps until it senses motion often misses the first seconds of what you needed. Wireless cameras also still need power at the camera.

We wire the cameras and use wireless only as a link — across a lot to a pole, or between two buildings — where a cable cannot run.

“Cloud cameras are safer because thieves can’t steal the recorder”

A stolen or smashed recorder is a real risk, which is why we hide or lock the recorder, put it in a room the thief has no reason to enter, and copy the most important cameras off site. But cloud-only systems depend on your internet line, and in a Nassau storm the line often goes down with the power.

Keeping the primary recording on site, on battery, and sending selected cameras to the cloud as a backup gives you both protections rather than trading one for the other.

“The police plate readers will catch whoever did it”

Nassau does have more reported plate-reader cameras than any county in New York, according to a July 2026 Investigative Post report. Those readers belong to agencies, sit on public roads and are searched for their purposes.

They will not show who walked through your back door, what was carried out or which employee was on shift. Only your own cameras record that.

“More cameras mean a safer warehouse”

A Nassau warehouse of 15,000 to 20,000 square feet — about the average size in recent sale listings — is usually covered best by fewer, better-placed cameras: every door, the key and cash locations, the front lot and the exits, then the aisles.

Thirty cameras pointed at racking produce hours of footage nobody watches. We start from the exits and add a camera only when it answers a question you would actually ask after a loss.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Open the Ceiling Before You Price the Job

The most common surprise in a Nassau warehouse is not a hard wall or a high ceiling. It is what is already above the tiles. Buildings that have had four or five tenants carry four or five generations of cable: dead telephone runs, coax from an analog camera system, network cable from an office that left, alarm wire to sensors that were removed. Some of it is good. Most of it is unlabeled. A quote written from the floor guesses; a quote written after lifting a few tiles knows.

So we lift them, trace what we can, and test anything that looks reusable before deciding. Sometimes an old cable run to the far wall saves a day of labor. Sometimes the ceiling shows us textured tiles or old insulation we will not disturb until the owner has a survey. Either way, you hear about it before you sign, and the estimate reflects the building you actually have — which, in a county that is not building new warehouses, is always somebody else’s old one.

The same habit pays off at the end of a lease. Because we label every run at both ends and leave a camera map with the recorder, the next person to open that ceiling — a new tenant, the landlord’s electrician, or us coming back to add a camera — knows what each cable is and where it goes. In Nassau, where buildings change hands and tenants faster than they are rebuilt, a labeled ceiling is worth almost as much as the cameras under it.

FAQ

Nassau County Warehouse Security Camera Installation FAQ

Before we sign a lease on space in a Nassau building, what camera questions should we put to the landlord?
+

Ask four things and get the answers into the lease or work letter. Whether you may mount cameras on the exterior and run cable through walls and ceilings. Whether any roof work or penetration is allowed, and under whose detail. What happens to the existing cameras and recorder on the premises, and whether the landlord’s own system covers your doors and lot. And whether you can get access after hours for installation and service. Settling these before signing avoids arguments after.

Our building gets its power from Freeport Electric or Rockville Centre’s village system. Does that change anything for the cameras?
+

Very little for the cameras themselves. Freeport Electric and Rockville Centre Electric are village-owned utilities, so outage reports and service questions go to them rather than PSEG Long Island, and Rockville Centre’s department says its own plant engines run during emergencies when transmission lines are out. Either way, we put the recorder, switch and modem on battery backup sized for your site, and any new circuit is wired by an electrician licensed in your town or village.

Our building is empty while we look for a tenant. What is the least we should run so the vacancy doesn’t invite trouble?
+

Cover every way in and the lot: one camera on each overhead and walk door from the outside, one on the main entrance and one or two on the lot, recording on a recorder in a locked room with battery backup and alerts to your phone after hours. A cellular router keeps remote viewing alive if the internet service was disconnected with the last tenant. The system can stay for the next tenant or be moved when the building is let.

A delivery driver says he was hurt on our dock. How do we stop the clip from being overwritten?
+

Act the same day. Most recorders overwrite the oldest footage once the drive is full, so find the incident, export the clip with a few minutes either side to a USB drive, and also lock or protect that segment on the recorder if it supports it. Write down who exported it and when. Then give a copy to your insurance carrier or lawyer as they direct. We show every client how to do this at handover.

Snowplow contractors keep hitting poles in our lot. Where should camera poles go?
+

Ideally nowhere in the plow’s path. On most small Nassau lots a camera on the building corner or above the doors, with the right lens, covers the lot without a pole at all. Where a pole is needed, we place it inside a curbed island or against the fence line where snow is pushed, not in the open lot, and protect the base with a bollard. Ask your plow contractor where they pile snow before we decide.

Our IT company manages our office network. Will the cameras sit on it, or separately?
+

We prefer the cameras on their own network: a dedicated PoE switch with the cameras and recorder isolated from your office computers, and only the recorder connected to the internet for remote viewing. That keeps camera traffic off your office network and keeps the cameras out of reach of anything else on it. We coordinate with your IT company on the connection, addresses and firewall rules so nobody is surprised.

Our office ceiling is used as a return-air plenum. Does camera cable have to be plenum-rated?
+

Yes. Where the space above a ceiling carries return air for the heating and cooling system, the electrical code calls for cable listed for plenum use, rated CMP and tested to the NFPA 262 smoke and flame standard, or cable run in a suitable raceway. We check at the walk-through whether your ceiling is a plenum and price plenum-rated cable where it is.

Can a camera watch the sprinkler riser and fire pump room for tampering?
+

Yes. A camera on the riser room door, or inside the room looking at the valves, records anyone who enters or touches them, which helps if a valve is ever found closed. It does not replace the supervisory switches your fire alarm system uses to report a closed valve; it adds the picture of who was there. We keep the camera clear of sprinkler heads and piping.

Would thermal cameras help on the dark back fence behind our building?
+

They can. A thermal camera sees the heat of a person or vehicle in complete darkness, through light fog and without being dazzled by headlights, which makes it good at detecting someone along a long, unlit fence. It will not show a face or a plate, so we pair it with a regular camera at the point where someone would cross or enter, and use the thermal one to trigger the alert.

+
