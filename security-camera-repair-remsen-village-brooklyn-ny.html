The two-family with garages behind: the longest cable on the block

The commonest Remsen Village house we work in is a brick two-family with a doorway per apartment, a driveway down one side and a garage at the back of the lot. On a house like the ones on East 91st Street, with a two-car garage behind and one space per apartment, the camera that watches the garages may be 80 or 100 feet of cable away from the recorder in the cellar. That cable has to leave the house somewhere, cross or skirt the yard, and climb the garage. In our experience it was usually stapled along a fence, laid across the yard and covered with mulch, or pushed through a length of garden hose, and the garage end of it was spliced in a box under the eave that takes rain every time the wind blows from the right direction.

So the repair starts by testing that run from both ends before anything is replaced. A camera that shows a picture by day and drops out after rain points to a wet joint. A camera that went dark the week someone reset a fence post or dug a planting bed points to a cut. A camera that flickers on and off when it switches to night vision often has a cable too long or too thin for the power it pulls, and the infrared lights tip it over the edge. Each has a different fix: a new weatherproof joint, a spliced and protected section of cable, a replacement run in conduit along the garage wall, or power fed from the far end. We price the run before we open anything, and if a new run has to cross a shared driveway, we tell you that before you decide, because it may involve the neighbor.

The owner's duplex over a garden rental: the recorder is on the tenant's side

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Remsen Village Two-Family After the Power Blinks

This is the order we follow on a two-family with a garage out back when the owner calls to say "some of the cameras didn't come back". It is the same every time, because doing it in this order is what keeps footage safe and keeps us from replacing a part that was not broken.

We start at the recorder, not the camera. Before anything is restarted, we look at the screen, note which channels say "No Video" or "Offline", read the recorder's log for the moment of the outage, and check the clock against a phone. If the owner needs any clip from before or after the outage, it goes onto a USB drive first. Then we check the drive's status in the storage menu, because a drive that will not mount after a power cut is a different repair from a camera that will not wake up.

Next comes power, in the order the electricity travels: the outlet and the panel it comes from, any surge strip or battery backup, the PoE switch or the central power box, and then each camera's cable. On a house with a garage at the back we test the garage camera's run from the cellar end and from the garage end, because a long outdoor cable is the part most likely to have taken the hit. Only when we know where the fault is do we price the repair, and the owner hears the cause and the number before any part comes off the truck. When the job is done, every camera is checked by day and again with the yard and driveway lights off, and the warnings for a lost camera and a drive error are sent to the owner's phone as a test, so the next outage ends with a text message rather than a surprise.

Fault by Fault

Lightning, Long Yard Cables, Wet Cellars, the Wrong Meter and Porch Light: Why Remsen Village Camera Systems Fail

Most camera failures we see in Remsen Village come down to six causes. None of them are exotic, and most of them leave clues on the recorder screen if you know where to look.

Lightning and surges: the outdoor cable is the antenna

A strike does not have to hit the house to damage a camera system. A nearby strike or a surge on the power lines can travel in through the electrical service, and a long outdoor cable to a garage or a back fence can pick up a surge of its own. The parts that usually fail are the cheap ones closest to the surge: a plug-in adapter, a channel on a central power box, a port on a PoE switch, or the network chip in the camera at the end of the longest run. On the morning of November 6, 2026, the outage that reached ZIP code 11203 was blamed on lightning, and that is the kind of event after which we find one or two dead channels on a system that otherwise came back normally.

The repair is to find each damaged part and replace only that, which is why we test every port and every run rather than swapping the recorder on a hunch. Then we look at prevention: surge protection where the outdoor cables come into the house, a battery backup that cleans up the power to the recorder and the switch, and grounding where the installer left none. Lightning and surge damage are not covered by any equipment warranty, ours included, so we write those repairs up separately and say clearly which protection would have stopped which part of the damage.

Long yard cables: the run that lives outdoors

On a lot with a garage at the back, at least one camera cable spends its whole life outdoors. Sunlight cracks indoor-rated jacket, water finds its way into any joint that was taped rather than sealed, and the cable gets stepped on, mowed over, chewed or cut by anyone working in the yard. A run of ordinary indoor cable stapled along a wooden fence will look fine for a year or two and then start dropping the camera in wet weather.

We find the fault by measurement and inspection from both ends, then repair the damaged section with an outdoor-rated splice in a sealed box, or replace the run with outdoor-rated cable in conduit along a wall or a fence. If the run has to go underground, we tell you before the work is priced, because trenching across a yard or a shared driveway is a bigger decision than a splice and may involve a neighbor.

Wet cellars: the recorder on the low shelf

Cellars in these houses are where the recorder, the power box, the modem and the switch usually end up, because the cables from the front and the back meet there. In a storm like the one on October 30, 2025, when the rain came far faster than the sewers were built to handle, water comes in through floor drains, Bilco doors and old foundations. Even without a flood, a damp cellar corrodes connectors and the recorder's fan pulls that air through the box all day.

A recorder that sat in water should not simply be dried and switched back on. We copy off any footage that matters if the drive will still read, check the board, the power supply and the drive, and give you an honest answer on whether the unit is worth keeping. Then the equipment goes up off the floor onto a shelf or a wall bracket, with a battery backup beside it and the power strip off the floor too.

The wrong meter: cameras on somebody else's power

In a two-family the cameras and the recorder are often plugged into whatever outlet was handy, and that outlet may be on the tenant's meter. Everything works until the tenant moves out and the account is closed, a breaker trips on a panel inside the tenant's apartment, or the tenant unplugs something to use the outlet. Then the owner upstairs finds the system dark and has no idea why.

We trace each part of the system back to its panel and meter and tell you what is on whose power. Where it is practical, the system moves onto the owner's circuit; where a new outlet is needed, we tell an electrician exactly what and where, since we do not do electrical work ourselves.

Porch light and street light: the picture that changed without the camera changing

Houses, two-families and garages out back

Quite possibly. Our driveway camera only drops out on rainy days and comes back once things dry. What does that usually mean?

Water is getting into a joint somewhere along the run, most often in the splice box under the garage eave or where the cable enters the house. We are putting in a new fence along the side of the yard. How do we keep the contractor from cutting the garage camera cable?

Mark it before the work starts. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Remsen Village

AI answers and listing sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give tidy general advice about camera repair. Some of it holds up. Some of it falls apart on a Remsen Village lot with a garage at the back, a recorder in a wet cellar and a power line that just took a lightning hit.

"A power outage won't damage your cameras; just wait for them to reboot"

Most of the time a clean outage does no harm, and the system comes back by itself. The trouble is that outages caused by lightning are rarely clean. The November 6, 2026 outage that reached ZIP code 11203 was put down to suspected lightning strikes, and with lightning the power can surge, sag and flicker before it settles.

What we find after events like that is a system that mostly came back: four cameras fine, the garage camera dead, a switch port gone, or the recorder up and running with its clock reset to a factory date and its recording schedule cleared. The owner checks the front door camera on the phone, sees a picture and assumes the rest are fine.

So after any outage, check every channel on the recorder, not just the one you look at most, and check that the date and time are right. If anything is missing, call before you start unplugging and replugging, because the recorder's log of the outage is the best clue to what failed.

"Wireless cameras solve wiring problems"

The advice to replace a troublesome wired camera with a Wi-Fi one sounds sensible until you try it on a garage 80 or 100 feet behind a brick house. Wi-Fi from a router inside the house has to get through brick walls and across the yard, and the signal that reaches the garage is often too weak for steady video, especially at night when the camera's infrared lights draw more power and every passing car triggers a clip.

A Wi-Fi camera still needs power, too, so the garage camera either needs an outlet out there or a battery that will need charging far more often in the cold. And every Wi-Fi camera on the house stops reporting the day the router or the password changes.

For a garage or the far end of a driveway, a properly repaired wired run, outdoor-rated and protected, is usually the more reliable answer. Wi-Fi works well for a camera inside the house near the router, and we say so when that is the case.

"If a camera stops working, replace the camera"

Listing sites price camera repair as if the camera were the usual failure. On Remsen Village properties, it often is not. The camera at the end of a yard cable is fine; the joint in the splice box under the garage eave is full of water. The front camera is fine; its plug-in adapter died in the outage. The camera is fine; it is plugged into the tenant's outlet and the tenant's account was closed.

Replacing the camera in those cases spends money and leaves the fault in place, and the new camera goes dark the same way a few weeks later.

That is why we test from the outlet to the camera before anything is replaced, and why the price we write down names the part that actually failed. When the camera itself is the problem, we replace it; when it is the cable, the power or the connector, we fix that instead, which is usually cheaper.

"Your landlord or the city has to keep building cameras working"

There is no general New York City law that requires an apartment building to have security cameras, or to keep the ones it has working. Building rules cover locks, self-closing doors, lighting and, in many buildings, intercoms, and HPD writes violations for those. Cameras are a different matter, and Outerbridge Law notes that "New York has no law on how long landlords must keep film."

That does not mean a tenant has no say. A broken front door lock or intercom is reportable to 311, and a lease may promise more than the law does. But a 311 complaint about a dead lobby camera will not, on its own, get it repaired.

For owners, the practical point is the opposite one: if you have cameras and store footage, you are responsible for it, including who can see it. A repair is a good time to put the recorder login in the right name, set how many days it keeps and decide who handles requests for video.

"Hard drives in recorders last as long as the recorder"

A recorder writes video to its drive every minute of every day, and drives wear out. The drive is usually the first part of a recorder to fail, and when it fails the recorder may beep and show "HDD Error", or it may simply carry on showing live pictures while saving nothing.

"A CCTV box never beeps at random," as Security Camera King's guide to recorder alarms puts it. The beep is the recorder's only way of telling you something is wrong, and in a cellar or a back room nobody may hear it.

On a repair we read the drive's health, replace a failing drive with a surveillance-rated one before it takes the footage with it, and send the drive error alarm to your phone so the next warning does not go unheard.

"Anyone handy can repair a camera system"

Plenty of camera faults are simple, and some owners can fix a loose plug or re-aim a camera themselves. But New York State requires companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for other people to be licensed by the Department of State, and an owner hiring a handyman or a relative to work on the system has no one accountable if the footage is lost.

The bigger risk is to the footage. The most expensive mistakes we see are a recorder that was reset to factory settings, a drive that was formatted because the screen asked, or a camera unplugged and moved before anyone saved the clip from the night of the theft.

If footage matters, leave the recorder alone and call. We copy the clip first, and only then start repairing.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

National averages are built from simple jobs: one consumer camera, re-paired to an app. A Remsen Village repair is more often a long yard cable, a recorder in a wet cellar, a switch port after a surge, or a two-family system that has to be traced across two households' power.
