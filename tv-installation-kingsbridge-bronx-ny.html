The Neighborhood

Why Kingsbridge Properties Need TV Installation

Kingsbridge packs an unusual range of buildings into a very small valley, and every one of them puts a different wall behind the TV. Wikipedia gives the neighborhood an area of about 0.31 square miles, roughly bounded by Manhattan College Parkway to the north, the Major Deegan Expressway or Bailey Avenue to the east, West 230th Street to the south and Irwin Avenue to the west, in ZIP code 10463 and Bronx Community Board 8. Inside those lines it lists detached, semi-detached and attached houses alongside apartment buildings. Brick Underground's 2023 guide to the neighborhood adds Tudors, the attached brick houses New Yorkers call "Bronx bricks," co-ops in towers and mid-rises, and very few condos, and it notes that the homes here are "older and have high ceilings." For a TV installer, that sentence is the whole job description. High ceilings, old walls and a mix of building ages mean the bracket in the TV box is rarely the end of the conversation.

The same guide calls the elevated 1 train "the focal point of the neighborhood," and that is where many Kingsbridge TVs start their journey. Best Buy's store at 171 West 230th Street sits in Broadway Plaza, a few blocks from most Kingsbridge apartments, and lists Geek Squad installation, delivery and setup among its services. So a lot of screens here are bought on a Saturday, carried or driven a short distance, and leaned against a wall to wait. That is when the questions start. Geek Squad's own description of its basic TV mounting is a mount on "a standard frame wall" or compatible furniture, with exposed cables neatly dressed. A standard frame wall means drywall over wooden studs. Many Kingsbridge living rooms do not have one. The pre-war apartment houses along Broadway and the side streets are typically plastered, the brick buildings have masonry behind the plaster, and the post-war co-ops can have concrete walls that a wood screw cannot enter.

Residents describe the result in their own words. On r/nyc, a renter in a pre-war building wrote that the walls "are made of plaster" and that they had "no luck finding studs" for a 55-inch TV. On r/DIY, a New Yorker trying to hang a set reported, "When I drill through the wall there is no stud, even though the stud finder hits 16ins apart." A repair company's guide to mounting TVs in New York apartments puts the reason plainly: "Modern stud finders often struggle with plaster because the density fools the sensor," and "For a 55" + TV on plaster, you absolutely need to hit studs — not just wall anchors." None of those posts is from a Kingsbridge address, and we do not claim they are. They describe exactly the walls we expect behind the paint in the older Kingsbridge buildings, and they explain why a TV here should not be hung on a guess.

Then there is who lives here. Most Kingsbridge households in the apartment buildings rent, and renting changes what a TV install can be. Brick Underground's column on landlords and walls advises tenants to read the alteration clause in their lease first, notes that some leases forbid fixing anything to the walls, and says tenants should ask before installing things that cause more damage than a nail, naming "closet systems and TV brackets" specifically. A New York landlord on r/Landlord put the other side bluntly: "NEVER allow wall-mount TVs. If they wish to mount the TV, I would require them to provide evidence of hiring an insured professional." Between those two positions sits most of our Kingsbridge rental work: a bracket that the landlord has agreed to in writing, a cord cover instead of an opened wall, or a TV on its stand with no holes at all. We set out each of those options further down.

Building condition matters too. BuildingHealthX reports that the city's housing agency, HPD, issued 59.9 violations per 100 apartments in the Bronx over the twelve months to September 2026, against 30.1 citywide, with immediately hazardous Class C conditions at 19.8 per 100 apartments against 9.2 citywide. Those are borough figures, not Kingsbridge ones, and they say nothing about any particular building. What they mean for a TV job is practical: in an older Bronx apartment, the wall you want may have water staining, a patched crack or plaster that has come loose from its backing, and a bracket fixed into that is only as good as the patch. When we see damage like that on the probe hole or the tap test, we stop and tell you, and the repair belongs to the landlord, not to the TV.

Finally, Kingsbridge has a busy, loud main street and a very quiet hillside a few blocks away. A resident on r/AskNYC said Broadway's elevated train "can be a little unpleasant (train noise, less sunlight, lots of pigeons)," and another on r/bronx said the area is "pretty quiet just a few blocks from broadway." The Riverdale Press has reported that the MTA is repairing and repainting the elevated steel between West 225th and West 242nd Streets in sections, with the last stretch due by summer 2027, and Bronx Community Board 8 announced structural steel repairs on Broadway from November 5 to November 8, 2026. A TV installed in an apartment facing the tracks lives with that noise, that shade and that work outside the window. A TV installed on a quiet street up the hill toward Riverdale does not. Both get the same price list, but they do not get the same advice about where the screen and the sound should go.

From the Store to the Wall

Bought on West 230th Street, Hung on a Kingsbridge Wall: What the Box Does Not Tell You

Having a big electronics store inside the neighborhood is convenient, and it shapes how Kingsbridge TV jobs arrive. A family picks a screen at Broadway Plaza, the carton goes home in a car or on a hand truck along West 230th Street, and the bracket is either in the same bag or ordered online that evening. By the time we hear about it, three decisions have usually been made already: the size of the TV, the type of bracket, and the wall it is supposed to go on. Our job is to check whether those three decisions fit together in your particular building, before anyone drills a hole that cannot be undone.

The bracket box assumes drywall

Most retail TV brackets ship with lag bolts for wooden studs and a bag of plastic or nylon anchors for anything else. That hardware assumes a modern wall: half-inch drywall over studs set at regular spacing. Geek Squad's published basic mounting service describes the same assumption, a mount "on a standard frame wall/compatible furniture." In a Kingsbridge pre-war apartment, the wall may be plaster on wood or metal lath, plaster laid directly on brick or clay block, or, in the post-war co-ops, concrete. The lag bolts may be too short to reach wood through thick plaster. The plastic anchors may spin in brittle plaster, and they do nothing at all in concrete. We bring fixings for each material and choose them only after the probe hole shows what is there.

The size was chosen in a bright showroom

A screen looks smaller under store lights than it will in a Kingsbridge living room, especially the narrow front rooms of the older apartment houses. Before we mount, we measure from the seats to the wall and look at the ceiling height. High ceilings, which Brick Underground mentions as a feature of the neighborhood's older homes, tempt people to hang the TV high to fill the space. That is the most common placement mistake we correct. A screen should be watched with the head level, not tipped back, and in a room with ten-foot ceilings that usually means the middle of the screen sits close to seated eye height, with empty wall above it. We tape the outline on the wall so you can sit down and judge it before the bracket goes up.

The carton has a right way up

Flat-panel TVs are printed with handling marks on the carton, and most manufacturers want them carried upright, not laid flat across a back seat or carried face-down up a stair. Carrying a large carton upright up a narrow, turning pre-war stair is a two-person job, and on screens of 65 inches and more we plan it that way. If the TV is already home, leave it in its box, upright, against an inside wall away from the radiator until we arrive. If it is still in the store, tell us your floor and whether there is an elevator, and we will tell you whether it is better delivered before the visit or carried in with us on the day.

Renting in Kingsbridge

Getting a Landlord's Yes Before the Bracket Goes Up in a Kingsbridge Rental

Most of the TV calls we expect from the apartment buildings along Broadway, Kingsbridge Avenue, Bailey Avenue and the side streets between them come from tenants. A tenant can own a TV outright and still not have the right to bolt it to the landlord's wall. That is not a reason to give up on a wall mount; it is a reason to sort out permission first, in writing, and to choose the install that matches what the landlord agrees to. Here is how we approach it, based on the sources we have read and on how we work. None of it is legal advice, and your lease is the document that decides.

Start with the lease, not the wall

Brick Underground's advice to a renter whose landlord objected to things on the walls was to "check the alteration provision in your lease first," and it notes that if a restriction is not in the lease, it would not be enforceable. Some leases say tenants cannot affix anything to the walls. Others come with a one-page rider on what can and cannot be done. Many say nothing about TVs specifically but require the apartment to be returned in the condition it was received. Read the clause, then ask. The same column recommends asking permission before installing anything that causes more damage than a nail, and it names TV brackets among them. An email that describes the bracket, the wall, the size of the holes and who is doing the work gives the landlord something concrete to approve.

What a landlord usually wants to know

Landlords worry about three things: a TV falling and damaging the floor or a person, holes they will have to repair, and a stranger drilling into their building without insurance. The landlord on r/Landlord who said he would only allow a wall mount with "evidence of hiring an insured professional" was describing the third worry. We can answer all three. We send the type and number of fixings we expect to use and what they will go into, the approximate size of the holes, and a certificate of insurance when a landlord or managing agent asks for one. We also tell you honestly if the wall you want is one we would not mount on, which tends to reassure a landlord more than any paperwork.

The security deposit question

Tenants ask whether bracket holes will cost them their deposit. The New York Attorney General's tenants' rights guide says a security deposit is limited to one month's rent and that a landlord may use it for the reasonable cost of repairs beyond normal wear and tear. Brick Underground's column draws the line in everyday terms: a nail-sized hole "is fine," while a hole "larger than the size of a nail" is considered damage, and a landlord must give an itemized list of repair charges and return the rest of the deposit within two weeks of the tenant leaving. A TV bracket leaves holes bigger than a nail. So the honest answer is that bracket holes can be charged against a deposit unless the landlord has agreed otherwise. That is one more reason to get the yes in writing, and to ask the landlord whether they want the holes left, filled or the bracket left in place for the next tenant.

Public housing at the Marble Hill end

The Marble Hill Houses, which Wikipedia says were completed in 1952 with four of their eleven towers in Kingsbridge, are run by NYCHA, and NYCHA's house rules apply inside. The rules we read say residents "may not damage, deface or destroy the apartment or any NYCHA property," and that residents "may only install TV antennas inside their apartments," not on the outside of the building. The rules also forbid making holes in the door, frame or walls around the door for a camera or doorbell. They do not set out a specific policy on TV brackets in the version we read, so a resident there should ask the development's management office before booking a wall mount. Until there is an answer, a TV on its stand with an indoor antenna or a streaming device is the install that stays inside the rules.

Stairs and Steps

Walk-Ups, Step Streets and the Climb From Broadway

Kingsbridge is a valley. Broadway runs along the low ground under the el, and the streets rise steeply on both sides, west toward Riverdale and east toward the Deegan. Wikipedia notes that the neighborhood has step streets with as many as 160 steps. Atlas Obscura describes the West 230th Street step street as three staircases connecting Irwin, Johnson, Edgehill and Netherland Avenues, 295 feet long and 38 feet high, the longest step street in the city, with houses whose entrances open directly onto the steps. The nearest subway, it says, is 231st Street. For a TV job, those staircases and the walk-ups beside them change the first half hour of the visit more than any wall does.

Walk-ups without elevators

A handyman who works across the borough wrote that "five- and six-story walk-ups without elevators are everywhere from Kingsbridge to Hunts Point," and asked customers to "tell me your floor upfront." We ask the same thing for the same reason. A 55-inch TV in its carton is awkward for one person on a straight stair and needs two on a turning one. A 75-inch carton up five flights of a pre-war stair, with radiators and banisters at every landing, is a planned lift with two technicians, padded corners and the route cleared before anyone picks it up. Tell us the floor, whether the stair turns, and how wide the landings are. A photo of the stairwell from the bottom helps.

Houses on the steps themselves

A house whose front door is on a public staircase has no curb outside it. The van stops at the nearest street at the top or the bottom of the steps, and everything, the TV, the tools, the bracket and the ladder, is carried the rest of the way. We plan which end is shorter and less steep, and we bring only what the job needs in one trip if we can. In ice or snow, we may ask to move a large-screen visit by a day, because a 65-inch carton on frozen steps is a risk to the screen and to the people carrying it. On dry days, it is simply a longer carry, and we build the time into the booking rather than rushing the wall at the end.

The busy corner at the bottom

The Kingsbridge Historical Society notes that "most area residents know that 231st and Broadway is a busy intersection," describing double-parked cars, buses, pedestrians and left turns. Parking near Broadway is the other half of the access question. A technician who spends twenty minutes circling for a spot near West 231st Street is twenty minutes late to your wall. If your building has a garage, as the 1974 co-op on West 232nd Street does in Brick Underground's listing, or a driveway on a side street, tell us when you book. If not, tell us which side of the block is easier, and we will plan for it.

Under the El

Broadway Apartments Beside the 1 Train: Screens, Sound and the MTA Steel Work

The apartments that face the elevated tracks on Broadway are their own kind of TV job. The windows look onto steel, the light is softer than on the side streets, and every few minutes a train goes by at window height. None of that stops a good install, but it changes where we suggest the screen goes, how the sound is set up and, right now, when we schedule the noisy part of the work.

Sound that keeps up with the trains

A TV's built-in speakers point down or back, and they lose voices first when a train passes. In apartments facing the el, the most useful addition to a wall mount is a soundbar under the screen with its dialogue or speech setting turned on, set from the sofa rather than from a menu at the TV. On the quieter streets west of Broadway, the same soundbar is more often tuned down for the neighbors through the floor. A wall-mounted soundbar is $129 and one placed on a console is $79 on our list, and we set the level from your seat at the end of the visit.

The MTA's steel repairs outside the window

The Riverdale Press reported that the MTA's painting and steel repair project covers about 1.2 miles of elevated structure from West 225th Street to West 242nd Street, the first major rehabilitation since 1988, working north in sections and finishing the last stretch, 240th to 242nd Street, by summer 2027. It said work generally runs from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., that abrasive blasting is done inside containment with noise mitigation, that crosswalks and building access are maintained and that traffic patterns may change. Bronx Community Board 8 also posted that the MTA would perform structural steel repairs on Broadway from November 5 to November 8, 2026. For a TV install inside an apartment, the practical effects are three. Parking on Broadway may be harder on work days, so we plan for the side street. The street outside may be louder than our hammer drill, which actually makes drilling into brick easier on the neighbors. And on blasting days we keep your windows shut while we work and cover the screen and the open carton, so that nothing from outside settles on a new panel. None of it is a reason to postpone, but tell us if the crews are directly outside your building that week.

Brick facing the tracks

The outer walls of the older buildings along Broadway are brick, and the wall facing the tracks is often the one the room is arranged around. Mounting on brick is a hammer drill and masonry anchors set into the face of sound brick, not into the mortar joints, and it adds $65 to the base price. Where the plaster inside is laid straight onto the brick, the anchors pass through it into the masonry. Where it is held off the brick on strips, leaving a gap, we use longer fixings and spacers so the bracket does not crush the plaster. Either way, the probe hole tells us which one it is before the first anchor goes in.

Pre-war apartment houses: plaster first, brick second

The apartment houses are where most Kingsbridge TVs go. Their inside walls are usually plaster, and what lies behind that plaster varies from wall to wall in the same apartment. A partition between two rooms is likely to be plaster on lath over wooden or metal studs. An outer wall, or one shared with the building next door, is likely to be plaster on brick or on hollow clay block. We tap the wall first: plaster on lath sounds hard and a little hollow, plaster on brick sounds dead and flat. Then the probe hole, under the bracket plate, tells us what the bit actually meets. Plaster on lath with sound studs takes lag bolts long enough to reach well into the wood. Plaster on brick takes masonry anchors set into the brick, not the mortar. Clay block, which is hollow inside, takes anchors that open behind its face. The plaster line on our list, $45, covers the extra time and care that plaster needs; the brick line, $65, applies where we are fixing into masonry instead.

Tudor houses: thick plaster, real fireplaces

The Tudor houses on and around Tibbett Avenue are owner-occupied homes, which makes the permission question easy, but they bring the hardest placement questions. The listing Brick Underground describes at 3226 Tibbett Avenue, a 1938 Tudor, has leaded glass, hardwood floors and a fireplace. A fireplace wall in a room like that is tempting for a TV and often wrong for it. A screen over a mantel sits high, and the chimney breast may carry heat. If you want it there anyway, the above-fireplace line is $130 and the brick line applies to the breast. We first check where the flue runs, how warm the breast gets when the fireplace or boiler is in use, and whether the screen would sit at a height you can watch comfortably. Often the better wall is the one across from the windows, where the leaded glass is to your side rather than behind the screen. On those walls the plaster is usually thick and hard, laid by hand on lath, and the probe hole is especially important because stud finders give unreliable readings on it.

Co-op towers: concrete, boards and balconies

The co-ops built in the 1960s and 1970s, like the 1961 tower on Tibbett Avenue with balconies and the 1974 building on West 232nd Street with its own garage, are a different trade again. Walls in buildings of that period are often poured concrete or concrete block, sometimes with drywall or plaster over them and sometimes painted directly. Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Kingsbridge Wall Before It Is Drilled

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's home unless we can document them, so this is the routine our technicians follow, not a tale about one address. It is written for the room Kingsbridge has more of than anything else: a front room in an older apartment house, plaster walls, a radiator under the window and a new TV in its carton against the sofa.

Blankets go down before the carton is opened. We sit where you sit and look at the wall with you, because the height is about your eyes and the ceiling is usually higher than it needs to be. We knock along the wall and listen. A hard, slightly hollow sound with a duller spot every so often suggests plaster on lath and a stud behind the dull spot. A flat, dead sound all the way across suggests plaster straight on brick or block. We run the scanner, then drill one small probe hole where the bracket plate will cover it and watch what comes out on the bit: pale plaster dust and then wood shavings, or plaster dust and then red brick dust, or grey concrete. We look under the window for the radiator pipe and check the wall for an outlet and a cable line. Then we tape the outline of the screen on the wall and step back so you can look. Only when you have agreed does the first real hole go in.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Kingsbridge: Questions Answered

Leases and landlords

Our lease has an alterations clause that never mentions TV brackets. Who decides whether a bracket counts as an alteration?

In practice, the landlord does, which is why we ask you to get an answer before we book a wall mount. Brick Underground's advice is to read the alteration provision first, because a restriction that is not in the lease would not be enforceable, but a clause written broadly enough to cover "alterations" or "fixtures" can reasonably be read to include a bracket bolted into the wall. The same column suggests asking permission before installing anything that makes more than a nail hole and names TV brackets in particular. The simplest route is a short email to the landlord or managing agent: the TV's size, the wall, the type of bracket, the number and size of holes, how the cables will be handled and who is doing the work. We can give you that description in writing. If the answer is yes, keep the email. If it is no, or there is no answer yet, a TV on its stand at $137.99 needs no permission at all and can become a wall mount later.

When we move out, the landlord can deduct for damage beyond normal wear and tear. Would the holes from a TV bracket land on the damage side?

They can, unless you and the landlord agree otherwise. The New York Attorney General's guide says a landlord may use the deposit, which is capped at one month's rent, for the reasonable cost of repairs beyond normal wear and tear. Brick Underground's column puts the line at the size of a nail: a nail hole is fine, anything bigger counts as damage. A typical flat or tilting bracket leaves four to six bolt or anchor holes, larger than a nail, plus the small probe hole we drill behind the plate. Many landlords are happy to keep a properly fixed bracket for the next tenant, and some ask for the holes to be filled. Ask yours which they want and put it in the same email as the permission. The column also notes that the landlord has to give you an itemized list of charges and return the rest of the deposit within two weeks of you moving out.

Kingsbridge rooms that make the wall choice hard

The studio in our 1970s co-op on West 232nd Street has a Murphy bed that folds down from the long wall. Where can the TV go?

Not on the bed wall, because the bed and its cabinet need that wall clear to fold down, and a screen above it would be in the way of the bed frame or too high to watch from the bed. In a studio like the one Brick Underground describes in that building, the usual answers are the side wall facing the bed and the sofa, or a full-motion arm on a short wall that can swing the screen toward the bed at night and back toward the sofa in the day. We check that the arm clears the bed when both are open, that the wall it is fixed to is solid concrete or block rather than a hollow partition, and that the co-op's rules allow the drilling. The cables go into a paintable cord cover, at $59, because a concrete wall will not take an in-wall kit.

Our pre-war living room is L-shaped, with the dining table around the corner from the sofa. Can one TV serve both?

Often, yes, with an extending arm fixed on the wall nearest the corner of the L. Folded flat, the screen faces the sofa. Pulled out and turned, it can face the table for the evening news at dinner. The arm puts more leverage on the wall than a flat bracket, so it has to be fixed into studs or masonry, never just into plaster, and we confirm that with the probe hole before committing to the spot. We also check that the arm, fully extended, does not swing into a doorway or a window frame. If the walls near the corner are weak, the other answer is a TV on the sofa wall and a small second screen in the dining area. We will tell you which we think works better once we have seen the room.

The walls in our 1938 Tudor on Tibbett Avenue are rough, hand-troweled plaster. Does a bracket need anything different on a textured wall?

Two small things. A rough surface means the bracket plate touches the wall only on the high points, so when the bolts are tightened, the plate can rock or crack the ridges under it. We tighten in stages and check that the plate sits evenly, and where the texture is very heavy we shim behind the plate so it bears on the wall evenly. Thick, hand-laid plaster also hides the studs well, so a stud finder's reading on it is a suggestion, not an answer; the probe hole under the plate is what we rely on. The good news is that the texture hides the probe hole and any later patch better than smooth paint does.

The store, the bracket and the el

The store's basic mounting package says it is for a "standard frame wall." Is our Kingsbridge wall one of those?

Probably not, if you live in one of the older buildings. Geek Squad's published description of its basic TV mounting is a mount on "a standard frame wall/compatible furniture," which in the trade means drywall on wooden studs. That is the wall in a newer house or a renovated apartment. In a pre-war Kingsbridge apartment house, the walls are more likely plaster on lath or plaster on brick or clay block; in a post-war co-op tower they may be concrete. None of those is a standard frame wall. If you are not sure, knock on it: drywall sounds light and hollow between studs, old plaster sounds hard, and plaster on masonry sounds flat and dead. Better still, send us a photo and tell us the building's age, and we will tell you which line of our list applies.

The MTA's steel crews are working on the el outside our Broadway windows this month. Should we hold off on the TV install until they move up the line?

There is usually no need. The Riverdale Press reported that the MTA works in sections along the elevated structure, generally between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m., with containment around abrasive blasting and building access kept open, and Community Board 8 announced steel repairs on Broadway from November 5 to November 8, 2026. Inside the apartment, our work is not affected by theirs. What changes is outside: parking on Broadway can be harder, so we plan for the nearest side street, and on days when blasting is going on close by we keep your windows shut and cover the TV while we work.

Screens for Kingsbridge bars

Most of our customers stand when the Gaelic games are on across the road. Does a standing crowd change how high our bar's screens go?

Bar screens for standing crowds go higher, with a tilting bracket angling them down so the picture is not washed out from below, and they are spread so that no part of the room has to look past a pillar or the back of the bar. Our Installation Process

Every Kingsbridge TV job follows the same eight steps, whether it is a 43-inch set in a walk-up bedroom or a row of screens behind a bar on Broadway. In buildings this old, the order matters: most sagging brackets we are called to fix were hung before anyone found out what the wall was.

1. Send the wall and the building

A photo of the wall, the TV model and size, the street and floor, and whether you rent, own, hold co-op shares or run a business. We match the job to the Bronx price list before you book.

2. Settle permission and access

The landlord's, board's or NYCHA office's answer in writing, the stairs or elevator, the super, the nearest place to park and any MTA work on Broadway that day.

3. Read the wall

A tap test, a scan, and one small probe hole where the bracket plate will hide it, to confirm plaster on lath, plaster on brick or block, concrete or drywall.

4. Tape the screen and agree the height

The TV's outline taped on the wall, checked from the sofa, the bed or the bar, and agreed with you before any real hole is drilled.

5. Fix the bracket

Into studs or masonry with fixings rated for that material, tightened in stages on textured plaster, and levelled to true rather than to an old floor.

6. Run the cables

Through an in-wall rated kit in a hollow wall, under a paintable cord cover on brick, concrete or a rental wall, or down into a console, exactly as booked.

7. Hang the TV and add the sound

Two people lift the screen onto the bracket; then the soundbar, streaming device and cable box are connected and the cords strapped out of sight.

8. Test and hand over

Every input tested from your seat, remotes and settings shown, dust cleaned up, and a note left of what the bracket is fixed into for the day you move or upgrade.
