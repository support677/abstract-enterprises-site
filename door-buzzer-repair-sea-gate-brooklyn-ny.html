The Neighborhood

Why Sea Gate Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Sea Gate is the only part of Brooklyn where a visitor passes a manned gate before reaching your front door, and that is exactly why so many of its buzzers get neglected until they fail outright. The Sea Gate Association, formed in 1899, runs the community as a private corporation at the western tip of Coney Island. Its website tells residents to use the Neptune Avenue and Surf Avenue entrances and sends guests to a visitors lane at Surf Avenue, and a 2013 Kings County court decision describes the same thing: access only through manned security gates. With that much attention on the gate, owners tend to think of the house's own bell as an afterthought. It isn't. The gate decides who reaches the street. It has no idea which of the four households in a converted house a guest has come to see, and it cannot open your front door.

The houses explain the rest. Wikipedia describes Sea Gate as mostly single-family, roughly 850 houses in styles from Queen Anne to Mediterranean by a New York Times count, and cityneighborhoods.nyc writes of large frame houses with wraparound porches and turrets. But the same Wikipedia entry, citing a broker, puts the share of rentals at 54 percent, and current listings show what that means at the front door: a brick two-family on Surf Avenue laid out two bedrooms over two bedrooms, a house on Maple Avenue sold as a legal four-dwelling, a four-family on Neptune Avenue, and a three-story five-family built in 2014. Every one of those houses has one front door shared by several households, and every one of them depends on a button, a wire and a release that work every time a tenant's guest walks up the porch steps.

Then there is the water. The National Weather Service's account of Sandy's coastal flooding lists major flooding and structural damage in Sea Gate, and CBS New York, reporting from a storm-damaged house there afterwards, described dead wires hanging frayed from a ceiling. Bell wire is the thinnest, cheapest cable in a house, it usually runs along the cellar ceiling or the porch framing, and in October 2012 much of it sat in salt water. Some of it was replaced in the rebuild. A great deal of it was dried out, twisted back together and closed up behind new drywall. We cannot prove that a given fault dates from the storm, and we won't claim it does, but corroded splices, green terminals and bell circuits that work in dry weather and fail in wet weather are among the most common things we find when we open a Sea Gate house.

Finally, Sea Gate has no stores of its own, according to Wikipedia, and no hardware counter around the corner. A buzzer repair here is a planned visit: the resident registers the technician at the gate, the right parts come in the van, and the people who need to be home are home. Owners who also want the porch watched while they are away often pair a buzzer repair with a camera at the door, and we keep the two systems separate in every report.

The Visit

Two Locks Between the Street and Your Hall: Planning a Buzzer Repair Behind the Sea Gate Entrance

Every Sea Gate house has two layers of entry, and only one of them is yours. The first belongs to the Association: the manned gates, the turnstiles, the Pedestrian Access Key each resident carries and the Tap2Open system that guests are entered into. The Association's 2026 beach application even notes that beach access is added to a resident's pedestrian key remotely, which tells you the community already runs its own electronic credentials. None of that is part of a door buzzer repair. We don't service the Association's gates or its visitor system, and nothing we do at your house connects to them. The second layer is the one we fix: the button or panel on your porch, the wire to each household, the handsets or chimes inside, the release, and the strike, latch and closer on your own front door.

The first layer still shapes the visit. A technician cannot simply pull up and knock. The Association asks residents to register guests, and the visitors lane at Surf Avenue is where they are checked in. One commenter in a Reddit r/AskNYC thread about Sea Gate put the practical risk plainly: "Depending on the guard you may not be let in." We have found no published Association rule written specifically for contractors or service vendors, so we don't guess at one. What we ask is simple: when you book, register the technician as your guest the way the Association tells residents to, and if you have any doubt about how a service visit should be cleared, check with the Main Office before the day.

Who registers the technician matters in a divided house. In a two-family where the owner lives downstairs, the owner normally books and clears the visit. In a house where the owner lives elsewhere, as many Sea Gate landlords do, the tenant may be the only person who can register a guest, while only the owner can approve the price. So settle the roles before the day: who clears us at the gate, who opens the cellar, who is home on each floor, and who signs off on the price.

The Association's 2024 Rules and Regulations restrict construction to weekdays between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m. and require special permission on weekends. A buzzer repair is small, quiet work, usually a few hours with hand tools, and it isn't the kind of alteration project those rules were written for, but how the office treats a service visit is the Association's call, not ours. The same rules bar weekend construction within 300 feet of a house of worship. We book early-day weekday windows inside the gate by default, and we'll schedule around whatever the office tells you.

Storm Damage

Fourteen Years After Sandy: Tracing the Buzzer Faults the Water Left Behind

The storm reached Sea Gate from more than one side. VIN News, reporting from inside the gate on October 31, 2012, described facades torn off, houses pulled from their foundations, Atlantic Avenue covered in sand and debris, and three feet of water in one house. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2015 that Sandy had damaged or destroyed most of the bulkhead and carried sand and debris into streets, homes and sewers, and that federal money for a first phase of repairs covered about 1,177 feet of it between Beach 48th and Beach 50th Streets. Their bell circuits were rebuilt with them, patched, or not touched at all.

That history produces a kind of buzzer fault we see far more often behind the gate than in the brownstone neighborhoods: the slow, intermittent failure. A splice that sat in salt water and was dried out and taped back together doesn't fail on the day. It corrodes from the inside, its resistance climbs, and the bell starts to ring faintly, then only in dry weather, then only some days. A terminal on a transformer that was underwater grows a green crust that makes and breaks contact as the house warms and cools. A strike whose coil was submerged may work for years and then start to hum and stick. None of these is certain to be a storm fault, and no source ties any particular Sea Gate buzzer failure to Sandy, but when a house took water and the bell has been unreliable ever since, it is the first place we look.

The repair follows the water. We start at the lowest point the wire passes, usually the cellar ceiling, a crawl space or the framing under the porch, and work upward. Every splice we can reach gets opened, not just tested from the ends, because a corroded joint can pass a quick continuity check and still fail under the load of a ringing bell and a held strike. Where the copper inside has turned dark or green, we cut back to bright metal and remake the joint with a gel-filled connector in a box that can be reached again. The transformer or power supply is measured at rest and with the strike held, and if its terminals are corroded or it has been running hot, we replace it rather than clean it. We also check whether the transformer is still mounted where the water reached. If it is, we price moving it higher as a separate line, because that is new work, not repair, and the choice is yours.

Repair or replace after water damage

Owners often ask whether a flooded system is worth saving at all. The answer depends on how much of it the water reached and how it was handled afterwards. Cable that was submerged briefly, dried and left with its jacket intact can often keep working once its splices are remade; cable whose jacket is cracked, or that has been carrying salt inside it for years, usually can't. Parts that never got wet are rarely the problem. A transformer or supply that was underwater should be replaced on principle. We put all of that in writing so you can see which parts of the system are sound and which are not, and if the honest answer is that a door buzzer replacement would cost less than chasing faults through the whole house, the report says so.

6sqft wrote in 2022 that Sea Gate was still rebuilding. If a contractor is about to open a ceiling or a porch, have the bell wire marked first, so a door buzzer repair after construction doesn't become the last job on the list.

Shared Houses

One Porch, Two to Five Households: Repairing Buttons and Releases in Sea Gate's Divided Houses

The Village Voice's 2005 profile of Sea Gate described "stand-alone three-story houses" along Surf Avenue and the Beach streets. Many of them no longer hold one household. Redfin lists a Maple Avenue house as "Legal Four dwellings," StreetEasy lists a four-family on Neptune Avenue, Homes.com shows a two-family brick house on Surf Avenue with two bedrooms on each floor and a three-story five-family built in 2014, and another Redfin listing describes a two-family brick house on a 22-by-100 lot with a rental on the ground floor and another unit on the second. The pattern behind those listings is the one that produces most of our Sea Gate buzzer calls: an owner on one floor, one to four rentals on the others, and a single porch where every visitor starts.

In houses like these the buzzer was rarely designed. It was added when the house was divided, then extended when another unit was created, then patched when something failed. So the faults are less about worn-out parts than about a system nobody has mapped. A button labeled for the second floor rings the ground floor because two pairs were swapped at a splice during the last renovation. A tenant can hear the porch but can't release the door, because only the owner's floor was ever wired for release. The top-floor unit's chime is faint because it is the last of four devices on an undersized transformer. A button that rings nothing turns out to be wired to a unit that was merged with another years ago. Each sounds the same on the phone and needs a different fix.

So in a divided house we map first and repair second. With someone on each floor, we press every button and note which household actually answers; we test whether each one can talk to the porch and release the door; and we follow each pair from the porch to the transformer and up to its unit. Because several households often share conductors, a fault in one unit can silence another, which means we need access to every household on the line during the visit, not just the one that complained. Once the map is confirmed, we label every button and every pair the way the owner wants, so the next tenant, the next contractor and the next technician start with a plan instead of a guess.

When the button count and the household count disagree

A house that is legally three or four apartments but has two buttons on the porch is common in older parts of Brooklyn, and Sea Gate is no exception. Sometimes the extra units share a button by arrangement; sometimes one was never given its own. Adding a button, a pair and a release for a household that never had one is new work rather than repair, and we price it separately, but we flag it on every visit because it is the single most common reason tenants in divided houses end up propping the front door or giving out keys. The reverse happens too: a plate with five buttons on a house that is now two households, with three buttons wired to nothing. We take dead buttons out of the circuit, cap and label the abandoned pairs, and if the owner wants, blank them on the plate so visitors stop pressing them.

The Front Door

Wind, Salt and Heavy Old Doors: Strikes, Closers and Latches on the Water Side

Sea Gate has water on three sides: the Atlantic to the south, the Lower Bay and the Narrows to the west, Gravesend Bay to the north. A porch door on Atlantic Avenue faces the ocean; one on the north side faces the bay; almost none is more than a few blocks from open water. That exposure works on a front door's hardware in ways a door on a sheltered brownstone block never sees. Steel strikes and keepers rust in their pockets. Brass and plated buttons pit and lose contact. Closers lose their adjustment as wind loads the door every time it opens. And a front door that the wind pushes against its frame can hold a perfectly good strike shut, so the buzzer sounds, the strike releases, and the door still doesn't move until someone leans on it.

That is why every Sea Gate buzzer repair we do on a front door includes the door. Before we condemn a strike, we test it with the door pushed and pulled while the release is held, measure the voltage at the strike rather than at the transformer, and look at the latch, keeper, hinges and closer together. On older frame houses the cause is often the frame itself: a shifted jamb, a rusted hinge screw that lets the door drop, or a keeper painted over until its lip catches the latch. Often the fix is an adjustment rather than a part: a keeper moved a few millimeters, a closer slowed or given more latch speed so it pulls the door past the wind, a hinge screw replaced so the door stops sagging. Where the strike itself has failed, we match the replacement to the existing pocket, so an old wood jamb is not cut larger than it needs to be.

On replacement parts, salt air changes the shopping list. Where a strike, button or plate has to be replaced on a porch that faces the water, we choose stainless or sealed parts where they exist for that system, use stainless fasteners, seal the cable entry behind the button, and leave a drip loop so water runs away from the terminals rather than into them.

Power cuts, storms and the strike

A door buzzer not working after a power outage is one of the most common calls from waterfront neighborhoods, and the cause is usually simple once it is found: a tripped breaker or blown fuse on the circuit that feeds the transformer, a plug-in supply that didn't come back, or a transformer that failed when the power returned with a surge. We check the feed first, then the transformer or supply, then the strike and the stations. If a surge did damage, the report lists every failed part.

Outages also raise the question of which kind of strike belongs on the door. A fail-secure strike, as Allegion's guide explains, stays locked when power is lost, while the lever or knob still lets people out from inside; a fail-safe strike unlocks when the power goes. On a house's street door, fail-secure is usually the right choice, because a power cut shouldn't leave the house unlocked during a storm. A magnetic lock is a different matter, because it releases without power and needs a separate way out; if your door has one, we check its release and its exit device before anything else. Whatever is on the door, we explain the choice before we replace it.

The Rules

Who Owes a Working Buzzer Behind the Gate: The City's Rules, the Tenant's Route and the Owner's Record

Sea Gate's streets are private, but its houses are still New York City housing, and the city's rules for apartments apply inside the gate the same way they do on the other side of West 37th Street. The NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide sets out the starting point: multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that buzzes the door open. Older multiple dwellings must have self-locking doors as well, and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments ask for one.

Most Sea Gate houses are one- or two-family, which the city does not treat as multiple dwellings. A three-, four- or five-family house usually is one, and so are the larger buildings on Surf and Neptune Avenues. For those, keeping an existing buzzer working is part of the owner's general duty to keep the building in repair under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code; HPD writes buzzer defects under that section with wording such as "properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system from the building entrance door to panel." NYC 311's apartment maintenance article lists "doorbells, buzzers, or intercoms that are broken or missing" among the conditions a tenant can report, and HPD's own guidance tells tenants to contact the landlord first and to file with 311 if the landlord doesn't respond. We are not lawyers, and a particular house's obligations depend on its legal use and history, but the practical rule is clear: if the house has tenants and a buzzer, keep the buzzer working and keep a record.

The door is part of that. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C violation that has to be corrected within 14 days of the notice, and it reinspects within 20 days of the correction date. A door the wind keeps from latching is exactly that kind of defect.

Former hotels and larger buildings

The two Abraham Residences that Met Council lists in Sea Gate, 75 single rooms at 3915 Neptune Avenue and 41 at 3811 Surf Avenue, are a different kind of building from the houses around them. Both were hotels before they became housing for people 55 and older, and buildings of that kind often have a front entrance controlled from a desk or office as well as from the rooms. A repair there is organized more like a small apartment building: the managing organization approves the work, staff arrange access to the equipment room and to every affected room, and the release from the desk is tested along with the street panel and the door. For older residents the release timing matters, so we check how long the door stays unlocked after a buzz and lengthen it where the equipment allows.

If you have an HPD order or a 311 service request number for any Sea Gate building, send it when you book. We repair to what the order describes, test the same function at the neighboring units on the line, and leave the owner a record that matches the order's wording.

How the building type changes the repair

The large frame house usually has the simplest system and the longest wires. A button on the porch, a transformer in the cellar and a chime or bell in a back hall can be fifty feet or more apart, and the top floor of a three-story house with a turret room often can't hear the bell at all. Faults are typically a corroded button, a tired transformer or a splice under the porch; a second chime for the top floor is new work, priced separately.

The brick two-family is where we most often find buttons swapped, a tenant with no release, or two households sharing one chime by accident. The fix is mostly mapping and labeling, plus whatever part has failed.

The three-, four- and five-family house is a small building system. Several households hang on shared conductors, so one unit's station can silence another, and the release may be wired from some floors and not others. We need access to every household on the line during the visit, and because these houses are usually multiple dwellings under city law, the door's self-closing and self-locking function is part of every job.

The house built or rebuilt since the storm often has a video door station or an app-based panel rather than a plain bell. When one of those fails, we check power, network connection, the app account and the panel's door relay before blaming the hardware, then the strike and the latch, because a working release on a binding door looks exactly like a broken app.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Sea Gate: Questions Answered

Houses shared with tenants

Our tenant's guests are checked in at the Surf Avenue lane and walk to our porch, but they say her button does nothing, while it rings fine when she tests it with us standing there. What makes a button fail only for visitors?

Usually the way it is pressed. A button with corroded contacts often needs a firm, held press to make contact, and a resident who knows the house leans on it while a visitor taps it once and waits. A faded label that sends visitors to the wrong button is the other common cause. We test the button with a quick, light press as well as a firm one, open it to check the contacts and the wire behind it, and replace it with a sealed button if the contacts are pitted, then relabel the plate so every household's name can be read from the steps.

Our house on Maple Avenue is a legal four-family, but two of the apartments share one porch button. Do we have to give each household its own?

Whether the law requires it depends on the house's legal use and history: the Attorney General's guide ties the full intercom requirement to buildings converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments, and to older buildings when a majority of the apartments ask. In practice, a shared button means one household answers for another's visitors, and that is how doors end up propped. A separate button, pair and release for each household is new work rather than repair, so we price it as its own line, usually alongside whatever repair brought us to the house.

Our five-family was built in 2014, and its video entry panel now shows the porch on every unit's screen while only some units can talk back. Is a panel that young worth repairing?

Almost always. When video reaches every unit but voice only reaches some, the panel and the power are working, and the fault is in the audio side of the units that fail: a station's microphone or speaker, a loose conductor at a station, or the audio pair to that floor. We test each unit's talk path separately, repair or replace the failing stations with ones built for that system, and check whether the panel model is still supported, so the owner knows what to expect from it over the next few years.

Water, salt and storms

Our house took water in 2012, and the bell has rung faintly ever since, worse after rain. Can a splice from the rebuild still be failing fourteen years later?

Yes, and it is one of the most common things we find inside the gate. A joint that sat in salt water and was dried and taped back together corrodes slowly from the inside, so its resistance creeps up and it fails first in damp weather. We open every splice from the lowest point the wire passes, cut back to clean copper where it has darkened, remake the joints in sealed connectors inside reachable boxes, and measure the bell under load before and after so the improvement can be seen, not just heard.

Our Atlantic Avenue porch faces the ocean, and the strike in our front door has rusted out twice in five years. Is there a strike that holds up better that close to the water?

There are strikes with stainless faceplates and keepers, and some makers offer sealed or weather-rated versions for exterior doors; whether one fits depends on the door, the frame pocket and the voltage your system supplies. Just as important is keeping water out of the pocket: a sealed cable entry, a drip loop on the wire, and a frame that is painted and caulked around the strike rather than left open. We price a strike that suits the door and the exposure, and show you the alternatives before you choose.

During our post-storm rebuild, a contractor left a coil of old wires above the front hall ceiling, and nobody knows which, if any, belongs to the bell. Is it safe to close them up, and can you tell which one is live?

Old bell and intercom wire carries low voltage, so the risk is less about shock than about confusion: a live pair left loose can short against something later, and a dead one gets mistaken for the bell by the next contractor. We test each conductor to find which, if any, still connects the button, the strike or a station, trace the others as far as they go, and confirm they carry nothing before they are cut back. Live wire gets a proper splice in a box; dead wire gets capped and labeled as abandoned.

Doors and weather

Our glazed porch door rattles in its frame during storms, and twice the strike has let the door blow open without anyone pressing release. Can wind really open a buzzer strike?

Wind can't energize a strike, but it can defeat a worn one. If the strike's keeper has worn or its locking mechanism has loosened, or the latch only catches the edge of the keeper because the door has shrunk or sagged, a hard gust can push the latch out. A strike set to fail-safe will also release if a storm cuts the power. We check which type is on the door, how far the latch engages, and the condition of the keeper and hinges, then tighten the fit or replace the strike so the door holds against a gust.

Larger buildings and institutions

In our building, a former hotel, the street panel still lists rooms by the numbers they had before the conversion. Can the directory be matched to the way rooms are numbered now?

Usually, yes. On an electronic directory panel the listing is a setting, so once we can sign in or reach the panel's programming, each entry can be renamed or renumbered to match the current rooms. On an older button panel, each button is wired to a pair, so we confirm with someone at each room which button actually reaches it before relabeling. Either way, we test every entry against every room and give the managing organization a printed list that matches the doors.

Buying, renting and the paperwork

We are buying a three-family inside the gate, and the seller describes the buzzer as "mostly working." What should a pre-closing test cover?

Every button from the porch to its household, voice in both directions, release from every unit with the door pushed and pulled, the strike, latch and closer, and the transformer or supply measured under load. In a Sea Gate house we also look at where the transformer and splices sit relative to any old water line on the cellar wall, and open a sample of splices to check for corrosion. The written report tells you what works, what doesn't and what each repair would cost from the Brooklyn list, which is useful in a negotiation.

Our tenants' lease starts in June, and the house has been closed since the fall. What should a check before the new tenants arrive include?

The same three functions in every unit, ringing, talking and release, plus a look at anything that sits outside or below the living floors through a winter by the water: the porch button and its seal, the strike and latch, the closer, and the transformer and splices in the cellar. Houses that sit closed often come back with a chime adapter unplugged, a breaker tripped or a button seized by salt. Booking door buzzer repair before tenant move-in costs less than a call from a tenant on the first weekend.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Sea Gate

AI summaries, national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and vendor blogs are where many Sea Gate owners start, and few of them picture a divided frame house near the ocean behind a manned gate. Here are seven claims we see repeated and how they hold up inside the gate.

"A gated community doesn't really need a working door buzzer."

It sounds reasonable: if guards check every visitor, why worry about the front door?

But the gate and the door do different jobs. The Association's gates decide who reaches Sea Gate's streets; they don't know which household in a four-family house a guest wants, and they can't open your door. With 54 percent of homes rented, according to Wikipedia, many Sea Gate houses have several households behind one porch.

In those houses, the buzzer is what lets each household receive its own visitors without the front door being propped, and for the houses that count as multiple dwellings, city rules on self-closing, self-locking doors apply inside the gate as anywhere else.

"Most intercom repairs cost $150 to $400."

Swiftlane's 2026 cost guide says minor repairs typically run $150 to $400, and summaries repeat that range as if it were a price.

"Intercom repair" covers everything from one corroded button to a storm-damaged circuit that has to be traced through a cellar, a rebuilt hall and three occupied floors. No summary can tell you which one you have.

Our Brooklyn list puts the service call at $250 with diagnosis included, single components from $250 to $950, a panel repair at $650 to $1,800 and line or riser work at $850 to $2,400. The written price after diagnosis is the only number that applies to your house.

"If the house flooded, the whole buzzer system has to be replaced."

After Sandy, plenty of owners were told exactly that, and some systems really were beyond saving.

But a flood doesn't damage every part equally. Buttons and stations on the upper floors were often never wet, and cable that was submerged briefly and kept its jacket can keep working once its splices are remade. What should be replaced on principle is anything electrical that sat in salt water: transformers, supplies and the joints themselves.

We test before anyone assumes the system has to go, price only the parts the water actually damaged, and say plainly when replacement would cost less than repair.

"A wireless doorbell is the easy fix for a dead buzzer."

Battery video doorbells and plug-in wireless chimes are sold as a way around old wiring, and for a single-family house that only needs a bell, one can be a sensible stopgap.

In a divided Sea Gate house it rarely solves the problem. A wireless doorbell rings a phone or a chime; it doesn't release the strike in the front door, so the tenant on the third floor still has to walk down.

If the existing wire tests sound, repairing the wired system usually gives every household ringing, voice and release for less, and a wired video station can use the same wire where the system allows.

"Only buildings with eight or more apartments have to keep an intercom working."

Summaries often compress the rule into that one number. The Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide ties the eight-apartment threshold to buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968, and requires an intercom in older multiple dwellings when a majority of apartments ask.

That leaves out what matters most in Sea Gate's three-, four- and five-family houses: once a building has a buzzer, HPD cites its upkeep under the Housing Maintenance Code's general duty to keep the building in repair, and 311 accepts tenant complaints about broken doorbells and buzzers.

We are not lawyers, and a particular house's obligations depend on its legal use. The practical point is that a buzzer which exists is expected to work, and a repair record is the simplest way to show it does.

"Switch to a cloud intercom and the wiring problems disappear."

ButterflyMX markets its call box as requiring no building wiring, and for the link between a panel and each apartment that is broadly the point.

But the door doesn't disappear. The strike, the closer, the power supply and the wire from the panel to the lock are still there and still fail, and on a porch facing the ocean they fail sooner. Swiftlane puts software for these systems at $5 to $15 per unit per month, and the panel depends on internet service and an administrator account.

When a Sea Gate house already has a networked panel and it stops working, we check power, network, account and settings before blaming the hardware, and the door hardware before blaming any of them.

"A new intercom costs about $2,500, so an old one isn't worth repairing."

HomeAdvisor gives a national average of around $2,500 to install an intercom system, and summaries use that figure to argue that repairing an old one is wasted money.

In a Sea Gate two- or three-family the comparison rarely works that way. A national installation average says nothing about a house where the cable is sound and one corroded splice or one tired transformer has failed.

We diagnose first. If the supply, the buttons and most stations are sound, repairing the failed part is cheaper and faster. If the water got into everything, we say so.

Whichever door visitors are meant to pass first when nobody comes down. If the storm door locks, the strike usually belongs on it, with the inner door left on its key; if the storm door is never locked, the inner door is the one to release. Many old porches end up with the strike on the inner door and a storm door that has started locking on its own, which leaves visitors stuck between the two. How many hours should both households in our two-family plan to be home for the visit?

I live in one of the senior residences on Neptune Avenue and my room's intercom is dead. Can I book a repair myself?

Our tenants' handsets are a mix of three different brands added over the years. Do they all have to match?

Our chime rings on its own on foggy nights when nobody is on the porch. What causes a phantom ring?
+

Usually moisture. When fog or spray gets into a worn button or a corroded splice, it can bridge the two conductors just enough to make the chime ring, and the ringing stops when the air dries out. A pinched wire that touches its neighbor in damp weather does the same. We open the button and the splices nearest the porch, look for corrosion or damaged insulation, and replace the button with a sealed one if water has been getting in.
