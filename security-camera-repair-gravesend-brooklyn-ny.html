Houses with a garage below

Attached and semi-detached brick houses with the garage set into the front at street level and the living floors above. The recorder is often in the garage, and cables pass through the garage ceiling and around the overhead door.

Two-families, past and present

1920s houses that became two-families in the Depression, and some that have since gone back to one. Systems were often put in by each household separately, and two recorders or two apps on one house are common.

Large houses north of Avenue U

The big houses built since the 1990s in the northeast blocks, often behind fences, hedges and gates, with stucco walls, tile roofs and twenty or more cameras wired to a rack in the basement.

Ocean Parkway apartment buildings

Prewar and postwar apartment houses and co-ops along the parkway and its service roads, with long lobbies, laundry rooms and basement corridors, and a recorder in the super's office or a locked basement room.

Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue buildings

Larger apartment buildings and co-ops on the southern edge near Gravesend Bay and the Belt Parkway, with garages, roof cameras exposed to wind off the water, and long runs between floors.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Two- and three-story buildings on Avenue U, Kings Highway, McDonald Avenue and 86th Street with a shop on the ground floor and apartments above, where the store's cameras and the building's cameras often share a cellar and sometimes a recorder.

Garage-below houses: the recorder shares a room with the car

On a great many Gravesend blocks the house is built with the garage set into the front at street level, a short driveway or apron in front of it, and the living floors above. When cameras went in, the installer usually put the recorder, the PoE switch or the power box in the garage, because that is where the cables from the front, the driveway and the side path come together and where the electric meter and the internet line often enter. That choice makes the first install easy and every later year harder. A garage is cold in January and hot in August, dust from the street blows in each time the door opens, and the car brings in water and road salt in winter. Hard drives in a recorder sitting on a garage shelf run hotter in summer and see condensation on cold mornings; plug-in power adapters on a garage outlet are the first thing a surge reaches.

The cables have their own problem in a garage: the overhead door. Camera runs are often stapled across the garage ceiling or down the wall beside the door track, and over the years the door, its opener, its springs or a ladder hung on the wall rub, crush or snag them. A driveway camera that flickers whenever the door moves, or that died the week a new opener went in, almost always has a damaged cable within a few feet of the track. On a repair we check that stretch first. When the recorder has to stay in the garage, we lift it off the floor, give it a ventilated spot away from the door, put it and the switch on a battery backup, and route the cables in a protected path away from the moving parts. When the owner would rather move it into the house, we price the move as part of the repair.

Two-families: one house, two systems, two sets of logins

Gravesend's 1920s one-family houses were widely converted into two-families during the Depression, and some have been converted back. That history shows up in the camera systems. In a working two-family, the cameras may run on the upstairs apartment's outlet or the downstairs tenant's internet, so they go dark when that apartment's power or router does. In a house that went back to one family, there are often two recorders, two apps and two sets of cables, each put in by a different household, with nobody quite sure which camera belongs to which box.

The repair starts by drawing the house as it is now: which cameras exist, which recorder each one feeds, whose outlet powers what, and whose phone gets the alerts. Then we move power and network onto the owner's side where that is practical, so a change in one apartment does not take down the house, and where a house has two recorders that no longer make sense, we price combining them onto one. Inheritance is the other recurring issue. When the relative who set up the system is gone, and so is the password, a recorder can often be recovered on site, but some makers require proof of ownership or a reset that wipes settings. We tell you which route your recorder needs before anything is erased.

Large houses north of Avenue U: big systems, built by someone else

The northeast part of Gravesend, around Avenue S, Avenue T and Avenue U between Ocean Parkway and McDonald Avenue, has some of the largest houses in Brooklyn. Many were built in the 1990s and since, often after a smaller house was torn down, with Mediterranean or Spanish-style stucco walls, terra-cotta tile roofs and fences, hedges or gates around a lot the house nearly fills. Their camera systems are bigger than anything else in the neighborhood: twenty or more cameras, a rack in a basement mechanical room with a recorder, one or two PoE switches, the home network and sometimes a home automation controller, all put in by the builder's low-voltage contractor.

Three faults dominate in these houses. First, nobody in the family knows what each box on the rack does, and the contractor who built it may be gone, so a single failed switch takes out half the cameras and the family has no idea where to start. Second, the cameras share the house network with everything else, so a busy evening of streaming and video calls can make cameras stutter and drop out of the app. Third, cameras mounted under tile eaves and on stucco are hard to reach without a tall ladder, and the cables often run across or under the roof tiles. A repair here begins with a map of the rack and every camera, labels on every cable, and a separate network for the cameras where the equipment allows it. Work under a tile roof is done from a ladder whenever possible; we do not walk on clay tiles unless there is no other way, and we tell you before we do.

Ocean Parkway apartment buildings: lobbies, laundry rooms and a super's office

The apartment houses and co-ops along Ocean Parkway's Gravesend stretch have the same few camera locations in building after building: the outer and inner lobby doors, the mailbox wall, the elevator, the laundry room, the basement corridor to the boiler room and the side or back door to the trash area. The recorder sits in the super's office or a locked basement room, and the cameras were usually added over decades by more than one company. The failures follow from that. Coax cameras from the 2000s run beside network cameras from the 2020s, some on a recorder and some on nothing at all; drives are full and set to overwrite in a few days; and the camera in the laundry room or the back corridor hangs low enough to be reached.

The 2021 laundry-room burglary near East 3rd Street and Avenue W, where the man covered the camera before breaking into the machines, is the pattern we design repairs around in these buildings. The camera in a laundry room or a service corridor should be high, should see the person coming before they reach it, and ideally should be watched by a second camera from the door. We also check what the board or managing agent needs from the system: how many days of video they want kept, who is allowed to export clips, and how the building answers a request for footage. Brick Underground has noted that landlords generally are not obligated to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and city buildings are not required to have working cameras at all, so those decisions belong to the board or owner, in writing.

Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue buildings: wind, garages and long runs

The larger apartment buildings at the south end of Gravesend, near the Belt Parkway and the water, add height and weather. Roof and upper-floor cameras face wind off Gravesend Bay, garages under or beside the buildings have cameras on ramps and in parking rows, and cable runs between the basement and the roof pass through risers that plumbers, electricians and internet installers also use.

Water is the other enemy at this end of the neighborhood. The National Weather Service's account of Hurricane Sandy recorded inundation reaching inland to Avenue X in Bensonhurst, Gravesend and Sheepshead Bay. A recorder or switch on a basement floor at this end of Gravesend is one storm away from a failure, and part of the repair is getting it onto a shelf or into a room above the floor, with its battery backup beside it.

Field Notes: Repairing a Gravesend Camera System From the Garage Out

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical Gravesend garage-below house, two-family or small apartment building when several cameras are out and nobody is sure why. It is our working order, not a story about one address.

Copy before diagnosing. If anyone needs footage from a recent night, the technician exports it first, before restarting or unplugging anything, and notes the recorder's clock against the real time.

Start where the recorder sits. Price before parts, and leave a record. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, the technician checks every camera by day and after dark, turns on alerts for lost video and drive errors, and leaves you the logins and a list of what is connected where.

Fault by Fault

Storms, Garages, Reach, Daylight Motion and Lost Logins: Why Gravesend Camera Systems Fail

Five causes account for most of the camera failures we find on Gravesend houses, buildings and storefronts. Knowing which one you are dealing with is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Storms: trees, overhead lines and the moment power returns

The July 2026 storm on West 8th Street is the Gravesend version of a story we hear across south Brooklyn: a tree comes down, the lines come with it, and the block sits without power while crews work around live wires. A camera system takes three kinds of damage from an event like that. The recorder may have been writing when power dropped, which can corrupt its drive and leave it asking to be set up again. The surge when power returns can kill adapters, power boxes and PoE switch ports, so some cameras come back and others never do. And a falling branch can rip down a camera, or the cable strung from the house to a garage or a fence post. A battery backup that rides through short outages and shuts the recorder down cleanly in long ones prevents most of the first two. The third is a repair: a new run on a protected route, not strung through the trees.

Garages: heat, cold, dust and a door that moves

The garage is the most common recorder location on Gravesend houses and the hardest on equipment. Drives that run hot all summer and see condensation in winter fail sooner; adapters plugged into a garage outlet shared with a freezer or a compressor take every spike; and the overhead door wears through cables mounted near its track. When the recorder has to stay there, the repair is to lift it, ventilate it, put it on backup power and move the cables out of the door's way. When it can move, a closet inside the house is a better home.

Reach: a camera someone can cover is the first thing they deal with

The 2021 laundry-room burglary near Avenue W showed what happens to a camera at arm's height: it recorded the man until he reached up and covered it. In laundry rooms, vestibules, back corridors and small shops, a camera mounted low on a grid or a wall is easy to cover, turn or knock off. The repair is rarely more cameras. It is moving the camera higher and farther from where people stand, aiming a second camera so the first is in its view, and turning on the tamper or video-loss alert so someone hears about a covered lens the same night.

Daylight motion: why a clear day can still give you a smeared plate

Owners are often surprised that a camera which shows a sharp parked car can turn a slowly moving truck into a blur in broad daylight. On r/Brooklyn, a poster in a thread tagged as a Gravesend package theft wrote, "I have surveillance footage but unfortunately no clear plate yet." Two settings usually explain it. Many cameras are set to a slow shutter so the picture looks bright, which smears anything moving, and many record the sidewalk at a wide angle where a plate is only a few pixels across. A repair can raise the shutter speed, narrow the view of a varifocal camera onto the curb, or add a camera aimed only at the lane where vehicles pass. A camera that shows people well and a camera that reads plates are usually set up differently.

Lost logins: the system works, but nobody can get in

A large share of Gravesend repair calls are not hardware at all. The cameras record, but nobody can see them, because the app account belongs to a relative, a former tenant, a super who left, or a builder's contractor who has moved on. Volunteer patrols and neighbors who registered a camera may also hold old passwords; Shmira Public Safety's registration form, for example, asks owners for the camera's brand, network address, port, username and password. The fix is to recover the recorder, set new passwords, put the account in the owner's name, write down who holds it, and decide on purpose which outside parties should keep access after the passwords change.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or service security systems, and its rules cover closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. An owner can work on their own system, or have an employee do it, without a license; a company working for someone else needs one. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch staff. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get acknowledgment. If a repair adds or moves a camera that watches a stockroom, a work station or a back office, the notice should be updated.

Footage requests. Detectives may ask for a copy of a clip; a copy on USB with original timestamps usually satisfies the request without the recorder leaving the store. Ring ended its tool for police requests through its app in January 2024, and law enforcement can still obtain video with a warrant. Whether to share a clip with a neighbor or a patrol group is the owner's decision.

Individual landmarks. The Lady Moody–Van Sicklen House at 27 Gravesend Neck Road and the Old Gravesend Cemetery are designated city landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says no permit is needed for ordinary maintenance and repair of existing safety and utility equipment; moving a camera or adding conduit on a designated building follows the commission's rules for where equipment may be attached. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Gravesend: Questions Answered

These are the situations Gravesend homeowners, co-op boards, managing agents, school offices and shop owners describe when they call. Each answer is written for the houses, buildings and storefronts here.

Shops on the avenues

I work alone and spend most of the day in the back room of my salon on Avenue U. Can the camera at the front door tell me the moment someone walks in, so I am not finding out from the playback later?

Yes. Most recorders and many cameras can send a phone alert, sound a chime or pop the door camera up on a screen when someone crosses the doorway during business hours. We set a detection line just inside the door so passers-by on the sidewalk do not trigger it, put a small monitor or tablet at your work station showing the front live, and aim the door camera to catch faces coming in. Usually that is settings and a screen on the cameras you already have, not new equipment.

There is a police camera on the pole at McDonald Avenue and Kings Highway, a block from our store. Is it still worth repairing our own camera that faces the same sidewalk?

Yes. The NYPD cameras approved for that corner in 2013 watch the intersection, not your door, your counter or your back entrance, and you cannot pull footage from them yourself. Your own camera is the one that shows a face at your door at the right moment and the one you can export the same night. A street camera may help police follow someone afterward, but it does not replace yours.

A scrap-metal truck took something from our curb in broad daylight and drove off slowly. The camera recorded the truck clearly, but the plate is a smear. Why, when it was daytime?

Usually because of shutter speed and angle, not light. A camera set to a slow shutter blurs anything moving, and a wide view of the street gives a plate only a few pixels. We can raise the shutter speed, zoom a varifocal camera onto the curb lane, or add a camera aimed only at where vehicles pass. That does not recover the plate from the clip you already have, but the next one will be readable far more often.

Apartment buildings, laundry rooms and schools

In our building's laundry room near Avenue W, the man who broke into the washing machines reached up and covered the only camera first. Where should the repaired camera go so that moment is recorded too?

Higher, farther from the machines, and ideally watched by a second camera. We mount the laundry camera where nobody can reach it without a ladder, aim it so it sees the doorway and the path to the machines before anyone gets close, and add a small camera at the door or in the corridor that has the first camera in its view. Then we turn on the covered-lens alert so the super or managing agent hears about it the same night.

The graffiti outside our school was painted just before seven on a November morning, and our footage from that grey half-hour before sunrise is murky. Is the camera failing?

Probably not. At dawn and dusk many cameras sit between their night and day settings, and some switch back and forth or stay in a noisy color mode when there is too little light for it. We adjust the day-night switch point and the exposure, check the infrared, and test the camera at that hour. If streetlights or the school's own lights do not reach the wall, a camera with its own light may be the better repair.

Our building's recorder sits in a locked metal cabinet in the boiler room, and its fan has become loud and the case is hot to the touch. Is it about to fail?

It is a warning. A loud fan usually means it is worn or clogged with dust, and a hot case means the drive and the board are running above their range, which shortens their life. We clean or replace the fan, read the drive's health, copy footage you may need, and move the recorder somewhere cooler or give the cabinet ventilation. A recorder beside a boiler is one of the most common causes of early drive failure we see.

Houses with garages, driveways and tile roofs

The cable to our driveway camera runs across the garage ceiling next to the overhead door track, and the picture flickers whenever the door opens. Is the camera going?

Almost always it is the cable, not the camera. A run that the door, its opener or its springs rub against gets crushed or worn until the signal drops each time it moves. We replace the damaged section, reroute it away from the track in a protected path, and test the camera with the door moving. If the camera's connector also sits near the door, we check and seal that too.

The cables to the cameras under our eaves run over the clay tile roof. Does a repair mean someone walking on the tiles?

We avoid it whenever we can. Most eave cameras can be reached and repaired from a ladder, and many cable faults are at the camera or where the cable enters the house. If a run across the roof is damaged, we look first for a route that does not cross the tiles, such as through the attic or down a corner. If the roof truly must be walked, we tell you before and price it, because clay tiles can crack underfoot.

Our cameras share the house network with everyone's phones, laptops and televisions, and in the evening they stutter and drop out of the app. Is the system failing?

Usually not. On a large house with many cameras, a busy home network can starve the cameras of bandwidth, or a consumer router can struggle with dozens of devices. Where the recorder allows it, we put the cameras on their own network behind the recorder or on a separate switch, so streaming and video calls do not compete with them. That often clears the drops without touching a single camera.

We converted our two-family back into one house, and we are left with two recorders, two apps and cameras nobody can match to either. Can it all become one system?

Usually, yes. We trace every cable to its recorder, test each camera, and check whether the newer recorder can take the cameras from the older one. If the two use different camera types, a hybrid recorder may take both. You get a written price for combining them and a separate price for leaving both and only fixing what failed, so you can choose.

Storms and power

A tree came down on our block in the storm and took the camera cable we had strung from the house to the garage at the back. Can it just go back up the same way?

It can, but it is better not to. A cable strung through the air between buildings is exposed to every branch and storm. Where possible we run the replacement along a fence or wall in protected cable or underground in conduit, and we check the camera and connector at the garage end, which often take damage when the cable is ripped down. If an overhead run is the only option, we use a supporting wire so the cable is not carrying its own weight.

Our internet box goes down every time the power flickers, and it takes a while to come back even after the cameras do. Can the cameras still reach our phones during that gap?

Yes, two ways. The cheapest is to put the modem and router on the same battery backup as the recorder, so a short flicker does not knock out the connection at all. For longer outages, some recorders can send alerts through a cellular backup router. The recorder keeps saving to its own drive either way; the question is only whether your phone hears about it.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we find on Gravesend houses, apartment buildings, schools and shops, and the faults each one tends to develop here.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common on houses and apartment buildings wired fifteen or more years ago. Typical faults: water in connectors under the eaves, failed plug-in adapters and worn infrared.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected through one data cable, standard on the newer large houses and in many shops. Typical faults: dead switch ports after a storm, address conflicts, shared home networks and lost passwords.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the footage, in a garage, cellar, closet, boiler room or back office. Typical faults: hot or worn drives, settings lost after an outage, short retention, and loud or failed fans.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with an adjustable zoom, common in lobbies and on driveways. Typical faults: a lens set too wide to recognize anyone, focus that drifted after a knock, and covers scratched or clouded.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving cameras over courtyards, lots and long driveways, and fisheye cameras over shop floors. Typical faults: lost presets, sticking motors and views pointed away from the spot that matters.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Plug-in and battery cameras inside windows, in back yards and on shelves. Typical faults: weak signal through brick and stucco, router changes, cold-weather battery drain and accounts tied to someone who has left.

Video door stations

Door stations with their own camera at the front door or gate, often on their own app. Typical faults: video that reaches one phone but not another, failed call buttons and night pictures washed out by porch lights.

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, often on a garage shelf or in a basement rack with no backup. Typical faults: dead ports or whole units after a surge, and power budgets exceeded when cameras were added.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, adapters that fail after a surge, and backups whose batteries quietly died years ago.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Gravesend

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is partly true somewhere, and each sends Gravesend owners toward the wrong repair or a lost clip.

"If you can see the camera live on your phone, it is recording"

Quick answers often treat a live picture as proof that the system works. Open the app, see the driveway, and you are covered.

Live view and recording are separate jobs. A recorder can show every camera live while its drive is full, failed, unmounted or set to record nothing, and a camera can stream to the app directly while never reaching the recorder at all. Many of the Gravesend calls that start with "we needed last Tuesday" end with the discovery that the live picture was fine for months and nothing was being saved.

The test is playback, not live view. On every repair we play back each camera from the night before, confirm how many days the drive actually holds, and turn on the alerts that tell you when recording stops.

"Daylight footage will always show a license plate"

Answers about plates tend to say the problem is darkness, and that any camera can read a plate in daylight.

The r/Brooklyn poster who wrote "I have surveillance footage but unfortunately no clear plate yet" about a truck tied to a Gravesend package theft is a good example of why that is wrong. Moving vehicles blur on a slow shutter even at noon, and a wide view of a whole street gives a plate too few pixels to read. Glare off a windshield or a white truck body makes it worse.

Reading plates is a setup, not a side effect. A narrower view of the lane where vehicles pass, a faster shutter, and a camera placed so plates face it, not slide across it, get far better results. For many houses the honest answer is a camera that shows the people well and accepts that plates will sometimes be missed.

"The police have cameras on the main corners, so a private camera adds little"

Some answers suggest that city cameras at busy intersections make private cameras unnecessary.

The NYPD did approve camera sites in Gravesend, at McDonald Avenue and Kings Highway and at Avenue W and West 11th Street, as part of a 640-camera rollout reported by the Brooklyn Eagle in 2013. But those cameras watch the intersection. They do not see your stoop, your driveway, your laundry room or your counter, and you cannot open them yourself. When the 62nd Precinct released images after the swastikas outside the McDonald Avenue yeshiva in 2025, and when a salon on Avenue U shared its video, the useful pictures came from cameras close to where it happened.

A street camera may help police follow someone afterward. The camera that shows the face at your door is yours, and it only helps if it is recording.

"Forgot the recorder password? Just factory reset it"

Password answers often jump straight to the reset button or a reset code.

On many recorders a factory reset erases the camera settings, the recording schedule and the network setup, and on some it can make the existing footage hard to reach. In Gravesend houses that passed from one generation to the next, or two-families where a tenant set up the system, the footage on the drive is often the reason for the call. Some makers also require proof of ownership before they issue a reset code.

Try the routes that keep your settings first, and copy any footage that matters before a reset. We check what your recorder's maker allows, recover the login where we can, and tell you before anything is erased.

"Any battery backup will keep your cameras running through an outage"

Outage advice often says to plug the recorder into a UPS and stop worrying.

A small UPS bought for a computer may carry a recorder for minutes, not hours, and it does nothing for cameras powered by a separate switch or by adapters on another outlet. A backup whose battery died years ago carries nothing at all. An outage like the one on West 8th Street in July 2026, with a tree in the lines for days, is beyond any ordinary backup; what a backup can do is ride through the short flickers and brownouts, then shut the recorder down cleanly so its drive is not damaged.

Size the backup to the recorder, the switch and the internet box together, check its battery every year, and decide which one or two cameras matter most in a long outage. We set that up as part of an after-storm repair.

"Once a camera system is up, it runs for years with no attention"

Many answers describe cameras as install-and-forget equipment.

In Gravesend we find the opposite. Drives wear out in hot garages, connectors under eaves take in water, trees and hedges grow into views, garage doors wear through cables, and new routers knock cameras off the app. The 2021 laundry-room camera near Avenue W was working, and it still lost the moment that mattered because it could be reached. None of that shows up until someone needs the footage.

A short yearly check, by day and after dark, with alerts set for lost video and drive errors, catches most of it while the fix is still small.

"Camera repair is a handyman job"

Some answers suggest any handy person can fix a camera system, and the parts are all online.

An owner can work on their own system, and New York's licensing rules allow that. A company doing it for someone else needs a license from the Department of State, because the state treats CCTV used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement as a security system. More practically, the hard part of most Gravesend repairs is not swapping a camera; it is knowing which of five possible faults caused the problem, saving the footage first, and not making a recorder unrecoverable along the way.

Find the cause first. The $150 service call tells you what failed and what will work with your system, and the written price lets you compare our part against one you buy yourself. We also fit customer-supplied equipment at labor-only rates.

FAQ

Gravesend Security Camera Repair FAQ

Our recorder has no label we can read. How do we find out its make and model before we call?
+

Look for a sticker on the bottom or back, which usually lists the model and serial number. If there is none, the recorder's own menu often shows it under system or device information, and the phone app may show it in the device settings. A photo of the front, the back with its connectors, and the start-up screen is usually enough for us to identify it, so send those and we will tell you what it is.

The company that put in our cameras still has the recorder password and wants a fee to hand it over. Can you get us in without them?
+

Often, yes. Many recorders can be recovered on site by the owner through the maker's own reset process, which may need the serial number and proof that the property is yours. Some reset routes keep the settings and footage and some do not, so we check yours first and copy any footage you need. Once we are in, the system is put in your name with new passwords, and the old installer no longer has access.

Our indoor camera sends an alert every time the cat walks past. Can it ignore the cat and still tell us about people?
+

Usually. Many newer cameras and recorders can tell people from other movement, and older ones can be set with motion zones and sensitivity that skip the floor where a pet walks. We check what your equipment supports, set person detection where it exists, and test it with the cat and with a person before we leave.

We park on one side of the street or the other depending on the street cleaning day. Can one camera watch both spots?
+

Sometimes. A camera on the house with a wide enough view may cover both curbs, but at a distance faces and plates get small. A pan-tilt camera can be set with two presets on a schedule, or a varifocal camera can be aimed between the two spots. We look at the distances from your house and tell you which gives a usable picture of each spot.

Can outdoor camera repairs be done in January when it is below freezing?
+

Yes, in most weather. Cameras, connectors and cables can be replaced in the cold, though sealants and some mounts need dry conditions to set properly, and ladder work waits out ice, heavy snow or high wind. If the weather on your booked day is unsafe for outdoor work, we do the indoor part of the repair and rebook the outside work.

Our semi-detached house shares a wall with the house next door, and there are cameras on both halves. How do we know which one is ours before you work on it?
+

We trace it. Each camera's cable leads to one recorder or one outlet, and testing at your recorder shows which cameras belong to it. If a camera turns out to belong to your neighbor, we do not touch it without their permission. We label your own cameras so the question does not come up again.

Our old recorder plugs into an old computer monitor, and the new recorder you are quoting only has an HDMI output. Can we keep the monitor?
+

Many new recorders still have a VGA output beside HDMI, and if yours does not, a small converter usually works with an older monitor. We check the monitor's inputs before choosing the recorder, and if the old screen is too dim or too small to recognize anyone, we tell you and price a replacement separately.

My nephew plugged our dead camera straight into the recorder with a short cable, and it worked. Does that mean the long cable is bad?
+

Very likely, or one of its connectors is. A camera that works on a short test cable but not on its own run usually has a damaged cable, a corroded connector, or, on a network camera, a run too long or too poor to carry power and data. We test the run end to end, replace the connector or the bad section, and only replace the whole run if it fails along its length.

Will the technician need our home Wi-Fi password during the repair?
