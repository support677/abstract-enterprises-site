The Neighborhood

Why Hunts Point Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Hunts Point is a peninsula that never fully goes to sleep, and that changes what a camera system has to do. According to the NYC Food Policy Center, the Food Distribution Center covers 329 acres, nearly half of the entire peninsula, and it runs 24 hours a day, seven days a week, still relying on infrastructure first put in place in the 1960s. Wikipedia counts more than 800 businesses in the Hunts Point Industrial Park. Around that hub are produce, meat and fish wholesalers, cold-storage operators, food producers, auto shops, recyclers and trucking yards. A camera plan written for a store that closes at nine does not fit a dock that is busiest at two in the morning. Here the question is rarely "what happens after we lock up." It is "what happens at door 14 while everyone is working at door 3."

The working side of the peninsula leans on equipment that cameras have to see around. Gothamist reported that roughly two dozen market operators use refrigerated trailers because the 1960s-era warehouses do not have enough refrigeration capacity. That means rows of trailers that move every night, block yard views, and hold product worth stealing. amNY reported that nearly 80,000 trucks a day travel to and from the peninsula, about 13,000 of them on residential side streets. Trucks bring headlights that wash out night pictures, diesel grime that coats lenses, and constant motion that sets off badly tuned alerts. A Hunts Point camera job begins with those conditions, not with a camera catalog.

The history explains why industry and homes sit so close together. NYC Parks traces the name to Thomas Hunt, an early settler in the 1670s. The area joined the city in 1874 and grew quickly after the IRT subway line reached it in 1904. Forgotten NY describes Hunts Point Avenue being rebuilt and widened in 1908, with coal yards and cement factories rising along the East River and houses and apartment buildings filling the high ground in the northern half. Between the 1890s and the 1920s, according to The POINT CDC, it was a working-class Jewish neighborhood, later home to Puerto Rican, Dominican and Jamaican families. The Produce Market opened in 1967 and the Meat Market in 1974, and the city designated an industrial park in 1980. NY1 notes that the Bruckner Expressway, completed in the early 1970s, cut the peninsula off from the rest of the borough. The decades of arson and abandonment that followed left many lots empty and many buildings rebuilt inside old shells.

The homes that remain are mostly rented. Wikipedia reports that 91.7 percent of occupied housing units in Hunts Point are renter-occupied. That single number shapes residential camera work more than any other. In most buildings, the owner or managing agent decides what goes in the lobby, the halls, the rear yard and on the facade, while a tenant's own plan stays inside the apartment. Impera Security, summarizing New York practice, notes that tenants generally cannot install cameras in shared hallways or lobbies without the landlord's permission. RecordingLaw notes that landlords cannot put cameras inside apartments, aim them at tenants' windows, or place them anywhere people reasonably expect privacy. We follow both rules on every job.

Local reporting shows where cameras do their work. CBS New York reported a burglar who targeted at least three Hunts Point businesses after April 21, cutting chains and slipping through metal gates, and in one case driving out with a forklift. CBS also reported an armed robbery at Fratilli's Pizza on Hunts Point Avenue near East Bay Avenue around 7:45 one evening, where surveillance video showed the man walking in with a gun and a worker said the shop's cameras may have spooked him. In another report, a street robbery around 11 a.m. near Bryant and Lafayette Avenues was caught on camera, and a resident told CBS that robberies were becoming more common. More recently, NYPD Crime Stoppers circulated surveillance images in a robbery case involving the 41st Precinct, which serves Hunts Point and Longwood from its station house at 1035 Longwood Avenue. We do not rank any block from these reports. They show that gates, store doors and street corners are where clear video decides what anyone can prove.

Two more facts change where we put equipment. The first is water. City Limits reported in 2022 that nearly a third of the Food Distribution Center sits in the current 100-year floodplain, that storm water during Sandy reached the New Fulton Fish Market parking lot, and that a city study named power outages as the main threat to the produce and fish markets. Recorders, switches and battery backups therefore go up high, off the floor and away from loading-level doors. The second is landmarks. The Manida Street Historic District, designated in 2020 as the city's 150th, covers 42 semi-detached row houses between Lafayette and Garrison Avenues, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews security cameras on those facades under its rules. A camera on a Manida Street front is planned with that review in mind before a ladder goes up.

  • Half the peninsula is a food hub: the Food Distribution Center covers 329 acres and runs around the clock, so docks, yards and gates need cameras that work every hour, not just overnight.
  • Trailers as storage: about two dozen operators use refrigerated trailers to make up for missing cold-room capacity, creating moving rows that block fixed views.
  • Heavy truck traffic: nearly 80,000 trucks a day reach the peninsula, so night pictures must handle sweeping headlights and lenses must be easy to clean.
  • Mostly renters: 91.7 percent of occupied homes are rented, so owners plan common-area cameras and tenants keep theirs inside the apartment.
  • Floodplain sites: nearly a third of the food center sits in the current 100-year floodplain, so recording equipment is mounted high and on battery backup.
  • A landmark street: the 42 row houses of the Manida Street Historic District need camera positions that suit Landmarks Preservation Commission review.

What Hunts Point owners often add next. Warehouse managers who start with dock and gate cameras usually come back asking about fob readers on the office door or the cold-room corridor, so the camera footage and the door log can be matched. Row-house owners on Manida Street often want the camera at the stoop shown on a video intercom inside. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port at the recorder for that next step, so walls and ceilings only have to be opened once.

Reading a Hunts Point Site the Way a Thief Would

The CBS report of a burglar cutting chains, slipping through metal gates and leaving with a forklift describes the weak points of a working yard better than any checklist. Thieves on an industrial site do not come through the front office. They look for the gate that is chained rather than locked, the stretch of fence behind a parked trailer, the dock door that stays cracked for ventilation, and the equipment that can be driven out. Before we suggest a camera for a Hunts Point warehouse, yard or market unit, we walk the site as someone looking for those gaps would, and we ask the same three questions at each point: what can move through here, who is supposed to move it, and who would see it happen. Start at the exits, not the entrances

Most owners ask first for a camera on the front door or the office. On a Hunts Point site, the more useful question is how stolen goods or equipment would leave. A forklift can only go out a gate wide enough for it. A pallet can only leave through a dock door or over a fence where a vehicle can pull up. So we trace every exit route a vehicle could take, then put the clearest cameras where those routes narrow: the gate opening, the end of a trailer row, the ramp up to the street. A camera that shows a vehicle head-on as it passes through a gate, at a height that sees the driver and the front of the truck, is often worth more than three cameras scattered across the yard. Once the exits are covered, we fill in the docks and the fence.

Name each camera by dock number and gate, not by channel

A recorder that shows "Channel 9" is no help when a receiver says a pallet went missing from door 11 between two and four in the morning. We name every camera exactly as the people on the floor talk about the site: "Door 11-12," "Freezer A door," "Halleck gate," "Trailer row east," "Fence behind lot C." The same names go on the printed cable labels at the recorder and in the written camera map we leave behind. When a new night supervisor starts, or the tenant changes, the next person can read the system the way the building and yard are actually used, which also makes it far faster to pull the right clip for a driver, an insurer or the police.

Who sees which views, and who gets woken up

On a 24-hour site, alerts on every camera would buzz phones all night and soon be ignored. We set them by zone instead. The far fence line, the equipment parking and the inside of a closed gate send alerts whenever someone is there, because those places should be empty. The working docks and the trailer rows record continuously without alerts while a shift is running. The owner or general manager holds the main administrator account, the only one that can change settings or delete footage. A night supervisor sees the docks, the gate and the cold rooms; office staff may see only the office door and the parking area. Where a guard company or the market's own public safety officers need to view a camera, they get view-only access to that camera alone. We write down who has what before we leave.

Market and cold-storage buildings: work from the warm side

The Food Policy Center notes that the market still runs on infrastructure built in the 1960s, and many of these buildings were designed long before anyone thought about cameras. Their walls are usually concrete block or insulated panel, their docks are long, and their cold rooms swing between near-freezing and the outdoor temperature every time a door opens. That swing is the main enemy of a camera. A lens moved from cold air to warm, humid air fogs, and moisture creeping into a housing can ruin a camera in a season. So we keep cameras on the warm side of cold-room doors wherever possible, looking through the opening rather than living inside it. Where a camera must sit in the cold, we choose a housing rated for it, often with a built-in heater, and seal every cable entry. Cable runs along the dock in conduit or on supports above the reach of forklift masts, and the recorder goes to a raised office or mezzanine, out of the cold and above the floor.

Reclad buildings: plan the camera before the panel goes up

New York YIMBY reported that Building C at the Hunts Point Cooperative Market, built in 1972, is getting an $18 million recladding with a sealed insulated metal facade. Projects like that change camera work. A camera bolted straight through an insulated panel without a plan can let in water and break the seal the owner just paid for. On a reclad building, the best time to plan cameras is before or during the new skin, so that mounting points land on the structure behind the panel and cable enters through planned, sealed openings. Where the panel is already up, we use mounts designed for metal panels, find the framing, and seal every opening so the wall still keeps out weather and keeps in cold. We also check with the owner or the panel contractor whether drilling affects any warranty on the new wall before we begin.

Converted factories: someone else owns the pathways

Wikipedia describes the former American Bank Note Company plant at 1201 Lafayette Avenue as a 1909 to 1911 brick building on a steel frame with concrete floors, about 405,000 square feet, now known as The BankNote and home to offices, a charter school on several floors and a city agency. Buildings like that are really many tenants under one roof. A business renting a suite controls its own space, but the corridors, elevators, telecom room and every hole through a concrete floor belong to the landlord. So the first step is the building's rules: where tenant cable may run, which pathways are approved, and who signs off. Inside the suite, the camera job is usually simple, with cameras on the entry door, the reception area and any storage room. The care goes into routing cable through approved pathways and keeping every camera aimed inside the tenant's own space.

Pre-war walk-ups: the cellar, the shaft and the rear yard

The residential blocks around Hunts Point Avenue, Bryant Avenue, Faile Street and Barretto Street hold many five- and six-story walk-ups from the 1920s, such as the 49-unit building at 760 Hunts Point Avenue built in 1928. In these buildings, the cellar is usually the camera hub. It holds the meters and the internet provider's equipment, and it is the best place for a locked recorder, set high and away from the boiler and any spot that takes water. The camera for the street door goes inside the inner vestibule door, framed so residents and visitors walk toward it. Hall cameras on each landing are aimed down the stair, stopping short of apartment doors. At the back, a camera at the cellar or yard door covers the rear yard at ground level, aimed at the ground and the gate, not up at the windows. Cable rises through a shaft, a stacked closet or slim painted raceway, and every floor it passes through is sealed with rated firestop.

Manida Street row houses: two houses, one facade

The Historic Districts Council describes the Manida Street houses as modest Renaissance Revival row houses, and Ephemeral New York points to the details that matter for cameras: mirror-image facades, rounded projecting bays, low stone stoops, simple cornices with steep parapets, and ornament gathered around the doors and windows. Each building is really two houses sharing one front, so a camera on one half sits a few feet from the neighbor's door. We keep cameras off the carved surrounds and the cornice, mounting on plain brick beside or above the entrance and aimed down the owner's own stoop and walk. Cable enters through a mortar joint rather than the brick face, where the joint can be repointed later, and runs inside to the basement. Because the district is designated, exterior positions are written up for the Landmarks Preservation Commission before drilling, and rear-yard cameras are planned the same careful way.

Wholesale units and cold storage: docks, trailers and the night shift

A wholesale unit in one of the market buildings is a long room with a dock on one side, cold rooms behind, a sales floor or office at one end, and trucks backing in at every hour. The camera plan follows the product. First the dock doors, one view for each pair, mounted high and aimed so the trailer opening, the dock plate and the pallet crossing it all show. Then the cold-room doors, from the warm side. Then the sales counter or order window, where buyers pick up and pay, with a camera above and in front showing faces and paperwork. Then the office door, the equipment parking and the route to the gate. Gothamist's reporting on trailers used as extra cold storage means many units also need trailer-row coverage, mounted high on the building or on a pole, looking down the row so each trailer door shows. All of this records continuously, because on a 24-hour site there is no quiet period to rely on, and the recorder keeps enough days to cover the time it usually takes a buyer or driver to raise a dispute.

Yards, salvage lots and equipment: the gate is the camera that pays

Hunts Point has many open yards, from truck parking to pallet and salvage operations. The CBS report of a burglar cutting chains and leaving with a forklift shows exactly where they are weak. For a yard, the gate camera comes first: mounted inside the fence, at a height and angle that shows each vehicle head-on as it passes through, with a second, tighter view of the chain or lock so a cut shows clearly. Then long views down each fence line, with alerts tuned so only movement inside the fence counts. Then the equipment parking. Yards are often far larger than the 100 meters a standard network camera cable can carry, so far cameras connect through outdoor-rated switches in sealed enclosures, or by fiber where cameras sit on another building or across a truck lane. Power for distant poles is planned on the quote, not discovered on the day.

Shops on Hunts Point Avenue and Southern Boulevard

The CBS report from Fratilli's Pizza, where surveillance video showed an armed man walking in, shows what a storefront camera system is for. Most shops on Hunts Point Avenue and Southern Boulevard are small rooms on the ground floor of apartment buildings, often staffed by one or two people. They need three views above everything else: the front door, framed for faces as people walk in; the counter, close enough to show hands, the register and the customer; and the back door or stock room. A store without a back office needs a locked cabinet for the recorder, mounted high and away from the counter, so anyone who takes the register cannot take the footage too. Any camera on the outside wall or a sign box needs the building owner's written agreement, and the store's system stays separate from the residential system upstairs.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Hunts Point

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, drivers' lounges and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we check every view for mirrors and windows that might show one.
  • The landlord or market management decides outside the unit. Shared docks, building facades, common corridors and yards owned by someone else need written agreement before any camera goes up.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is mounted on a self-closing fire door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it latching.
  • Openings stay sealed. Every hole through a rated wall or floor gets rated firestop, and every hole through a cold-room wall or insulated panel is sealed against moisture.
  • Food stays clean. Drilling is done with dust controlled and product moved or covered, and nothing is mounted directly over open food.
  • Landmarks need their sign-off. Exterior cameras on the Manida Street houses and on designated buildings are planned for Landmarks Preservation Commission review first.
  • Audio stays off. Business cameras record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • The installer should be licensed. New York licenses businesses that install and service security systems through the Department of State; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords, market management and insurers usually ask to see.

Field Notes: Walking a Hunts Point Yard at Shift Change

We do not publish stories about particular sites or customers, so this is the routine rather than an anecdote: how we look at a Hunts Point warehouse or yard before recommending a single camera.

We try to arrive close to a shift change, when the site shows both faces at once: the last trucks of one shift pulling out and the first of the next backing in. We start at the street, watching where trucks wait, where drivers walk, and how long the gate stays open. Then we go in through the gate and stand where a gate camera would go, checking whether headlights shine straight into that spot as vehicles turn in. Next comes the yard: where the trailers are parked tonight, where they were parked last week according to the supervisor, and which stretches of fence disappear behind them. We look at the chains and locks on every gate, because a camera's tight view of a chain tells you a lot more than a wide view of a dark fence. On the dock we watch forklift paths, note the height of the masts, and look for a route for cable that no load will ever touch. At the cold rooms we open a door and watch the air roll out, which shows where a lens would fog. Inside, we find the office or mezzanine, check whether it sits above the level the site has flooded to, and look for power and the internet equipment. Only then do we write the plan, gate by gate and door by door.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Hunts Point: Questions Answered

Docks, cold rooms and trailers

Our cold dock and the warm truck side meet at the door seal. Which side of the door should the camera be on?

Usually the inside of the dock, a few feet back from the door, high on the wall or a column, looking out through the opening. That keeps the camera in one steady temperature instead of swinging between cold and warm air each time a trailer is opened, which is what fogs lenses and drives moisture into housings. From that spot the camera sees the trailer opening, the dock plate and every pallet crossing it. If the dock area itself is refrigerated, we use a housing rated for the cold, often heated, and seal the cable entry so the warm air outside never condenses inside the camera.

Trailers parked as extra cold storage block our yard cameras, and the rows move every night. How do we keep them in view?

Go high and aim along the rows, not across them. A camera mounted near the roofline of the building or on a sturdy pole, looking down the length of a trailer row, sees every trailer door in that row no matter which trailers are parked there tonight. A camera aimed across the yard at a fixed spot loses its view the moment a trailer stops in front of it. Where the rows are long, we put one camera at each end looking toward the middle. The trailer doors are what matter, because that is where product leaves, so we frame for the doors and the ground behind them.

Drivers and our receivers argue over when a load arrived and what shape the pallets were in. Can dock cameras settle that?

Often, yes, if the cameras are set up for it. Each dock camera needs an accurate clock synced to the network, so the time on the recording matches the time on the paperwork. The view needs to show the trailer opening and the dock plate clearly enough that a broken pallet or a short count is visible as the load comes off. We name each camera by door number so a supervisor can find the right clip fast, and we size the recorder to keep footage long enough to cover the window in which your buyers or carriers usually raise a claim. A clip exported with its time stamp is then something both sides can look at.

Our processing room is hosed down at the end of every shift. Which cameras survive that?

Cameras with a high water-ingress rating, mounted high and out of the direct line of the hoses, with every cable connection inside a sealed box rather than hanging in the open. The bigger risk is usually the connections and the mount, not the camera body, so we seal cable entries, avoid open connectors on the wall, and use stainless or coated hardware that stands up to cleaning chemicals. Nothing is mounted directly over open product. We ask your sanitation lead which chemicals and pressures are used, because that decides which housings and mounts are suitable.

Yards, gates and fences

The burglar CBS reported in Hunts Point cut chains, slipped through gates and once left with a forklift. What views would have shown him leaving?

Three. A head-on camera at each vehicle gate, mounted inside the fence at a height that shows the driver and the front of the vehicle as it passes, because anything large that is stolen has to go out that way. A second, tighter camera on the chain or lock itself, so the moment it is cut shows clearly with a time stamp. And a view of the equipment parking area, so you can see a forklift start up and roll toward the gate. Together those show the break-in, the vehicle and the exit, which is far more useful than a wide shot of a dark yard.

Our yard runs farther from the office than one camera cable can reach. How do the far cameras connect?

A standard network camera cable is good for about 100 meters, and many Hunts Point yards are bigger than that. For far cameras, we place an outdoor-rated switch in a sealed, locked enclosure partway out, powered and protected by a battery backup, and run short cables from there to each camera. Where the distance is longer still, or the cameras sit on a separate building, we link back to the office with fiber, which carries the picture over long distances and does not conduct electricity between buildings. Power for those far points is planned and priced on the quote.

Two of our buildings stand on opposite sides of a truck lane. Should the camera link cross overhead or go underground?

It depends on what is already there and what the lane carries. Overhead, the cable must be supported on a proper messenger wire well above the tallest trucks that use the lane, and anchored securely at both buildings. Underground, it needs a conduit under the pavement, which may already exist from an old service or may require saw-cutting and trenching. We look for existing conduit first, because reusing it is usually simplest. Either way, a fiber link is the better choice between separate buildings, and we write the route, the support and any paving work on the quote so you can compare.

Row houses and rental buildings

Our Manida Street house shares a mirror-image front with the house next door. Should both halves put cameras in matching spots?

It often looks best and reviews best that way, but it only works if both owners agree. A matching pair of small cameras on the plain brick beside each door keeps the facade symmetrical, which suits a district designated for its uniform fronts. Each camera is aimed down its own owner's stoop and walk, so neither house records the other's entrance. If only one owner wants a camera, we place it on that owner's half, set back from the shared center line, and black out any part of the picture that reaches the neighbor's door.

Can a camera sit on the rounded front bay of a Manida Street house?

We avoid the bay. Its curved brick and window surrounds are among the features that give these houses their character, and a camera there would sit right against window glass, which causes glare at night. A better spot is usually the plain brick beside or just above the main door, or under the edge of the entrance where the stoop is covered from rain. From there, the camera looks out along the stoop and the sidewalk in front of the house. The cable enters through a mortar joint, and the plan is written up for Landmarks review before work starts.

Our rowhouse has been divided into single rooms that share a kitchen. Where can the owner put cameras?

At the front door from the inside, in the entry hall, on the stair and at the rear door, all aimed so they never look into a tenant's room when its door opens. Shared bathrooms are never recorded. A shared kitchen is a sensitive space where people spend private time, so we advise against a camera there unless the tenants agree and the owner has a clear reason, and even then it should cover only the doorway. A short written notice at the entrance saying which shared areas are recorded helps tenants trust the system, which matters in a building where many households share one front door.

Our walk-up's fire escape comes down the back into the yard. Can a camera cover the bottom of it without watching apartments?

Yes. The camera goes at ground level on the rear wall or the yard door, aimed at the bottom of the drop ladder, the yard and the gate, with the top of the picture stopping below the first-floor windows. Most cameras also allow parts of the picture to be permanently blacked out, which we use for any window that still shows at the edge. The point is to see anyone reaching the yard by the fire escape or the fence, not to record the windows above. Nothing is ever fixed to the fire escape itself.

Offices in converted buildings

Our suite is in a multi-tenant industrial building, and the lease lets the landlord inspect our space. Does that give the landlord access to our camera recordings?

No, not by itself. A right to inspect the premises is not a right to your recordings or your accounts. Your system, installed in your suite and paid for by you, can stay entirely in your name, on your own network, with the landlord having no login unless you decide to give one. If the lease does say something about cameras or data, read that clause, or have your lawyer read it. Where a camera of yours looks at a shared corridor the landlord controls, the landlord's written agreement is needed for that position.

A charter school uses other floors of our converted building. Are there extra limits on where a tenant's cameras can point?

Keep every camera inside your own suite, aimed at your own door, reception and storage, and keep shared elevator lobbies and stairs to the landlord's system. With children using the building, a tenant camera that reaches into a shared hall the school uses will draw questions quickly, and the landlord is the right party to decide those spaces. In practice, a camera inside your door looking in, rather than out into the corridor, shows everyone who enters your space without recording anyone passing by.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Hunts Point

Our build environment could not display Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask results for Hunts Point, so we will not invent quotes from them. These are claims that turn up repeatedly in quick online answers, vendor blogs and cost guides about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about Hunts Point. Cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they give a rough sense of scale, but none of them has stood at a freezer door while a trailer was unloaded.

"Hunts Point is an industrial zone, so camera jobs there are all warehouses."

Quick summaries often describe Hunts Point only as the food market. The market is huge, covering nearly half the peninsula, so the shortcut is understandable.

But the northern half is residential, with walk-ups along Hunts Point Avenue and the side streets, newer affordable and senior buildings, and the 42 row houses of the Manida Street Historic District. Wikipedia reports that 91.7 percent of those homes are rented.

That means a large share of Hunts Point camera work is about lobbies, halls, stoops and rear yards, and about who has permission to cover them. We plan those jobs as carefully as the docks.

"Any outdoor-rated camera will work on a refrigerated dock."

Product listings stress weather ratings, and quick answers repeat that an outdoor camera handles cold and wet. Weather ratings describe rain and dust, not the jump from freezing to warm air many times an hour.

At a Hunts Point cold-room door, each opening rolls cold air out and warm, damp air in. A lens caught in that swing fogs, and moisture that gets inside a housing can kill a camera in a season.

So placement matters more than the rating. We keep cameras on the steady side of the door where we can, and where one has to live in the cold, we use a cold-rated, often heated housing with every cable entry sealed.

"Wireless cameras are the simple choice for a big yard."

Answers aimed at homeowners often recommend wireless cameras to avoid running cable. On a small house, that can be fair.

A Hunts Point yard is a different place. Metal buildings, steel racking, refrigerated trailers and long distances all weaken Wi-Fi, and trailers that move every night change the signal from one shift to the next. A camera that drops off at three in the morning records nothing.

For yards, we wire cameras back to the recorder, using outdoor switches or fiber for the far points. Wireless still has a place for a renter's indoor camera or a short link we can test, and we will say when it fits.

"Sites with their own security force don't need cameras."

The Hunts Point Cooperative Market has a privately run Department of Public Safety, formed in 1985, which Wikipedia says employs about 45 special patrolmen. Some answers treat that as covering every business on the site.

Officers patrol a large market and respond to calls. They cannot watch the inside of every unit, every dock door and every trailer at once, and they are not there for businesses outside the market's own grounds.

Cameras do a different job: they record what happened at a specific door at a specific minute. Where a business wants the market's officers or a guard company to see a camera, we can give view-only access to that one camera, but the system and the footage stay with the business.

"Houses in a historic district can't have security cameras."

Some answers suggest landmark rules forbid cameras on designated houses, which would rule out Manida Street entirely. That is not what the rules say.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook groups security cameras with window bars, lighting and intercom panels, which have their own staff-level rules under Rules Section 2-20, and LPC permits show cameras reviewed under that section.

In practice, small cameras on plain masonry, away from ornament, with cable through a mortar joint, are the kind of work those rules are written around. We plan Manida Street positions that way and prepare the paperwork before drilling.

"A city law requires a sign under Local Law 50 wherever cameras record."

At least one installer's blog says New York City requires a posted notice under "Local Law 50" for common-area cameras. We could not confirm that law from any second source.

So we do not tell Hunts Point owners that a particular sign is legally required. We do recommend a short notice at the entrance saying the common areas and yard are recorded and who can view the footage, because it helps tenants, drivers and staff trust the system.

Businesses that use face-matching software have separate city rules and should speak to a lawyer. Ordinary recording at a dock or a lobby does not involve that.

"Camera installation costs a set price per camera."

Cost guides quote a range per camera for labor, and one vendor gives $80 to $200 per camera in New York City. AI answers often repeat a number like that as if every camera were the same job.

In Hunts Point the gap is enormous. A camera above a row-house stoop is a short visit. A camera at the far end of a truck yard may need an outdoor switch, a sealed enclosure, power and a long run in conduit; a freezer-door camera may need a heated housing and a sealed wall entry.

So we do not print one camera price for the neighborhood. Every Hunts Point job is priced on our Bronx rate, quoted line by line after photos or a visit, and booked within 2 hours.

FAQ

Hunts Point Security Camera Installation FAQ

Our business never really closes. How should camera alerts work when there is no after-hours?
+

Set alerts by place instead of by time. Areas that should always be empty, such as the far fence line, the inside of a closed gate, the roof or the equipment parking between shifts, alert whenever someone appears there. Working areas such as the docks, the sales floor and the trailer rows record continuously without sending alerts, because on a 24-hour site they would go off every minute and soon be ignored. Each alert goes to the supervisor on duty who can actually walk over, not to an owner who is asleep across town.

Can the camera recorder live inside a refrigerated building, or does it need its own warm room?
+

It needs a room that stays at ordinary office temperature and humidity, such as the main office, a mezzanine or a closed electrical or network closet. Recorders and switches are built for that kind of space, and cold, damp air shortens the life of their drives and connections. On the peninsula we also keep them well above the floor and away from loading-level doors, because parts of the food center sit in the floodplain. A battery backup sits with the recorder so a short outage does not leave a gap in the footage.

Do our refrigeration and electrical contractors need to be on site when cameras go into a cold room?
+

Usually not, but we want their input before we drill. A cold-room wall or ceiling may hide refrigerant lines, heater wires or sensors, and the insulated panels need to stay sealed afterward. We ask the owner to check with the refrigeration contractor where it is safe to penetrate, and we seal every opening so the room holds its temperature. If a camera needs new power near a cold room, that is electrical work, and our quote names it as a separate line for an electrician.

How do you keep drilling dust and debris off food and packaging?
+

We plan the work with your floor manager. Product near each camera spot is moved or covered first, drilling is done with dust collection, and work above open product is scheduled when that area is empty. Cable ends, clips and scraps are bagged as we go rather than swept up later. In food production rooms, we follow your site's hygiene rules on clothing and access, and we leave each area clean enough for your own sanitation team to sign off before product returns.

Our building was reclad in insulated metal panels. How is a camera fixed to that wall without breaking the seal?
+

We locate the structural framing behind the panel and mount to it with hardware made for metal panel walls, rather than hanging a camera on the thin outer skin. Cable goes through a single small opening at each camera, sealed against water and air, so the wall still keeps weather out and cold in. Before starting, we ask whether the new cladding carries a warranty that limits drilling, because some do. If a recladding is still being planned, the cleanest result comes from agreeing camera points with the panel contractor beforehand.

Our pickers use handheld scanners on the warehouse Wi-Fi. Will adding cameras slow them down?
+

Not if the cameras are wired and kept on their own network. We run camera cable to a dedicated switch and recorder, separate from the network the scanners and order system use, so video never competes with your pickers for wireless bandwidth. Wireless cameras, by contrast, would share the air with the scanners, which is one reason we avoid them inside busy warehouses. If you want to view cameras from the office computers, we connect the two networks in a controlled way that keeps the camera traffic contained.

Can a yard camera warn us about a fire starting in our pallet stacks?
+

A security camera is not a fire alarm and should not be treated as one. Ordinary cameras can show smoke or flames on a recording, and some specialized thermal cameras can flag heat, but none of that replaces proper fire detection and a sprinkler or alarm system designed for your site. What our cameras do well is show who was in the yard and when, which matters after a suspicious fire like the 2020 pallet yard fire on Drake Street. For detection, speak to a fire alarm contractor about what your yard needs.

Trucks park overnight on our residential block. Can a camera on our house watch our own curb?
+

Yes. A camera on your own facade, mounted high near the door and aimed along your frontage, can show your curb, your car and the sidewalk in front of your house. We frame it so it covers your stretch of street rather than the whole block or the windows across the way. Expect headlights and parked trucks to change the picture through the night, so we test the view after dark and adjust the angle and exposure. The camera records what happens at your curb; it will not stop trucks from parking there.

Is it legal for our warehouse camera to record the public street and sidewalk outside our fence?
+

A business can generally record what is openly visible from its own property, such as its frontage, gate and the sidewalk in front, as long as cameras are not aimed into homes or other private spaces and do not record audio. We frame street-facing views on your own gate and curb, which is also what makes them useful, and we black out any edge that reaches a neighbor's windows. Fixing anything to city lampposts or signposts is not allowed. If you have a specific legal concern, ask a lawyer; this is general guidance, not legal advice.
