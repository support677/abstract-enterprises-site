The Neighborhood

Why Kingsbridge Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Kingsbridge is small on the map and busy on the ground, and that mix is what makes camera work here particular. Wikipedia draws its edges at Manhattan College Parkway to the north, the Major Deegan Expressway and Bailey Avenue to the east, West 230th Street to the south and Irwin Avenue to the west, about a third of a square mile in Community District 8, ZIP code 10463, policed by the 50th Precinct from 3450 Kingsbridge Avenue. CityNeighborhoods draws the lines a little differently, with Broadway on one side and Riverdale Avenue on the other. Either way, the elevated 1 train runs down the middle on Broadway, with stations at 231st and 238th Streets, and the ground climbs steeply on both sides of it. Step streets carry people up the slopes; the stair at West 230th Street, which Atlas Obscura calls the longest step street in the city at 295 feet, has houses whose front doors open straight onto the steps. On a block like that, the street a building faces and the street its side door faces may sit at different heights, and one of them is almost always quieter than the other.

The local reporting shows where the trouble tends to start, and it is rarely the front of the building. CBS New York reported that an 85-year-old man was robbed at gunpoint at Cannon Place and West 238th Street, grabbed as he entered the side door of his building, and that the robbery was caught on camera; the landlord moved quickly to improve security. The Riverdale Press reported a man climbing the fire escape of a five-story building at 3004 Heath Avenue and prying open a window, recorded by cameras outside. Norwood News reported that someone used a crowbar to pry open the front door of the lobby of the same residential building near Corlear Avenue and West 231st Street twice in 2024, around 3 a.m. on March 25 and again just before 3 a.m. on April 5. On Broadway, thieves cut the security gate of a Chinese restaurant at 5625 Broadway in 2018, and the Press noted there was no video from inside, only the chance that neighboring shops had cameras. More recently, the Press reported that tenants of an Irwin Avenue building had car break-ins since December and that someone smashed a display case at a phone store at 145 West 231st Street and took seven cellphones. In nearby Riverdale, a restaurant burglar took the DVR box for the security cameras along with everything else. We are not ranking any block, and none of this describes every building. What it shows is that side doors, fire escapes, lobby doors in the small hours, cut gates, parked cars and the recorder itself are the places a Kingsbridge camera plan has to cover.

Three sets of rules decide where cameras can go. The first is permission. Security-systems.nyc points out that most New York City apartment buildings are not legally required to have cameras, so the decision belongs to the owner, the co-op board or the housing company. Brick Underground notes that renters need the landlord's permission to install equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside their own apartment door, and Seneca Security advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to document the security reason in writing and notify tenants before installing. The second is landmarks: the Kingsbridge Armory and St. John's Church at 3021 Kingsbridge Avenue are designated city landmarks, and NYC Business explains that any work on a landmark needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit first. The third is fire and privacy. Stair and corridor doors must close and latch on their own, so nothing we mount touches a door leaf or closer, and every opening through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop. Cameras never go inside an apartment, hallway cameras are aimed so they cannot see into a unit when its door opens, and outdoor cameras that face the yards are set to show the ground and the fire-escape ladders, never the windows.

Kingsbridge facts that shape a camera plan

  • Side doors on other streets: the Cannon Place robbery began at a building's side door, and on a sloping, corner-heavy grid many buildings have a second entrance nobody in the lobby can see.
  • Fire escapes over quiet yards: the Heath Avenue break-in was a climb up a fire escape to a window, which only a yard-level camera aimed at the ladder records.
  • Doors pried in the small hours: the same lobby near Corlear Avenue and West 231st Street was forced twice in 2024, both times close to 3 a.m.
  • Entry security on the record: Community Board 8 lists main doors without working locks and buzzers among the district's security concerns, and one in five residents is 65 or older.
  • A crowded shopping strip: the Kingsbridge BID counts about 200 merchants on Broadway from West 230th to West 236th Street and on West 231st Street from the Deegan to Corlear Avenue.
  • Change coming: the Kingsbridge Armory redevelopment was approved by the City Council, with the first phase expected to start in 2026 and finish in 2030, and the MTA is repairing the elevated 1 train structure from 225th to 242nd Street through summer 2027.

What Kingsbridge owners often add next. A building that starts with cameras at the lobby and the side door often comes back asking whether that side door can be put on a fob reader, or whether the lobby camera can show on the intercom. Shopkeepers under the el usually ask next about an alarm on the back door. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port at the recorder for that step, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Four Ways Into a Kingsbridge Building, and What Each Camera Has to Do

An owner calling about cameras almost always starts with the front door. In Kingsbridge that is the door least likely to be the problem, because it is the one with people passing and a buzzer panel facing the sidewalk. The local cases point the other way: a side door on Cannon Place, a fire escape on Heath Avenue, a lobby door near Corlear Avenue pried while the block slept, a cut gate on Broadway. So we sort every opening into four groups, the front, the side, the back and the way up, and ask two questions about each: who is supposed to use it, and who would notice if the wrong person did. The side door: the entrance the lobby camera never sees

Many Kingsbridge apartment houses sit on corner lots or run through to a side street, and the step-street blocks mean the second door may be a few feet higher or lower than the first. That side door is where older residents often come and go because it saves them a flight of front steps, it is where the super props a door for a delivery, and it is where nobody is watching. A camera inside the door, aimed so people walk toward it, catches the face of whoever comes through. A second, wider view from just inside the glass or from above the frame shows the sidewalk for a few yards either side, so a person standing and waiting there is on the recording before anyone opens the door. Many recorders can also flag someone who stays in a marked area longer than a set time, and on a side door that alert can go to the super during the hours the building is quiet. None of that stops a determined robber, but it turns an unwatched door into a watched one, and it gives police a face and a time rather than a description.

Fire escapes and rear yards: record the climb, not the windows

Rear fire escapes are a second staircase that nobody counts. On most six-story buildings the drop ladder ends a short way above the yard, and the climb from the yard to the first landing is the only moment a burglar is exposed. A camera mounted low on the rear wall or on a yard fence post, aimed up the line of the ladders rather than at the windows, records that climb clearly. Because fire escapes run past bedroom windows, we set the frame so the windows fall outside it, and where the camera can black out parts of the picture we use that on any edge that still catches a window. Yard lighting matters as much as the camera. A dark yard gives a grainy, colorless picture, so we check the yard after dark and recommend a light where one would make the difference. Nothing is ever fixed to the fire escape itself, which has to stay clear for anyone who needs to use it.

The recorder: out of reach, and backed up

In nearby Riverdale, a restaurant burglar took the DVR box for the security cameras, which is the most direct way to erase what the cameras saw. In a Kingsbridge building the recorder usually lives in the cellar, because the power, the internet equipment and the cable paths meet there, and that is fine as long as it is in a locked cabinet or room that does not open onto the cellar door anyone could walk in through. In a store, it goes into a locked box high on a wall or above the ceiling, away from the counter. On top of that, we can set the system to copy key clips, such as motion at the side door or the gate overnight, to a phone or a cloud account, so that losing the recorder does not mean losing the evidence. The recorder also goes on a battery backup with the internet modem and switch, so a short power cut does not leave a gap at the exact moment someone is working at a door.

Courtyard apartment houses: the court is the real front door

CityNeighborhoods describes the six-story buildings on Kingsbridge, Heath and Sedgwick Avenues as brick with pre-war ornament and courtyard entrances. For a camera plan, that court is the point where the street ends and the building begins, so the first camera watches the gate and the walk from a spot near the building entrance, looking back toward the sidewalk, so people coming in walk toward it. The next camera sits inside the inner vestibule door for faces, and a third gives the lobby and mailboxes as an overview. If the building has more than one entrance off the court, each gets its own entry view, named for its side. Exterior cameras go on plain brick rather than the ornamental band or carved trim, and they are mounted high enough that nobody can reach up and twist them. Cable for the court and lobby cameras usually drops into the cellar a few feet away, which keeps these the simplest runs in the building.

Walk-ups: cellar first, stair hall second, yard third

In a pre-war walk-up the cellar is the hub. It holds the meters, the internet provider's box and the boiler, so it is where the recorder goes, in a locked, dry spot away from the boiler's heat and away from any cellar door to the street. From there, cable for the vestibule and lobby climbs a short way, cable for the landings goes up a pipe chase or stacked closet if one exists, and only where there is truly no hidden path do we use slim raceway painted to match the stair hall. Yard cameras for the fire escapes are fed through the rear cellar wall, with a single drilled opening sealed afterward. Every floor the cable passes through is sealed with rated firestop. In buildings where the intercom still runs on old wiring, we trace those wires first and keep well clear of them, so tenants never lose their buzzers while we work.

Houses and two-families: drives, stoops and narrow side passages

The Tudor and revival houses west of Broadway and the brick two-families on Terrace View Avenue and Kingsbridge Terrace need a different kind of plan, built around the front door, the drive or side passage, the back door and the yard. On a Tudor we keep cameras off leaded glass, decorative half-timber and stone trim, mounting on plain brick or under an eave, and run cable through the attic or basement to a closet. On a semi-attached house, the shared side is someone else's home too, so cameras on that side are aimed along our own wall and walk, not at the neighbor's door. On a frame house like the porch houses on Fort Independence Street, the porch roof often gives the best spot for the front camera, high, sheltered and looking down the steps. A house usually needs four to six cameras and a small recorder, and the job is often done in a day.

Post-war co-ops: risers, garages and the board

The co-ops built from the 1960s onward, such as the 224-unit tower at 3125 Tibbett Avenue and the 72-unit building with a garage at 315 West 232nd Street, tend to have concrete or block construction, a lobby with a desk or a door attendant, a laundry room, and in some cases parking. Cable for added cameras goes up the building's riser or another approved chase, never into the elevator hoistway, and where floors are far from the recorder we add small PoE switches along the way. The board, not any one shareholder, approves the system and decides who holds the administrator account, so we write the plan for the board's meeting, with each camera's position, what it shows and who can view it. In buildings within the co-op complexes of Van Cortlandt Village, the same goes for the housing company's management office.

Stores under the el: gate, door, counter, back

A camera mounted high on the store's own façade or inside the sign box, with the landlord's written agreement, looking along the gate and the sidewalk, records the cutting. The second is the door from inside, framed for faces at head height as customers walk in. Bars, halls and late-night rooms

A Kingsbridge bar or a bingo hall is busiest when it is darkest. Inside, a dim room needs cameras that handle low light without switching to a grainy black-and-white picture every evening, placed so the entrance, the bar or counter, the register, and the restrooms' door (never inside) are covered. A hall that counts cash after a session needs a camera over the count table, a camera on the door to that room, and a recorder in a separate locked space. Busy nights and match crowds from Gaelic Park mean the outside door camera should be framed tight on faces rather than wide on the crowd, because a wide picture of fifty people identifies none of them.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Kingsbridge

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we test every view so a mirror does not show one.
  • The landlord decides outside the store. The façade, the sign box, the gate housing and shared hallways belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement first.
  • City and MTA structures are off limits. Nothing private goes on the el columns, lampposts or signposts; a sidewalk view comes from your own wall.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is mounted on a self-closing door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it latching.
  • Openings stay rated. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Landmarks need their permit. On designated buildings such as the Armory and St. John's Church, exterior camera positions need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before any drilling.
  • Audio stays off. We set business cameras to record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • The installer should be licensed. New York licenses businesses that install and service security systems through the Department of State; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords and insurers usually ask to see first.

Apartment houses on the hill and along Broadway

The man robbed at Cannon Place was grabbed at his building's side door. How can a side-door camera let the super know someone is waiting there before a tenant walks up?

Use two views and one alert. One camera inside the door, at head height, records the face of whoever comes through. A second, wider view takes in the sidewalk a few yards either side of the door. On that wider view we draw a zone around the doorway and set the recorder to flag anyone who stays inside it longer than a minute or so during the hours the building is quiet, sending a notice to the super or the managing agent's on-call phone. It will not stop a robbery, and nobody should promise that, but it means a person hanging around the side door at night is noticed and recorded, rather than discovered afterward.

The Heath Avenue break-in was a climb up a fire escape to a window. What should our yard camera show so the climb itself is on the record?

It should show the ground under the drop ladders and the first landing, from low on the rear wall or a fence post, looking up along the line of the fire escape rather than straight at the building face. That catches the person in the yard, the climb to the ladder and the first few rungs, which is the moment a face is most likely to turn toward the camera. Windows are kept out of the frame, and where the camera can mask parts of the picture we black out any that remain. A yard light on a motion sensor helps a great deal, because the camera's night picture is far sharper when there is some light on the climber.

Our vestibule door still has its old leaded-glass panels. Can a camera inside the vestibule see clearly through them?

Not well. Leaded glass is made of small panes set at slightly different angles, often textured or tinted, and a camera looking through it sees a broken, wavy picture, with reflections at night when the vestibule light is on. We put the camera on the inside of the door instead, aimed at people as they step through into the vestibule, and if the outer stoop needs covering we mount a small camera outside on plain brick beside or above the door. That keeps the glass untouched and gives a clean face rather than a mosaic.

Our co-op has a garage under the building. Where do the cameras go so the garage door and the cars are both covered?

Start at the door. One camera inside the garage, facing the door from far enough back that a car and the person driving it are both in frame, records everything that comes in under it, including anyone who slips in on foot behind a car. A second view facing out shows the ramp or curb cut. Overview cameras then cover the parking rows from the ends, mounted high and in hard housings, so they show who walks between the cars. The pedestrian door from the garage into the building gets its own face camera. Garages are dim and long, so we use cameras built for low light and choose lenses that fill the frame at the distances involved.

Shareholders in our tower co-op want to put their own cameras on their balconies. What should the board's rule cover?

A short written rule saves arguments later. It should say whether balcony cameras are allowed at all, whether they may be drilled into the building or must be free-standing, how high they can sit, that they must be aimed only at the shareholder's own balcony and not at neighbors' balconies, windows or the building's grounds, that no audio is recorded, and that they come down without damage when the apartment is sold. Boards often require shareholders to submit a photo of the planned spot and the camera's view before installing. If the board wants, we can look at sample balcony positions and describe what each would and would not see.

Our co-op is overseen by state housing regulators. Does a camera project need anything from them?

The Amalgamated Housing Cooperative, for example, says in its own published FAQ that it is supervised by New York State Homes and Community Renewal, and that apartments are subject to its prior approval. We found no published HCR rule about common-area cameras, so we do not tell boards one way or the other. The practical answer is to ask your management office in writing whether the project needs any notice to or approval from the agency, and to get the answer before ordering equipment. We prepare a written plan, positions, coverage and access list that the office can pass along if it is needed.

Houses, blocks and parked cars

Tenants on Irwin Avenue have reported car break-ins for months. How many parked cars along the curb can one camera on a building actually identify?

Fewer than most people hope. A typical camera on a building front shows the whole block in the picture but only identifies people and car details clearly within roughly the first few car lengths, and at night less than that. So we aim each curb camera at the cars directly in front of the building, slightly down the block, rather than trying to cover the whole street with one lens. Two cameras pointing in opposite directions along the curb cover the building's own frontage well. A camera with a narrower lens can watch a specific spot farther away, but then it sees nothing else. We set expectations before the install, so nobody is disappointed by a wide shot that shows a shape breaking a window but no face.

After something happens at our building, what can realistically be done in the first week?

Quite a lot, if the decisions are made quickly. CBS New York reported that after the Cannon Place robbery the landlord moved quickly to improve security, and that is the right instinct. In the first week we can walk the building, write a plan and a price, and on approval put cameras on the door involved, the other entrances and the recorder, often within days depending on equipment. Meanwhile, the owner should save any existing footage from the incident to a USB stick, check that every door closes and locks, and fix any broken lock right away, because cameras are not a substitute. A full system for the rest of the building can follow on the same cable paths.

Broadway and West 231st Street businesses

Our bar is dim and loud at night. Can a camera over the bar still give a face?

Yes, with the right camera in the right place. A bar is too dim for many cameras, which then switch to an infrared black-and-white picture and catch the glare of the bottles. Cameras made for low light hold a color picture in a dim room, and placing one at the end of the bar, at roughly head height, looking along the line of customers, shows faces rather than the tops of heads. The entrance camera does the most important work, though, because everyone walks through the door under the same light, facing the same way. Noise does not matter, since we record video only.

A bingo hall counts its cash after every session. Where do the cameras go in the count room?

One camera directly above or just in front of the count table, close enough to show bills and hands clearly, and a second on the door to the count room, so every person entering and leaving is recorded. If cash moves from the floor to the count room in a bag or box, a camera along that route covers the walk. The recorder should live in a separate locked space, not the count room itself, with access limited to the owner and named managers. On a crowded session day, the entrance cameras do the rest: faces at the door as people come in, set apart from the cash areas.

Does the Kingsbridge BID help stores with their cameras?

When the city created the BID in 2001, its role included helping merchants with security, and its office is at 3109 Kingsbridge Avenue. We do not know of any current BID program that pays for store cameras, so ask the BID directly what it offers now. When the Panda gate was cut, the Riverdale Press pointed to neighboring stores' cameras as the hope for video. Shops that agree to check their footage for each other after an incident make the whole strip harder to work.

We rent a unit at a self-storage building on Broadway. Can we put our own camera inside it?

That is the facility's decision, and the rental agreement or the manager will tell you. Many storage buildings have their own camera systems in halls and at doors, and some restrict anything powered inside a unit. If a camera is allowed, there is usually no outlet and weak cellular or Wi-Fi signal behind a metal door, so a battery camera that records to a memory card is the realistic choice, checked when you visit. Ask the manager first, in writing, before you leave anything running in the unit.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Kingsbridge

"Kingsbridge and Kingsbridge Heights are the same neighborhood."

"New York City requires apartment buildings to have security cameras."

Quick answers sometimes roll locks, intercoms and cameras into one legal requirement. Security-systems.nyc says plainly that most NYC apartment buildings are not legally required to have cameras.

What the law does require is different: in many buildings, self-closing and self-locking entrance doors, and in larger buildings built or converted after 1968, a working intercom. Community Board 8's complaint about main doors without working locks and buzzers is about exactly those requirements.

So cameras are the owner's or board's choice, and a camera never clears a lock or intercom problem. In Kingsbridge we often find the best first step is fixing the door, then putting a camera on it to prove the fix holds.

"One good camera on the front door covers an apartment building."

This one appears in product pages and kit bundles built around a single doorbell or entry camera. It assumes a building has one door that matters.

The Kingsbridge reports say otherwise. The Cannon Place robbery started at a side door, the Heath Avenue break-in was a fire-escape climb, and the Corlear Avenue lobby door was pried twice in the small hours. A front-door camera alone would have seen only one of the three.

We count every opening before we count cameras: court, vestibule, side door, cellar, yard, fire escape, roof and garage. Some buildings need cameras on only two or three of them; the point is to decide that on purpose.

"Cloud cameras mean you never have to worry about the recorder."

Subscription camera marketing often suggests that storing everything in the cloud solves the problem of a stolen or smashed recorder.

It does solve that problem, but it brings others in an old masonry building: weak Wi-Fi at side doors and yards, upload limits on a building's internet line, and monthly fees for every camera. And the burglar who took the DVR in Riverdale shows the local recorder needs protecting, not abandoning.

The approach that holds up here is a wired system recording to a locked local recorder, which keeps everything regardless of the internet, plus off-site copies of the clips that matter most, such as overnight motion at the doors and the gate.

"A camera inside the store is enough."

Retail-security advice often focuses on the sales floor and the register, and plenty of small shops put their only camera behind the glass.

When the Panda restaurant's gate at 5625 Broadway was cut in 2018, the Riverdale Press reported there was no video from inside, and the hope for footage rested on neighboring stores. An inside camera behind a closed gate sees the gate from the back, if at all.

On Broadway we treat the outside of the gate as its own camera job: high on the store's façade or in the sign box with the landlord's agreement, looking along the gate and the sidewalk, recording all night.

"Anyone can put up cameras; there is no license involved."

Some articles say no license is needed, and some say every camera installer needs one. The state sources are not perfectly clear: General Business Law section 69-m requires a license for the business of installing security systems, while a Department of State application says installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require licensure.

We do not pretend that conflict away. What is clear is that a licensed and insured installer is what landlords, boards and insurers usually ask for before work begins in a building.

AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and shows it, along with insurance, to owners, agents and boards in Kingsbridge before anyone climbs a ladder.

"Cameras keep burglars away."

Quick answers often say cameras deter break-ins, as if a dome on the wall were a lock.

The Kingsbridge reporting shows something more modest and more useful. The Cannon Place robbery and the Heath Avenue fire-escape break-in were both caught on camera; the cameras did not prevent them, but they recorded what happened, which is what police and the owners needed next.

That is how we place cameras: to show clearly who came through each door and when, mounted where they are hard to knock off aim, recording to a recorder nobody can carry away. Clear records help owners fix doors, change locks and hand police real evidence.

Kingsbridge Security Camera Installation FAQ

Is a Kingsbridge apartment building legally required to have security cameras?
+

In most cases, no. Security-systems.nyc notes that most New York City apartment buildings are not legally required to have cameras, and Seneca Security says there is no general city law mandating them. What the rules do require in many buildings is self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, in larger buildings built or converted after 1968, a working intercom. Cameras are a decision for the owner, the co-op board or the housing company, and they never take the place of a working lock or buzzer.

Our building is rent-stabilized. What should we put in writing before hallway and lobby cameras go up?
+

Seneca Security advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to document the security reason for the cameras in writing and to notify tenants in advance. A short notice usually covers which common areas will be recorded, that there will be no cameras inside apartments and no audio, roughly how long footage is kept, and who can view it. Keeping a copy of the notice and the reason, such as a break-in or a door that keeps being forced, gives the owner a clear record if questions come up later.

Is there a historic district in Kingsbridge, and does Landmarks review cameras on ordinary houses here?
+

We found no historic district covering Kingsbridge in the sources we checked, though owners should confirm their own address on the Landmarks Preservation Commission's map. Individually designated landmarks are a different matter: the Kingsbridge Armory and St. John's Church at 3021 Kingsbridge Avenue are city landmarks, and any work on a landmark needs an LPC permit first. For an ordinary Kingsbridge house or apartment building outside a district, Landmarks is not involved, but the owner, board or landlord still has to agree.

After a break-in, what footage helps most with an insurance claim?
+

Clips that show how the person got in, at what time, and what they took or damaged. That usually means the door or window that was forced, the route through the property and the exit, exported with the recorder's time stamp intact. Export the clips to a USB stick or your phone as soon as possible, before the recorder writes over them, and keep the original files rather than screen recordings. Ask your insurer what format and details they want, and give police the same clips if there is a report.

Can outdoor cameras be installed in winter in Kingsbridge?
+

Yes. Cable runs, cellar work, indoor cameras and recorder setup are not affected by the season at all. Outdoor mounts on brick and stone can be done in cold weather too; we choose anchors and sealants rated for the temperature and avoid working on icy ladders or slick step streets. On very cold or stormy days we may move the outdoor part of a job by a day or two and do the indoor part first, so the system is still recording at the doors that matter most.

Marble Hill is legally part of Manhattan. Does that change anything for a camera job there?
+

Very little in practice. Marble Hill uses the Bronx ZIP code 10463, Community Board 8 serves it, and the 50th Precinct covers it, so police requests for footage come from the same precinct as in Kingsbridge. What matters for the camera plan is the building. For residents of the Marble Hill Houses, NYCHA's own rules decide what can go on doors and in common areas, so those questions go to NYCHA first. For private buildings and stores, the owner or landlord signs off as anywhere else.

Our funeral home on Broadway wants cameras. Which rooms should stay off camera?
+

Families expect privacy in the rooms where they grieve, so viewing rooms, chapels and any preparation areas are usually left uncovered, along with restrooms. The cameras do their work at the entrances, the lobby, the office where payments and paperwork are handled, the parking area and the rear or service door where vehicles arrive. Placing a camera at each entrance framed for faces means everyone who comes in is recorded without anyone feeling watched during a service. Audio stays off throughout.

Do we need to hold a parking spot for the install crew near Broadway and West 231st Street?
+

It helps a great deal. The Kingsbridge Historical Society describes 231st and Broadway as a busy intersection with double-parked cars, buses and turning traffic, and a van that cannot stop nearby adds carrying time. If your building has a driveway, a garage, a loading area or a side street with easier parking, tell us when you book. If not, we plan for early arrival and keep ladders and cable inside the building rather than on the sidewalk, so the day stays short.

My parent doesn't use a smartphone. Can our home's door camera show on the TV in the living room?
+

Often, yes. Many recorders have a video output that can feed a spare TV input directly, and some cameras and recorders work with streaming devices plugged into the TV, so the front door view comes up with the remote. For someone who does not use apps, a direct connection to a small monitor near the door or a dedicated TV input is usually the simplest choice. Yes.
