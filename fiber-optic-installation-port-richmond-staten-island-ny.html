The Neighborhood

Why Port Richmond Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

Port Richmond grew up as a landing. Ferries crossed from its docks to Bergen Point in what is now Bayonne, coaches on the New York–Philadelphia road stopped here, and oyster captains built houses along Richmond Terrace in the 1840s and 1850s. The village incorporated in 1866, the North Shore railroad arrived in the 1880s, and by the early twentieth century Port Richmond Avenue had a Carnegie library, a domed synagogue and, from 1924, the 2,126-seat Ritz Theater. Planners would later remember the avenue as the “5th Avenue of Staten Island.” Then the Bayonne Bridge opened in 1931, the ferries stopped, the North Shore rail line closed in 1953, and the place settled into what it is now: a working neighborhood of small businesses, old houses and a waterfront that still earns its living.

That history matters for fiber because it explains who calls us. The city's 2024 needs assessment of the commercial district counted 392 storefronts along Port Richmond Avenue and Forest Avenue, and of the 201 merchants it surveyed, 67 percent rent their space and only 16 percent own it. The typical business has been open about eight years and has three full-time employees. These are restaurants, bodegas, barbershops, check-cashing counters, clinics, auto shops and churches, and very few of them have an IT department or a landlord who wants to talk about cabling. When their network fails, it is almost never because the provider is missing; it is because the connection lands in a cellar, a hallway or a corner of the building that has nothing to do with where the register, the camera recorder or the back office sits.

North of Richmond Terrace the picture changes. The waterfront between the avenue and the bridge is zoned M3-1 for heavy industry, and the Department of City Planning's Port Richmond brownfield study described its open land as fleet storage, auto repair, junk storage, bus parking, maritime support and vacant lots. Most of those businesses own or lease a yard rather than a building. Their computers live in a shed, a trailer-sized office or a scale house, and the same study warned that updated federal maps would likely place most land north of the Terrace in Flood Zone A, with some buildings needing to be raised by as much as seven feet. A copper run across a wet yard to a low office is exactly the kind of link that fails first; a properly sealed fiber link with its electronics mounted high is the kind that comes back on when the water goes down.

The side streets are the third Port Richmond. Heberton Avenue, Bennett Street, Park Avenue and the blocks toward Post Avenue hold frame houses, a few of them from before the Civil War, that have been re-sided, divided and rewired by whoever happened to be working on them in each decade; more than half of Port Richmond's homes are now rented. Some are individual city landmarks; most are simply old. Fiber suits them better than another round of copper, because one thin cable fished through a single cavity can replace the bundle stapled along the baseboards, and it carries no current that could cause trouble near old electrical work. For the copper that finishes the job inside each room, see our Staten Island structured cabling page.

In Port Richmond that usually means short links that cross something awkward: a cellar that belongs to someone else, a yard that floods, a house that was never meant to carry cable.

Building Specialists

Fiber Optic Installation for Port Richmond's Architecture

Port Richmond has no single building type. A short walk from the Reformed Church at the foot of the avenue to Forest Avenue passes Greek Revival frame, brick commercial blocks from the 1870s, a converted movie palace, low one-storey stores on slabs, metal sheds on the waterfront and a handful of newer apartment buildings. Each one asks the fiber to do something different.

Village-Era Frame Houses

Wood houses on Heberton Avenue, Cottage Place and the streets around them, some from the 1850s, with plaster on lath, narrow stair halls and fieldstone or brick foundations. No riser exists, so the route is a single cavity, a closet stack or a carefully placed exterior raceway, chosen with the owner before any hole is drilled.

Commercial Blocks With Flats Above

Two- and three-storey brick buildings along Port Richmond Avenue, some dating from the 1870s, with a store on the ground floor, apartments or offices above and a shared cellar below. Fiber usually rises through a rear stair or a chase beside the chimney, and the store and the upper floors are kept on separate termination points.

One-Storey Stores on Slabs

Low commercial buildings, including the strip buildings near Forest Avenue, with no cellar at all. The line comes in through a wall or the roof, and the only protected space for equipment may be a back-room shelf or a cabinet above the drop ceiling, so the layout is drawn around the stockroom.

Big Rooms: Theatres, Halls and Churches

The Ritz became a tile showroom in 1985, the old Empire Theater building was used by a lumber business until 2009, and the neighborhood's churches and halls have large open rooms. Fiber has to cross a wide floor or a high plaster ceiling without a grid to hide in.

Waterfront Sheds and Yard Offices

Steel buildings, block garages and small offices on the M3-1 waterfront, often on fill and inside the expected Zone A. Entries are sealed against water, enclosures go high on the wall, and outdoor runs use cable made for burial or wet conduit.

Newer Apartment Buildings

Newer apartment buildings on and near the avenue. These usually have a telecom closet; the work is extending clean fiber from it to a ground-floor store, a management office, a camera head-end or a laundry room.

Commercial Fiber Optic Installation

Business Fiber Optic Installation in Port Richmond

The commercial district studied by the city runs along two streets: Port Richmond Avenue from Richmond Terrace to Forest Avenue, and Forest Avenue from Manor Road to Port Richmond Avenue. The waterfront along Richmond Terrace is a third economy entirely. Here is how fiber work tends to differ along each.

Lower Port Richmond Avenue

From the Terrace up past Bennett Street: the oldest commercial buildings, the Reformed Church, restaurants and food shops. Expect shared cellars, party walls and stores whose provider entry sits in a neighbor's space. Traffic counts on this stretch were about 10,320 vehicles a day in 2023.

Upper Port Richmond Avenue

Toward Castleton Avenue and Post Avenue: clinics, salons, money-transfer counters, professional offices and offices above stores. The jobs are mostly two short links — front counter to back office, store to upstairs — with careful separation between tenants.

Forest Avenue

Busier and newer, with about 19,638 vehicles a day near Jewett Avenue in 2023 and a mix of chains, strip buildings and auto businesses. Low buildings on slabs and pad sites mean roof or wall entries and equipment that cannot live in a cellar.

Richmond Terrace Waterfront

Maritime support, auto repair, fleet and bus parking, construction-debris transfer and open storage, much of it on M3-1 land in the expected flood zone. Fiber here runs between buildings across a yard, not up through floors.

Castleton Avenue and the Side Streets

Corner stores, churches, day cares, small offices and the Castleton bus depot nearby. Many of these are house-scale buildings converted to business use, so the frame-house methods apply.

Houses of Worship and Community Groups

The assessment noted a prominent nonprofit and social-services presence on the avenue. Congregations and community groups often need an office, a hall and a pantry or classroom linked on one property without a large budget.

Rules that apply to commercial fiber work here

Department of Buildings. Low-voltage cabling inside an existing building generally does not need a DOB permit on its own, but any work tied to a larger alteration, a change of occupancy or a new building follows that job's permits. Where we core through a rated floor or wall, the opening is closed with a listed firestop system and photographed before it is covered.
Landmarks. Port Richmond has individual city landmarks — among them the Reformed Church, the Carnegie library branch on Bennett Street, 121 Heberton Avenue, the former P.S. 20 on Heberton Avenue and 29 Cottage Place — but no designated historic district. Exterior work on a designated building needs Landmarks Preservation Commission approval; neighboring buildings do not.
Flood zone and zoning. Most land north of Richmond Terrace was expected to be mapped Flood Zone A, and the waterfront is zoned M3-1. Neither rule stops low-voltage work, but both shape it: equipment goes above the base flood elevation where it can, and any future flood-proofing or elevation project should be planned with the fiber route in mind.
Digging. Any trench or post hole on a yard must be called in to New York 811 first, and many waterfront lots carry the legacy of industrial use. Where a site has a remediation history or an environmental cap, we stay out of the ground until the owner's environmental consultant has said where digging is allowed.
Renting the Space

Fiber for Merchants Who Rent Their Storefront

Two out of three Port Richmond merchants rent, and that single fact changes how a fiber job should be designed. A renter needs a link that works now, that the landlord will agree to, and that will not become an argument when the lease is renewed or ends. An owner can think in decades; a renter has to think in lease terms. We plan for both, but we start by asking which one you are.

The first step is a short written description of what we intend to do, in plain language, that a renting merchant can send to the landlord or the managing agent. It says where the cable will enter, which walls or floors it will pass through, how each opening will be sealed, where the small enclosure at each end will be mounted and what it will look like. Most landlords on the avenue are small owners, often living elsewhere on the island or off it, and a clear page with a sketch gets a faster yes than a phone call. If the landlord wants to see us, we will meet them at the building.

The second step is deciding which parts of the job are fixtures and which are equipment. Cable fastened inside walls and ceilings, sleeves through masonry and firestopping become part of the building in practice, and we design them to be useful to whoever comes next: labelled, documented and left with spare length coiled at each end. The switch, the media converter and the patch cords are the merchant's, mounted so they can be unplugged and carried to the next location in an afternoon. Writing that split into the proposal means nobody has to remember it later.

The third step is keeping the route inside the merchant's own space wherever the building allows it. In older blocks the provider's line often comes up in a cellar that is rented to a different store, or along a hallway that belongs to the apartments upstairs. Where that cannot be avoided, the run through someone else's space is kept short, enclosed and clearly marked with the merchant's name, so a future tenant downstairs does not mistake it for abandoned cable and pull it out.

None of this makes a renter's job more expensive than an owner's. It makes it more predictable. A storefront that knows exactly what was installed, where, and who it belongs to can renew a lease, sell a business or move three doors down without paying for the same cable twice.

The Waterfront

Richmond Terrace Yards: Fiber on Land That Works and Floods

North of Richmond Terrace, Port Richmond still looks the way the brownfield study described it: a strip of M3-1 land between the road and the Kill Van Kull, used for fleet and bus storage, auto repair, construction-debris transfer, maritime support and open storage, with the Bayonne Bridge rising over its western end. The study counted 37 sites with about 12.5 acres of open land in its first subarea alone, concentrated along the water. These are businesses that own or lease ground, not floors, and their networks are spread across it: an office near the gate, a shop or garage at the back, a scale or a camera mast somewhere in between.

Two things decide a fiber design on such a lot. The first is height. Because most of this land was expected to fall in Flood Zone A, every splice enclosure, switch and power supply goes as high on the wall as the building allows, with the cable entering from below through a sealed gland so water running down the conduit cannot reach the connectors. The cable itself can sit in wet conduit for years if it is the right kind — gel-free, water-blocked outdoor cable made for it — but electronics cannot. Storm Sandy showed what happens otherwise: a few minutes west along the Terrace in Mariners Harbor, one business owner told DNAinfo in December 2012 that the surge had topped his eight-foot seawall, and a neighbor said his building had been gutted.

The second is the ground. Waterfront lots in Port Richmond carry a long industrial history; the city's study named former paint, boat and lead sites among its strategic properties, one of them part of an EPA Superfund listing. We never trench through a yard on assumption. The route is called in to New York 811, the owner's records are checked for caps, liners or restricted areas, and where digging is off-limits we use surface-mounted conduit along fences and building walls, or an overhead span between two structures the owner controls. On land that carries heavy trucks, buried conduit goes deep enough and is sleeved where it crosses the driving lanes.

If your yard also needs cameras over the gate and the stacks, our Staten Island construction and yard camera crews can put them on the same fiber, and the warehouse camera team handles larger sheds.

Old Houses

Village-Era Houses: One Thin Cable Instead of Another Bundle

The houses off Port Richmond Avenue were built long before anyone imagined a network. The Landmarks Preservation Commission dates 121 Heberton Avenue to about 1859–61, the work of carpenter-builder James G. Burger, who also designed the Reformed Church; 29 Cottage Place is a two-storey Greek Revival frame cottage in the old saltbox form. Most of their neighbors are not landmarks, but they share the same bones: balloon or early platform framing, plaster on wood lath, stone or brick foundations and, very often, decades of telephone, cable and alarm wire stapled along the outside or the baseboards.

In a house like that, the goal is to add one path and remove three. We look first for a cavity that already runs from cellar to upper floor without fire-blocking — a closet stack, a chase beside an old chimney, a pipe chase from a bathroom added later — and fish a single small cable through it with a rod and a pull string, opening plaster only where an enclosure or outlet will sit. Where a cavity is blocked, we drill the plate carefully from below, never notching a joist, and close the opening around the cable with an approved fire-block material afterwards.

If no inside path exists, an exterior raceway is the honest alternative. Run vertically in a back corner, painted to match the siding and entering through a sealed sleeve under the eaves, it is less visible than the bundle of drop wires it replaces. On a designated landmark that exterior work needs the Commission's approval first; on the house next door it does not, but we still plan it so it can be removed without leaving scars.

Fiber has one more advantage in an old house: the glass carries light, not electricity. It can share a cavity with old wiring without adding heat or a shock risk, although we never open or disturb electrical work and will tell you if what we find needs an electrician's attention. When the house is also getting a doorbell camera or a smart lock, the same visit can include our Staten Island doorbell camera or smart lock installation crews.

Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in Port Richmond: Questions Answered

These are the situations Port Richmond owners, merchants and yard operators describe when they call, grouped by the kind of property. Each answer is the one we would give on a site walk.

Storefronts and Mixed-Use Buildings

Our storefront still has its old pressed-metal ceiling — can fiber get to the back of the shop without cutting into it?

Usually, yes. Pressed-metal panels are nailed to wood furring, and the space above them is rarely accessible from below without damage, so we do not try to go through the ceiling at all. The cable travels along the top of a wall in a small painted raceway or inside the crown molding line, drops through the stockroom wall, or runs below the floor from the cellar and comes up behind the counter. If a panel has to come down for another reason — a light fixture, a sprinkler head — we can use that opening, and the panel goes back on its original nails. We photograph the ceiling before work starts so there is no argument afterwards about what was already dented.

Our family's shop and our apartment upstairs share one internet account — how do we split them onto separate, separately billed lines?

The provider has to open a second account, but the wiring is ours to arrange. We bring a small fiber cable or two strands from the entry point to an enclosure in the store and another to the apartment, so each account terminates in its own space with its own equipment. The business then has a clean line for card terminals, cameras and the office computer, and the household's streaming and homework never compete with it. Splitting them also helps at tax time and makes the shop easier to sell or lease separately later, since the store's network is already self-contained. If you want cameras watching the shop from upstairs, that is done on purpose through a firewall rule, not by accident through a shared router.

Our one-storey store is built on a slab with no cellar — where does the provider's line come in, and where does our equipment go?

With no cellar, the line comes in through an outside wall, usually at the rear or the side, or occasionally down through the roof where the drop arrives overhead. We core a sealed sleeve at that point and mount the provider's terminal and our splice enclosure on the inside of the same wall, high enough to be out of the way of stock and mops. From there fiber runs above the ceiling or in a surface raceway to wherever the network equipment will live. In small slab stores that is often a ventilated wall cabinet in the stockroom or office rather than a floor rack, because floor space is worth more as selling space. If the building's roof carries rooftop units, a roof entry is flashed by a roofer, not by us.

Our showroom fills one big hall with a finished ceiling we can't open — how does fiber reach a sales desk standing in the middle of the floor?

There are three honest routes, and the walk-through decides which one fits. If the floor is wood over a cellar, we drill up from below directly under the desk and finish the hole with a flush floor box. If it is a slab, we look for an existing floor box or trench, or run along the nearest column and down a surface raceway to the desk. If neither is acceptable, the desk can sit at the end of a short, low-profile floor cord cover from the nearest wall, which is less elegant but leaves the building untouched. Fiber helps in all three cases because the cable is so thin that a single small channel can carry the data for the point-of-sale, a screen and a camera at once.

Yards, Shops and the Waterfront

Our yard's truck scale and scale house sit by the gate, a long way from the office — can fiber connect the scale terminal to the office computer?

Yes, and fiber is often the best answer for exactly that distance. Many scale indicators still talk over a serial connection, and some newer ones use a network port; either can be carried by a fiber link with a small converter at each end, so the reading appears on the office computer as if the indicator were next to it. Because fiber does not conduct electricity, a lightning strike near the scale or a fault on the scale house's power cannot travel down it into the office. We run outdoor cable in conduit, sleeve it where it crosses the truck lane, and mount the scale-house converter well above the floor. Your scale company stays responsible for the indicator and its calibration; we only build and test the link.

Can a fiber run go into an auto-body paint booth, or does it have to stop outside?

The cable can sometimes pass through; the electronics stay outside. Spray booths are treated as hazardous locations because of the solvent vapour, and any device that could spark — a switch, a camera, a converter, a power supply — must either stay outside the booth or be specifically rated for that classification. A passive, all-dielectric fiber cable carries no current, which is why it is sometimes chosen to link equipment on either side of a booth wall. In practice we keep the network outside the booth, run fiber to a rated or remotely located device when a booth camera or controller is needed, and follow the booth manufacturer's and the fire inspector's requirements. If they say no penetration, there is no penetration.

Our building north of Richmond Terrace is due to be flood-proofed in the next few years — what fiber work should go in now, and what should wait?

Put in what will survive the project and leave out what the project will move. The entry sleeve, the route above the expected flood line and the main enclosure on an upper wall can usually go in now, because a flood-proofing design will want the network up high anyway. What should wait is anything at or below floor level — a floor box, a low cabinet, a buried run that the contractor may excavate — and anything attached to walls that will be rebuilt. We ask to see the engineer's drawings if they exist, mark the fiber on them, and leave extra cable coiled at the enclosure so a raised floor or a new wall can be accommodated by re-routing rather than re-pulling. If the building will be physically elevated, plan for the link to be disconnected and reconnected as part of that job.

Community Uses

We run a licensed day care on the ground floor — how is fiber run so children can't reach the cable or the equipment?

By keeping everything above reach and behind something. Cable runs inside walls or ceilings, or in a closed raceway mounted high, never as loose cord along the floor or across a play area. The enclosure, switch and Wi-Fi access points go on walls above about six feet, in a locked cabinet or the office, with no exposed patch cords. Fiber itself is a small hazard compared with copper because it carries no electricity, but a broken strand can leave a tiny glass splinter, so we clean up offcuts on a mat and check the floor before we leave. Work is scheduled when the children are not there, and we can supply the details of the installation for your licensing file if your inspector asks.

Equipment

Types of Fiber Optic Cabling We Install

Most Port Richmond links are short and cross one difficult thing, so the choice of cable is driven by what it crosses rather than how far it goes.

OS2 Single-Mode

Our default for anything leaving a building or likely to be upgraded: a yard link to a scale house, a store-to-apartment split, a hall to a rectory. Inexpensive glass that will carry far faster equipment than any shop needs today.

OM4 Multimode

For short runs inside one building where the equipment at both ends already uses multimode optics, or where an existing multimode backbone is being extended rather than replaced.

Indoor/Outdoor Water-Blocked Cable

Dry water-blocking tapes instead of gel, so one cable can run from a damp cellar or wet yard conduit into a building without a transition splice at the door.

Armored Cable

A metal or heavy dielectric armor layer where cable could be crushed or chewed: under shop floors, in yard conduit that trucks cross, along a cellar beam where stock is stacked.

Storms and Overhead Lines

When the Wind Takes the Pole: Fiber and North Shore Storms

Staten Island is the city's overhead borough. In the city's 2013 study of power lines, 72 percent of the island's installed wire was on poles, compared with 22 percent in Queens and 12 percent in Brooklyn. Telephone and cable plant share many of those poles, which is why Port Richmond's internet service usually reaches a building as an aerial drop from the street. When Tropical Storm Isaias hit in August 2020, Con Edison reported 29,000 Staten Island customers without power, much of it from trees and branches bringing down overhead lines.

Private fiber cannot stop a provider's drop from coming down, and nothing on this page pretends otherwise. What it can do is make the owner's side of the network the part that does not fail. A fiber run inside the building is immune to the lightning surges and ground faults that travel along copper during storms, and a link to a yard building or an upstairs apartment comes back the moment the provider restores service, without a dead switch or a burnt port to replace.

Three simple choices make a Port Richmond network ride through storms better. Put a small battery backup under the switch and the provider's equipment at the main end, so a short outage does not reboot everything. Keep the provider's entry and our enclosure high on the wall, away from the water that follows a storm surge. And where a business cannot be offline at all, plan a second path — a cellular backup or a second provider entering by a different route — and let the fiber carry whichever one is working to the rooms that need it.

The Bridge and the Rails

Living Beside the Bayonne Bridge and the Old North Shore Line

Two pieces of regional infrastructure cut through Port Richmond, and both come up when owners plan a route. The Bayonne Bridge lands on the neighborhood's western edge. Between 2013 and 2019 the Port Authority raised its roadway inside the original 1931 arch to let larger ships reach the port, opening the new northbound deck in February 2017 and completing the southbound side in February 2019 at a total cost of about $1.7 billion. The land under its approaches belongs to the bridge, not to the businesses beside it.

The North Shore Branch of the Staten Island Railway is the other. The Baltimore and Ohio, which owned the line, raised the Port Richmond section onto a viaduct in 1935; the branch closed in 1953, and the old Port Richmond station still stands. The city's brownfield study recommended environmental review for a bus rapid transit line on the former rail right-of-way, so its future use is a public question.

For a fiber job, the lesson from both is the same: a private link is planned on private land. We do not attach to bridge structures, rail viaducts or public rights-of-way, and we do not route a customer's cable across them on the hope that nobody minds. When two buildings an owner controls sit on opposite sides of one of these, the practical choices are a circuit leased from a provider, a point-to-point wireless link between rooftops, or a route that goes around rather than across. We will tell you which one makes sense at the site walk.

FAQ

Port Richmond Fiber Optic Installation FAQ

Do you also cover Elm Park, Mariners Harbor, West Brighton and Westerleigh, next door to Port Richmond?
+

Yes. We work across the North Shore, so a job in Elm Park or Mariners Harbor to the west, West Brighton to the east or Westerleigh across Forest Avenue is booked and priced exactly like one on Port Richmond Avenue. Crews drive over from our Brooklyn base, and the site walk is arranged the same way wherever you are on the island.

Does armored fiber have to be grounded, and who does that?
+

If the armor is metal, yes. A metallic armor or strength member is a conductor, so the electrical code requires it to be bonded to the building's grounding system where the cable enters. We fit the bonding kit and leave the connection ready, and an electrician makes the final tie to the grounding electrode when one is needed. Where grounding would be awkward, as on a link between two separate buildings, we use all-dielectric cable so there is nothing to bond.

Will the router and switch we already have plug into the new fiber, or do we need new hardware?
+

Often they can stay. Fiber ends in a connector, not a regular network port, so something has to convert it: a small media converter beside your existing router, or an SFP module if your switch already has a fiber slot. We check the make and model of what you have during the site walk and tell you whether it can stay, needs a converter or should be replaced. Nothing is bought without your say-so.

How do you keep a new fiber run from being cut by the next electrician or contractor who opens the wall?
+

By making it obvious and recorded. Cable that runs inside walls and ceilings is labelled at both ends and at every access point with the owner's name and our number, enclosures carry a strand map, and we photograph the route before it is closed in. The owner keeps those photos and the sketch, so any later contractor can be shown exactly where the fiber is before they drill. Where it crosses a spot likely to be opened again, we sleeve or armor it.

Will plastic conduit on a sunny brick wall expand and pull apart in summer?
+

It can if it is installed without allowance for movement. PVC grows noticeably in length as it heats up, so a long run on a south- or west-facing wall gets an expansion fitting and straps that let it slide, and the joints are glued properly rather than pushed together. For short exterior runs on houses we often use painted metal raceway instead, which moves far less. The fiber inside is not harmed by the heat either way.

Does fiber need routine maintenance after it's installed?
+

Very little. The glass inside the walls does not wear out, so the care is at the ends: keep enclosures closed, keep dust caps on any unused connector, and do not let anyone unplug and replug fiber cords without cleaning them first, because a dirty end face is the most common cause of trouble. If you rearrange equipment or something stops working, a quick inspection and clean of the connectors usually fixes it.

Will your crew need to park in front of our store, and for how long?
+

Usually for part of the day. On Port Richmond Avenue and Richmond Terrace, where sidewalks are narrow and the city counts only about 152 on-street spaces along the avenue, we plan parking before the work day: a legal spot near the building, a side street, or the owner's lot or yard. The van is needed close by mainly while drilling and pulling cable; splicing and testing go on inside without blocking the curb.

Does fiber make our card terminals process payments faster?
+

Not the transaction itself; that speed is set by the payment processor. What fiber improves is the link between your terminals and the internet: it does not slow down when the store is busy, it is not disturbed by electrical noise from refrigerators, compressors or neon signs, and it does not fail because a copper wire corroded in a damp cellar. The result is fewer declined or stalled sales, especially at the busiest hours.
