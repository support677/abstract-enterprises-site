The Neighborhood

Why Tottenville Properties Need Cat6 Repair: Retrofit Runs in Houses Wired for Phones

Tottenville is the last stop. It is the southernmost neighbourhood in New York City and in New York State, bounded by the Arthur Kill on the west and north and by open water to the south, and the Staten Island Railway has ended here since June 2, 1860. The trip back up to St. George is fourteen miles and about forty-two minutes. Most people here own the place they live in — a little under 83 percent of homes are owner-occupied — and most of those homes are houses rather than apartments. That shapes what a Cat6 repair looks like. It is almost always somebody’s own house, wired one room at a time over the years it has been lived in.

The houses come from two building booms a century apart. The first is the old village around Main Street, Bentley Street, Hopping Avenue, Satterlee Street and the western end of Amboy Road: Greek Revival and Italianate houses from around 1850, Victorian and Gothic Revival cottages from the 1880s and 1890s, Colonial Revival houses from the early 1900s, many of them built for oyster catchers, boat builders, ship joiners and railway men. About one home in five in Tottenville dates from 1939 or earlier. The second boom began when the land south-east of Hylan Boulevard, which had been almost empty, filled with new houses in the 1990s. More than half of today’s homes were built between 1980 and 2009, and the median house dates from 1989. By 2006 a local broker quoted in The Cooperator was describing the newest houses as “Florida Style” centre-hall colonials with stucco and brick fronts, many of them replacing 14-foot-wide bungalows.

Neither generation was built with network cable in mind. A house from 1890 was wired, if at all, for electric light long after it was framed. A house from 1995 was handed over with telephone jacks and coaxial outlets, and the computer, the camera and the Wi-Fi point came later. So the Cat6 that serves most Tottenville rooms today is retrofit cable: fished down a stud bay, laid behind a knee wall, stapled across a garage ceiling, run down the outside of the house and in through the rim joist, or tucked behind a downspout to reach a camera at the back. Those routes were chosen for speed, not for twenty years of service, and they are where faults collect.

The third kind of house is the newest. Superstorm Sandy hit the Tottenville shore hard in October 2012; the Staten Island Advance described a wave tearing through the end of Yetman Avenue near Billop Avenue and leaving bare foundations full of muddy water, and the state later wrote that waves had knocked homes off their foundations. Many shore houses were repaired, raised or rebuilt, and in September 2024 the state finished the $111 million Living Breakwaters project offshore. Network cable in those homes is newer, but it was usually threaded through a house built in a hurry, around flood-resistant details, by trades who were not thinking about data. Our Staten Island Cat6 repair page covers the flood-line side of that in detail; this page is about the routes.

Tottenville facts that shape a Cat6 repair

  • 5,412 homes, median year built 1989. Of those, 2,998 — about 55 percent — went up between 1980 and 2009, the era of phone-and-coax wiring (American Community Survey estimates, via Point2).
  • 1,101 homes, about 20 percent, date from 1939 or earlier — the plaster-and-lath village where balloon framing and knee walls are common.
  • 51.6 percent detached houses, 24.4 percent in two-unit buildings, 19.8 percent attached single-family (same survey). Long runs to garages, yards and second units are normal; shared risers are not.
  • 2.505 square miles in the city’s southernmost neighbourhood; the wider Tottenville–Charleston tabulation area counted 16,089 residents in 2020, down 4.6 percent from 2010 (Wikipedia, citing the census).
  • Intense home building south-east of Hylan Boulevard in the 1990s, on land that had been almost uninhabited, with a second shopping core growing along Page Avenue and west of Amboy Road.
  • Living Breakwaters, completed in September 2024: eight partially submerged breakwaters, 2,400 linear feet, designed to bring waves near homes and roads below three feet, with sand added from Manhattan Street to Loretto Street.

Mid-1800s Village Houses

Greek Revival and Italianate houses from about 1850 on Bentley Street and Amboy Road, many built for boat builders and ship joiners. Heavy timber, plaster on lath, low cellars and few straight paths between floors.

Late-Victorian Cottages and Porch Houses

Gothic Revival and other Victorian houses of the 1880s and 1890s on Hopping Avenue and nearby streets, often with wrap-around porches, sunrooms and dormered top floors with knee walls.

Early-1900s Colonial Revival Houses

Houses of 1900–1925 with larger rooms and attics, still usually balloon-framed or close to it, where a cable can drop a long way inside one cavity.

1980s–2000s Colonials and Two-Families

The houses south-east of Hylan Boulevard: centre-hall colonials, side-hall houses and two-family homes with attached garages, finished basements and vinyl, stucco or brick fronts. Built with phone and coax; network cable added later.

Townhouse and Condo Communities

Attached houses in managed communities, where the outside walls and roof may belong to the association and any new hole for a camera run needs its approval first.

Raised and Rebuilt Shore Houses

Homes near the beach and the Conference House Park side that were repaired, raised or rebuilt after Sandy, with entries, enclosures and utility space arranged around flood rules.

How the repair changes with each house

  • Village houses: we look for the open stud bay the original installer used and decide whether the replacement can use it again, then restore any fire blocking we pass through. Holes in old joists stay within the usual boring limits.
  • Porch and dormer houses: runs behind knee walls are reached from the hatch or a small opening at the base of the wall, and new cable is supported off the floor of that space so stored boxes do not crush it.
  • 1990s colonials: the garage, the attic over it and the outside walls are where the shortcuts are. We re-route away from the opener and the door track, and move outdoor boxes onto mounting blocks.
  • Two-family houses: each unit’s runs are traced to their own end before anything is cut, because the two networks frequently share a basement wall.
  • Townhouse communities: outside work waits for the association’s approval; inside work does not, and we can usually test and repair indoor links on the same visit.
  • Shore houses: terminations and equipment stay above the flood-resistant levels the house was rebuilt to; only continuous cable passes lower.

Materials we match, era by era

A repair should look as if it belongs to the house. In the village houses that usually means low-profile plates in white or ivory that sit flat on uneven plaster, mounted on old-work brackets that grip the lath rather than a box that needs a large hole, and cable kept out of sight along the cellar joists rather than across the baseboards. Where an old house has original trim, we go under it or behind it, never through it.

In the colonials, the existing plates and media panel set the standard. Builders of the 1990s and 2000s often used one brand of plate throughout the house, sometimes with phone, coax and data on the same plate; we match the style and colour of the plate and keep the other services on it working when we replace the data jack. Outside, boxes and raceway come in white, beige or grey to sit quietly against the siding or stucco, and on brick we follow the mortar lines.

In the shore rebuilds the materials are dictated more by the rules than by looks: flood-resistant finishes below the design flood level, sealed boxes, and continuous cable only. Upstairs, on the living floors, the work is the same as in any newer house.

What a South Shore storefront needs from a repair

The pattern is the same up and down Amboy Road: a counter with a card terminal, a receipt printer and sometimes a kitchen printer; a back office with one or two desks; a handful of cameras on the front and the parking lot; and a router the internet company left on a shelf. The links that fail first are the ones that move or get kicked — under the counter, behind the printer, at the front door — and the outdoor camera runs. We test the counter first, keep it working through opening hours, and leave the longer work for when the shop is quiet.

Compliance callouts for Cat6 repair in Tottenville

  • Permits: a Cat6 repair in a Tottenville house normally needs no electrical permit. Section 28-105.4.7 of the city’s Administrative Code exempts signalling, alarm, communication and data circuits under 50 volts, provided the person doing the work is a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person under the Electrical Code.
  • Landmarked houses: several Tottenville buildings are individual city landmarks, among them the Bedell House at 7484 Amboy Road, the Rutan-Journeay House at 7647 and the library at 7430. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s guidebook ties interior work to a permit only when it also needs a DOB permit or affects the outside; any change to a landmark’s exterior, such as a new camera cable entry, is for the owner to clear with the Commission first.
  • Separation from power: indoor communications cable is kept at least two inches from light and power conductors except where the code allows otherwise, which matters in older houses where data cable was pushed through the same holes as electrical wiring.
  • Abandoned cable: dead runs left behind in walls and ceilings make the next fault harder to trace and crowd the holes a new run needs; we offer to take out whatever is accessible when we replace a run.
  • Fire blocking: where a balloon-framed house has had fire blocking added in its stud bays, a cable that passes through it is sealed again afterwards, so the blocking still does its job.
  • Structure: holes through joists follow the usual boring limits; in the model residential code, bored holes stay at least 2 inches from the top and bottom edges and no larger than one-third of the joist’s depth. Anything beyond that is a question for a carpenter or an engineer, not for us.
Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Tottenville: Questions Answered

These are the questions Tottenville owners put to us about the routes their cable takes. They are grouped by the part of the house where the trouble lives, and each is answered straight away.

Inside the village houses

In our 1880s house the wall cavities run straight from the cellar to the attic — when a Cat6 run dropped down one of them fails, can its replacement go down the same space?

Usually, yes, and that open cavity is the best thing about an old balloon-framed house from a cabling point of view. In that way of building, long studs run from the sill to the roof, so a single stud bay can be a clear vertical path from the cellar to the attic. The failed cable is often still the easiest pull line for its replacement: we test it first to find where the damage is, tie the new Cat6 to its end and draw it through, which keeps the plaster closed.

There are three things we check before relying on that path. The first is what else is in the bay: old knob-and-tube or later electrical cable, a gas pipe, a plumbing vent. Data cable is kept at least two inches from power conductors, so a crowded bay may mean moving one stud bay over. The second is whether a later owner added fire blocking — This Old House’s writers describe those open bays as acting “like chimneys” that let fire travel from basement to attic in minutes, which is why renovators block them. If our cable passes a block, we seal around it again with a suitable fire-rated material. The third is what the cable rubbed on: in old houses the damage is often at a sharp edge where the bay meets a floor plate or a nail, and the replacement gets a grommet or a sleeve at that point.

When the bay is blocked solid, we look for the next easiest path — a closet stacked over a closet, a chase beside a chimney that is still in use only for the boiler, or a run along the cellar ceiling and up an inside wall — and we show you the options before opening anything.

The only cable to our dormer bedroom runs through the low space behind the knee wall — how is a failed run there reached?

Through the knee-wall hatch if the house has one, or through a small opening at the base of the wall if it does not. A knee wall is the short wall under a sloped ceiling on the top floor of a one-and-a-half-storey house, and behind it is a cramped triangular attic. Tottenville’s cottages and porch houses have plenty of them, and they are a favourite route for cable because they connect one end of the top floor to the other without opening a finished room.

The trouble is what happens to cable in there over the years. It is laid loose on the floor joists, so stored boxes, suitcases and Christmas decorations end up on top of it. Insulation gets added and the cable gets buried. In houses where the knee-wall space is outside the heated part of the house, it is hot in summer and cold in winter, and the jack at the end often sits in a wall that moves with the temperature. We test the run to find the damaged length, then replace it supported off the joists on hooks or in a light raceway along the back of the knee wall, where storage cannot reach it.

If there is no hatch at all, we can usually make one small opening, low down where furniture hides it, and fit a removable access panel so the next repair needs no cutting. We will tell you before we start whether the run needs that or can be reached from the attic above.

How large a hole can be drilled through the old floor joists for a replacement run, and does where it goes matter?

Size and position both matter, and in a house with timbers more than a century old we stay well inside the usual limits. The model residential code says bored holes in joists must not be within 2 inches of the top or bottom edge and must not exceed one-third of the joist’s depth. A single Cat6 cable needs a hole well under an inch, so the rule is easy to keep; the risk comes from earlier trades who notched the bottom of a joist or drilled several holes side by side. Before we drill, we look at what is already cut, keep our hole near the middle of the joist’s depth, and avoid the ends where joists bear on beams or walls.

Old joists are often deeper and rougher than modern lumber, sometimes with old bark edges or splits. If a joist is already split, notched or sagging, we route around it or under it and point it out to you; deciding what that joist needs is a job for a carpenter or an engineer, and city rules for your house are theirs to apply. In practice, most Tottenville cellar runs can follow the joists rather than cross them, which needs no drilling at all.

Around the newer colonials

Which way should the Cat6 to the camera above our garage door cross the ceiling so the door and opener can never reach it?

Off to one side of everything that moves, and down the wall beside the door frame rather than across the header. An opener’s rail, its trolley and chain or belt, the door panels as they roll up, the torsion spring and its cables all sweep the area above and beside the door every time it opens, so a cable stapled close to them gets brushed, pinched between a panel and the track, or caught when the door is serviced. Opener makers take care even with their own thin control wires: LiftMaster’s manual tells installers to staple the door-control wire to the wall and ceiling without piercing it, because a pierced wire can short or open.

In practice the replacement crosses the garage ceiling well to one side of the rail, then drops to the camera along the wall beside the door frame, supported every few feet with hardware that grips without crushing. If the camera sits right over the door, the last few feet go in a short protective raceway. The staples that held the old cable come out too, because a staple driven hard through a jacket is often where the fault started in the first place.

While we are in the garage we also check the run that goes on into the house. In many colonials the router or switch lives on a garage or basement wall, and the garage is unheated, so connections there get the full range of seasonal temperature; a clean, well-supported termination copes with that much better than a cramped one.

Someone ran our backyard camera cable down inside the gutter downspout — is that why it keeps failing, and where should it go instead?

Almost certainly. A downspout looks like a ready-made conduit, which is why people use it, but it is a pipe built to carry all the water off a roof. Inside, the cable sits in running water through every rainstorm, in standing water when the bottom elbow clogs, and in ice when that water freezes. Leaves and grit grind against the jacket. Water wicks along any nick to the plug at the bottom, and most of these runs end in an unsealed coupler or a bare plug at the foot of the spout. Even outdoor-rated cable is not made to live inside a drain.

The better route depends on the house. On a sided colonial we usually take the cable inside at the soffit or the rim joist close to the camera and run it through the attic or the basement, so only a few feet are ever outdoors. Where it has to stay outside, it goes in its own small UV-stable conduit or raceway beside the downspout, with sealed fittings, a drip loop where it enters the wall, and an outdoor-rated box at the camera. The old cable comes out of the downspout, which also lets the gutter drain properly again.

If the camera has stopped working at all, we test the run before blaming the camera; a soaked plug can look exactly like a dead camera, and a cable that tests clean after the plug is replaced means you saved the price of a camera.

The box where our camera’s Cat6 meets its patch lead is screwed straight to the vinyl siding and keeps pulling loose — how should it be remounted?

On a mounting block, fixed to solid backing behind the siding. Vinyl siding is designed to slide a little as it expands in the sun and contracts in the cold, and siding makers say plainly that fixtures must not be attached to it directly: CertainTeed’s guidance is to attach external fixtures to solid backing such as exterior-grade plywood, never directly to the vinyl, and to allow for the siding’s movement. A box screwed through the vinyl alone is being pulled back and forth every day, so the screws loosen, the box tilts, the gasket opens and the plug inside works itself out.

We replace that arrangement with a mounting block sized for the siding profile, fastened through to the sheathing and framing, with the weatherproof box on the block and the cable entering from below with a drip loop. The connection inside the box is remade with a fresh termination, because a plug that has been rocking in a damp box for a season is rarely worth keeping. On stucco or brick fronts the box goes on masonry anchors set into sound material, and any crumbling around the old holes is patched so water cannot get behind it.

If the siding itself is due to be replaced, it is worth telling the siding contractor about the camera runs before they start, so the blocks go in under the new siding and the cables are not cut or buried; we can leave the cable coiled and labelled ready for them.

The bonus attic over an attached garage

In many colonials the easiest way from the router to the upstairs rooms is through the low attic above the garage, and that is where a lot of retrofit cable lies. It is reached through a pull-down hatch in the garage ceiling or a small door from a bedroom closet, it is usually unfinished and unheated, and it is where the household keeps whatever does not fit anywhere else. Cable laid loose across the ceiling joists there gets trodden on, crushed by stored boxes and sometimes stapled by whoever added insulation. When we repair a run through that space we lift it onto supports along the side of a rafter or a top plate, out of the walking and storage area, and replace any section the tester shows has been crushed.

Routes that turn up in every kind of house

Runs that share a hole with power, water or gas

Retrofitters like an existing hole. In both old and new Tottenville houses we find Cat6 pushed through the same bored hole as an electrical cable, wrapped around a gas pipe across the cellar ceiling, or sharing a sleeve with a water line to an outside tap. Apart from the two-inch separation from power, these runs get disturbed whenever the plumber or electrician works on their own lines. When we replace such a run, it gets its own hole or its own support line, labelled as data, so the next trade knows to leave it alone.

Porch and sunroom jacks on the outside of the old wall

Tottenville is known for Victorian houses with wrap-around porches and sunrooms, and over the years some of those porches have been closed in as rooms. A jack added in an enclosed porch is often fed through what used to be an outside wall, sometimes through the old clapboard and sheathing, sometimes from under the porch floor. Those runs see more damp and temperature change than any indoor run, and the termination should be treated as semi-outdoor: sealed box, a cable with a suitable jacket, and an entry that does not let water track along it into the house.

Second-unit links in a two-family house

Nearly a quarter of Tottenville homes are in two-unit buildings. Where the owner shares an internet connection with the second unit, or has run a camera feed from it, the cable usually crosses a basement wall or a stairwell that both households use. We trace each unit’s runs to their own ends before cutting anything, label them by unit, and keep the repair inside the space the owner controls wherever possible.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Tottenville

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so we have not quoted it. What follows are seven claims that cost guides, product blogs, forum answers and AI summaries commonly repeat about network cable in houses, set against what we actually meet on Tottenville streets.

“Cat6 is Cat6 — any cable will do outdoors”

The category describes how a cable performs electrically. It says nothing about the jacket. Ordinary indoor Cat6 has a jacket made to sit in a dry wall cavity, and when it is run along a soffit or a back fence it hardens and cracks in sunlight and lets water travel along the pairs to the nearest plug.

Many of the outdoor faults we find on Tottenville houses start this way: a camera installed in a hurry with the cable left over from the indoor job, clipped along the siding and taken through a window frame or a vent. It works perfectly for a season or two, which is why the shortcut seems fine at the time.

The repair keeps outdoor exposure to the minimum, using cable and fittings made for it: the run comes inside close to the camera, any length that must stay outside is outdoor-rated and protected, and every outdoor ending sits in a sealed box with a drip loop below it.

“A house built in the 1990s came pre-wired for Ethernet”

Some did, as an option, but most houses of that decade were handed over with telephone jacks and coaxial cable-TV outlets, which is what buyers asked for at the time. In Tottenville, where the median home was built in 1989 and more than half went up between 1980 and 2009, that means most network cable was added by later owners.

Where a builder did put in “data” cable, it was often Cat5 or Cat5e run to a small panel in a closet or the basement, sometimes on the same plate as the phone jack. Cable of that age can still pass a test and carry a perfectly good connection, but it is not the same as a fresh Cat6 run, and the plates and panels are now thirty years old.

So the first step on a 1990s house is finding out what is actually in the wall. We test it, read the printing on the jacket where we can see it, and tell you whether the run should be repaired as it is, re-terminated to modern hardware, or replaced.

“Just drill wherever the cable needs to go”

Drilling is easy; drilling in the right place is the skill. A joist can be weakened by a hole too near its edge, and the model residential code sets the usual limits: no closer than 2 inches to the top or bottom, and no wider than a third of the joist’s depth. Old village houses have often been notched and drilled by earlier trades already.

Walls have their own hidden traffic. Electrical cable runs up the same bays, plumbing vents and gas lines cross floors, and some balloon-framed houses have had fire blocking added between floors that a careless hole would defeat. On the outside, a hole through siding or stucco in the wrong spot lets water straight into the wall.

Before we drill, we look at what is already cut, find the safest path, and agree the location with you. Every hole we make through the outside of the house is sleeved and sealed, and every hole through fire blocking is sealed again.

“You can screw a camera box onto the siding like anything else”

Vinyl siding moves. It is hung so that it can slide as it expands and contracts with the weather, and siding makers tell installers never to fasten a fixture directly to it but to use solid backing and allow for that movement. A box screwed straight through vinyl is being worked loose every day.

The result is predictable: a box that tilts, a gasket that opens, a plug that works out of its socket, and a camera that drops off the network in the first cold snap. We see the same thing on stucco fronts where anchors were set into the stucco skin alone and the material crumbled around them.

The fix is a proper mounting block fastened through to the sheathing and framing on sided houses, sound anchors in masonry on stucco and brick, and a fresh termination inside a sealed box once the mounting is right.

“Repairing the cable will fix slow internet”

Only if the cable is the bottleneck. A Cat6 run in good order can carry far more than most home internet plans deliver, so a slow connection is just as often the plan, the router, the Wi-Fi in the room or the device itself. A damaged run usually shows itself differently: a link that drops, a jack that connects at a fraction of its normal speed, or a camera that goes offline.

That is why we test before we quote. A certification result that passes tells you the cable is not your problem, which is worth knowing before you spend money on it, and it gives your internet provider one less thing to blame.

When the test does fail, we can usually tell you how much of the slowdown the cable was causing, and once the run is repaired we check the device again at its full link speed so you can see the difference.

“Running new cable through a century-old house means tearing into plaster”

Old houses are sometimes easier than new ones. A balloon-framed house of the 1880s or 1900s may have open stud bays running from the cellar to the attic, which makes a vertical replacement run straightforward, and knee-wall spaces on the top floor connect rooms without touching plaster.

The challenge is different: knowing what else shares those cavities, respecting fire blocking, and protecting old plaster and woodwork when an opening is unavoidable. That takes more care and more looking first, not more demolition.

In practice, most of the village houses we work in are repaired through existing openings, a hatch, a closet floor or a small access panel at the base of a wall, and we tell you before we start whether any opening at all will be needed.

“Expect to pay $125–$250 a drop”

That range is real — the cost guide Fixr gave $125–$250 per new Cat6 drop including materials and labour, and $45–$75 an hour for labour, in January 2025 — but it is a national average for new installation, not for finding and repairing a fault in a lived-in house.

Repair is mostly time spent testing, tracing and getting at the fault, and it depends on the route rather than the number of jacks. A worn jack in a basement panel is quick; a run through a downspout and across a garage ceiling is not.

Outside the House

Why Outdoor Cat6 Has a Harder Life at the South End of the Island

Tottenville has water on three sides: the Arthur Kill along the west and north, and the bay to the south. Houses near the shore and the park get wind off the water, and every house gets the long summer sun on its south and west walls. None of that is unusual for a coastal neighbourhood, but it matters for a cable that was only ever meant to live indoors and has ended up stapled along a soffit or tucked behind a gutter.

The outdoor faults we see follow a pattern. Sunlit indoor jacket goes brittle and cracks first on the side facing the sun. Plugs left in unsealed boxes collect condensation and dust and lose contact a pin at a time, which shows up as a camera that reconnects every few minutes. Boxes fixed straight to vinyl or into crumbling stucco tilt and let water in. Cable that crosses a lawn or a patio on the surface gets stepped on, mowed over or chewed. And anything run through a downspout gets the full force of every storm.

The repair principles are simple and the same on every house: bring the cable indoors as close to the device as possible; use outdoor-rated cable and protected raceway for the parts that must stay outside; mount boxes on proper blocks or sound masonry; seal every entry and give it a drip loop; and leave every outdoor ending tested and labelled. Shore houses that were rebuilt after Sandy get one more rule, set out on our borough page: keep every ending above the flood-resistant level of the house, and let only continuous cable pass below it.

It is also worth asking before any big outside job — new siding, a new roof, a re-stuccoed front, a replaced garage door. Those trades are the most common cause of outdoor cable failures we meet, not because they are careless but because nobody told them the cable was there. A ten-minute check and a few labels before they start saves a repair visit afterwards.

FAQ

Tottenville Cat6 Repair FAQ

Will you still work in our attic or behind a knee wall during a summer heat wave?
+

Yes, but we plan it differently. The space under a roof is often far hotter than the rooms below on a July afternoon, so attic and knee-wall work is booked for the morning when we can, the technician takes breaks, and the hatch is left open with a fan running if you have one. If the forecast is extreme, we may test and trace from inside the house on the first day and do the attic part early the next morning. We will tell you that when we book rather than on the day, and it does not change the price, which is set by the work, not the weather.

Every December our front camera drops off the network once the holiday lights go up along the gutter — could the decorations be damaging its cable?
+

Quite possibly, and it is one of the easier faults to prevent. Light clips, staples and extension cords end up along the same soffit, gutter and porch rail that a camera’s Cat6 follows, so the cable gets clipped over, pinched under a hook, or tugged when the strands come down in January. We test the run to find where it was damaged, replace that length with outdoor-rated cable inside a small raceway so clips cannot reach it, and label it on the outside so whoever hangs the lights next year leaves it alone. If you put the decorations up yourself, a photo of where the camera cable runs saves guessing.

The weatherproof data outlet on our back deck has stopped working — can it be repaired, or does the whole outdoor outlet have to be replaced?
+

Usually the outlet can stay and only its insides change. Outdoor data outlets sit in a box with a hinged or gasketed cover; when the cover is left open or the gasket hardens, rain and dew reach the jack and its contacts corrode a pin at a time. We test the run from the router end first to make sure the cable behind the box is sound, then fit a fresh jack of the same category, renew the gasket or cover, and check that the box drains instead of holding water. If the cable itself has been wet for a long time, the tester shows it and we replace the outdoor length back to where it enters the house. The outlet is certified like any indoor link, so a laptop or camera plugged in out there gets a full connection.

The builder left a box of spare cable and jacks in our basement — can you use it for the repair?
+

Often, yes. We look at what is in the box first: the category printed on the jacket and the jacks, whether the cable is solid copper rather than copper-clad aluminium, and whether it is rated for where it has to go. Indoor cable cannot be used outside, and older Cat5e left over from the build may not match a Cat6 run. If the parts are suitable we install them and charge for time only, with the same testing; the manufacturer, not AESS, warrants parts you supply. If they are not suitable, we tell you why before starting and quote ours instead.
