The Neighborhood

Why Madison Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Madison is the quiet northern part of Sheepshead Bay, a purely residential section just south of Midwood. Wikipedia gives its edges as Kings Highway to the north, Avenue U to the south, Ocean Avenue to the west and Nostrand Avenue to the east; CityNeighborhoods.NYC stretches the eastern edge to Gerritsen Avenue as well. It takes its name from James Madison High School, uses ZIP code 11229, is served by Brooklyn Community Board 15 and is patrolled by the 61st Precinct. The 2020 census figures that City Planning publishes put between 20,000 and 29,999 white residents and between 5,000 and 9,999 Asian residents in the northern section it calls Madison, with fewer than 5,000 Black and fewer than 5,000 Hispanic residents. The B2, B7, B31, B82 and B82 Select Bus Service, B100 and BM4 buses serve the section, and the B44 and B49, which Wikipedia lists for Sheepshead Bay, run along Nostrand Avenue and Ocean Avenue.

Ask anyone who sells houses here what Madison is made of and you will hear the same answer. A local brokerage describes the common property types as detached homes, semi-detached homes and two-family houses, with select condos or co-ops around the neighborhood edges. CityNeighborhoods.NYC describes the inner blocks as quiet lanes of detached brick and stucco homes with driveways, and places the larger apartment buildings along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue, with the commercial life on Kings Highway, Avenue R and Nostrand Avenue. The city's tax-lot file agrees. When we counted the lots on October 22, 2026, we found 2,739 lots with homes on them and 6,322 homes in all. Ninety-five percent of the lots hold one or two families: 1,811 one-family houses and 792 two-family houses. The apartment buildings are few, but they are large: 47 buildings with 20 or more homes hold 2,486 homes, about four in ten of all the homes in Madison.

Those 47 buildings are where nearly all of the buzzer work in Madison happens, and they come in three distinct shapes. Twenty-seven of them went up between 1921 and 1941, almost all on Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway and the short side streets beside it; they are mostly six stories, with a median of 48 apartments, a frontage on the street that averages about 119 feet, and a depth of about 102 feet. Eight went up between 1954 and 1963, most of them on Nostrand Avenue; they are six stories as well, but they average about 229 feet along the street and only about 73 feet deep, and they hold a median of 73 apartments each. The other twelve went up between 2005 and 2025, half of them on Ocean Avenue between Avenue S and Avenue U; they are narrow, 50 to 100 feet wide, and six to nine stories tall. Four of the 1950s buildings and four of the prewar ones are classed as cooperatives, and seven of the twelve newer ones as condominiums.

A buzzer behaves differently in each of those shapes, which is why this page keeps coming back to geometry. A long, shallow building has a short climb from cellar to roof and a very long walk underneath. A deep prewar building typically wraps its apartments around a light court or along a long hall, and its lines climb in several places. A narrow new building is the opposite of the long one: a short footprint and a tall stack. And on the inner streets, where no apartment building stands, the buzzer is a house doorbell with a transformer in the cellar and, in the two-family houses, a release button upstairs. The first thing we want to know when someone calls from Madison is which of these the door belongs to.

Across, Not Up

Why Madison Buzzers Are Measured Along the Building

Most guides to buzzer repair sort buildings by height: walk-ups against elevator buildings, five floors against six, low-rise against tower. That sorting is useless in Madison, because nearly everything with an elevator is six stories. What separates one Madison apartment house from another is its footprint, and the footprint decides three things a technician cares about: how far the wire has to travel from the supply to the farthest apartment, how many separate vertical lines of apartments the system feeds, and how many doors the building has. We call this measuring the building across rather than up, and it is the first thing we do on a Madison call, usually from the tax map and a photo before anyone arrives.

Start with the long buildings. Between 1954 and 1963 the east side of Madison gained a row of six-story co-ops and rentals on Nostrand Avenue that look nothing like the prewar buildings across the neighborhood. According to MapPLUTO, two of them stand side by side on lots 290 feet wide, with buildings about 254 feet long and only about 57 feet deep, built a year apart; another lot further south carries two buildings with 177 apartments between them and a frontage of about 600 feet. In a building like that, wherever the lobby sits, the apartments at the far end are reached by a cable that first travels along the cellar, in the longest buildings well over a hundred feet, before it climbs. Every foot of that cable adds a little resistance. A voice signal hardly notices; an electric strike, which needs real current to pull its keeper back, does.

The prewar buildings on Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway are a different problem. They are shorter on the street, around 60 to 240 feet, but they run back from it: the Kings Highway buildings in our study area are as much as 139 feet deep. In our experience, buildings of that shape put their apartments along halls and around light courts, so their lines climb in several places, front and rear, and the rear lines are often reached through cellars that have been rearranged many times over ninety years. Here the question is less about distance and more about which line a dead apartment belongs to, and what has happened to that line's route since 1930.

The newest buildings reverse the long building's geometry. A condo on lower Ocean Avenue that went up after 2004 typically sits on a lot 50 to 85 feet wide and rises seven or eight floors. Its entry wiring makes a short trip from the lobby to a single vertical path, and in many of them the system is networked rather than wired in pairs. Distance is rarely its enemy; configuration, accounts and power are. And the houses on the inner streets are the shortest circuits of all: a button, a transformer, a bell, and sometimes a release button upstairs in a two-family house.

This matters for the bill as much as for the diagnosis. In a long building, a technician who starts at the lobby panel and works outward can spend an hour proving that the panel is fine. A technician who has measured the building first starts at the far end, where a weak release shows up first, and compares what the strike receives from the near apartment with what it receives from the far one. That comparison, made in the first half hour, usually tells us whether we are looking at the supply, the run along the cellar, one line, or one apartment.

The Public Record

What the City's Files Say About Madison's Doors

The city keeps two public records that touch Madison's front doors. HPD, the housing agency, writes violations when an inspector finds a defect in an apartment building, and it writes the intercom ones under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code. 311 logs complaints, including a category for bells, buzzers and intercoms. We pulled both for the tax blocks inside our study area on October 22, 2026, and kept only records filed under Community Board 15, which removes the buildings across Kings Highway in Midwood.

The headline is how small and how concentrated the record is. Since 1985 HPD has written 52 intercom violations in Madison, at 22 buildings: 42 in Class B and 10 in Class A, none in Class C, and six still open on the day we looked. Every one of them names a particular apartment, usually in the wording inspectors use, such as a defective intercom 'from 1st story public hall vestibule' to a named apartment. And every one of the 22 buildings stands on Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway or Nostrand Avenue, or on a side street within a block of Kings Highway. Not one intercom violation sits on the inner streets, the grid of numbered East streets between Kings Highway and Avenue U, away from the avenues. That is not because the inner streets have better buzzers. It is because they have houses, and a one- or two-family house is not the kind of building HPD's intercom rule is written for.

Split by the three footprints, the record leans toward the long buildings. The eight buildings put up between 1954 and 1963 hold 624 homes and account for 20 of the 52 violations, about 3.2 for every 100 homes. The 27 prewar buildings hold 1,437 homes and account for 25, about 1.7 per 100. The twelve buildings put up since 2005 hold 425 homes and account for three, all at one building. The remaining four violations were written at three small buildings on Nostrand Avenue. These are small numbers spread over four decades, and one 1960 building with eight violations moves the first figure a long way, so we read them as a hint rather than a rule. The hint fits what the geometry predicts: the longest circuits produce the most complaints that reach an inspector.

311 tells the same story at a smaller scale. From January 2020 to October 22, 2026 Madison's tax blocks produced 37 buzzer complaints from 17 addresses: 3, 6, 14, 4, 2, 5 and 3 a year. Sixteen of the 17 addresses are on the three avenues or a few doors off Kings Highway; the exception, an address on East 21st Street, filed two complaints in October 2022. Compare that with the doors themselves: on the same blocks HPD has written 557 Class C orders for doors that fail to close and lock by themselves, at 49 buildings, and 110 orders about locks at entrance or vestibule doors. In Madison the city spends far more ink on the door than on the buzzer, and a buzzer repair that leaves the door unable to close and latch has not finished the job.

The wording of those violations works like a small map. Madison's inspectors trace each defect from the lobby to a named apartment, 'from 1st story public hall vestibule', 'from building entrance' or 'from building front', and end it at a panel 'in the foyer', 'in the private hallway' or, once, 'in the kitchen'. Read that way, each order names the two ends of a circuit: the lobby panel and the station in the apartment the inspector visited. In a long building the useful next question is where that apartment sits along the run, near the lobby or at the far end, because that decides where a technician starts.

How the method changes with the footprint

Long slabs. We start at the far end. With the strike disconnected from its keeper, we read the voltage the far apartment's release delivers at the door and compare it with the near apartment's. A big difference points to the run or the supply; a small one points to the far apartment's button or station. Where the far lines share one supply with the near ones, we check the supply under the load of a strike, not just at rest. If a section of cable has to be replaced, we size it for its length, and we label each line at the supply so the next person does not have to walk the cellar to find it.

Deep prewar buildings. We start by finding which line the dead apartment belongs to. In a building with front and rear lines, two apartments that look like neighbors on the hallway can be on different risers, and a rear line can travel through a light court wall or a cellar that has been partitioned since it was wired. We trace the line, look for the newest work along its route, and check old splices first, because in ninety-year-old buildings that is where the trouble usually is.

Narrow newer buildings. We start with power and the network. A panel that calls phones or apps depends on a power supply, a network link and an account that someone controls. We confirm each of those before touching the door hardware, and we write down who holds the administrator account when we leave.

Telling the footprint from the sidewalk

You do not need the tax map to know which kind of building you live in. Stand across the street. In our experience, if the building runs a long way along the avenue with a flat, even front, has no visible court, and the lobby door is one of the few breaks in a long brick wall, you are almost certainly in one of the 1954 to 1963 slabs. If the front is shorter, perhaps with a recessed entrance court or a U-shaped plan, decorative brick or stone around the door, and the building goes back a long way into the block, it is one of the prewar buildings. If it is narrow, tall for the street, with large windows, a glass lobby door and often balconies, it went up after 2004. Inside, slab buildings of this period usually have one long corridor on each floor, while prewar ones tend to have shorter halls that turn corners.

It also helps to know where your apartment sits in the building. When you call, tell us whether you are near the lobby or at the far end, at the front or the rear, and on which floor. In a slab building, the far end of the top floor is where a weak release shows first. In a prewar building, the rear lines are the ones most often rerouted. That one sentence on the phone often saves the first half hour of a visit.

Houses. We start at the transformer. A house bell that has gone faint or dead is most often a transformer at the end of its life or a loose connection near it; a release that works from one floor and not the other is most often on the wire to the floor that has lost it.

Rules that apply at a Madison entrance

  • Intercoms in multiple dwellings. The New York State Attorney General's tenants' rights guide says multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments a two-way voice intercom that opens the door. Older buildings must have them if a majority of apartments ask (Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a). Every Madison apartment house built before 1964 predates that line; the buildings put up since 2005 do not.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as a Class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice (Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2041.1). On Madison's blocks HPD has written 557 such Class C orders. A repair that leaves the lobby door unable to close and latch is not finished.
  • Intercom defects. HPD writes intercom violations under Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2005, and in Madison every one of them names an apartment. A repair that fixes the lobby but not the named apartment's station or line will not clear the order.
  • Line-voltage work. The New York City Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician for line-voltage electrical work. Moving a supply to a new outlet or circuit, or adding one, needs that electrician; we do the low-voltage side and coordinate with them.
  • Egress and reach. Doors released by magnets and motion sensors must still let people out under building-code egress conditions, and the 2010 ADA Standards put the highest forward reach at 48 inches, which matters for lobby panels and release buttons in public-facing entrances.

For a store or an office, card or fob entry and an intrusion alarm are separate services we can quote alongside the door.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Madison: Questions Answered

These are questions we expect from Madison boards, owners, managing agents and residents, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Length, Not Height: The Long Buildings

Our co-op on Nostrand Avenue is a single six-story building more than 250 feet long. The apartments at the far end hear visitors clearly, but when they press the door button the strike barely clicks. Why would distance weaken the release and not the voice?

Because the two signals ask very different things of the wire. A voice is a small signal; even a long, thin pair carries it well enough to be understood. An electric strike is a small motor: to pull its keeper back it needs a certain current, and the voltage that arrives at the strike drops with every foot of wire and every tired joint between the supply and the door. In a short building nobody notices. In a building where the far apartments are reached by a run along the cellar, the release can arrive at the strike just strong enough to make it hum and too weak to free the latch.

Whether distance can matter at all depends on how your system is wired. In older bell-and-buzzer systems, the current that drives the strike actually travels out to the apartment's button and back, so the far end feels every foot. In systems with a relay or door-release module near the supply, the apartment sends only a small signal and the strike is driven locally; in those, a weak release at the far end points instead to the far apartment's button, its station or a bad joint on its line. We tell the two apart on the first visit by measuring what the strike receives from a near apartment and from a far one, under load.

The supply for our intercom sits in the cellar right under the lobby, at one end of a very long building. Would moving it, or adding a second one, help the apartments at the other end?

Sometimes, and it is worth measuring before anyone moves anything. If the far apartments' release is weak because of the length of their run, a supply placed nearer the middle of the building, or a second supply that powers the strike locally from a relay, can cure it. If the weakness comes from a corroded splice or an undersized section of cable, moving the supply only moves the problem, and the cheaper fix is to repair or replace that section.

When moving or adding a supply is the right answer, the new location needs a permanent power source, which in New York City means a licensed electrician for the line-voltage side. We do the low-voltage side, write down where each line is fed from, and label both supplies so the next person does not have to guess. Because this is not a fixed line on our price list, we quote it on paper first, at our hourly rate plus parts, and tell you before the work starts.

A pipe leak in one line of apartments shorted the intercom, and the whole building went silent, not just that line. Can the lines be separated so one fault stays in one line?

Often, yes. In many older systems every line hangs off the same supply and the same pair of common wires, so a short anywhere, water in a station, a crushed cable behind a radiator, pulls the whole system down. The fix for the leak itself is to dry or replace whatever got wet; the fix for the next leak is to give each line its own protection at the supply, so a short on one line opens that line's protection and leaves the rest working.

How easy that is depends on the system. Where the lines already come back to a terminal board in the cellar, adding protection per line is a modest job. Where they were spliced together along the way decades ago, they have to be separated first, which means finding the splices. In a long building that is worth doing once, properly, and writing down. We price the repair of the wet line from the list and quote the separation of the lines as a separate item, so the board can decide whether to do both now.

Deep Prewar Buildings on Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway

In our 1930s building on Kings Highway, the apartments facing the back lose the intercom far more often than the ones facing the street. What is different about the rear lines?

Usually their route. In a deep prewar building the rear apartments are often fed by lines that climb at the back of the building, and to get there the cable crosses the cellar under the rear rooms, passes storage areas, boiler rooms and laundry rooms, and sometimes runs up or along a court wall. Every one of those places has been renovated more often than the front of the building, and every renovation is a chance to nick a cable, bury a splice or move a line onto a temporary path that became permanent.

We trace the rear lines from the supply to the first apartment that has failed, look for the newest work along the route, and test the joints we find. Where a section of the route is exposed to weather or damp, for instance along an outside wall, we replace it with cable suited to that place rather than patching it again. The result is written down: which apartments each rear line serves, where it runs, and what we changed, so the next failure takes minutes to locate rather than an afternoon.

Our street door and our lobby door are far apart, with a long entrance hall and a few steps between them. Visitors push the lobby door at the wrong moment and miss the release. Can the door show them when it is open?

Yes, and on a long approach it helps a great deal. The simplest aid is the sound of the strike itself, which many older strikes make loudly and newer, quieter ones barely make at all. If the strike is quiet, or the door is far from where the visitor waits, a small light or a buzzer at the lobby door that comes on only while the release is active tells them when to push. Some panels also give a tone at the panel when the door has been released.

Before adding anything, we check two things. First, the release time: if the strike lets go for only a second or two, a visitor coming up the steps will never make it, and the release time can usually be lengthened at the relay or panel without leaving the door unlocked for long. Second, the door itself: if the closer drags the door hard against the strike, even a fully released strike can feel locked. A visual signal is quoted on paper before it is fitted, at our hourly rate plus parts.

When One Apartment Affects Everyone

Nobody in our building can talk to the lobby any more, but every apartment can still buzz the door open. Could one handset left off its hook be blocking the whole line?

It could, in many older audio systems. In those systems the voice travels on a shared pair, and a handset that is off its hook, or a station whose hook switch has stuck, keeps its microphone and speaker connected to that pair. Depending on the system, that can load the line so heavily that the lobby is drowned out for everyone, or it can lock the system into a call that never ends. The release often still works, because it travels on different wires.

Before calling, ask residents to check that their handsets are hung up properly, and look for one that has been knocked off its cradle or taped down. If that does not clear it, we isolate lines at the supply one at a time until the voice comes back, which narrows the search to one line, and then work along that line to the station at fault. A stuck hook switch is replaced with the station; the rest of the building is tested before we go.

One apartment keeps buzzing in anyone who rings, including strangers. Can the door release be switched off for that one apartment without affecting the others?

On most systems, yes. On a wired system each apartment's release reaches the strike through its own button and wire, so the release for that apartment can be disconnected at the station or at the supply while its voice and its bell stay connected. The resident can still talk to visitors and come down to let them in. On a networked or app-based panel, the release permission is usually a setting on that apartment's account.

This is a building decision, not ours. In a rental building it belongs to the owner, and in a co-op to the board, and it is wise to put it in writing and tell the resident before it is done. We make the change, note it on the dated report, and explain how to reverse it. It does not affect the self-closing and self-locking door, which stays as it was.

After a stranger was let into the building, the board wants to know which apartment pressed the release. Can our system tell us?

An older wired system cannot. It does not record anything: a button closes a circuit, the strike lets go, and nothing remembers which button it was. Networked panels in newer buildings often keep a log of calls and releases with the apartment and the time, and if your building has one, its administrator can usually read that log. We can show the board where it is and what it records.

If the board wants that kind of record in an older building, the choices are a camera at the door, which records who came in but not who let them in, or a newer panel that logs releases. Neither is a repair; both are quoted separately. What a repair can do is make sure every apartment's release works as it should, so residents are not tempted to prop the door or hand out codes.

Narrow New Buildings and the Records on the Avenues

Our condo is a narrow eight-story building that went up after 2005 on lower Ocean Avenue. Is it simpler to repair than the long six-story co-ops near it?

In terms of distance, usually yes: the wiring runs a short way from the lobby to a single vertical path, so the far-end problems of the long buildings rarely appear. But narrow new buildings have their own weak points. Many have panels that call residents' phones or apps, and those depend on a power supply, a network link and an account that someone must keep current. Many have glass lobby doors with magnetic locks and motion sensors instead of a strike in a wood or steel frame.

So the first questions are different: who holds the administrator account, whether the panel's internet or cellular service is paid up, and what the door hardware is. A repair can be quick when those answers are known and slow when nobody knows them. For condominium boards, we leave a written record of the settings we changed and who holds the account, which matters more in a building with a few dozen owners and no live-in super than any single part.

Every HPD intercom violation we can find in Madison is on Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway or Nostrand Avenue. Does a busy avenue wear out a door system faster?

The records do not show that, and we would not claim it. The simpler explanation is that the avenues are where the apartment buildings are. HPD's intercom rule is written for multiple dwellings, and Madison's inner streets are almost entirely one- and two-family houses, so there is nothing there for an inspector to cite. All 52 violations since 1985 sit at 22 apartment buildings, and those buildings line the three avenues and the side streets beside Kings Highway.

What a busy avenue does change is the entrance itself. A lobby door on Kings Highway or Nostrand Avenue is used by more visitors and deliveries than a house door on East 25th Street, its panel faces the street, and its closer and strike cycle many times a day. That is wear on the door hardware, and it is why we check the closer, the latch and the strike on every apartment-building call, whatever the original complaint was.

Equipment

Types of Door Buzzer Hardware We Repair in Madison

Electric strikes

The part in the door frame that lets the latch go. Fail-secure strikes stay locked when power is lost, and the door can still be opened from inside by the lever; fail-safe strikes unlock. Which kind a lobby door needs depends on the door, the frame and what should happen in a power cut.

Transformers and supplies

The 16 to 24 volt transformer of a house bell, or the supply and amplifier of an apartment intercom. In a long building, the supply's capacity and its distance from the far lines decide whether the far strikes get enough to release.

Riser pairs and cellar runs

The cable from the supply to each line of apartments. In Madison's long buildings the cellar run is often longer than the climb, and its splices, not the panel, are where many faults hide.

Apartment stations

Handsets and hands-free stations with talk, listen and release buttons. Replacement stations have to match the building's system, and their hook switches are a common cause of whole-building voice failures.

Lobby panels

Button plates with name windows in prewar vestibules, larger plates and directory panels in the 1950s buildings, and networked panels with cameras and keypads in newer ones.

Relays and door-release modules

Small units that let a weak signal from an apartment switch the strike's power locally. Fitted near the door, they take distance out of the release circuit.

Magnetic locks and sensors

Common on glass doors in newer buildings. A magnet needs constant power and a sensor or button to let people out, and it has to meet egress rules.

House bells and chimes

Buttons, chimes, transformers and the upstairs release button of a two-family house, the circuits we repair most often on Madison's inner streets.

National brands and a building's buzzer

Ring sells video doorbells for a single household's door, and ADT and SimpliSafe sell alarm systems and monitoring. Those products are built around one home and one door. A Madison co-op's lobby panel calling 79 apartments over shared riser pairs, with a strike on a heavy lobby door and a supply in the cellar, is a different kind of system, and none of those brands sends a technician to trace its cellar runs. A video doorbell can be a sensible choice for a house on an inner street; it does not replace a building intercom that the law requires. We repair what the building has, and we will tell you plainly when a house would be better served by a simple doorbell than by a repair.

Houses that want more than a bell often add a keypad or app lock with a keyed override; that is quoted separately.

“Minor intercom repairs typically run $150 to $400”

This range comes from Swiftlane's 2026 guide to apartment intercom replacement costs, and a summary that repeats it without context makes it sound universal. For a loose wire behind a faceplate in a small building it may be fair.

In Madison the costly faults are rarely minor in that sense. A weak release at the far end of a 250-foot building is a question of distance and supply, and finding the cause takes measurement at both ends. Our Brooklyn list starts with a $250 service call that includes the diagnosis, and the most common long-building repairs land on the supply, strike and riser lines, $325 to $2,400.

The honest version: a small fault in a small building can be cheap; a fault that depends on the length of a long building usually is not, and nobody can price it before measuring it.

“The intercom rule only applies to buildings built after 1968”

ButterflyMX describes the New York City requirement as generally applying to buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, and adds that older buildings need closer review. A short summary can easily drop that second half.

Most of Madison's apartment houses went up between 1921 and 1963, before that date. Under Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a, as the Attorney General's tenants' guide explains, older multiple dwellings must still have a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments ask for one, and once one is installed HPD cites it when it fails: 52 times in Madison since 1985, almost all in pre-1968 buildings.

If your building has an intercom, it is expected to work, whatever year it was built.

“The national average for an intercom is about $2,500”

HomeAdvisor's figure is the kind of national average that cost sites such as HomeAdvisor, Angi and Fixr publish. It is an installation average across the country, not a repair price.

It says nothing about repairing an existing system in a six-story building with seventy or more apartments, and it is not a repair price. A Madison repair is priced from our Brooklyn list, line by line, after the $250 diagnosis; replacing a whole system in a large co-op is a separate installation quote and would not resemble a national average.

Use national averages to understand the range of products on the market, not to budget a repair on Nostrand Avenue.

“A lot of times it's user error”

Brick Underground's 2019 guide to New York intercom repair quoted a technician saying this, and it is sometimes true: a handset left off the hook, a button pressed too briefly, a door pushed before it was released.

In a long building some of what looks like user error is distance. A resident at the far end who says the door never opens may be pressing correctly; the strike simply does not receive enough current from that end. Telling the two apart takes the comparison between near and far apartments described on this page.

Check the simple things first, then measure. Do not let a board conclude that the far end is careless before anyone has read a meter there.

“Modern call boxes require no building wiring”

ButterflyMX says of its apartment call box that it requires no building wiring, and it is easy to stretch that into a claim that old wiring no longer matters.

A panel that calls phones can do without the riser pairs to the apartments, but it still needs power at the door, a network or cellular link, and a connection to the strike. In Madison's long buildings, the strike and its supply are often where the trouble was in the first place, and a new panel does not fix a weak strike circuit or a door that does not close.

Replacing the panel can be the right decision for a board. It is not a repair, and it does not make the door hardware or its wiring irrelevant.

“Repairs run $195 to $250 an hour plus parts”

Brick Underground reported service-call estimates of $195 an hour and $250 an hour plus parts from New York companies, and quoted alone that can read as the whole of the bill.

Our Brooklyn list works differently. “Cloud intercom software costs $5 to $15 per unit per month”

Swiftlane's installation guide gives that range for software subscriptions, and it is a common yardstick for comparing old and new systems.

For a Madison co-op of 79 apartments the arithmetic is roughly $395 to $1,185 a month at that range, our multiplication rather than a quoted price. A subscription can be worth it for features like remote access and logs; it should be a deliberate choice, not something a board discovers when the panel stops calling.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk to the Far End Before You Open the Panel

This is our standard method for a long Madison building, written as a method, not as an account of one job. The technician does not open the lobby panel first. He walks to the far end of the building, to the line that complains most, and has a colleague or the super press the release from an apartment near the lobby while he watches the strike, then from the far apartment. If the strike snaps open for the first and murmurs for the second, the panel is almost certainly fine, and the next twenty minutes are spent between the supply and the far line, not behind a faceplate.

The same walk is repeated at the end of the job, in the same order, and the readings go on the note we leave. A repair that makes the far apartment work on the day but leaves its release just barely strong enough is not one we count as finished; we would rather tell the board that the far lines need their own supply than come back in a month for the same apartment.

FAQ

Madison Door Buzzer Repair FAQ

If a line has to be re-run, what kind of cable do you use for a long stretch of cellar?
+

Cable sized for the distance and the current the strike needs, not simply what was there before. A thin pair that was adequate for a short run can be the reason a far line is weak, so on a long stretch we use a heavier conductor for the release circuit or a cable with spare pairs that can be doubled up. Where the run passes damp areas or follows an outside wall, the cable is rated for that location. The size and route are written on our note so the next repair does not undo it.

If our board replaces the whole system in a few years, can the riser wires you repair now be used by the new one?
+

Often they can. Many replacement systems are designed to work over existing two-wire or multi-pair risers, and a riser that has been repaired, tested and labelled is worth more to a future installer than one nobody understands. Some systems need more pairs than an old riser has, or run over a network instead. If replacement is on the board's horizon, tell us when you book, and we will note on the report how many pairs each line has and what condition they are in, which makes a later replacement quote more accurate.

Our mail carrier opens the lobby door with a key switch. Is that switch part of the intercom?
+

Its wiring usually is, and its lock is not. The Postal Service uses its own universal key, the arrow key, for apartment panels and building access, and questions about the postal lock or key belong with your local post office. Behind the lock, the switch is simply another button wired to the door release, often on the same circuit as the apartments. If the buzzer works from upstairs but the carrier's switch does nothing, we can test and repair the switch's wiring to the strike; a problem with the postal lock itself goes to the post office.

The apartment next to our lobby hears the strike buzz every time someone is let in. Can the release be made quieter?
+

Usually, yes. The loud buzz comes from strikes that run on alternating current, which is what most older building transformers supply. Strikes powered by direct current are normally chosen when a quiet release is wanted, and many strikes can be set up either way. Changing over means a direct-current supply for the strike or a strike suited to the existing supply, and it should be paired with some other sign that the door is released, because visitors and residents have relied on the buzz. We price it from the strike line on our list.

Will you write down the voltages you measured at each end of the building, so we can compare them the next time?
+

Yes. On a long building our note records what the strike received from a near apartment and from a far one, before and after the repair, and the supply's voltage at rest and under load. A year later, the same two readings tell a board whether the far end is drifting toward trouble again, long before a resident files a complaint. Keep the note with the building's records; any technician, ours or someone else's, can repeat the same two readings and compare them.

Is the emergency phone in our elevator cab part of the intercom you repair?
+

No. The phone or call button inside an elevator car belongs to the elevator's own equipment and is maintained by the elevator contractor, not by the building's door intercom company. We do not work on it. The two systems can share a cellar room or a telephone line in some buildings, so if both stop working at once, it is worth telling the elevator company and us at the same time.

Our cellar has old insulated pipes along the route the intercom cable takes. What happens if the repair runs into them?
+

We route low-voltage cable without disturbing pipe insulation, using existing openings and supports wherever we can. Old pipe insulation in a prewar cellar can contain asbestos, and city rules place the responsibility for an asbestos survey by a city-certified investigator on the building owner before work that could disturb it. If the only workable route would mean cutting into or moving that material, we stop, explain the alternatives, and let the owner decide how to proceed; we do not handle it ourselves.
