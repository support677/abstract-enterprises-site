The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Chinatown Properties Need TV Installation

Brooklyn Chinatown grew up fast and on top of an older neighborhood, and both facts show up on the wall behind a TV. Wikipedia's history of the Brooklyn Chinatowns records that before 1984 there were only about thirty small shops on Eighth Avenue, and that by 1988 most of the original storefronts had been empty. Then the openings came one after another: Choi Yung Grocery on Fort Hamilton Parkway in 1983, the Canton House seafood restaurant between 55th and 56th Streets in 1985, Winley Supermarket at 5523 Eighth Avenue in 1986, and the Brooklyn Chinese-American Association's office on the avenue in 1988. The buildings those businesses moved into were not new. They were the brick houses and mixed-use buildings left from the decades when this part of Sunset Park was home to Norwegian immigrants, the "Little Norway" the same article says had largely emptied out by the 1950s, and the families who came with the shops moved into the apartments upstairs and the two-family houses on the side streets. Most of the TVs we hang here go onto walls that were plastered or laid in brick long before anybody imagined a flat screen.

The neighborhood has since spread well past its first blocks. Wikipedia describes Eighth Avenue from roughly 42nd to 68th Street as the spine, the stretch from 50th to 65th as the original core, and newer arrivals from Fuzhou settling onto Seventh and Ninth Avenues and north into the 40s. The Census figures it cites put Sunset Park's Asian population at 31,400 in 2020, and it counts the growth of Brooklyn's foreign-born Chinese population from 86,000 in 2000 to 128,000 in 2013. Those people live in the same houses and walk-ups that were here before them, and in a small apartment where a room does double duty, the one good wall for a TV is often also the wall with the radiator, the window or the doorway to the next room. A TV installer who does not ask how the room is used, and who watches from where, ends up putting the screen somewhere the family cannot actually see it.

The CUNY Macaulay project "Welcome to Chinatown NYC" describes the residential streets as lined with brick two-family homes and notes that the commercial buildings on Eighth Avenue mostly have people living above them. Those are the two buildings we work in most, and they behave differently. In a brick two-family the side walls are usually solid masonry shared with the house next door, and the partitions inside are plaster over wood lath, so the same living room can need two different anchors on two different walls. In an apartment above a shop, the wall behind the TV may carry things the family does not see: a duct from the bakery oven below, a refrigeration line from the market, a run of conduit for the store's sign, or the cables of every tenant who has lived there. We scan for all of it, and each wall's price is on our list.

Language and television go together here in a way they do not on most Brooklyn blocks. Wikipedia notes that Cantonese speakers settled the area first and dominated for decades, that Fuzhounese now predominates, that Mandarin grew through the 2000s and 2010s, and that Hakka has appeared as well. In practice that means a family may want a Hong Kong drama channel for a grandparent, mainland news for a parent and English streaming apps for the children, all on the same TV. Some of the boxes sold to bring overseas channels into the home are licensed services; others are sold with a "lifetime" subscription and a security record that researchers have taken apart in public. Setting up the TV properly includes helping you tell those apart, which we explain in its own section below, because it is the one part of a Brooklyn Chinatown TV job that a generic installer is unlikely to raise.

Same-Day Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown

Need Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown? A Bracket Loose in the Mortar, a Screen Tipping on Old Plaster, or a Dead Box Behind the Set

The urgent TV calls from buildings like these come in three kinds. The first is a bracket on a brick wall that was fixed into the mortar between the bricks rather than the bricks themselves. Mortar in a century-old wall is softer than the brick around it, and an anchor set into a joint can hold for months and then start to turn. The screen begins to sit a little lower on one side, and the gap at the top of the bracket plate grows. The second is a TV on an interior plaster wall held by plastic plugs or light toggles that were chosen for modern drywall. The third is not a mounting problem at all: the TV is fine, but the box that brings in the channels has stopped working, the remote no longer controls the soundbar, or the picture freezes at the same hour every night.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back against the wall. If two people can lift it safely, take the TV off the bracket, unplug it and lay it face up on something soft, then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the take-down added, so a 55-inch TV taken down and re-hung on brick comes to $329.99 before any new bracket. If the problem is the box or the settings rather than the wall, a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks, and we tell you from photos and a description which of the two it is likely to be before you book.

One thing we will say plainly if we find it: when a bracket has failed because the wall itself is failing, such as a crack running through the plaster from the bracket to the ceiling, a loose brick, or a damp patch spreading under the paint, the fix belongs to the owner of the building and a mason or plasterer, not to a TV installer. We will tell you what we see and photograph it for your landlord.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Brooklyn Chinatown's Architecture

Six kinds of building cover nearly every TV call we get between Seventh and Ninth Avenues. They differ in three ways that matter far more than the brand of the TV: what the walls are made of, what else runs inside those walls, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

Brick two-families between the avenues

Two stacked homes, brick outside and along the party walls, plaster on lath inside, often with an owner on one floor and relatives or tenants on the other.

Homes above the avenue's storefronts

Apartments over markets, bakeries, restaurants and offices on Eighth Avenue, where the wall behind the TV may carry ducts and lines that serve the business below.

Walk-ups off Seventh and Ninth

Three- and four-story buildings with narrow stairs, a mix of masonry and plaster walls, and more households per staircase as the neighborhood has grown outward.

Rowhouses in the 1890s historic rows

Brownstone and brick rowhouses to the west, in the Sunset Park historic districts the city designated in 2019, with plaster over brick on the party walls and decorative mantels.

Steel-frame infill on the side streets

Mid-rise mixed-use buildings like the eight-story building at 816 58th Street, with steel frames, drywall partitions and walls of glass that leave one solid wall per room.

Concrete towers on Eighth Avenue

Large reinforced-concrete buildings like the 28-story tower rising at 6208 Eighth Avenue, with management rules, concrete walls and drywall partitions side by side.

Brick two-families between the avenues: one living room, two kinds of wall

The brick two-family is the most common home on Brooklyn Chinatown's side streets, and in most of them the living room runs across the front of the house. The longest clear wall, the one everyone wants the TV on, is usually one of two walls. Either it is the party wall shared with the house next door, which is solid brick under a coat of plaster, or it is the interior wall with the stair or a bedroom behind it, which is plaster on wood lath over framing. A family can stand in the middle of the room and not know which is which, because both are painted the same and both feel hard to the knuckle. They behave nothing alike once a drill goes in. The brick takes a hammer drill and masonry anchors and holds a heavy screen superbly. The lath wall takes a slow drill, fixings that find the framing, and care around old cracks. On our list the first is the $65 brick line and the second the $45 plaster line, so a 55-inch TV is $244.99 on the party wall and $224.99 on the interior one, plus the bracket.

The other thing that shapes a two-family job is who lives on the other side of each wall. The floor belongs to the household below, which in many two-families is the owner. If you rent the upper floor, a short message to the owner downstairs before the visit, saying which wall, how many holes and where the wires will go, prevents most of the arguments we have seen. If you own the house, putting one well-anchored bracket in the rental unit and asking tenants to use it saves you from a new set of holes every time someone moves.

Homes above the avenue's storefronts: the business downstairs is part of the wall

Most of the commercial buildings on Eighth Avenue have apartments above the shops, and those apartments are where we see the most surprises behind a TV wall. A building that has held a bakery, a seafood market and a restaurant over forty years may have exhaust ducts, refrigeration lines, gas pipes and conduit for signs and shutters running up through the floors, some of them in use and some abandoned. They rarely run where an outsider would guess. Before we drill above a shop, we ask what the business downstairs is, look at where its equipment sits relative to your TV wall, scan the wall for metal and voids, and drill a single pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it. If anything warm, hollow or metallic is close to where the bracket wants to go, we move the bracket rather than gamble.

These apartments also have quirks of access. The street door to the upstairs homes is usually beside the shop rather than through it, the stairs are steep and narrow, and the hallway often holds the meters, mailboxes and bicycles of every household. A 65-inch TV in its carton has to make the turns, so we ask about the stair on the phone and bring a second pair of hands when the box is long. And the people working below are your neighbors for the length of the visit. Hammer drilling a brick wall travels through a building, and a quick word with the shop before we start, especially if it is a clinic or an office where people sit still, is a courtesy that costs nothing.

Walk-ups off Seventh and Ninth: more households on every stair

Inside, a walk-up apartment of this age usually has masonry around the outside and along the walls shared with the next building, and framed plaster partitions between rooms. The living room's long side wall is often the masonry one, which is excellent for a large screen but means the cables cannot be fished inside it. That decides the cable plan: either the TV goes above a console so the wires drop out of sight behind the furniture, or a paintable cord cover runs from the screen to the outlet. On a framed partition the in-wall power and cable kit becomes possible, but in a rental it is the kind of change your landlord may want to agree to first, because it means two openings to patch when you leave.

Rowhouses in the 1890s historic rows: plaster on brick and a mantel in the way

West of the Chinatown core, the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated four Sunset Park historic districts in June 2019. CityLand's report puts the largest, Sunset Park South, at about 285 buildings on 54th through 59th Streets between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, mostly from the 1890s, with Central Sunset Park on 47th and 48th Streets built from 1897 to 1906. Many families from the Chinatown blocks live in or near these rows, and their houses have the walls of their era: party walls of brick under plaster, interior partitions of plaster on lath, and parlors with a mantel on the party wall.

The Brownstoner forum's long thread on mounting a TV above a Brooklyn fireplace sums up the problem. One contributor pointed out that brownstone party walls are plaster over brick with no studs at all, and that "It's much easier to mount things on a non-party wall, with plaster over wood lath." Another warned that a TV over a mantel sits high and can strain the neck. Both points hold. Above-fireplace mounting is a $130 add-on on our list because it means drilling a chimney breast and placing the screen higher than eye level, and we look for the old flue inside the breast before choosing where the anchors go. Landmark designation covers the outside of these houses; a TV on an inside wall is not the kind of exterior change the Commission reviews, but an outdoor TV or anything fixed to the front of a designated house is a different question, and one to ask before buying the hardware.

Steel-frame infill on the side streets: glass, drywall and one solid wall

The building New York YIMBY photographed at 816 58th Street shows where much of the newer construction in the neighborhood is heading: eight stories on an interior lot, a steel superstructure, commercial space and a community facility on the lower floors, and a handful of apartments with floor-to-ceiling windows and terraces. YIMBY reported in October 2024 that work had stalled at about 90 percent and that the project had sold for $9.8 million. Whenever buildings of this type are finished, their apartments tend to have drywall partitions on light-gauge steel studs rather than wood, and a living room where one wall is glass and another is shared with the corridor.

Steel studs are the wall that catches out handymen. They look and sound exactly like drywall on wood until a lag bolt spins in the thin metal and refuses to bite. The right fixing is a toggle made for steel, spread across more than one stud, or a backer that gives a full-motion arm something solid to pull against. We price that wall from your photos before the visit, because the fix depends on the bracket and the screen. The other thing these buildings bring is paperwork: a management office or condo board that wants a certificate of insurance, set working hours and a clear answer on whether any wall will be opened. We send the certificate and the answer before the visit.

Concrete towers on Eighth Avenue: no fishing a cable through poured walls

The largest building in the neighborhood's pipeline is the reinforced-concrete tower YIMBY reported at 6208 Eighth Avenue: 28 stories, 497 apartments, a retail podium of about 100,000 square feet and amenities that include a screening room and a coworking lounge, with completion expected in late 2027. Apartments in buildings like that usually combine poured concrete on the outside, around the shafts and sometimes between units, with drywall partitions inside. A concrete wall holds a TV as firmly as any wall can, but it takes a hammer drill, carbide bits and anchors set to a precise depth.

What concrete does not allow is a cable inside it. There is no cavity to fish through, and cutting a channel in a structural wall of a managed building is not something any office will approve for a TV. So in a concrete room the cable plan is settled before the bracket: either the screen goes on the concrete wall directly above a console, with the wires dropping behind the furniture, or it goes on a drywall partition where an in-wall power and cable kit is possible, or a paintable cord cover runs on the surface. We show you each option with tape on the wall and the price of each before deciding.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Questions a Technician Asks Before Drilling Above an Eighth Avenue Shop

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like Brooklyn Chinatown's. They are our practice, not a story about any one address, and no customer is named.

What is downstairs, and where is it? Before the scanner comes out, the technician asks what business is under the apartment and looks out of the window to see where its sign, its shutter and its exhaust sit. A bakery or restaurant usually means a duct somewhere in the back of the building; a market can mean refrigeration lines; almost every shop means conduit for a sign. Knowing roughly where those run tells us which part of your wall to treat with suspicion.

Which wall is brick? In a building on the avenue, the walls shared with the buildings on either side are almost always masonry. The technician knocks along the wall at screen height and runs a scanner in deep mode. A scanner can be fooled by dense plaster and by metal lath, so the knock and the next step count for more than the beep.

One pilot hole, hidden. The technician drills a single small hole where the bracket plate will cover it and reads what comes out. White powder followed by wood is plaster on lath. Red grit is brick. Grey grit is block or concrete. A sudden drop into nothing means a hollow behind the plaster. If what comes out changes the wall line from the one we quoted, you hear the new total before any more holes are drilled.

Who watches, from where? Painter's tape marks the screen, and everyone who watches sits where they usually sit, a grandparent's chair included, before anything is drilled.

What brings the channels in? Before the TV goes up, the technician asks whether the family uses a licensed Chinese TV box, apps on the TV, an English streaming service or all three, where the router is and whether it is in the shop downstairs. The answer decides where the box sits, which HDMI port it takes and whether a wired connection is worth discussing.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Plaster, Steel or Concrete: How a Brooklyn Chinatown Wall Holds a TV and What It Adds

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, with the bracket sold separately. Few walls in a century-old two-family are drywall over wood studs, so the wall lines come first in every quote, each printed so you can add up the total yourself.

Brick or stone (+$65). The party walls of the side-street two-families, the shared walls of buildings on the avenue and the outer walls of most walk-ups are solid brick under plaster. Brick is one of the best walls there is for a heavy screen, as long as the anchor goes into the brick and not the mortar joint. Old mortar can be soft enough to scratch away with a key, and a fixing set into it can loosen as the TV is pulled and pushed over the years. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and we keep each hole a safe distance from the edges of the brick. Brick that has been painted, plastered or covered with drywall is still brick, and still the brick line.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). Interior partitions in the older houses are thin wooden strips nailed across the studs and covered with coats of plaster that can add up to an inch thick. Plaster is strong when pushed and weak when pulled. A screw into lath alone, or a plastic plug in plaster alone, can feel solid on the day and fail months later. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the framing behind, with the pilot hole drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward, or uses a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Studs in these houses were not always set on modern spacing, so we find each one rather than measuring from a corner.

Drywall on steel studs (priced from photos). Newer mid-rise buildings and renovated apartments often have light-gauge steel studs. Lag bolts strip out of them, so we use toggles made for steel, spread across more than one stud, or a backer that spreads the load. The fix depends on the screen and the bracket, so we price it from your photos and the model number before you book.

Poured concrete or concrete block (priced from photos). Concrete towers and some basements and rear extensions have concrete or block walls. Hollow block has thin faces with voids behind them, and an anchor placed into a void holds very little, so we place fixings into the solid webs or use anchors made for hollow block. Poured concrete is the hardest drilling of all, and a reinforcing bar inside it is the one thing that stops a hole; we shift the bracket rather than drill through steel. Because the bit, the anchor and the time vary so much, we price these walls from photos before the visit.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common request after "put it on the wall" is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord through a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. Gizmodo's widely shared summary of the National Electrical Code points to section 400.8, which says flexible cords shall not be used where they are "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," and names the proper arrangement as "a new electric socket installed directly behind the TV." A cord inside a wall can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected. In a building where the apartment sits over a restaurant or a market, nobody wants that risk inside a wall.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into the existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits plaster on lath and drywall, and it does not suit brick, block or concrete. On those walls the honest options are a paintable cord cover at $59, which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match, or placing the TV directly above a console so the cables drop behind the furniture and are never seen.

In a rental, an in-wall kit means two neat openings that will need patching when you leave, and a cord cover means a handful of small screw holes. Either is easy to restore, but either is the kind of thing a landlord may want to agree to first. We say which one your wall can take before you ask.

Channels From Home

Licensed Chinese TV, "Lifetime" Boxes and Your Home Network: Setting Up the Channels Safely

For many Brooklyn Chinatown families the TV is how a grandparent keeps up with Hong Kong dramas, how parents follow news from the mainland or Taiwan, and how everyone watches the Spring Festival programs together. Getting those channels onto the screen is part of the job, and it is where we see the biggest difference between a setup that works for years and one that causes trouble. This section is written by installers, not lawyers, and nothing in it is legal advice. It explains what the public research shows and what we recommend, so you can choose before the technician arrives.

There are licensed ways to watch. Several companies sell Chinese-language TV in the United States as a licensed service. iTalkBB, for example, publishes a line-up of more than 30 channels, including CCTV4, China Movie Channel and Hunan International, with a premium tier that adds TVB Jade, and it says its programming is "legally licensed." TVB, the Hong Kong broadcaster, runs its own overseas streaming service, TVB Anywhere. Some services come as a dedicated box that plugs into the TV's HDMI port; others are apps that run on a smart TV or a streaming stick. Some boxes are a security problem as well as a copyright one. In November 2025 TorrentFreak reported on a study by researchers at Korea University, presented at the USENIX Security conference, that took apart EVPAD boxes, a line of Android TV boxes made in China and sold with a cheap "lifetime" subscription. Why the network matters to us. A TV box sits on the same Wi-Fi as the family's phones, laptops, cameras and doorbells. If you already have such a box, we will not lecture you, but we will tell you what the research says, and if you ask, we will set up a licensed service instead and leave the old box disconnected for you to decide about. If a family insists on keeping an unknown box, the safer arrangement is a separate guest network that keeps it away from the computers and cameras, which your router may already offer.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Shared Houses and Deposits: Mounting a TV When the Wall Belongs to Someone Else

Many Brooklyn Chinatown households rent, and many others own a two-family and rent the second floor to relatives or tenants. So the question of whose wall it is comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and what we have learned doing this work, so you can ask the right question before you book.

Start with the lease, in whatever language it is written. The clause that matters is usually headed alterations, fixtures or improvements. If it says nothing about hanging things on walls, a bracket is generally treated like other wall hangings. If it requires written consent for alterations, ask the landlord in writing, by message or letter, and keep the reply. A landlord who knows the installer is licensed and insured, that the bracket goes into the brick and not the mortar, and that the power will not be a cord pushed inside the wall, is usually easy to persuade.

Understand where wear and tear ends. Brick Underground put the question to a property manager and a tenant attorney in October 2020. Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group said that "a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame or shelving system would fall within wear and tear," while "a huge hole in the wall or floor is damage." The attorney, Justin C. Brasch, said that "careless drilling of the wall or drilling to hold heavy hangings" can be "viewed as much more than normal wear and tear," and also that "You are not responsible for any damage from a previous tenant." A TV bracket leaves four to six holes, each larger than a nail hole, and an in-wall kit leaves two openings. All of it can be filled and painted, and the sensible routine is to patch before you leave, photograph the repaired wall and ask for a move-out walk-through.

Consider not drilling at all. If the lease is strict, or the family expects to move within the year, a TV on its own stand on a good console is a legitimate choice. If you own the two-family. Compliance notes for Brooklyn Chinatown businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. The ADA and ANSI protruding-object rules, as EVstudio summarizes them, limit objects whose leading edges sit "between 27 and 80 inches above the floor" to projecting no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a clinic corridor or a narrow shop aisle can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount the screen above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power and cable kit with its own rated cable, or a cord cover on the surface. Any new circuit or outlet is work for a licensed electrician, not a TV installer.
  • Insurance paperwork first. Commercial landlords and managed buildings often want a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent before work starts. We send it ahead of the visit.
  • Exits, sprinklers and alarms stay clear. We do not mount screens where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and we keep cables off the floor along any route people use to leave.
  • Rated hardware, with margin. Every bracket we hang is rated for more than the screen's weight, and on masonry the anchors are rated for the load. A business screen gets bumped, leaned on and cleaned far more than one at home.

Boxes, apps and TVs from overseas

An electronics shop sold us a box with a "lifetime" subscription to Hong Kong and mainland channels. Is there any risk in keeping it connected to our home Wi-Fi?

There can be. Researchers at Korea University who studied one widely sold line of these boxes, EVPAD, reported at the USENIX Security conference that the boxes' authentication could be bypassed, that their updates were not checked for integrity, that they allowed apps from unknown sources to be installed system-wide, and that a single crafted network packet could shut a box's service down. A device like that shares your Wi-Fi with your phones, computers and cameras. Our advice is to use a licensed service, and if you keep the box, to put it on a separate guest network if your router offers one. We will tell you which kind of box you have if you send a photo of its label, and we can set up a licensed alternative during the visit.

Our family brought a TV back from Guangzhou. Will a bracket bought here fit it, and is there anything else to check before it goes on the wall?

The bracket is usually the easy part. Most flat TVs use the VESA mounting pattern, an international standard for the spacing of the four bolt holes on the back, so a bracket bought in Brooklyn will normally fit a TV sold in China if it is rated for the weight and the pattern. What we check first is the rating label on the back of the set: it states the voltage and frequency the TV accepts, and a TV that does not list 120 volts must not be plugged into an American outlet, with or without a plug adapter. We also check the model number for weight, because a TV from overseas may not have a box or manual, and we bring an assortment of mounting bolts in case the originals were lost on the journey.

We are choosing between a licensed Chinese TV box and an app that runs on the smart TV itself. What does that change about the installation?

It changes where things go. An app needs nothing but power and a good network connection at the TV, so the wall can be as bare as you like. A box needs power of its own, a free HDMI port, a place to sit and, if its remote uses infrared, a clear view from the sofa. On a wall-mounted TV that normally means a console under the screen with an HDMI cable running down to it inside a cord cover or an in-wall kit. A box hidden behind the screen often cannot see its remote. Decide before the visit, and we plan the cable route around it.

Homes above kitchens and walls with a past

The apartment over our family's bakery has the oven exhaust running up inside the back wall. Can the TV go on that wall?

Usually not directly over the duct, and sometimes not on that wall at all. An exhaust duct carries heat and, from a bakery or a kitchen, grease, and the wall around it can be warm to the touch while the ovens are running. A TV's electronics do not like steady heat, and an anchor must never go into or near the duct itself. We find the duct's path with a scanner and by asking where the ovens and the roof fan are, then look for a stretch of the same wall well clear of it or move the TV to another wall. If the wall feels warm in the afternoon, that answers the question on its own: the TV goes elsewhere.

You can see the outline of an old bricked-up window in the plaster of our TV wall. Should the bracket avoid it?

Yes, if possible, or at least avoid its edges. A window opening that was filled in years ago usually has a lintel across the top, the original frame sides, and infill that may be brick, block or even framing behind plaster, so one part of the patch can be solid and the next hollow. Anchors set across the joint between old wall and infill tend to hold unevenly. We scan the patch and drill the pilot hole away from its edges, and if the best viewing position falls right over it, we either use a wider bracket that reaches solid wall on both sides or move the screen a few inches. The price follows whatever the wall turns out to be: brick at $65, plaster at $45.

The family who rented the apartment before us left four large anchor holes where their TV was. Will we be charged for them when we leave?

You should not be, but protect yourself now. Brick Underground quoted the attorney Justin C. Brasch saying plainly, "You are not responsible for any damage from a previous tenant." Photograph them with today's date, send the photos to your landlord by message or email, and keep the reply. If you want your new TV in the same spot, we can often place the bracket so its plate covers the old holes, or choose a position where the new fixings land in sound brick or framing beside them. Either way, the old holes are not yours.

Shops, markets and clinics
