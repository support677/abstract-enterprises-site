Door Stations Placed by the City's Height Rule · Fixings Kept Off Carved Stone and Cast Iron · Wire Hidden Under the Stoop · Written Brooklyn Quote

Boerum Hill is a compact grid of mostly three-story rowhouses built between 1840 and 1870, running from Schermerhorn Street down to Warren or Wyckoff Street and from Smith or Court Street east to Fourth Avenue. Most of the houses are brick with brownstone trim, and most have a stoop climbing to a parlor-floor door with a second entrance tucked beneath it. That shape decides almost everything about an intercom here: where the call panel can sit, how the wire gets from the stoop into the hall without a run of conduit down the front, and who answers when one house holds an owner's duplex and a rental or two upstairs. Much of the neighborhood is also inside the Boerum Hill Historic District, designated in 1973 and more than doubled in 2018, so anything fixed to the front of a house falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Around the rowhouses sit neo-Grec flats buildings, Victorian storefront rows on Atlantic Avenue, the restaurants of Smith Street and new rental towers near Fourth Avenue, each with different doors and different rules. This page explains how we plan an audio or video intercom for each of them, what the city's rule on panel heights says, what Landmarks staff look for, and how we quote: in writing, for your building, before anyone drills.

The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Intercom Installation

For an intercom, that history means three things. First, the typical building is small: Wikipedia sums up most of the housing as three-story row houses built between 1840 and 1870, which means most jobs here are rowhouse systems with one to four apartment stations, not big lobby consoles. Second, the typical front door is up a stoop. A stoop house usually has two street entrances, the parlor-floor door at the top of the steps and the garden-level door underneath, and very often a vestibule with an outer and an inner door at the top. Each of those decides where a door station can go, which lock it releases and how the wire gets inside. Third, a large share of these houses sit inside a historic district, where the Landmarks Preservation Commission oversees exterior changes. A call panel is an exterior change if it goes on the face of the building, and the Commission has written criteria for exactly that, which we explain further down the page.

The people inside the houses shape the job as much as the masonry does. Many Boerum Hill rowhouses were divided long ago into an owner's duplex on the garden and parlor floors with one or two rental floors above, and others are now fully rented, with the owner living elsewhere. That mix raises questions a one-family doorbell never has to answer. Which floors ring when a visitor presses one button? Can the tenant upstairs open the street door without also opening the owner's inner door? Who keeps the old handset working on the third floor when the owner switches to a video monitor downstairs? And when the house has eight or more apartments, the question is no longer only practical. The city's rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY §42-01, says buildings of that size covered by the rule must have self-closing and self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system, sets the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer or a speaking and listening device, and fixes the heights at which the buttons and the speaker go. A neo-Grec flats building on Pacific Street or a converted house with eight units is squarely in that territory.

Same-Day Repair in Boerum Hill

Need Repair in Boerum Hill? A Door That Won't Release, a Handset That Only Hums, or a Stoop Panel Full of Rainwater

The urgent intercom calls from older Brooklyn rowhouses come in a few familiar forms, and the age of Boerum Hill's housing makes all of them common. The first is a door that buzzes but does not open. The visitor presses, the handset upstairs rings, someone presses the door button and hears the strike click or hum, and still the door stays shut. Most of the time the electronics are fine and the problem is mechanical: a door that has dropped on its hinges so the latch now presses hard against the strike, or a strike that was set for a door that has since been planed or rehung. A strike under pressure cannot release, however much power reaches it. The fix is often adjusting the door or the strike keeper, and that is a short job if we know about it ahead of time.

The second is a handset that hums, crackles or rings faintly. In an old two-wire or multi-wire audio system, that usually points to a failing transformer in the cellar, a corroded connection at the stoop where rain has been getting into the panel for years, or a splice somewhere in the wire run that has worked loose as the house moved. The third is a stoop panel that has simply rusted or filled with water. Panels mounted flat on a front face with no hood, or set into a door surround where water runs down the stone, take decades of rain and freeze-thaw. If yours is in that state, call (347) 934-8335 and describe what happens when you press the button. If we can make the existing system work safely until a planned replacement, we say so and do that; if the panel or the wire is past saving, we tell you before charging for a repair that will not last. If the problem is the door itself, a frame that has gone soft or a hinge pulling out of rotted wood, we say that plainly too, because no intercom can fix a door that will not close.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Boerum Hill's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every intercom call we expect from Boerum Hill. They differ in how many doors face the street, how far the wire has to travel and who besides the occupant has a say. The equipment matters less than those three things, which is why we start with the building.

The one-family stoop rowhouse

Three stories over a garden level, brick or brownstone fronted, a stoop to the parlor door and a second door beneath it. One household, two street doors and a long hall.

The owner-plus-rental brownstone

The same house divided: an owner's duplex below and one or two rental floors above, often with old handsets still working on the upper floors.

The neo-Grec flats building

Later nineteenth-century apartment buildings of the kind the 2018 extension recognized, with a single street door, a vestibule and stacked apartments.

The Atlantic Avenue storefront row

Rowhouses with Victorian wood storefronts between Hoyt and Nevins, where a shop door and a narrow residential door share a front.

The condo or co-op building

Small condominiums such as 457 Atlantic Avenue from 2007, and taller ones such as The Boerum at 265 State Street, with boards and house rules.

The new rental building

2025-era buildings such as 540 Atlantic Avenue, with virtual doorman systems and in-unit intercoms already installed and run by management.

The one-family stoop rowhouse: two doors, one hall and a long way down to the cellar

In a classic Boerum Hill rowhouse, the formal entrance is the parlor-floor door at the top of the stoop. Behind it there is usually a vestibule, a small chamber between an outer door and an inner door, and then the hall with the stair. The garden-level door under the stoop opens into what was once the service floor and is now often a kitchen or a family room. For an intercom, the first decision is which door visitors will actually use. Many families today use the garden door daily and keep the stoop door for guests, or the other way round. A door station only helps if it sits where people ring. If the household uses both doors, the question becomes whether both get stations or whether one station and a clear sign does the job, and the answer depends on the layout more than on the equipment.

The wire route is the second decision. In most of these houses, the honest path for an intercom cable runs from the door station into the vestibule or the frame, down into the cellar through the floor, along the cellar ceiling and then up through the house inside an existing chase: beside a radiator riser, inside a closet, behind the stair stringer or alongside old plumbing. Fishing cable through plaster on wood lath is possible but slow, and every hole in a nineteenth-century cornice or ceiling medallion is one too many. So we look for the paths the house already offers. Where the stair runs up one side of the hall, the space under the treads and behind the stringer often gives a clear vertical route. Where there is a dumbwaiter shaft or an old speaking tube, as some houses of this age had, that opening can sometimes carry cable without any new holes at all. We look before we drill, every time.

The owner-plus-rental brownstone: who rings, who answers and who opens which door

The divided rowhouse is where intercom planning stops being about hardware and starts being about people. A typical arrangement puts the owner on the garden and parlor floors and one tenant on each floor above, all coming in through the same stoop door and vestibule. The outer door releases for everybody. Whether the inner vestibule door also releases for everybody, or only opens with a key for the owner's duplex while tenants have their own apartment doors upstairs, depends on how the house was divided. We map it before we choose anything: which doors lead where, who has keys to which, and where a stranger let in by one household could walk.

The call plan follows from the map. Most owner-plus-rental systems we plan have one button or directory entry per household, with the owner's call ringing on stations on both of the owner's floors so nobody has to run up or down a flight to answer. Each tenant's call rings only in their apartment. If the owner wants to be able to see who is at the door when a tenant's guest rings, that is a choice to make openly with the tenants, not a feature to switch on quietly. When the owner wants to upgrade to video but a long-standing tenant is happy with an audio handset, many current systems let both types of station share the same backbone, so nobody is forced to change. That flexibility is worth more in a rental house than any single feature on a spec sheet.

The neo-Grec flats building: a lobby panel, stacked apartments and a city rule that applies

The Commission's 2018 designation summary called out neo-Grec apartment buildings among the extension's architecture, and the type is familiar across this part of Brooklyn: a narrow street front, a vestibule, a single stair and apartments stacked on each floor, sometimes two per floor. Once a building like that has eight or more apartments, the city rule on entrance doors applies to it, and the rule is specific. The entrance doors must be self-closing and self-locking. The intercom must, at minimum, be a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. No push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. Those numbers decide the panel before the brand does.

Wiring a flats building usually means one riser, because the apartments stack. The old system's riser often runs up a corner of the stair hall or inside the wall beside the stair, and in many buildings it can be reused as a pathway even if the old wire inside it is replaced. Each apartment then gets a short branch from the riser. On the panel side, flats buildings benefit most from a directory that someone can update without a technician, so that when a tenant moves, the name changes the same week rather than the following year. Paper strips behind a scratched window tell visitors nothing; an electronic directory or a numbered button system with a clean, legible card does. We also look at the vestibule door hardware, because a lobby intercom that releases a door that does not reliably close and lock is not doing the job the rule asks of it.

The Atlantic Avenue storefront row: a shop door and a home door, side by side

The 2018 extension brought rowhouses with Victorian wood storefronts on Atlantic Avenue between Hoyt and Nevins Streets into the historic district. In many buildings of this kind, the street front holds two doors: the wide shop entrance and, beside it, a narrow door leading to a stair and the apartments above. The intercom job is to give the apartments a proper door station without tying it to the shop's own systems. We keep them separate. The residential station rings only the apartments, the shop has its own arrangements, and deliveries addressed to the shop are directed there, not upstairs. On a historic storefront, the residential door's frame is often original wood, and the cleanest location for the station is usually within or immediately beside that door opening, which is also the location Landmarks staff criteria favor.

The other feature of these buildings is the long, narrow stair. Wire from the street door to the upper apartments typically follows the stair, and in older buildings the stair wall is often the only continuous vertical route. Shop cellars below may belong to a different lease and a different tenant, so a route that would be easy in a one-family house, down into the cellar and up again, may not be available. We ask who controls each space before we plan a route through it.

The condo or co-op building: a board, house rules and a system that belongs to everyone

Boerum Hill's condominiums range from small boutique buildings such as The Atlantic at 457 Atlantic Avenue, 21 units built in 2007, to taller elevator buildings such as The Boerum at 265 State Street. In both, the entry system is a common element. An individual owner can usually change what happens inside their own unit, a newer monitor, a second station, but the door station, the controller and the wiring in common areas belong to the building and are controlled by the board or the managing agent. Work there usually needs approval, a certificate of insurance and scheduling within the building's contractor hours.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Boerum Hill Stoop Before Anything Is Unpacked

Stand on the landing, not the sidewalk. The first measurement is taken from where a visitor actually stands. On a stoop, that is the top step or landing, and the city's heights for buttons and speaker are measured from there. A panel that looks right from the street can end up far too high or far too low once you are standing at the door.

Find the plain surfaces. Next the technician looks at what is around the door: a carved brownstone surround, a wood frame, plain brick, a cast-iron railing, the recess of the vestibule. The fixings go into plain masonry, a mortar joint or the wood, never into carved stone or historic ironwork. In many houses the vestibule recess offers a plain jamb that is out of the rain and out of the view from the street.

Follow the old wire. The old panel, if there is one, is opened and its wire followed into the house. Where it enters tells you the route the original installer found, and that route has often been the least destructive one available for decades.

Go to the cellar. Under the stoop, the technician finds where the wire comes through, what else shares that space, and whether there is a dry place for the power supply. Boilers, water meters and gas lines are noted and given room.

Tape, then ask. A small square of painter's tape marks the proposed panel position at the door. The owner looks at it from the landing and from across the street before any decision. Moving tape is free; moving a hole in brownstone is not possible.

The Front of the House

Brownstone, Brick, Wood Frames and Iron: Where a Door Station Can Go on a Landmarked Boerum Hill House

Because so much of Boerum Hill is inside a historic district, the front of the house is where most intercom planning begins. The Landmarks Preservation Commission oversees exterior changes to buildings in the district, and its Permit Guidebook has a chapter on health and safety equipment, which includes intercoms and doorbells. Its Guidelines for Restoring Architectural Features also include a section on installing intercom boxes as part of its doorway standards. We are installers, not lawyers or preservation consultants, and the Commission makes the decisions. What follows is how we read the published criteria, so you can make an informed choice before anyone contacts Landmarks.

Location: in or beside the door opening. The Permit Guidebook allows staff to issue permits for an intercom or doorbell installed within or adjacent to the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the building. It limits the count to one intercom and two light fixtures per opening. In practice, the strongest location on a stoop house is often inside the vestibule recess, on the plain jamb beside the outer door, where the panel is reachable from the landing, sheltered from rain and barely visible from the sidewalk. A panel in the middle of the front façade, away from the door, is the kind of placement the criteria steer away from.

Attachment: plain masonry, mortar joints, wood or metalwork. The guidebook asks that equipment be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and not to cast iron or decorative features. That matters a great deal on Boerum Hill fronts. HDC describes the original district's buildings as mostly brick with brownstone trim, in Greek Revival, Italianate and Queen Anne styles. Brownstone is a sandstone, and carved brownstone around a doorway is exactly the kind of decorative feature a drill should not touch. Fixings go into the mortar between bricks or into the wood door frame instead, and a mortar joint can be repointed if the panel is ever removed. A hole in carved stone cannot be undone.

Conduit: concealed, or short and painted. The guidebook asks for conduit to be concealed, and where some must be visible, kept to about ten inches and painted to match. That rules out the familiar sight of a grey plastic conduit running from a panel down the front of a building to the sidewalk. Instead, the cable usually passes straight back from the panel through the jamb or frame into the vestibule, then down inside the house, or under the stoop into the cellar. We plan the wire and the panel location together, because a good location with no hidden route for the wire is not a good location.

Replacing like for like. The guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair differently from new installation. If you are repairing an existing intercom in its existing position, the rules are lighter than if you are adding a new device to the front. If you are moving a panel, adding a camera or changing the front in a visible way, assume Landmarks is part of the process and allow time for it. The Commission's own hearing agendas include enforcement cases about intercoms installed without permits elsewhere in the city, which is a reminder that the rules are applied, not decorative. Where the building is a landmark or in a historic district and a Department of Buildings permit is also needed for the wider project, Landmarks approval comes first.

When the station can go inside. The simplest answer of all, for many houses, is to put the door station inside the vestibule and leave the outer door as it is. That works when the outer door stays unlocked in the day or when visitors can reach the inner door. It does not work when both doors are locked, because the visitor cannot reach the panel. We talk through how your house actually works before suggesting it.

Height rules on a stoop: what four to five feet means when the landing is above the street

The city's rule says the speaking and listening device must be installed not less than four feet and not more than five feet from the floor, and no push button may be more than six feet from the floor. On a flat-fronted building the floor is the sidewalk. On a stoop house the person ringing is standing on the landing, so we measure from the landing. That sounds obvious, but older panels in this part of Brooklyn are often set by eye and can be well above or below the window. When a building is subject to the rule, we set the panel within it; when it is not, we still use those heights as a practical guide, because a speaker at mouth level for a standing adult works better than one at belt height or overhead.

Video door stations add a third consideration: the camera's view. A lens set within the four-to-five-foot band sees faces well, but a recessed vestibule can put a visitor in shadow while the street behind them is bright. We test the picture at the time of day when the light is worst, usually when the sun is behind the visitor, before fixing anything, and choose a station with a wide dynamic range where a doorway faces into strong light.

Owners, Tenants and Boards

Rental Floors, Boards and Shared Doors: Planning an Intercom When Several Households Use One Stoop

Many Boerum Hill rowhouses hold more than one household, and the intercom touches all of them. Nothing here is legal advice. It is what we have learned from the public rules and from planning these systems, so owners and tenants can ask the right questions early.

Count the apartments first. The city's rule on entrance doors applies its intercom requirement to covered buildings with eight or more apartments. A single rowhouse with an owner's duplex and two rentals is well below that number, so the intercom there is a matter of choice and of what the lease says, not of the rule. A flats building or a fully converted house with eight or more apartments is in a different position: the building must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and at least a buzzer or speaking system. Vendor summaries of HPD enforcement describe a non-working intercom in a covered building as a hazardous violation that tenants can report through 311. If you own such a building and the intercom has failed, treat it as urgent.

Tell tenants before the change, not after. In a divided house, replacing the intercom means replacing equipment in other people's homes. Give tenants notice of the visit, tell them what will change in their apartment and how long the door will be on a temporary arrangement, and agree a time when someone can be home. We can work around tenants' schedules, and we leave each apartment station tested with the person who lives there.

Be careful with apps and data. Many modern intercoms can call phones, keep logs and store photos of visitors. In New York City, HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law sets rules for smart access systems in Class A multiple dwellings: a written policy, collection of only limited data, consent, and limits on how long authentication data is kept. It also says owners may not require tenants to use smart access to enter their own apartments. Whether a given feature in a given house falls under the law is a question for the owner's lawyer. What we can do is set the system up with the minimum features the house actually needs, leave phone calling off unless a household asks for it, and keep ordinary handsets or monitors for anyone who prefers them.

Keys still matter. An intercom releases a door; it does not replace the keys to it. The Housing Maintenance Code requires locks and keys on entrance doors in multiple dwellings, and an electric strike on a vestibule door should still work with a key when the power is off. We never install a door release that would leave a household unable to get in during a power cut.

Boards and managing agents. In a condo or co-op, the entry system belongs to the building. Before quoting, we ask for the managing agent's contact, any alteration rules, contractor hours and the insurance names they need. Most boards want a written scope, a timeline and a plan for the front door during the changeover, and we provide all three.

Compliance notes for Boerum Hill businesses and mixed-use buildings

  • Keep operable parts within reach. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, operable parts in a forward approach must generally sit no higher than 48 inches and no lower than 15 inches above the floor, with a clear space in front. In public-facing businesses we keep buttons on door stations within that range, which also sits inside the city's six-foot limit for push buttons.
  • Egress comes first. A door that releases on an intercom must still open from the inside for anyone leaving. If a business uses a magnetic lock instead of a strike, the city Building Code's rules for sensor-release and electromagnetically locked egress doors apply, including release on loss of power and a manual release near the door. We use strikes wherever the door allows, and follow the code where it does not.
  • Landmarked storefronts follow the front rules. On the Atlantic Avenue storefront rows inside the 2018 extension, the Commission's criteria for intercom location, attachment and conduit apply to businesses exactly as they do to homes.
  • Keep shop and residential systems apart. In mixed-use buildings, the residential door station rings only residential units, and shop deliveries never release the stair door to the apartments.
  • Low-voltage wire stays separate. Intercom cable is kept apart from power wiring, runs in protected conduit where it passes through kitchens or cellars with equipment, and is labeled at both ends.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Boerum Hill: Questions Answered

These are the situations Boerum Hill owners, tenants, boards and businesses describe when they call: a stoop that changes the measurements, a door surround nobody wants drilled, wire that has to vanish, households sharing one front door, and Atlantic Avenue noise. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Placing the station on a stoop house

The top of our Wyckoff Street stoop is seven steps above the sidewalk. Is the panel height measured from the sidewalk or from the landing?

We measure from the landing, because that is where the visitor stands and speaks. The city's rule says the speaking and listening device goes between four and five feet from the floor and no push button more than six feet from the floor, and on a stoop house the floor in front of the door is the landing. Measured from the sidewalk, the panel would end up at the level of the visitor's knees. If your building has fewer than eight apartments the rule may not strictly apply to it, but we use the same heights anyway, since they put the speaker at mouth level and the buttons within easy reach. If a household member uses a wheelchair and comes in through the garden door instead, that door may need its own lower station.

Can the door station be fixed to the brownstone surround around our parlor door, or should it go on the wood frame?

Not on carved stone. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook asks that equipment be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and not to decorative features, and a carved brownstone surround is decorative. Brownstone is also a soft sandstone that can spall around a fixing. The better choices are the plain jamb inside the vestibule recess, the wood door frame where it is wide enough, or the mortar joints of plain brickwork beside the opening. Each of those can be repaired cleanly if the panel is ever removed. We mark the position with tape so you can see it from the landing and from the street before deciding.

Our stoop still has its original cast-iron railing and newel post. Could the panel go on the railing instead of the house?

We advise against it. The Commission's criteria steer attachments away from cast iron and decorative features, and an original newel post or railing is both. Mounting on it also means running cable along or through the ironwork to reach the house, which is hard to hide and exposed to weather. The criteria do allow an intercom on a fence or wall in front of a building, but on most Boerum Hill stoops the areaway fence is low, often historic and on the wrong side of the visitor. In nearly every case, a station in or beside the door opening is the better location for the visitor and the better choice under the Landmarks criteria.

Getting the wire into a nineteenth-century house

How does the cable get from the stoop into our hall without a strip of conduit running down the front?

Usually straight back through the door frame or jamb into the vestibule, then down. The guidebook asks for conduit to be concealed, and where some has to show, kept to about ten inches and painted to match, so a run from panel to sidewalk is the wrong answer. On a stoop house the vestibule floor sits above the space under the stoop, and a small opening there often drops the cable into the cellar or the under-stoop vault. From the cellar it travels along the ceiling and up the house through an existing chase. Where an old panel is being replaced, its wire path is often reusable as a pathway even if the old wire inside is not.

Our house has no closet or utility room. Where would the power supply and controller for the intercom actually live?

In most Boerum Hill rowhouses, the cellar. The power supply and any controller go on a board on a dry cellar wall near an outlet, away from the boiler and above any level water has ever reached. That keeps them out of the living space and close to the wire's entry point from the stoop. If the cellar is damp or belongs to another tenant, a high shelf in a hall closet or the back of a kitchen cabinet on the garden floor can work, with a dedicated outlet nearby. We label the power supply clearly, so anyone working in the cellar later knows not to unplug it.

Households that share one front door

We live on the parlor and garden floors and rent the top floor. Can our ring sound on both of our floors while the tenant only hears theirs?

Yes, and that is how we normally set up an owner-plus-rental house. Each household is its own call group. When a visitor presses the owner's button, stations on both owner floors ring at once, so nobody has to run up or down the stair to answer. When someone presses the tenant's button, only the tenant's station rings. Either household can release the street door. If you want the owner's floors to ring when nobody answers the tenant, for a delivery for example, that can be arranged, but we suggest agreeing it with the tenant first. We test each group with the people who will use it before we leave.

Our brownstone has a vestibule with an outer door and an inner door. Which one should the intercom release?

It depends on how the house is divided and who has keys. In a one-family house where the outer door stays locked, releasing the outer door alone lets a visitor into the vestibule, where you meet them at the inner door, and that is a sensible arrangement. In a divided house where tenants walk through the hall to reach their stair, the inner door usually has to release too, or tenants' visitors would be stuck in the vestibule. Releasing both doors at once is possible, but it means anyone let in by any household can reach the hall. We map who goes where before choosing, and both doors keep their keys whatever we install.

Our Dean Street house has three apartments. Does the city's intercom requirement apply to a building that size?

The intercom requirement in the city's rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY §42-01, applies to covered buildings with eight or more apartments, so a three-apartment house is below that line. A vendor summary we read says smaller buildings are not covered by the intercom requirement, though door rules still apply; check the rule text or ask a lawyer for your building's exact position. In practice, most owners of three-family houses want an intercom anyway, because without one every visitor and courier has to phone ahead or knock. Your lease may also promise one. If it does, it should work.

Flats buildings and the people in them

Our neo-Grec flats building on Pacific Street has a lobby panel with names on faded paper strips. What should the replacement panel do that the old one doesn't?

It should meet the city's heights, be readable and be easy to update. In a covered building, no push button may sit more than six feet from the floor and the speaker must be between four and five feet. Beyond that, the most useful upgrade is the directory. A visitor should be able to find the right apartment in seconds, and when a tenant moves, the entry should change within days, not years. An electronic directory updated by the owner or agent does that; so does a clean numbered button panel with a printed, protected card. Many owners also choose video, so residents see who they are letting into a shared hall.

A tenant on our top floor is hard of hearing. What kind of apartment station lets her know someone is at the door?

A video monitor with a large screen and a visual alert is the usual answer. Many current apartment stations flash an indicator or light the screen when a call arrives, and some can be set to a louder ring or connected to a separate flashing signal. With video, she can see the visitor and use the screen rather than relying on the audio alone, and many monitors also have adjustable call volume. We set the station at a height and location she can see from where she usually sits, set the alerts with her, and check it works from the street while she is in the apartment.

Shops, restaurants and Atlantic Avenue

Our Victorian storefront building on Atlantic Avenue has a shop door and a narrow residential door beside it. How do we stop shop deliveries from buzzing the apartments?

Keep the two systems separate and make the difference obvious from the sidewalk. The residential door gets a station that rings only the apartments upstairs, with a directory that lists residents and no shop name. The shop handles its own deliveries through its own door. A small, permanent sign by the residential station saying deliveries for the shop go next door helps more than any technology. If the shop occupies an upper floor too and shares the stair, it can appear in the residential directory as its own entry with its own hours. Because these storefront rows sit in the 2018 extension, the station goes in or beside the door opening.

Our Smith Street restaurant takes supplier deliveries at the side door during prep. Can the kitchen answer without someone leaving the line?

Yes. The answering station goes where the kitchen can reach it, usually on a wall near the pass or the prep area, out of splash range and away from heat. Hands-free stations let a cook answer by pressing one large button, talk to the driver and release the door without picking anything up. In a loud kitchen, we choose a station with a loud ring and a visual alert, and test it with the extraction running. If the side door opens into a passage that also serves apartments upstairs, the release is set so that a supplier reaches the kitchen without being able to go upstairs.

Traffic on Atlantic Avenue drowns out callers at our door. Can visitors and residents actually hear each other?

Usually, with the right station and placement. Older intercoms are often half-duplex, so only one side can talk at a time and every truck cuts the conversation. Current door stations are commonly full-duplex with noise suppression, so both sides speak naturally. Placement matters as much: a station inside a vestibule recess is shielded from the street, while one flush on the façade facing traffic is not. On a busy front we test with a real conversation at the loudest time of day, and where the noise is severe, video helps, because the resident can see the visitor even when they cannot hear every word.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Boerum Hill

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment, so nothing below quotes it. These are claims that appear in generic online answers and in national cost and how-to guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Boerum Hill's houses and rules actually show.

"Mount the call panel anywhere on the front of the house"

Generic installation guides treat the façade as a blank wall: pick a convenient spot near the door, drill four holes and run a cable down to wherever the power is.

Inside the Boerum Hill Historic District, the Landmarks Preservation Commission oversees exterior changes, and its guidebook points intercoms to locations in or beside the door opening, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, not cast iron or decorative stone, with conduit concealed or kept short and painted. A panel in the middle of a brownstone front with grey conduit to the sidewalk is precisely what those criteria are written to prevent.

We plan the location, the fixings and the hidden wire route together, often inside the vestibule recess, and tell you up front whether your choice is likely to need a Landmarks permit.

"Go wireless and skip the wiring"

Many answers recommend battery or Wi-Fi door stations as the easy fix for older buildings, with no cable to run at all.

A Boerum Hill rowhouse puts brick, brownstone and plaster between the stoop and most of the rooms where people answer, and a door station still has to release a lock, which needs power at the door. Wireless parts can help in specific spots, but a door that must open on command needs a wired strike, and a station that must work every time is better on wire.

We find a wired route through the cellar and existing chases, and use wireless only where it genuinely solves a problem, not as a way to avoid planning.

"Every New York apartment building must have an intercom"

Some summaries say flatly that city law requires an intercom in every residential building.

The city's rule on entrance doors applies its intercom requirement to covered buildings with eight or more apartments. Most Boerum Hill rowhouses, with one to four households, fall below that line, while neo-Grec flats buildings and fully converted houses can reach it.

We count the apartments first, tell you which side of the line the building is on, and plan accordingly, while recommending an intercom on most divided houses anyway because it makes daily life easier.

"A video doorbell is an apartment intercom"

Generic advice often treats a single video doorbell as interchangeable with an intercom, for any building.

An owner-plus-rental brownstone needs separate call groups, a way for each household to release the street door, a decision about the inner vestibule door, and stations for tenants who may never install an app. A doorbell designed for one household does few of those things.

For a one-family house a doorbell may be enough, and we say so; for a shared stoop we plan a real multi-household system.

"Height doesn't matter much; put it where it's convenient"

Guides rarely mention mounting heights, or suggest a height that suits the installer's reach.

The city sets them: no push button more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet. On a stoop, the floor is the landing, and businesses open to the public should also keep operable parts within the ADA's reach range.

We measure from where the visitor stands and set the panel inside those limits, then check the video picture at that height in the worst light.

FAQ

Boerum Hill Intercom Installation FAQ

What should I photograph so you can tell what kind of intercom our house has now?
+

Photograph the street panel close up, one apartment handset or monitor with its cover off if it unclips easily, any brand name or model sticker, and the box in the cellar where the wires gather. The number of wires on the handset terminals usually tells us whether it is a two-wire buzzer, a multi-wire audio system or an early video set.

Does each apartment monitor need its own electrical outlet?
+

It depends on the system. Many two-wire and networked systems power the apartment stations from a central supply, so no outlet is needed in each apartment. Some larger video monitors need local power. We tell you which applies before quoting, and plan the station position near an outlet where one is needed.

What happens to the intercom and the door release during a power cut?
+

Without power the stations go quiet. On most rowhouse vestibule doors we fit a strike that stays locked when the power is off, so the house stays secure, and the key always opens it. Where a building needs the intercom to keep working through short outages, a battery backup on the power supply can be added.

Will a video door station on our stoop also see the neighbors' doors and the sidewalk?
+

It sees what its lens faces. Mounted in or beside your door opening and aimed at the landing, a station mainly sees the person ringing. We set the angle so it captures visitors' faces and avoids looking into the house next door, and we check the picture with you before fixing the station.

I live in Wyckoff Gardens or the Gowanus Houses. Can I hire you to fix my building's intercom?
+

No, and you should not have to pay anyone for it. Those buildings belong to NYCHA, which runs its own access control and intercom maintenance contracts and a modernization program for both complexes. Report intercom problems to NYCHA so they are logged and handled under that program.

Can our old brass bell push stay on the door frame as a decorative piece after the new station goes in?
+

Often, yes. If it is original and you like it, we can disconnect it and leave it in place, or in some cases wire it to ring a chime inside. Keep in mind that the Landmarks guidebook limits each door opening to one intercom, so the old push should not look like a second working station to visitors.

Who updates the names on a lobby directory when tenants move out?
+

With an electronic directory, the owner or managing agent can usually update names from a computer or app without a visit. With a button panel, the name card is replaced by hand. We show whoever manages the building how to do it at handover and leave written steps.

Will you schedule around the Atlantic Antic street festival?
+

Yes. The Atlantic Antic has been held on Atlantic Avenue each fall since 1974, and the street is given over to the festival that day. We do not book Atlantic Avenue installations on festival day and tell customers on nearby blocks to expect closures and crowds.

Can our managing agent or condo board get a certificate of insurance before the work?
+

Yes. Send us the exact names and address the agent or board wants on the certificate, and we send it before the visit, usually on the same working day. We also confirm contractor hours and any elevator or lobby rules in advance.

Can a door station be installed in the middle of winter?
+

Yes. The wiring inside the house is unaffected by the weather, and stations rated for outdoor use are made to work in cold. We seal the station against masonry with products suited to low temperatures, and on very wet or icy days we may finish the exterior sealing on a drier day.

Can apartments in our building call each other or reach the super through the intercom?
+

Many current systems allow station-to-station calls, and a super or owner can be set up as a separate contact on every apartment station. Whether to switch that on is a choice for the building; some households prefer their station to ring only for the street door.

Will the new apartment stations cover the marks left by the old handsets on our hallway walls?
+

Sometimes, not always. New monitors are often a different shape from old handsets, so they may leave a screw hole or a paint outline showing. We try to place the new station over the old position, fill small holes we leave, and tell you before the visit if some touch-up painting will be needed.
