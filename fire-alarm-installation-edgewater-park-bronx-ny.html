Edgewater Park is a private cooperative of roughly 675 homes on about 55 to 60 acres at the eastern tip of Throggs Neck, held in by the Throgs Neck Expressway on the south and west, Weir Creek on the north and Eastchester Bay on the east. It began as a tent camp and then a summer bungalow colony, and the cottages built in the 1920s were winterized for year-round living during the Great Depression. Around 3,000 people now live in single-family bungalows and two-story homes standing so close together on narrow, mostly unmapped lanes that the city's own planners wrote that the tightness gets in the way of ordinary resiliency measures. That history decides fire alarm work here far more than any brand name. There is no lobby panel, because there is no lobby. Almost every job is a household system of smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, planned for a house whose rooms may have been added one at a time over nearly a century, whose heating may burn oil or gas a few steps from a bedroom, and which may now sit a full story above the ground it was built on. The Neighborhood

Why Edgewater Park Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The homes of Edgewater Park were not designed as homes. In 1923, according to the community's history, the land that had once held George T. Adee's 1856 mansion was leased to Richard W. Shaw Sr., an Irish immigrant who invited church youth to camp there. Church groups, Boy Scouts and city workers pitched tents and then put up tarp-covered cottages. The city's 2015 Resilient Neighborhoods report describes summer cottages and bungalows going up in the 1920s and being winterized for year-round use during the Great Depression. The residents who rented the land later bought it, and the community became a cooperative in 1988. That path from tent to summer cottage to year-round home matters to anyone planning fire detection, because each stage left something behind: framing that was meant for warm months, wiring added when heat and appliances arrived, and rooms that were tacked on as families grew. We have no records of the wiring inside any particular house here, and the sources do not describe it. What the history tells us is to expect layers, and to look before we drill.

The second thing that makes Edgewater Park different is how close everything is. The city's planners wrote that the structures sit right next to one another on narrow streets, close enough that the tightness inhibits standard measures to protect homes from flooding. Most of the streets inside the community are unmapped and have no sidewalks. When houses stand this close, a fire is not only a household's problem; a neighbor is a few feet away. Early warning inside each house gives the people in it the most time to get out and to raise the alarm for the people next door. The same narrow lanes shape how help arrives. Edgewater Park keeps the only volunteer firehouse still operating in the Bronx, and The City Reporter described its fleet as fitting the narrow streets and alleyways where FDNY trucks cannot go. We do not know, and could not find a source explaining, how a monitored alarm signal from a house here reaches the volunteers. What every household can control is how quickly its own alarms sound and how quickly someone inside hears them.

Six Edgewater Park facts that shape a fire alarm plan before any alarm is chosen

  • It is houses, not buildings. The DCP report counts 675 single-family homes and about 3,000 residents on roughly 55 acres; Wikipedia gives 60. The stock is bungalows and two-story homes, so the right equipment is almost always residential smoke, heat and carbon monoxide alarms, linked together, not a commercial panel.
  • Many homes began as summer cottages. Built in the 1920s and winterized during the Great Depression, according to the city's report. Expect additions, old conversions and wiring of different ages in one house.
  • The houses stand very close together. City planners wrote that structures sit right next to one another on narrow streets, most of them unmapped and without sidewalks. Early warning inside one house protects the house beside it too.
  • The community has its own fire company. The volunteer company was established in 1922, is one of nine volunteer departments in New York City, and runs small apparatus built for narrow, winding streets. A state-funded upgrade of its firehouse and community center includes a new fire alarm system for that building.
  • Part of the community floods. DCP said about 111 homes are in the flood zone, and Sandy left some waterfront homes with up to five feet of water. Where alarms, power supplies and wiring go in those houses depends on that line.
  • Many homes have been or will be raised. A 2013 zoning text change let buildings add flood protection, and the co-op voted to measure height from the design flood elevation while keeping its 25-foot limit. A raised house has a new ground floor, new stairs and new wiring paths, and the alarm plan changes with them.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Edgewater Park's Architecture

Six kinds of home and building account for nearly every fire alarm call inside Edgewater Park. What separates them is not their style but their history: whether they were built for summer, how many times they have been added to, whether they have been raised, and how close they sit to the water. Those things decide where an alarm can go and how it can be linked long before a model number matters.

The winterized summer cottage

A one-story bungalow from the colony's early decades, converted for year-round living, often with low ceilings, a small footprint and an old electrical service upgraded in stages.

The cottage that grew

A bungalow with a bedroom, a bath, a second level or an enclosed porch added over the years, so that one house holds rooms of several ages and several ceiling heights.

The two-story home

A full two-story house, sometimes a rebuild on an old cottage lot, with bedrooms upstairs and the kitchen and heating equipment below, like the two-story home that burned in 2023.

The house lifted above the flood line

A home raised so its living space sits above the design flood elevation, with a new stair, a new entry and an open or enclosed space beneath.

The home on the bay edge

Waterfront houses facing Eastchester Bay, some of which took up to five feet of water in Sandy, where salt air and storm exposure shape the choice of hardware.

The community's shared buildings

The volunteer firehouse and community center, whose state-funded upgrade lists a new fire alarm system, the community deli, and the handful of other shared spaces.

The winterized summer cottage: small rooms, low ceilings and a service that grew in steps

A cottage built to be used in July was not built around a furnace, a kitchen full of appliances or bedrooms that stay shut all winter. When these houses were winterized, they gained heat, insulation and more electrical load, and in many cases that happened more than once. So the first thing we do in a cottage is find out how the electricity actually reaches each room, because hardwired smoke alarms need a reliable circuit and the circuit has to be one that nobody can switch off by accident. We trace any existing hardwired alarms back to their source, check whether they share a circuit with something that trips, and look at the condition of the cable where it is visible. Where the old wiring is sound and accessible, a hardwired, battery-backed, interconnected set of alarms is the strongest choice. Where it is not, or where opening ceilings would do more harm than good, sealed long-life battery alarms with wireless interconnection give the household linked warning without new cable through old framing. Low ceilings help in one way: alarms are easy to reach, test and replace. They hurt in another: in a small house, the kitchen, the living room and the hall to the bedrooms may all sit within a few steps of one another, which is where nuisance alarms come from. Placement in a cottage is a matter of feet, and we measure it rather than guess.

The cottage that grew: finding the seam between the old house and the new rooms

The most common surprise in an older Edgewater Park home is not a fault but a seam. A bedroom added in one decade may have been wired from the nearest outlet in the original cottage. A second level may have its own small panel or none at all. An enclosed porch may have a ceiling a foot lower than the room beside it and an outlet on an extension of a kitchen circuit. These seams matter for fire alarms in two ways. First, alarms that are supposed to sound together have to be linked across them, and a hardwired interconnect that runs happily through the original attic may have no path into an addition with a separate roof. Second, the added rooms are often where people sleep, because that is why they were built. So we map the house by when each part was added, find out where cable can and cannot cross from old to new, and decide room by room whether the link is wired or wireless. Many homes end up with a mixed system: hardwired alarms where the framing allows, linked by a compatible wireless bridge to alarms in the additions, all from the same manufacturer's family so they talk to each other. The household gets one system that behaves as one, even if the house underneath it is three houses joined together.

The two-story home: bedrooms up, fuel down

The house lifted above the flood line: a new ground floor and nothing electrical in the wet zone

The city's Edgewater Park report counted about 111 homes in the flood zone, and The City Reporter described Sandy leaving some waterfront homes with up to five feet of water. The zoning change adopted in October 2013 let homes add flood protection, and the co-op's vote to measure its 25-foot height limit from the design flood elevation made raising practical. A raised house is, for alarm purposes, a new house. The living space is a full level higher, a new exterior stair and entry have been added, and the space below may be open, latticed or enclosed for storage and parking. The rule we follow is simple: nothing that needs electricity to warn the household should depend on equipment in the space expected to take water. Power supplies, any wireless bridge, and any connection point for monitoring go up in the living space, in a dry interior location. Cable that has to pass from the living space to anything below runs inside conduit, and every opening through the new floor is sealed. If the space below holds a car, fuel or a workshop, we talk with the owner about a heat alarm or carbon monoxide alarm on the living-space side and about what can safely be mounted below, because some devices are rated for damp and unheated spaces and many are not. When the raising is still being designed, the best time to plan the alarms is now, while walls are open.

The home on the bay edge: salt air, storm exposure and enclosed spaces that stay damp

The community's shared buildings: a firehouse with its own alarm upgrade, a center that doubles as a shelter

Edgewater Park's shared buildings are the exception to everything above. The volunteer firehouse and community center is a public gathering place, a polling place, and, under the $2.5 million resiliency project New York State Homes and Community Renewal announced, a building meant to serve as a storm shelter. A fundraising and project page for the firehouse lists a new fire alarm system among the funded improvements, along with electrical and HVAC upgrades, hurricane-proof windows and two generators. We have no connection to that project and make no claims about the system chosen or its installer. We mention it because it shows the line clearly: a building where many people gather, or where people may shelter overnight, falls under the code's rules for fire alarm systems with designs reviewed by the FDNY, which is a different world from a cottage's linked alarms. The community deli is another shared space, and the DCP report says the co-op is exclusively residential except for a single commercial use. For any work of that kind, a registered design professional prepares the plans where the code requires them, and our role is installation, labeling and testing to the approved drawings.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Walk an Edgewater Park House

Bedrooms first, with the doors closed. The technician starts where people sleep, notes every bed, including the sofa bed and the porch daybed, and closes each door to see how much a hall alarm would be muffled. In a cottage the bedroom doors are often thin; in a newer two-story home they may be solid, and that changes the plan.

Then everything that burns. Boiler or burner, water heater, range, fireplace, the door to an attached garage, the grill on the deck. Each gets measured against the nearest bedroom entrance, because the fifteen-foot rule for carbon monoxide alarms is a measurement, not an estimate.

Then the seams. The technician looks for where the original cottage ends and each addition begins: a step in the floor, a change in ceiling height, a different window, a second roof line. Those seams are where cable may not pass and where wireless links may be needed.

Then the power. Any existing hardwired alarm is traced to its circuit. A switch on that circuit, a shared circuit with a heater, or a breaker that trips often all go on the sheet. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent that year, so we also note where phones, scooters, tools and e-bikes are charged.

The space below last. In a raised or partly raised house, the space underneath is checked for what is stored there and how high water has reached. Only after the walk do we talk about equipment and price.

Compliance notes for Edgewater Park homes, community buildings and nearby businesses

  • Carbon monoxide in every dwelling with fuel. The FDNY's brochure says every dwelling unit, including one- and two-family homes, needs carbon monoxide alarms if fuel-burning equipment is in or attached to the building, and that they must be within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room.
  • Rented homes. HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors in one- and two-family homes that are not occupied by their owner when they contain fuel-burning devices.
  • Homes offered for sale. New York requires functioning smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in every one- or two-family dwelling, co-op or condominium constructed or offered for sale.
  • Small houses and the building system threshold. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has direct exit outside. Single-family cottages here are household-alarm jobs, not building-system jobs.
  • Plans before installation for required systems. The FDNY Fire Code chapter on fire protection systems requires design and installation documents for fire alarm systems to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation.
  • Repair or redesign. Replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is treated as a repair; moving or replacing a control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY, as ASM Integrators' summary explains.
  • The co-op's own rules. DCP describes an active co-op board that administers the community's bylaws. Our sources do not say whether the board must approve alarm work inside a home, so we ask the household to check with the co-op office before we schedule.
Ninety Years of Additions

Reading a Winterized Summer Cottage Before a Single Alarm Goes Up

Ceiling heights tell us where the house was joined. When we walk from room to room and the ceiling drops or rises, or the floor steps, we are usually crossing from one building period into another. Alarm spacing follows the ceiling in each room, so a house with three ceiling heights may need alarms placed differently in each part. More importantly, a change of ceiling usually means a change of roof framing above it, and cable that travels easily over one part of the house may have no way into the next.

Outlets and switches tell us how power arrived. A bedroom whose only outlet sits low on the wall it shares with the old cottage was probably wired by extending a circuit through that wall. An enclosed porch with a single ceiling light on a pull chain may never have had a circuit of its own. Hardwired smoke alarms need a dependable circuit that is not controlled by a wall switch, and before we connect anything we confirm which circuit it is, what else is on it, and that it is not one the household turns off.

Old alarms tell us what the last person did. A hardwired alarm with a manufacturer's date from long ago, three battery alarms of different brands, a carbon monoxide alarm plugged into an outlet behind a couch: each is evidence of a separate decision at a separate time. We record every existing device, its date and its location, and we test whether any of them are linked. Very often a household believes its alarms are connected when they are not, because they were bought at different times. We tell you exactly what you have before recommending what to keep.

The space above and the space below decide the cable route. In a one-story cottage, the space above the ceiling may be a low crawl, a sealed gap or nothing at all, and the space below may be a crawlspace, a slab or, in a raised house, an open level. We check both, because they are the only places hidden cable can travel. Where neither allows a route, we say so and plan wireless interconnection rather than opening finished ceilings.

The result is a plan with no guesses in it. At the end of the walk, the household gets a drawing of the house by period, with every bedroom, every fuel-burning appliance, every existing alarm and every proposed alarm marked, and the method of linking shown for each. That drawing is the basis of the price, and it stays with the house afterward.

Selling, Buying and Renting

What New York's Detector Rules Ask of an Edgewater Park Home When It Changes Hands

Because Edgewater Park is a cooperative, the homes here are bought and sold as co-op shares tied to a house, and listings appear regularly; one listing site showed about twenty co-ops for sale at a single point. Every one of those sales, and every rental, brings New York's smoke and carbon monoxide rules into play. This is not legal advice, and a seller or landlord should rely on their attorney. It is a plain description of the rules our sources describe and how we help households meet them.

When a home is offered for sale. New York law, as summarized by HomeCoin, requires functioning carbon monoxide and smoke detectors in every one- or two-family dwelling, co-op or condominium constructed or offered for sale. The practical meaning for a seller is that the detectors have to work, not merely be present. An alarm past its replacement date, one with a dead battery, one taken down because it kept going off when someone cooked, or a carbon monoxide alarm missing from a house with an oil burner are all things a buyer's inspector may note. We test and date every alarm, replace anything expired or missing, and give the seller a written list of what is installed where.

When fuel is burned in the house. The FDNY's brochure says every dwelling unit, including one- and two-family homes, needs carbon monoxide alarms if fuel-burning equipment is in or attached to the building, and that the alarms must be within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room. In a small cottage, one alarm in the short hall between the bedrooms may satisfy that distance for every bedroom. In a two-story home, or a cottage whose added bedroom is at the far end of the house, it may take more. We measure each one.

When the home is rented rather than lived in by its owner. HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors in one- and two-family homes that are not occupied by their owner when they contain fuel-burning devices. If an Edgewater Park owner moves away and rents the house, or lets a relative live in it while living elsewhere, the detectors become part of the owner's obligations to the occupant. We set up the alarms and leave the owner a dated record of what was installed.

When a buyer is the one calling. Buyers often call us after a home inspection report lists the alarms as old or incomplete. The first visit is the same as for a seller: test, date, map. The difference is that a buyer usually wants the house set up the way they intend to live in it, with alarms linked across the whole house, which is a natural moment to do it while the rooms are empty.

Old cottages and the wiring they hide

Our cottage began as a 1920s summer bungalow and has been added onto at least twice. How do you work out where the old wiring ends before planning alarms?

We read the house by its periods before we touch any wire. The technician walks every room looking for the changes in ceiling height, floor level, window style and roof line that mark where the original bungalow meets each addition, then checks the panel to see which breakers feed which rooms. Any existing hardwired alarm is traced to its circuit with a tester, and where a cable disappears into a shared wall we use a tone generator and probe to find where it actually goes. In houses that were winterized and extended over decades, it is common to find an addition fed from a circuit in the old cottage, a porch with no dedicated circuit, or a second level with its own small subpanel. None of that is necessarily unsafe, but it decides whether hardwired alarms can be linked straight through the house or whether a wireless bridge is needed to reach the newer rooms. If we see wiring that looks damaged, overloaded or improvised, we stop and tell you, because that is a job for a licensed electrician before any alarm is connected to it. You get a written map of the house by period with every circuit we relied on marked, so the next person who works on the house does not have to start from scratch.

Part of our living room used to be an open porch before someone enclosed it. Does that former porch need its own alarm?

If people use it as living space, and especially if anyone ever sleeps in it, it should be treated as part of the house. Enclosed porches are among the most common additions to old summer cottages, and they often differ from the rest of the house in ways that matter for detection: a lower or sloping ceiling, single-pane windows, little or no heat, and wiring that was extended from the nearest room rather than planned. A smoke alarm in a space that swings from cold to hot or stays damp may false-alarm or fail early, so the first question is whether the room is heated and closed like the rest of the house. If it is, a smoke alarm placed by the manufacturer's rules for its ceiling covers it, linked to the rest of the system so a fire that starts among the porch furniture is heard in the bedrooms. If it is unheated, a heat alarm is often the more dependable choice, with smoke detection in the heated room it opens onto. If the porch has become a bedroom, it needs a smoke alarm of its own, and the carbon monoxide distance to its door is measured like any other bedroom's. We also look at the porch's electrical supply before we connect anything to it.

The previous owner wired our hardwired smoke alarms to a light switch in the hall. Is that a problem?

Yes, and it is one we fix before anything else. A hardwired smoke alarm is meant to run from a circuit that stays on, with its battery as backup, so that the alarm and its link to the other alarms work all the time. When an alarm sits on a switched circuit, every time someone turns off the hall light the alarm drops to battery power, and in many models the interconnection depends on the household power as well. Over time the battery runs down, the alarm begins to chirp, someone takes it down or pulls the battery, and the house is left without the protection it appears to have. In a cottage where the wiring was added in stages, this happens more often than you might think, because the easiest place to take power for a new ceiling device was the nearest ceiling light. We trace the alarm circuit, move the alarms to an unswitched circuit where one is available, or install sealed long-life battery alarms with wireless interconnection where re-wiring would mean opening finished ceilings. If the only practical fix involves new line-voltage wiring, we will tell you when it needs a licensed electrician, and we can coordinate with one so the alarm side is finished in the same visit.

Close neighbors, narrow lanes and getting help

The house next door is only a few feet from ours. What can our alarms do about a fire that starts in our neighbor's home?

Your alarms detect fire inside your house, and that is what they are designed to do. A smoke alarm will not reliably sense a fire in the house next door until smoke enters yours, and by then you should already be leaving. What close-set houses change is the value of every household getting its own warning early, because the earliest alarm in the row protects everyone on the lane. The city's planners described Edgewater Park's structures as sitting right next to one another on narrow streets, and in that setting the most useful steps are simple. Make sure your own alarms are linked, so a fire in any room of your house is heard in every bedroom within moments. Keep the side windows and side passages between houses clear of stored fuel, firewood, propane cylinders and charging batteries, since those spaces are where a fire could pass from one house to the next. If the side of your home that faces the neighbor holds a bedroom, make sure its alarm is inside the room, not only in the hall. And exchange phone numbers with the households on each side, so whoever hears or smells something first can call the others as well as 911. A neighbor's alarm going off is a reason to knock and call, not to wait.

We recommend testing the account with the monitoring center once it is live, and checking the record again whenever anyone on the call list changes.

Fuel, heat and the bay

Our cottage is all-electric except for a gas range. Does New York still expect us to have a carbon monoxide alarm?

Yes. The FDNY's brochure says every dwelling unit, including one- and two-family homes, needs carbon monoxide alarms when fuel-burning equipment is in or attached to the building, and a gas range is fuel-burning equipment. It does not matter that the heat and hot water are electric. The same is true of a gas dryer, a fireplace, an attached garage where a car runs, or a fuel-fired generator kept in an attached space. The alarms have to be within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room, and in a small cottage, where the kitchen may be only a few steps from the bedrooms, a single well-placed alarm in the short hall can often cover every bedroom door. We keep carbon monoxide alarms out of the kitchen itself and away from the range, both because the instructions for most models say so and because cooking can produce brief readings that make people distrust the alarm. If you rent the house to someone else and do not live there, HPD's rules on carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied homes apply as well. A combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarm in the bedroom hall is often the tidiest way to meet both needs in a small house.

Our home faces Eastchester Bay and salt air rusts anything left outdoors. Does that change which alarms we choose or how long they last?

Inside a closed, heated house, the bay matters much less than you might expect, and in the spaces between inside and outside it matters a great deal. A residential smoke or carbon monoxide alarm is designed for the conditions of a living room or a bedroom, and in those rooms a home on the waterfront treats it the same as a home a mile inland. The problems come in the spaces that waterfront homes tend to have: unheated enclosed porches, sunrooms, vestibules that take a gust of wet air whenever the door opens, and garages or storage under a raised floor. Every alarm's instructions give a temperature and humidity range, and outside it the device may false-alarm, corrode or fail before its date. So we keep smoke alarms in conditioned rooms, use heat alarms for damp or unheated spaces, and avoid mounting any device right beside an exterior door or a window that is opened to the breeze off the water. Any fastener or conduit that is exposed to the outside air gets corrosion-resistant hardware. And we date every alarm on the device and on your list, so the ones in harsher spots can be checked first. The replacement date printed by the manufacturer still applies; salt air is a reason to watch them, not a reason to keep them longer.

Our house was raised and now sits on piers with a latticed space underneath where we keep bikes and a lawn mower. Does that space need any detection?

It deserves a decision, and the answer depends on what is down there and how it connects to the house above. A latticed space under a raised home is open to the weather, so ordinary smoke alarms do not belong there: they are not designed for outdoor conditions and would false-alarm or fail. What matters is the risk the space creates for the living floor above it. A lawn mower and its gasoline can, a car, a generator, or batteries on charge are fuel and ignition sources directly under the bedrooms. Our first recommendation is about storage, not devices: keep fuel and charging batteries out of that space if you can, and never run a generator or engine there. Our second is about the floor above: make sure the room directly over the space, and the stair or door that leads down to it, have smoke and carbon monoxide coverage inside the house, linked to the rest. If the space is fully enclosed and used as a garage or workshop, we look at heat detection rated for that environment and at whether any device can be mounted there and connected safely without putting electrical equipment in the area expected to flood. Anything that needs power stays in the dry living space.

FAQ

Edgewater Park Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Do smoke alarms have to go on the ceiling, or can some be mounted on a wall?
+

Both can be correct, depending on the model and the room. Many smoke alarms are designed for either the ceiling or the upper part of a wall, and their instructions give the exact distance from the corner and the ceiling line. In small cottages with low or sloped ceilings, a wall position is sometimes the better choice. We follow the instructions for each device and the shape of each room rather than one rule for the whole house.

Can a smoke alarm go inside a bathroom or right outside its door?
+

Not inside, and not so close that shower steam reaches it every morning. Steam is one of the most common causes of nuisance alarms in small houses, where the bathroom door often opens straight into the bedroom hall. Most manufacturers give a minimum distance from a bathroom door, and we place the hall alarm beyond it while keeping it close enough to the bedrooms to do its job.

Is a heat alarm a good substitute for a smoke alarm in the kitchen?
+

In the kitchen itself, often yes. A heat alarm responds to a rapid rise in temperature rather than to smoke, so ordinary cooking rarely sets it off, while a pan fire will. It does not replace the smoke alarms near the bedrooms, which are there to catch slower, smoky fires anywhere in the house. Linked together, the heat alarm in the kitchen sounds every alarm in the home.

Should a smoke alarm be kept away from a ceiling fan or an air conditioner vent?
+

Yes. Moving air from a fan, a window unit or a supply vent can blow smoke away from the sensor and delay the alarm, and it can also carry dust into it. Manufacturers give minimum distances from fans and vents. In a small cottage room that leaves limited choices, so we measure before mounting and may use a wall position if the ceiling is crowded.

Why replace a smoke alarm that still beeps when we press its test button?
+

The test button mainly checks the horn and the electronics, not how well the sensor still detects smoke. Every smoke and carbon monoxide alarm carries a replacement date from its maker, and sensors lose sensitivity with age. An alarm past that date should be replaced even if it sounds on test. We write each new alarm's replacement date on the list we leave you.

Which is better for a small cottage, photoelectric or ionization smoke alarms?
+

We generally install photoelectric smoke alarms, which respond well to the smoldering fires that start in furniture and bedding and tend to give fewer false alarms from cooking in small houses where the kitchen is close to everything. Some households prefer alarms that combine both sensing methods. We explain the options at the survey and use whatever type the household and the layout suit best.

Do you bring the alarms, or do we have to buy them before the visit?
+

We bring them. The price for a system on our list includes the alarms we supply, chosen to link with one another and to suit your rooms. If you have already bought compatible alarms, we can install them for labor only instead. Please do not buy anything before the survey unless you want to, because the right number and type often surprise people.

Can the work be quoted and done in phases, one part of the house at a time?
+

Yes. We can write the quote in phases, starting with the bedrooms and the carbon monoxide alarms near them, then the rest of the house, then any monitoring. Each phase is priced separately on the same written plan, and every alarm installed in the first phase is chosen so the later ones can link to it. The house is never left worse protected between phases than it was before we started.

Someone in our family sleeps on a sofa bed in the front room when they visit. Does that room need anything extra?
+

Treat any room where someone regularly sleeps as a sleeping room. In a small cottage the front room usually already has a smoke alarm nearby, but we check that one is close enough to wake a sleeper there and that the room is within the carbon monoxide distance of an alarm if the house burns fuel. Tell us about these occasional beds at the survey, because they are easy to cover and easy to forget.

Do carbon monoxide alarms have to be mounted low, near the floor?
+

Not necessarily. Carbon monoxide mixes with the air in a room rather than sinking, and the right height depends on the model. Some are made for the wall at a set height, some for the ceiling, and combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarms go where the smoke alarm belongs. We follow each manufacturer's instructions and keep them out of reach of curious children and pets where that matters.

Can you mount an alarm on a wood-paneled or tongue-and-groove ceiling?
+

Yes. Wooden ceilings are common in older summer cottages and enclosed porches. We fasten the mounting plate into solid wood or framing so it stays put, keep any cable hidden in the joint or above the boards where possible, and avoid splitting the boards. If you would rather not have holes in a ceiling you care about, a wall-mounted alarm or a wireless model with a minimal mounting plate may be the better choice.

Our smallest bedroom only has room for a bed. Does it still need its own smoke alarm?
+

Yes. Size does not change the purpose. A smoke alarm inside each sleeping room is what wakes the person in it, especially with the door shut, and small bedrooms in cottages are often in additions at the far end of the house. In a very small room we choose a position away from the window and any fan, and link the alarm to the rest of the house.
