The Neighborhood

Why The Hub Properties Need Intercom Installation

The Hub was named for transit. Wikipedia's history of the Third Avenue–149th Street station says the meeting of two rapid transit lines with the trolleys on Third Avenue and 149th Street, and the stores that grew up around them, led people to call the intersection "the Hub" early in the twentieth century. Between 1900 and 1930, by Wikipedia's count (which it flags as needing a citation), the Bronx grew from about 201,000 residents to 1,265,000, and East 149th Street became known as the Broadway of the Bronx, lined with department stores, theaters and restaurants. Many of the apartment buildings that housed that growth still stand on the side streets of Melrose and Mott Haven, and they are where a large share of our intercom work begins. A five- or six-story brick walk-up from the 1910s or 1920s was built long before anyone ran a voice line from the street door to an apartment kitchen. Whatever intercom it has today was added later, usually more than once, by different owners and different supers, each working with what the last one left behind.

The decades after the boom were hard on these buildings. Wikipedia describes deindustrialization, suburban migration and disinvestment from the 1950s through the 1970s, when many 149th Street businesses closed, and The Clio's history of the district says areas like The Hub became known for crowded tenements and aging infrastructure. The elevated Third Avenue line came down in 1973; Forgotten New York wrote that its removal drenched the Hub in sunshine for the first time in nine decades. What the buildings carried out of those years is visible in their cellars: risers spliced and re-spliced, handsets from three makers in the same hallway, a transformer screwed to a joist with no label, and panel buttons that no longer match the apartments behind them. One security vendor describes Mott Haven walk-ups whose original lobby intercoms are long gone or have been patched for more than fifty years. That is a description from a company that sells this work, not a survey, and we treat it that way, but it matches what we expect to find when we open a panel near the Hub.

Today the pressure on the street door comes from the sidewalk. The Bronx Times reported that the district sees some of the heaviest foot traffic in the city, at over 200,000 visitors a day, and it quoted Representative Ritchie Torres describing the area as an "open-air drug market" where syringes and trash litter the ground. The Third Avenue BID has said it plans to install dozens of new security cameras with state Downtown Revitalization Initiative funding, along with more than fifty new lighting fixtures. None of that work reaches inside a building's entrance. For the people who live above the stores and on the side streets, the line between a crowded sidewalk and their own stairwell is the street door: whether it closes and locks by itself, whether a visitor can reach the right apartment, and whether the person upstairs can talk to that visitor, and ideally see them, before pressing release. When the intercom fails, the usual workaround is the worst one. Someone props the door with a folded flyer or tapes the latch so deliveries and guests can get in, and the building's one barrier against the sidewalk is gone until somebody notices.

The newest buildings bring different questions. Haven Court, a ten-story building with 164 apartments on East 149th Street, opened its housing lottery in 2026 with a virtual doorman listed among its amenities, according to New York YIMBY, and La Central on Bergen Avenue is planned for 992 apartments across five buildings. These buildings are designed for networked panels, phone apps and fobs from the start, so their intercom work is about networks, power, accounts and construction phasing rather than old copper. Between the walk-ups and the new towers are the district's other entrances: 1920s office buildings on East 149th Street with clinics and offices upstairs, apartments over shops on Third Avenue, and two designated landmarks, the Haffen Building and the Bronx Opera House, whose fronts limit where anything new can be mounted. An intercom in The Hub is less about one product than about getting each of these entrances to do one job well: let the right person in, keep the door locked for everyone else, and keep working when the building is busy.

The Hub facts that shape an intercom plan before any equipment is chosen

  • Old buildings are covered too. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says that from January 1, 1969, every entrance from the street, court, yard or cellar of a building erected or converted before 1968 with eight or more apartments must have self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercommunication system. Most walk-ups near the Hub are that size.
  • New buildings are covered by state law. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom in class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments. Haven Court, La Central, the East 149th Street Development's 310 affordable apartments and Betances Family Apartments' 101 apartments all fall in that group.
  • The neighborhood is mid-rise. The architects of Betances Family Apartments, a 15-story building, noted that its surroundings are mostly five- and six-story buildings. That is the riser height we plan for most often: tall enough to need a proper riser, short enough to have no elevator.
  • The door gets used constantly. Over 200,000 visitors a day, per the Bronx Times, pass through a district of 613 storefronts. A closer, a strike and a panel here work harder than they would on a quiet block, and they are tested by more strangers.
  • Two landmarks, partly designated. The Haffen Building at 2804 Third Avenue was designated on June 22, 2010, and the Bronx Opera House at 436–442 East 149th Street on June 13, 2023, covering only its historic 149th Street front. We found no historic district covering the Hub's core.
  • NYCHA next door runs its own standard. NYCHA's Layered Access Control program uses key tags, a direct-call intercom and new door locks. Residents of nearby developments such as the Jackson and Melrose Houses report entry problems to NYCHA, which maintains those systems itself.

Where this page applies. Wikipedia places The Hub mainly in Melrose, along the northern edge of Mott Haven, and the district falls in Bronx Community Board 1 and the Third Avenue BID. Its core is the bow-tie where East 149th Street, co-named Eugenio Maria de Hostos Boulevard, crosses Third Avenue and meets Willis and Melrose Avenues, with Westchester Avenue beginning at Third Avenue and Bergen Avenue running past La Central. Because The Hub is a shopping district rather than a neighborhood with fixed lines, an address a few blocks off 149th Street may be described as Melrose, Mott Haven or The Hub depending on who is talking, and none of that changes the rules or the rate. If your building is closer to the Grand Concourse, see our Concourse intercom installation page, and for blocks further north, our Claremont intercom installation page. Same-Day Estimates for Repairs in The Hub

Need Repair in The Hub? Upper Floors That Never Ring, Buzzing With Nobody at the Door, or a Release That Clicks While the Street Door Stays Locked

Repair calls from buildings around the Hub tend to follow a few patterns. The first is a building where the lower floors get calls and the top floor or two do not. In a walk-up, that usually means a break or a corroded splice somewhere in the riser between the floors that work and the ones that don't, often where a previous repair joined new wire to old in a hallway ceiling or behind a mailbox. The second is buzzing when nobody is at the door, sometimes in one apartment, sometimes across the building, sometimes at three in the morning. A Bronx repair vendor lists a stuck call button, shorted riser wiring or a failed door-release relay as the usual causes, and in our experience the panel at a busy street door, pressed thousands of times by people who are not visitors at all, is a frequent suspect. The third is a release that hums or clicks upstairs while the street door stays shut. That is rarely the intercom. It is the strike, the latch or the door: a frame knocked out of line, a latch that lands a fraction of an inch away from where the strike was cut, or a closer pulling so hard that the strike cannot let go. The fourth shows up in newer buildings: a networked panel that freezes, restarts or stops placing calls, where the fault is often a tired power supply, a battery backup that has quietly died, or a network setting that changed without anyone noticing.

Until the repair, keep the street door locking with its key, and do not leave it unlatched so visitors can get in; in a district this busy, an open door is the bigger risk. If equipment in the cellar is hot to the touch, buzzing loudly or smells burnt, switch off its breaker and leave it off. Then call (646) 490-0629. Many single faults, such as a broken riser pair, a stuck button, a strike that needs to move to where the latch actually lands, or a power supply that has failed, are found and fixed on one visit, and on the phone we tell you honestly what the photos let us promise and what they don't. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we estimate the tracing time before we start. If HPD has written an order, tell us its date, because HPD's published correction periods are 90 days for class A, 30 days for class B and 14 days for a class C self-closing door order, and the repair has to fit inside them. If a repair would cost a large share of a new system, or the old parts are no longer made, we write down both choices so the owner doesn't pay to patch something that will need replacing next year.

The pre-war walk-up: a riser with a long memory

In a typical walk-up near the Hub, the original builder left no intercom at all. Sometime after the city's 1969 deadline, an owner added an audio system: a panel at the street door, an amplifier or power unit in the cellar, and a riser of multi-pair cable climbing the stair hall to a handset beside each apartment's kitchen or entry door. Since then, apartments may have been combined or split, handsets replaced with whatever was on the shelf, and riser breaks bridged with short lengths of new cable in hallway ceilings. By the time we arrive, the panel's button labels may not match the apartment numbers, two apartments may share a pair, and the top floor may have been dead for so long that tenants have stopped mentioning it.

Our plan starts by testing every riser pair from the cellar to each apartment, because the riser decides the job. Where most pairs are continuous, clean and free of leakage, a new panel and new stations, sometimes video, can run on them, and walls stay closed. Where a floor or a section has failed, we replace that section of the riser rather than the whole building's, routed up the stair hall in a small painted raceway or through an existing chase. Where the riser is beyond saving, we say so and price a new one. Under 1 RCNY 42-01, the rule's reach covers entrances from the street, court, yard or cellar, so a rear court door or a cellar door that people use gets the same attention as the front: a self-closing, self-locking door and, where visitors use it, a station. Before any strike goes in, we cycle the street door from fully open to make sure it closes and latches on its own. Every pair is labeled at both ends with its apartment, and the record stays in the cellar.

Apartments over a Third Avenue store: two businesses of entry in one narrow doorway

Third Avenue and the blocks of Willis and Melrose Avenues near 149th Street are lined with buildings that have a store on the ground floor and apartments above, reached through a narrow door beside the shop window. The residents' door and the store's door have nothing in common except the building, and the intercom must keep it that way. The apartments' station never releases the store, the store's staff never answers for the apartments, and the power supply for the residential system is not plugged into an outlet the store controls or switches off at closing.

The fire that ABC7 reported in May 2026, in a commercial building on Third Avenue between East 139th and 140th Streets with a grocery on the ground floor and apartments above, is a reminder of how these buildings work in an emergency. Firefighters found heavy fire at the front door. That building is about ten blocks south of the Hub's core, and we draw no conclusion about its cause or its entry system; what it shows is how much depends on the door, the stair and the hall beside a store. So on these jobs the intercom's cable and power stay out of the store's stock areas and away from the residential stair's path of travel, nothing we mount narrows the doorway or blocks the stair, and the street door always opens freely from inside without a key, a code or a button, as 1 RCNY 42-01 requires of self-locking doors. If the cellar is the store's territory, we look for a route up through the residential side of the building instead, or we agree in writing with both the owner and the store where the equipment will live.

The 1920s office building on East 149th Street: suites, clinics and hours

Forgotten New York describes office buildings on East 149th Street between the Grand Concourse and Bergen Avenue dating to the mid-1920s, and a twelve-story tower on East 149th Street between Third and Courtlandt Avenues. Upper floors in the district hold offices and clinics. Sun River Health The Hub is in Suite 202 at 459 East 149th Street, and Callen-Lorde expanded a fourth-floor clinic near the Third Avenue–149th Street station in October 2026. The BID's commercial space listings describe the Gotham Building at 384 East 149th Street as a doorman building with two ADA elevators. We name these only to describe the kind of building; none of the sources we read say anything about their entry systems.

In buildings like these, the intercom question is about hours and routing. During the day, a lobby desk or an open door may handle visitors. Before opening and after closing, someone at the street door needs to reach the right suite, and the right suite needs to be able to let that person into the lobby and no further. A directory panel with suite listings, calls that route to a suite's front desk during office hours and to a manager's phone or a building contact after hours, and a release that opens only the street door, with elevators and stairs handled by the building's own access rules, cover most of what these tenants need. Where a clinic's patients arrive in a steady stream, the panel's position, height and lighting matter as much as its features. We plan with the building's management rather than with one tenant alone, because a panel at the shared door belongs to everyone who uses it.

The new tower along 149th Street: required equipment on a contractor's schedule

The new buildings near the Hub are large. Haven Court, at 290 East 149th Street and 299 East 148th Street, is a ten-story, 100-foot building with 164 apartments under Department of Buildings job 210178948, with a first certificate of occupancy dated February 19, 2026. The East 149th Street Development turned an underused industrial site into 310 affordable apartments. Betances Family Apartments is a 15-story, 101-apartment building on a NYCHA site, with about 10,000 square feet of commercial space described as being in The Hub. Every one of these is a post-1968 multiple dwelling with eight or more apartments, so §50-a's requirement of an intercom with voice communication and remote door release applies to each apartment, whatever else the building offers.

For these buildings, the intercom is planned on the drawings, not after them. We work with the general contractor to fix the lobby panel's position and height, the cable path from the panel to an equipment closet and up to every apartment, strikes and closers on every entrance including service and garage doors, and a dedicated circuit for the equipment, which a licensed electrician installs. Where the building uses networked panels, the closet gets a switch on battery backup and a documented internet connection, and the owner, not the installer, holds the administrator account. Before move-in, every apartment is called from the lobby and from any secondary entrance, every release is tested while someone watches the door relatch, and the directory is loaded so visitors can find each household.

The multi-building complex: five front doors, one plan

La Central, at 556 and 600 Bergen Avenue within the Hub, is planned for 992 apartments across five buildings, and the Bronx Times reported in 2026 that its second phase is continuing. Complexes like this are built and occupied in stages, so the first buildings open while later ones are still steel and block. The intercom has to work in each building on the day its residents move in, without depending on equipment that sits in a building not yet finished, and it has to join the rest of the complex later without a rebuild.

Our approach is that every building stands on its own: its own panel, its own equipment closet, its own power and battery backup, and calls from its own lobby that reach its own apartments even if every link to the other buildings is cut. Links between buildings, for a shared management office, a security desk or a common entrance, are planned at the start and installed as each building is finished. NYCHA's design guidelines offer a useful benchmark: they require single-mode fiber optic cable for connections between buildings, because copper strung between structures picks up interference and lightning surges, and fiber does not. A private complex is not bound by NYCHA's guidelines, but the reasoning applies, and we price inter-building links that way unless the owner's engineer specifies otherwise. The same naming, numbering and account structure is used in every building so that a super who knows one knows all five.

The landmark front: reading the designation before touching the wall

The Haffen Building at 2804 Third Avenue, also known as 507 Willis Avenue, is a seven-story Beaux-Arts office building designed by Michael J. Garvin for the brewery heir Mathias Haffen and built in 1901 and 1902, according to the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report; it became a city landmark on June 22, 2010. The Bronx Opera House at 436–442 East 149th Street, an Italian Renaissance Revival building of 1912 and 1913 by George Keister, was designated on June 13, 2023. CityLand reported that only the part of the site facing East 149th Street, containing the historic portion of the building, was considered for designation, and the commission's report notes that the rear auditorium was demolished and replaced. The building has operated as the Opera House Hotel since 2013.

For any designated building, the work starts with the designation report and a conversation with the commission, not with a drill. On a designated front, a new panel, a camera or even cable on the face of the masonry would likely need the commission's review, and we plan for that time in the schedule. On parts of a site the designation does not cover, the rules may be different, and we confirm with the commission rather than assume. Wherever we mount, the cable reaches the station from behind, through the wall or the frame, and nothing is anchored into ornamental stone that could be repaired later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What the Survey of a Hub Building Looks For

Start on the sidewalk, at visitor height. The technician stands where a visitor stands and uses the panel as a stranger would. On a block this busy, it shows at once whether the panel is easy to find beside a shop window, whether its labels can be read, and whether the crowd on the sidewalk or the noise from Third Avenue makes a call hard to hear.

Call the top floor first. If the highest apartments answer clearly, the riser is probably sound below them. If they don't, the rest of the survey is about finding where the riser fails.

Count the apartments, then count the buttons. Panels near the Hub often list apartments that were combined years ago or miss ones that were split. The survey sheet records who lives behind the door today, because that number also decides which rules apply.

Find every door that people really use. The rule covers entrances from the street, court, yard and cellar. A rear door that tenants use to take out trash, or a cellar door that opens to the sidewalk, matters as much as the front.

Open the cellar and the closet. The age and condition of the amplifier, power supply or network gear, how it is powered and whether it is labeled tell us what can stay. In a mixed-use building, we also ask who controls the cellar.

Close the door and let it go. A street door that does not latch on its own from fully open is the first thing to fix, before any strike or panel, because an intercom on a door that drifts open protects nobody.

Risers, Doors and Power

Old Risers, Busy Doors and Networks That Stay Up: Wiring a Building Near the Hub

Most of what makes an intercom last happens out of sight, in the riser, the cellar, the closet and the door frame. A building near the Hub can hold a riser from the 1970s, a panel from the 2000s, a router from last year and a street door that has been replaced twice. This section explains what we check and what we do about each finding, so the quote reads clearly.

Testing the riser before trusting it. A walk-up's riser is usually a multi-pair cable that climbs the stair hall, with individual pairs broken out to each apartment. We check every pair from the cellar to the apartment for continuity, look for leakage between conductors and to ground, and note where a previous repair joined new cable to old. One vendor reports that many Bronx buildings can take a four-wire video intercom on existing copper, and some current systems run on as few as two wires per apartment, so a sound riser can save a building from opening every hallway. A riser that tests sound on most floors and fails on one is repaired on that floor. A riser full of splices is replaced, because a new system on a bad riser inherits every intermittent fault the old one had.

Routing new cable through an occupied walk-up. When cable has to be replaced, the stair hall is the shared space and usually the best route: a small painted raceway in a corner, or an existing chase or dumbwaiter shaft that can legally take low-voltage cable. We avoid routes through apartments wherever possible, and where an apartment has to be entered to reach its station, we schedule it with the tenant and keep the visit short. We show the owner or super the route before drilling.

Networks for networked panels. A vendor working in nearby Hunts Point describes cloud-connected panels whose faults trace to internet outages, a failed power-over-Ethernet switch or misconfigured networking, and that matches our experience with newer systems. So a networked panel gets its own switch, ideally on its own network segment, with the switch and the internet equipment both on battery backup. The panel's network settings, the internet account it depends on and the administrator login are written down and given to the owner. When the internet provider changes the building's equipment, someone should know the intercom depends on it.

Fitting strikes to doors that take a beating. Street doors near the Hub are used by residents, couriers, food delivery riders and people who are not supposed to be there, all day and much of the night. Steel doors and frames are common, and many have been knocked out of line by years of use. A strike only works if the latch lands where the strike expects it, so we check the gap, the hinge side and the frame before cutting. Where the frame has been bent or patched, it is repaired or reinforced first. We choose strike hardware rated for heavy use, and we set it so the door locks again the moment it closes.

Making the door close and latch every time. Local Law 62 of 2022, as quoted in NYCHA's design guidelines, defines a self-closing door as one that returns to the closed position and self-latches. In a multiple dwelling, the street door must do that after every release. We adjust or replace the closer so the door shuts firmly past the strike without slamming, then test by releasing from several apartments, including the top floor, and letting the door close on its own. A vendor in the Bronx says HPD often writes up the entrance door along with the intercom, and that replacing a panel on a door that doesn't latch clears nothing. Whether or not that is HPD's usual practice, the logic holds.

Putting the power where it belongs. The power supply or amplifier should be on its own labeled circuit, in a place the owner or super can reach and nobody can unplug by accident. In a mixed-use building, that means not on a store's circuit or in a store's locked stockroom. In a new building, it means a dedicated circuit in the equipment closet, with battery backup sized to keep calls working through a short outage. Any new line-voltage circuit is installed by a licensed electrician; the low-voltage intercom work is done under our New York State license #12000287431.

Leaving a record. Every job ends with a written note: which pair or cable serves which apartment, the power supply's location and breaker, the strike and closer models, the network details and administrator account for networked systems, and the date each entrance was tested. In buildings that change supers and managing agents, that page is what keeps the next repair short.

The Rules at the Door

Walk-Ups, New Towers and Small Buildings: What New York Rules Mean for an Intercom Near the Hub

The Hub's buildings range from four apartments over a shop to towers with hundreds. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, and where they disagree, so owners, managing agents, developers and tenants can ask the right questions of counsel, HPD or the Department of Buildings.

Buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968, with eight or more apartments. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling in this group to have an intercommunication system. Parker Custom Security summarizes the requirement as voice communication and remote door release. The new buildings around the Hub, including Haven Court, La Central, the East 149th Street Development and Betances Family Apartments, are in this group.

Buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968, with eight or more apartments. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says that on or after January 1, 1969, every entrance from the street, court, yard or cellar to a class A multiple dwelling of this age and size must have automatic self-closing doors and self-locking doors and an intercommunication system. Read as written, the city rule does not wait for a tenant request. Some summaries of state law describe older buildings as needing an intercom only if a majority of tenants ask for one. Those readings point in different directions, and for a pre-war walk-up near the Hub, an owner should get a lawyer's answer rather than rely on a summary, including this one.

What the system has to do. 1 RCNY 42-01 describes the minimum as a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, together with a return buzzer that releases the entrance door lock. For post-1968 buildings, §50-a's requirement, as Parker Custom Security describes it, is two-way voice plus remote release. The rule also says the system must be placed so a person can readily reach the door when the unlocking buzzer is activated, which in practice means the release has to stay open long enough for a visitor at the panel to get through the door.

Vestibules. Under 1 RCNY 42-01, where an inner vestibule door is self-closing and self-locking, the door from the street to the vestibule need not be. Many walk-ups near the Hub have a vestibule with mailboxes between two doors, and which door locks, and which door the intercom releases, is a design choice the owner makes with that rule in mind.

Free exit. The same rule says self-locking doors must open from inside without a key. Every system we install controls entry only. Nobody inside a building should ever need a key, a code or a button to leave.

Mounting heights. 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. On a building with a step or two at the entrance, we measure from where a visitor actually stands. Where a building is open to the public, accessibility reach rules usually put controls lower still.

Buildings with fewer than eight apartments. Parker Custom Security says these buildings are not covered by the intercom requirement, though the self-closing and self-locking door requirement still applies. Many buildings over stores on Third Avenue fall here. Owners often install an intercom anyway, because a self-locking door without one leaves tenants walking down to every delivery.

Self-closing doors. Local Law 111 of 2018 required doors opening onto interior corridors or stairs in R-1 and R-2 occupancy buildings to be self-closing by July 31, 2021. HPD lists a class C self-closing door order with a 14-day correction period. A strike must work with the self-closing door, never against it.

Violation classes. Here the sources do not agree. Parker Custom Security says a non-working intercom or buzzer is a class B hazardous violation. A 2026 guide on HPD violations lists "non-functioning intercom" under both class A and class C. A public HPD record for a building on Manor Avenue in the Bronx shows a class A order to properly repair or replace a broken or defective intercom from the building entrance door to the panel. We could not confirm one rule from HPD itself, so we don't state one. HPD's correction periods, by its own published page, are 90 days for class A, 30 days for class B and 14 days for a class C self-closing door.

Landmarks. The Haffen Building and the Bronx Opera House are individual landmarks; for the Opera House, only the historic 149th Street portion was designated. We found no historic district covering the Hub's core, but a search that found nothing is not a confirmation, so we check each address against the commission's records before work on a facade.

Licensing and electrical work. Intercom work in New York carries state licensing for the installer, and we work under New York State license #12000287431. Any new line-voltage circuit for a power supply is a job for a licensed electrician, with whatever permit the electrical work requires.

NYCHA developments. The Jackson and Melrose Houses and other NYCHA developments near the Hub follow NYCHA's own design guidelines and maintenance contracts. NYCHA requires a 20-amp, 120-volt receptacle within six feet of each new interior intercom, and single-mode fiber between buildings. Those are NYCHA's specifications for NYCHA buildings, and NYCHA residents report entry problems to NYCHA.

Responsibility and security

A tenant in our walk-up was robbed in the hallway by someone who walked in while the buzzer had been broken for weeks. Does the owner carry any responsibility for that?

Possibly, and that question is for a lawyer, not an installer. Brick Underground, citing the New York Attorney General's guide to tenants' rights, reported that if you are the victim of a crime because a door is unlocked due to a broken buzzer, your landlord could be held liable. That is the reason a broken intercom in a building near the Hub is never just an inconvenience. When it fails, the door tends to get propped or left unlatched so people can get in, and the building's protection from a sidewalk with more than 200,000 visitors a day goes with it. For a tenant, the practical steps are a police report for the crime, written notice to the owner about the intercom and the door, and a complaint through 311 so there is a public record. For an owner, the practical steps are to get the street door locking with a key immediately, whatever the state of the intercom, then repair the intercom inside HPD's correction periods, and keep dated records of every notice, visit and repair. We can document the condition of the door and the system at the survey, in writing and with photographs, so the owner and the tenants are looking at the same facts. What we cannot do is tell either side how a court would weigh them.

Management plans to drop the overnight security guard and rely on a new smart video intercom. Is that an even trade?

Not in our view, and Bronx tenants have said the same. ABC7 reported on a building in High Bridge where tenants said a new smart video intercom, which management said would improve security, was no replacement for a round-the-clock guard. The two do different jobs. An intercom lets a resident decide who comes in: it identifies the visitor, connects them to the right apartment, and releases the door only when someone chooses to. A guard watches the lobby, notices someone following a resident through the door, responds when something goes wrong, and deals with people already inside. In a building with eight or more apartments, the intercom is required regardless; a guard is a management decision. If the guard is going, tenants can fairly ask what fills the gap: whether the panel's camera records visits and who reviews them, whether the street door now latches on its own every time, how fobs are issued and cancelled, whether the lobby is lit and covered by cameras, and who residents call at night when something happens. A good intercom makes the door harder to get through, but it does not see what happens after the door closes.

Only the 149th Street front of our building is a designated landmark. Does a call station at the side or rear entrance still need Landmarks approval?

It may not, but confirm that with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before anything is mounted. A designation covers what its report says it covers. CityLand reported that for the Bronx Opera House, only the portion of the site facing East 149th Street and containing the historic building was considered for designation, and the commission's report notes that the rear auditorium was demolished and replaced and is not part of it. A building with a partial designation like that has two kinds of wall: the designated front, where a new panel, a camera or cable on the masonry would likely need the commission's review, and the rest, where the commission's rules may not reach. The safe order is to read the designation report, ask the commission whether the proposed location falls inside the designated area, and only then plan the work. Where the designated front is the only practical entrance, we design for the commission's review from the start: the slimmest suitable station, cable entering from behind rather than across the stone, and anchors in mortar joints rather than ornamental faces. We do not decide what the commission will approve, and we would not mount on a designated facade without its sign-off.

Where the rules begin and end

Our building has seven apartments over three stores on Third Avenue. Do the stores count toward the eight-apartment line in the intercom rules?

As the rules are written, the line counts apartments. 1 RCNY 42-01 speaks of class A multiple dwellings containing eight or more apartments, and Parker Custom Security describes the state requirement the same way, so a building with seven apartments and three stores appears to sit below the intercom threshold. That does not leave the door unregulated. Parker Custom Security notes that buildings with fewer than eight apartments still have to meet the self-closing and self-locking door requirement, so the residential door has to close and lock on its own. Two cautions matter here. First, the apartment count is what is actually in the building, not what an old certificate says, and a building where apartments were split or added may hold more than its paperwork suggests. Second, if the upper floors are ever reconfigured to reach eight, the requirement arrives with them. Confirm with counsel or HPD rather than any website, including this one. In practice, a building at seven apartments over Third Avenue stores gets the same plan from us as a building at eight: a station for every apartment, a release on a self-latching residential door, and nothing shared with the stores.

Several tenants have asked us to disconnect the release buttons in their apartments because strangers keep talking them into buzzing the door. Can we?

In a building the rule covers, removing the release from an apartment would likely leave that apartment without what the law asks for. Parker Custom Security describes §50-a's requirement as voice communication and remote door release, and 1 RCNY 42-01 includes a return buzzer that releases the entrance lock in its minimum system. So the better answer is to make the release harder to misuse rather than to take it away. A video station lets a tenant see who is asking before deciding, which is the single biggest change for tenants who are being worked on by strangers on a busy block. Calls should ring one apartment at a time, never several at once, and the panel should have no setting that opens the door without a resident's action. Management can remind residents, in writing, that nobody is obliged to buzz in a person they don't know, and that couriers can be met at the door or directed to a package arrangement. Where the system records which apartment released the door and when, management can see whether one station is being used again and again. Nothing about this stops a tenant from simply choosing not to press the button.

We run a small hotel near the Hub. Do the apartment intercom rules apply to our guest rooms?

The rules we cite on this page are written for class A multiple dwellings, the category for apartments people live in as their permanent home, and they speak in terms of apartments. A hotel's guest rooms are a different kind of occupancy, and its entrances are governed by its own classification, the building and fire codes and its operating plan, so its architect or code consultant should confirm what applies to that specific building. What we install in hotels and similar buildings is driven by how they run. A front desk usually handles arrivals during staffed hours. After hours, a door station at the guest entrance can ring the desk or a manager's phone, show the caller on video, and release only that door. Staff and service doors can have their own stations so deliveries never come through the guest entrance. Every door with an electric strike still opens freely from inside. If the building is a designated landmark, the station's position is planned with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before anything else. We say nothing here about how any particular hotel in the district is equipped; this is how we would approach one.
