The Neighborhood

Why Starrett City Properties Need Access Control

Starrett City was built on a marsh. Wikipedia’s history of the complex, drawn from decades of New York Times reporting, says a California investment group bought about 130 acres along Pennsylvania Avenue between Flatlands Avenue and the Belt Parkway in 1962, planned a middle-income project called Park Shore Village, and walked away. The United Housing Foundation, which was also building Co-op City in the Bronx, then proposed a cooperative called Twin Pines Village in 1967 and abandoned it in March 1971 after running out of money. A new investor group, Starrett City Associates, took over, turned the project from a co-op into rentals, filled the swampland with sand pumped from Jamaica Bay, and dedicated the complex on October 13, 1974, with Governor Malcolm Wilson and Mayor Abraham Beame in attendance.

What they built is unlike anything else in Brooklyn. The complex holds 5,881 apartments in 46 buildings of 11 to 20 stories, laid out as towers in a park and grouped into eight sections named after New York State towns: Ardsley, Bethel, Croton, Delmar, Elmira, Freeport, Geneva and Hornell. Each section has its own field, playground and courts, and its own five-story parking garage. The complex has a shopping center, a recreation center that is now the Brooklyn Sports Club, schools on its own grounds and, from its opening, a community newspaper: the Spring Creek Sun reports that its first issue appeared on June 13, 1974, as the Starrett City Sun. Wikipedia and the Brooklyn Eagle both describe it as the largest federally subsidized housing development in the United States.

That scale is why the doors work differently here. One owner holds every lobby, every garage, every laundry room and every hallway. Starrett City Associates sold the complex in 2018 to the Brooksville Company and Rockpoint Group, which took full control in 2021. The same year New York State Homes and Community Renewal announced an agreement keeping it affordable through 2069, under which the Brooklyn Eagle reports 3,569 apartments are covered by a federal Section 8 contract and 2,312 are not, and the owners must make about $140 million in further repairs and upgrades by 2030. Security on the grounds is the owner’s too: the Spring Creek Towers website says the grounds are patrolled 24 hours a day by New York City certified armed peace officers of its Department of Public Safety, on top of the NYPD’s 75th Precinct.

So for a resident, almost every door is somebody else’s decision. The lobby door, the garage stair, the laundry room and the corridor fire doors belong to the owner, and changes to them are made by the owner’s contractors. What remains is the apartment entrance door, and that door is governed by a short list of laws that matter more here than in a private brownstone: the state Multiple Dwelling Law, which lets a tenant add a lock of their own if the landlord can have a duplicate key; the city’s Housing Maintenance Code, which makes the owner supply the deadbolt and chain guard and keep the door self-closing; and HUD’s inspection standard for subsidized housing, which checks that entry doors lock and that fire-rated doors still work. A lock that ignores those rules can cost a tenant an argument with management or a failed inspection item. A lock that respects them is an ordinary, useful upgrade.

The need is real. In November 2024 the Spring Creek Sun reported that community affairs officers from the 75th Precinct visited the JASA senior program on the grounds to warn older residents about strangers at the apartment door. Their advice: do not open the door to anyone you do not recognize, ask to see identification through the peephole, and if a caller claims to be from Spring Creek Towers, telephone management to confirm before letting them in. The officers described people posing as delivery or utility workers, or as Spring Creek Towers staff, with a line like “a pipe broke down the block, and we need to check the water,” who then distract the resident and steal. A door that lets you see and speak to the caller, and a code system that lets the people you trust in without a key under the mat, are direct answers to that warning.

That repetition helps: once a door detail works in one tower, the same detail very likely works in the other forty-five. It also means that any mistake on a door is a mistake you can see from the next building.

Buildings of 11 to 20 stories reached through owner-run lobbies and elevators, where each apartment opens onto a common corridor. The city’s housing code makes the owner supply the latch set, deadbolt and chain guard on every unit door and keep it self-closing.

The section garages

Eight five-story garages, one per section, which since the heating overhaul also house the modular boiler plants. Stair and pedestrian doors here belong to the owner and are only ever bid work.

The shopping center and service buildings

Low commercial buildings with storefront glass at the front and steel service doors at the back, where most small-business access control requests begin.

Schools and the recreation center

Houses and townhouses next door

Nehemiah Spring Creek’s one-, two- and three-family homes east of the complex, built on former wetland and landfill, where owners control every door themselves.

What the materials mean for the install

We did not find a published specification for the apartment doors in the towers, so we confirm each door on the first visit rather than assume. Corridor entrance doors in high-rise apartment buildings of this era are most often steel, in steel frames, and where that is what we find, a second deadbolt goes through a steel skin and its strike plate lands in metal rather than in wood that can split. The catch is that such a door is usually a rated fire assembly opening onto a corridor, so we keep to factory bores and the manufacturer’s hole patterns where we can, and we never cut a door that carries a fire label without checking what the label and the door’s listing allow. On the commercial side, the steel service doors behind the shops are usually hollow metal with room for a concealed cable, while the storefront glass at the front takes a narrow-stile lock or a strike in the aluminum frame.

The site itself shapes outdoor hardware. The complex sits on fill pumped from Jamaica Bay, between the bay, the Belt Parkway and two tidal creeks, and AERCO’s case study of the heating overhaul notes the site’s high background pollution from the parkway, Pennsylvania Avenue and nearby Kennedy Airport. Readers and keypads on outside doors here get stainless or sealed housings, gaskets on every cable entry and drainage loops on every cable, and anything mounted outdoors is placed under an overhang where one exists. Indoors, a tower corridor is a dry, steady place for electronics; a garage or a rear service corridor is not, and we choose hardware accordingly.

Your Apartment Door

The One Door a Starrett City Tenant Controls, and the Rules Around It

Every apartment entrance door in the towers carries hardware that belongs to the owner. Section 27-2043 of the city’s Housing Maintenance Code requires the owner of a class A multiple dwelling to provide a heavy-duty latch set and a heavy-duty deadbolt, operable by a key from outside and a thumb-turn from inside, plus a chain door guard that lets the door open partway. The state Attorney General’s tenants’ rights guide adds that the landlord must provide a peephole. Those parts are maintained by the owner, and when they fail, the repair request goes to the management office, not to us.

What the tenant may add is set by section 51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law. Every tenant of a multiple dwelling, except one in a building run by a municipal housing authority, has the right to install and maintain a lock of their own in the entrance door of their apartment, separate from the owner’s lock, “not more than three inches in circumference,” provided a duplicate key is given to the landlord or agent on request. Any lease clause that charges extra rent or a fee for the privilege is void. Starrett City is privately owned under state supervision, not a New York City Housing Authority development, so the right applies here. Courts have taken the duplicate-key condition seriously: in the reported New York case Lavanant v. Lovelace, the tenant’s refusal to hand over a key to a privately installed lock was treated as a breach of a substantial obligation of the tenancy.

Three practical consequences follow. First, the added lock sits beside the owner’s deadbolt, not in place of it, and the chain guard stays. Second, the visible part of a keypad lock is bigger than three inches around, which is why we talk about the part of the lock that actually goes through the door rather than the keypad on its face, and why we put the size question to management in writing before drilling. Third, a keypad lock still has a key cylinder for emergencies, and that key is the duplicate the management office may ask for; we label it and hand it to you with a receipt so you can deliver it when asked.

A fire door that has to close by itself

The same door is part of the building’s fire protection. HPD’s self-closing doors page says apartment doors and hallway doors are required by law to swing closed and latch by themselves, so that in a fire no one has to remember to shut the door behind them, and section 27-2041.1 of the Housing Maintenance Code makes a broken self-closing door a class C, immediately hazardous violation that the owner must correct within 14 days. Under Local Law 71 of 2022, HPD also selects 300 class A multiple dwellings a year for proactive inspections of every self-closing door, apartment doors included where accessible. HPD tells tenants not to tamper with the closer, prop the door, tape the latch or override the closing device.

For a lock installer that means one test matters above all others: after the work, the door must still swing shut from fully open and the latch must click into the strike on its own. A second deadbolt whose bolt rubs the frame, a smart lock whose motor housing catches on the closer arm, or a viewer that stands proud of the door and hits the frame can each stop a door from latching. We open the door fully, let it go, and watch it close and latch, several times, before we pack up, and we show you how to do the same check yourself.

The inside of the door also carries a notice. HPD’s June 2024 bulletin reminds owners that the Fire Safety Notice required by section FC401.6 of the city Fire Code and section 401-06 of the Fire Department’s rules must be posted on each dwelling unit door, and HPD inspectors check for it. A large smart-lock housing or a mounting plate for an indoor screen can end up covering it. We place hardware so the notice stays readable, and where the notice is in the way of the only sensible spot, we tell you and the management office rather than move it ourselves.

The federal inspection

Because thousands of apartments here are under a HUD Section 8 contract, the property is inspected under HUD’s National Standards for the Physical Inspection of Real Estate, known as NSPIRE. HUD divides each inspection into three areas, the unit, the inside common areas and the outside, and inspects the items in each for whether they are present, operable and free of hazards. In its 2023 final rule on the standards, HUD said fire-labeled doors are not a new requirement but that fire doors which are present will be inspected and must operate effectively. An added lock that is broken, half-installed or holding a door open is therefore the kind of condition an inspector writes down. A neat second lock that locks, unlocks and lets the door latch is not.

Moving out without a fight

The right to add a lock does not settle what happens to it when you leave, and your lease and the management office do. Before we fit anything we photograph the door, note the existing holes, and keep the original parts we remove in a labeled bag for you. If the lease or management requires the door returned to its original condition, the smart adapter comes off in minutes and leaves the owner’s deadbolt exactly as it was; a second deadbolt leaves a bore that can be fitted with a matching blank plate or handed over with the lock in place if management prefers. Ask management which they want before move-out week, and keep their answer in writing.

Who Answers the Door

Working Inside a Complex With Its Own Officers and Its Own Office

Few places in New York have their own police-style patrol. Wikipedia’s account says the Department of Public Safety was created because the 75th Precinct had one of the city’s highest crime rates when the complex opened in the 1970s, that crime on the grounds was low in the complex’s early years mainly because of that force, and that a 1986 Penn State evaluation by Edwin Donovan and William Walsh called Starrett City statistically one of the safest communities in the United States. A photograph in the Spring Creek Sun’s June 2024 anniversary story shows a security officer at the complex checking a man with a metal-detecting wand. The patrol is the owner’s, and nothing we install for a tenant or a shop changes how it works.

What a patrol cannot do is stand at every apartment door. The impostors the 75th Precinct described in 2024 talk their way past the lobby and knock upstairs, and the advice the officers gave depends on the resident checking the caller before opening. That is where the tenant’s own equipment earns its keep. A digital viewer that fits the existing peephole hole, where management agrees to the swap, lets a resident who cannot comfortably stand and squint see the whole hallway on a screen; a chime-and-camera unit shows the caller on a phone, so a resident can say “wait there while I call the office” without opening the door; and a log on a keypad lock shows when a door was opened and with which code.

Some limits are worth stating plainly. A camera aimed into a shared corridor records neighbors as well as callers, and the management office decides what may be mounted on the corridor side of an apartment door, so the equipment we fit for tenants faces the tenant’s own doorway or sits inside the apartment. None of what we install talks to the owner’s systems or to the Department of Public Safety, and we never program a tenant’s lock with a code given to us by anyone other than the tenant. When something on the grounds is an emergency, the patrol and 911 come before any app.

Codes for the people who help

Many households here rely on visitors who come on a schedule: a home attendant three mornings a week, a grandchild after school, a nurse once a month, a neighbor who brings groceries. Handing each of them a key means the key list grows and never shrinks. A keypad lock gives each person their own code, active only on the days and hours they are expected, and switched off the day the arrangement ends without anyone returning anything. The lock’s history then shows whether the attendant arrived on time, which is often what an adult child living elsewhere actually wants to know.

We set those schedules with the resident, in plain words, and we write them down on a card that stays in the apartment: who has a code, what days it works, and how to switch it off from the keypad itself if the phone app is unavailable. For residents who do not use a smartphone at all, we choose locks that can be programmed fully from the keypad and that announce low batteries with a light and a tone you cannot miss.

A shop on the grounds opens to the public at the front but keeps a stock room, an office and a rear door to a shared corridor; a sports club has a front desk, a pool deck and a staff room; a school has classrooms, an office and a side door that should only open at dismissal. Access control here is mostly about deciding which of those doors unlock on a schedule, which open only with a credential, and who is told when one is forced or left open.

We prepare the consent package for you: a door-by-door drawing, the hardware cut sheets, how the lock behaves in a fire alarm and a power cut, where the power comes from, and a promise that the work is reversible. Management offices approve complete packages quickly; they stall on vague ones.

Rules that apply to Starrett City business doors

  • Free exit. NYC Building Code sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9 govern doors held by electric locks on the exit side: a door must open from inside without a key or special knowledge, and the electric lock must release on loss of power and, where a fire alarm exists, on alarm. We set every business door to meet that before we program a single credential.
  • Self-closing doors in residential buildings. HPD lists building entrance doors, stair doors, rubbish chute closet doors and doors from commercial space or garages to public halls among those that must be self-closing; a shop door that opens into a residential hall is one of them.
  • Tenant data. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021), described on HPD’s website, limits what owners of multiple dwellings may do with data from smart access systems. It reaches the owner’s systems, not a tenant’s own apartment lock or a shop’s back door, but we explain it to managers before any building-wide bid.
  • Landlord consent. Not a law but a lease term, and the most common reason a job here waits a week. Ask for it first.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Starrett City: Questions Answered

These are the questions residents, shopkeepers and program managers in and around the complex actually raise, grouped by the door they are about. The short answer comes first in each case.

The apartment door and the law

The state law says the lock I add can be no more than three inches around. Does a keypad deadbolt fit that rule?

The figure in section 51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law describes the lock installed in the door, and the part of a typical residential deadbolt that passes through the door is a cylinder well under that, while the keypad escutcheon on the outside face is larger. The statute does not say how to measure a modern keypad lock, and we are not going to pretend it does. What we do is show the management office the lock’s cut sheet, with the bore size and the outside dimensions, and ask for written approval of that model before we drill. In our experience the question that worries management is not the keypad’s face but whether they can still get in, which the duplicate key answers.

Management can ask for a duplicate key to my added lock. How does that work when the lock opens with a code?

Nearly every keypad deadbolt we fit still has a keyed cylinder behind a cover, for a dead battery or a failed motor, and that key is the duplicate the law refers to. We hand you both keys from the box with a written note of the lock’s make and model; you keep one and deliver the other when management asks. We do not give the code or the app login to anyone but you, because the statute speaks of a key, not of your personal codes, and the code list should stay yours.

Can I make the deadbolt I already have work from my phone without changing the key the office keeps?

Yes, with an adapter that mounts only on the inside of the door and turns the existing thumb-turn. The cylinder, the bolt and the owner’s key stay exactly as they were, nothing is drilled, and the outside of the door looks unchanged. The adapter must be one made for your deadbolt’s thumb-turn, it must not stop the door from latching, and it should come off at move-out with only the original screws reinstalled. It does not give you a keypad outside; for that, pair it with a small wireless keypad stuck beside the frame, if management allows anything on the corridor side.

My apartment door is a fire door. Will drilling it for a second lock void its rating?

It can if the work is done carelessly, which is why we do not do it blind. Fire-rated doors are tested and labeled as assemblies, and field changes beyond what the manufacturer and the listing allow can compromise the rating. On a labeled door we look first for an existing factory preparation for a second lock, then for a lock and template that fit the door maker’s published options, and we stop and ask management if neither applies. The inside-only smart adapter is often the cleanest answer on a labeled door precisely because it adds no holes.

The fire safety notice is posted on the inside of my door right where a smart lock would go. Where does the lock go instead?

The notice stays where it is and readable; the hardware moves. On most doors the lock’s inside housing occupies a band near the deadbolt and the notice can sit above or below it, so the fix is simply mounting height and housing size. If the notice is glued exactly where the lock must go, we leave it untouched, photograph the conflict and ask the management office to relocate it, because the Fire Code makes it the owner’s notice, not ours.

Could a lock I add cause a problem at the federal inspection of my apartment?

Only if it does not work or stops the door working. NSPIRE inspectors look at whether doors and their hardware are present and operable and whether fire doors close and latch as they should. A second lock that locks and unlocks smoothly and leaves the door self-closing gives an inspector nothing to write down; a dangling half-installed lock, a dead keypad or a door that no longer latches does. If an inspection is scheduled, check your added lock the week before: fresh batteries, a full close-and-latch test, and the duplicate key ready.

Living with the lock

My father’s hands shake and he struggles with small buttons. Which apartment lock is easiest for him?

One with large, well-spaced, backlit keys that click when pressed, a short code of four to six digits, and a lever or large knob on the inside rather than a small thumb-turn. Touchscreen keypads are harder for unsteady hands than physical buttons. A fob that he taps on the lock, worn on a lanyard or kept on his key ring, can be easier still, and some locks also accept a code typed on a phone by a relative who is with him. We let him try the lock before it goes on the door.

If my phone is lost or stolen, can whoever finds it open my apartment door?

Not if the lock and the phone are set up properly. The phone’s own screen lock is the first barrier, and the lock app should ask for the phone’s passcode or fingerprint again before it unlocks the door. If the phone goes missing anyway, log in to the lock account from another device and sign the lost phone out, or change the account password, and the phone’s key stops working. Keypad codes are unaffected, so you can still get in with your code. We set that up with you on the day and write down where the account is managed.

How will I know the batteries are running low before I am locked out in the hallway?

Good keypad locks warn for weeks before they fail: a flashing light and a distinctive beep each time you use the code, and a phone notification on connected models. If the batteries do die completely, most models have contacts underneath the keypad where a 9-volt battery held against them powers the lock long enough to enter the code, and the keyed cylinder remains as a last resort. We write the battery type and your last change date on the inside of the battery cover.

Shops, clubs and offices

Our shop’s back door opens onto a service corridor that other tenants of the center also use. How do we control our door without locking them out of the corridor?

Your reader goes on your door, not on the corridor. The corridor door stays under the landlord’s control and keeps working for everyone; your rear door gets a reader or keypad on the corridor side and free exit on the inside, so your staff badge in from the corridor and anyone in the shop can always leave. A door contact tells you if your rear door is left open, which in a shared corridor is the real risk, because a propped door lets anyone in the corridor into your stock room.

Our pool deck has its own gate from the locker rooms. How do we keep it locked whenever no lifeguard is on duty?

Put the gate on a schedule that only the guard’s credential can start. Outside posted pool hours the gate stays locked to members’ cards; during pool hours it unlocks only after a lifeguard badges in at the deck, and it relocks when the guard badges out or the session ends. Exit from the deck side stays free at all times. The log then records when each session opened and closed, which is the record a club wants if anyone ever asks.

Public Safety officers patrol past our shop at night. Can our door alarm send its alerts straight to them?

Not from equipment we install. The Department of Public Safety belongs to the owner of the complex, and we do not connect a tenant’s or shopkeeper’s system to the owner’s security operation. Your door alerts go to the people you choose, such as the owner of the business, a manager on call or a monitoring company you hire, and those people decide whether to call the patrol or 911. Give management an up-to-date after-hours contact list for the shop, so that if an officer notices a door ajar, they know whom to reach.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Starrett City

Our build environment could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches, so the claims below are the general answers that search summaries and home-improvement sites commonly give about apartment locks and building entry, set against what applies on this particular site. Each is followed by the local correction.

“Renters can’t change or add locks.”

This is the most common blanket statement about apartment security, and in New York it is wrong as stated. Section 51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law gives tenants of multiple dwellings the right to install their own additional lock in the apartment entrance door, separate from the owner’s lock, and voids any lease clause that charges for the privilege.

The right has conditions, and the summaries rarely mention them: the lock may be no larger than the statute’s three inches in circumference, the landlord can demand a duplicate key, and the exemption for buildings run by a municipal housing authority means the rule is not the same in every subsidized development in the city.

Starrett City is privately owned under state Mitchell-Lama supervision, not a NYCHA development, so a tenant here can add a lock. What a tenant cannot do is remove or replace the owner’s required deadbolt and chain guard, or alter the door so it no longer closes and latches.

“A smart lock is a five-minute DIY job.”

On a wooden house door with a standard bore, sometimes it is. Home-improvement guides and retailers’ pages tend to describe that case, and the videos make it look effortless.

A tower apartment door is more often a steel door in a steel frame that must close by itself and may carry a fire label. The bolt has to clear the frame without rubbing, the housing has to stay clear of the closer arm, the outside escutcheon has to sit flat on a door skin that may not be perfectly true, and the inside hardware has to leave the required Fire Safety Notice readable.

Most of our time on an apartment door goes into those checks, not into turning screws. The test that matters, a full swing from open with the latch clicking home on its own, is the one that DIY installations skip and inspectors notice.

“Starrett City runs on its own power plant, so outages don’t matter.”

The complex’s own power plant is a large part of its legend. Wikipedia says it opened in 1973 and kept the complex lit during the 1977 blackout, and the Spring Creek Sun’s anniversary story repeats that “Starrett has its own power-house.” Summaries and neighborhood sites still describe the plant as running today.

More recent sources describe a change. McGowan Builders, the construction manager, says a $25 million project replaced the 50-year-old central combined heat and power plant with eight modular boiler plants, one beside each section garage, supplying heat and hot water to all 46 buildings, and AERCO’s case study describes the same eight plants. Neither source says how the towers get their electricity now, and we could not confirm it.

For door hardware the lesson is the same either way. A battery keypad lock does not care where the building’s power comes from. A wired lock on a business door must be set to release or hold, as the code requires, when its power fails, and it needs a battery backup sized for the door, not an assumption that the lights never go out.

“Security guards make door hardware unnecessary.”

It is easy to read Starrett City’s history of a dedicated, armed public safety force and conclude that individual locks matter less here. The patrol is real, and Wikipedia credits it with the complex’s low crime rate in its early years.

But the warning the 75th Precinct gave residents in November 2024 was about the apartment door itself: impostors posing as delivery workers, utility workers or Spring Creek Towers staff who knock, talk their way in and steal. No patrol stands at 5,881 doors.

The precinct’s advice was to verify before opening, through the peephole and with a call to management. Equipment that makes verification easier, such as a clear viewer, a door camera answered from the couch, or codes so trusted helpers never need the door opened for them, supports that advice rather than replacing the patrol.

“A smart lock is the cheap way to secure an apartment.”

Retail prices for keypad deadbolts and smart adapters are in that range, and that is the figure summaries quote. It is a fair number for the hardware alone.

Installed access control on a business door is a different thing: a reader, a strike or mortise lock, a power supply, a controller, a request-to-exit device and cable, which on our Brooklyn list runs from $1,500 to $2,500 for one controlled door. A whole-building system for an owner runs into the tens of thousands.

For an apartment door, we do not publish a flat installed price, because the work depends on the door, its label and what management approves. We quote it in writing from your photos before anyone arrives.

“Any camera doorbell will work on an apartment door.”

Video doorbells are designed for house doors with a doorbell circuit or a flat wall beside the frame. An apartment door in a corridor usually has neither, and mounting on the corridor wall or frame is the owner’s decision, not the tenant’s.

A camera mounted in a common corridor also records neighbors walking past, which is why management offices restrict it and why we do not mount cameras in shared corridors for tenants.

The options that do work here are a digital viewer sized to the existing peephole hole, where management agrees, or a camera unit that sits inside the apartment and looks through the viewer, paired with a plug-in chime. Both answer the 75th Precinct’s advice without touching the corridor.

“Crime statistics tell you which system to buy.”

Neighborhood guides often lead with precinct crime figures and then recommend a product. The 75th Precinct covers all of East New York, a far larger and more varied area than the complex, so precinct-wide numbers say little about any one tower door.

What shapes a good choice here is the door: who owns it, what rules it falls under, who in the household needs to get through it, and who the occupant needs to keep out. Those questions have concrete answers for every apartment, shop and office in the complex.

We would rather spend the first conversation on those answers than on statistics. They decide whether you need a second lock, an inside adapter, a viewer, a set of scheduled codes, or a full wired system on a business door.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test the Door Before You Touch It

On an apartment door in a building like these, the first five minutes decide the job, and none of them involves the new lock. Before unpacking anything, we open the door all the way and let it go. If it does not close and latch on its own, that is the owner’s repair, not ours, and we say so before we start, photograph it and suggest the tenant reports it to management, because a lock fitted to a door that does not latch will be blamed for the problem it did not cause. Then we throw the owner’s deadbolt and listen: a bolt that scrapes is a frame that has moved, and a second bolt above it will scrape too unless the strike is placed for where the frame is now, not where it was in 1974. Then we look for a label on the hinge edge and for the Fire Safety Notice on the inside face, and only after all three checks do we choose between a second lock and an inside adapter. At the end we repeat the first test until the door latches every time.

FAQ

Starrett City Access Control FAQ

How much noise and mess should my neighbors expect while you drill a steel apartment door?
+

Very little. Drilling a bore through a steel door takes a few minutes of a drill’s noise, not hours, and the metal shavings are caught on a sheet and vacuumed before we leave. An inside-only adapter needs no drilling at all. We work within whatever hours management allows for contractors, keep the corridor clear so neighbors can pass, and never leave the door unable to lock while we step away.

How long does fitting a second lock or a smart adapter on an apartment door take, and do I have to be home?
+

An inside-only adapter usually takes under an hour and a second keypad deadbolt one to two hours, including the close-and-latch tests and setting your codes. You or an adult you choose needs to be there, because we set the codes with you and hand over the keys in person. We do not take keys from the management office or work in an empty apartment.

If I put my own smart lock on my door, can the management office see when I come and go?
+

Not from the lock. A lock you buy and register in your own account reports only to you, and we set it up under your email, not ours or the landlord’s. The management office can ask for a duplicate key to the added lock, but the law speaks of a key, not of your app or your entry history. The owner’s own systems, such as lobby fobs or cameras, are a separate matter governed by the owner’s policies and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law.

The chain guard on my door sits right where a second lock would go. Does one of them have to move?
+

The chain guard stays, because the housing code makes it the owner’s required hardware. We measure the gap between the chain track, the owner’s deadbolt and the peephole before choosing a position, and most doors leave room for a second lock a few inches above or below the chain. Where they would collide, the inside-only adapter on the existing deadbolt avoids the problem entirely, and we would rather recommend that than ask management to move its chain.

My mother’s home attendant comes from an agency that sends different aides. Should each aide get a code, or one code for the agency?
+

One code per aide is better if the agency can tell you the names in advance, because the lock’s history then shows who arrived and when. If the agency rotates staff at short notice, give the agency one code limited to the hours of care, change it whenever the agency changes, and ask the agency to tell you who is coming. Either way, the code should stop working the day the care ends.

Can I take my keypad lock with me when I move to another apartment?
+

Usually, yes, because it is your property. Before moving, ask management whether they want the bore left open, plated or fitted with a matching lock, and do what they ask in writing. Whether the lock fits the next door depends on that door’s thickness, its bore and backset, and whether it carries a fire label; send us photos of the new door and we will tell you before the move whether the lock can go straight back on.

Management is renovating buildings under its repair program. If my apartment door is ever replaced, what happens to the lock I added?
+

The 2021 state agreement requires about $140 million of repairs and upgrades by 2030, but we have not seen a published list of which parts of the buildings it covers, so we cannot say whether apartment doors are included. If your door is scheduled for replacement, ask management to return your lock to you before the old door goes, and have it refitted to the new door afterward with management’s approval, since a new labeled door may come with its own preparation for a second lock.

We bought one of the Nehemiah homes next to the complex. Is our house handled the same way as an apartment in the towers?
+

No. In a house you own, every door is yours, so there is no landlord approval and no duplicate-key question, and the work can be wired from the basement. The usual set is a keypad or video station at the front door, separate codes for a rental unit if the house has one, and a keypad on the door to the yard or garage. The only approval to look for is in any homeowners’ association rules or deed covenants that cover the outside of the house.
