The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need GeoVision Camera Installation

Long Island is not one neighborhood, and a GeoVision system that works well here has to be planned for the kind of place it is going into. According to the state's county profiles, Nassau County covers about 453 square miles with two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets, while Suffolk covers about 2,373 square miles and ten towns. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts about 477,860 housing units in Nassau and about 579,790 in Suffolk, and in both counties the large majority are one-unit detached houses: roughly 364,529 in Nassau and 463,095 in Suffolk. The same survey gives a median year built of 1955 for Nassau and 1970 for Suffolk. Put simply, most Long Island camera jobs are on detached houses with a driveway, a front door, a back door and a yard, built in the decades after the Second World War and changed many times since. A good GeoVision installation here is about covering those four things on a house whose walls, attic and floor were never planned for camera cable.

The postwar building boom explains a lot of what we find. Wikipedia's entry on Levitt & Sons records that the company broke ground on Levittown on July 1, 1947, and the development grew to about 17,447 houses by 1951, Cape Cods and then ranches on quarter-acre lots. An architecture firm's history of Levittown describes those ranches as built on concrete slabs, heated with hot-water radiant pipes laid in the slab, with an expandable attic and no garage. Encyclopedia.com notes Levitt switched from the Cape Cod model to the ranch in 1949, and a history of the town observes that so much remodeling has happened that very few original Capes remain. For camera cable this matters in a very practical way. There is no basement to run wire through, the floor may still carry heating pipes, and the attic of a house that has been expanded two or three times may now be split into separate spaces that do not connect. A GeoVision camera on the back corner of a Levittown-style ranch usually gets its cable through the attic and down inside an exterior wall or along the soffit, never through the slab.

The other reason Long Island owners ask about wired systems is what they keep reading in the news. CBS New York reported on a Long Island man who believes thieves jammed his home cameras so they could steal his personal watercraft from his driveway, and the security expert in that story gave blunt advice: "Any camera system that you have, hardline it." News 12 Long Island, covering burglars using Wi-Fi jammers in New Jersey, reported that police urge homeowners to hardwire their security systems rather than rely on Wi-Fi. GeoVision's current line is built around IP cameras on a network, and in our installs that network is a Cat6 cable from each camera back to a PoE switch or the recorder's own PoE ports. A jammer that blinds a Wi-Fi doorbell does nothing to a cable, and a camera fed with power over that cable does not die when its battery runs flat on a cold night.

The pattern of break-ins reported on the Island also shapes where the cameras go. In coverage of an international burglary crew, CBS New York reported that homes behind the Seawane Country Club in Hewlett Harbor had minimal lighting and that police believed the burglars came in from the rear of the property; the village's mayor said patrols were doubled and license plate readers added. Nassau's police commissioner, quoted in another report, said the group hit higher-end homes on both shores, naming Sands Point, Brookville, Great Neck and Woodsburgh. CBS also reported 21 burglaries in twelve months across Sea Cliff, Glenwood Landing and Glen Head with no arrests at the time. In October 2026 News 12 reported an arrest after a man allegedly went into a gated backyard on Melville Road in Farmingdale and forced his way in through the rear door, and a Patch story on a West Babylon break-in described the burglar getting in through a back window. Again and again the weak side is the back of the house: dark, out of sight of the street, reached across a yard. A GeoVision plan for a Long Island house almost always puts as much thought into the rear yard and the back door as into the front walk.

Then there is the equipment that is already on the wall. GeoVision has been a common brand with security dealers for a long time, and older recorders and video servers are still running in homes, shops and offices. In late 2024 the Shadowserver Foundation, as Security Affairs reported, observed a botnet exploiting a previously unknown flaw in end-of-life GeoVision devices, tracked as CVE-2024-11120 and rated 9.8 on the CVSS scale. BleepingComputer reported about 17,000 vulnerable GeoVision devices exposed on the internet, most of them in the United States. CISA later added CVE-2024-6047 and CVE-2024-11120 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and The Hacker News reported that federal agencies were required to apply fixes by May 28, 2025 or stop using the products. Because the affected models are past end of life, Bitdefender reported that no patch is coming and owners are told to replace them. If one of those boxes is sitting in a Massapequa basement or a Hauppauge equipment closet with its web page open to the internet, it is not just failing to protect the property; it may be helping someone attack other networks. That is why this page spends as much time on checking and replacing old GeoVision gear as on installing new cameras.

Businesses are part of the same story. CBS New York reported a run of nine burglaries in six months that hit delis, laundromats and gas stations across Suffolk County, with the suspects caught on various surveillance cameras. In September 2026 News 12 reported that the owner of Morning Delights in Uniondale received a motion alert from his security camera, went to the restaurant and found a burglary suspect there. Daily Voice reported in May 2026 that a Roslyn Heights homeowner watched two men on his camera system trying to break into a storage unit, and police tracked their car and arrested them. Each of those stories turns on the same thing: the camera saw something, the footage was usable, and someone was alerted or could hand video to police. A GeoVision system is only as good as its aim, its night picture, its recording and its remote access, and getting all four right on a Long Island strip-mall storefront is a different job from doing it on a waterfront house.

The Old GeoVision Box in the Closet: Which Long Island Systems Need to Be Isolated, Reset or Replaced

Most of the GeoVision calls we expect from Nassau and Suffolk are not about a bare house with no cameras. They are about a system someone else installed, sometimes a decade or more ago, that still records but that nobody fully understands. The original installer may have closed up shop, the password may be on a sticky note, and the remote viewing may have been set up by opening ports on the router. Before 2024 that was an annoyance. Since the botnet reports and the CISA listing, it is a question worth answering properly. What "end of life" means for a camera owner

When a manufacturer declares a product end of life, it stops writing software updates for it. For a doorbell or a recorder, that would be a nuisance; for a device that sits on your network and answers requests from anywhere, it is the real problem. CVE-2024-11120, as Security Affairs described it, is a pre-authentication command injection: an attacker does not need your password to make the device run commands. CISA's listing, reported by BeyondMachines, says both flaws let unauthenticated remote attackers run arbitrary system commands. Bitdefender reported that GeoVision will not patch these models and that owners should replace them. That means there is no setting, firmware file or clever configuration that makes an affected end-of-life unit safe on the open internet. The fix is to take it off the internet and, sooner rather than later, out of service.

Isolate, reset, replace: in that order

The recommended steps reported by BeyondMachines are to isolate affected devices from the internet, perform factory resets, and ideally replace them with supported models. On a Long Island job we follow that order and add one step at the front: save the evidence and the settings. Before we disconnect anything we photograph the labels, write down which cameras feed which inputs, and export any footage the owner needs, for example from a recent incident. Then we remove the device's path to the internet, which usually means deleting port forwards on the router and turning off any automatic port opening. If the device has to keep running for a few days while new equipment arrives, it runs isolated. A factory reset clears anything an attacker may have left in its settings, but on an end-of-life unit it does not remove the underlying flaw, so we treat the reset as a stopgap and the replacement as the real fix.

What can usually be kept

An old GeoVision system is rarely all scrap. The most valuable thing in it is often the cable. A Cat5e or Cat6 run that was pulled into the attic of a split-level fifteen years ago, or along the J-hooks of a warehouse ceiling in Hauppauge, can carry a new PoE camera perfectly well if it tests good end to end. Camera brackets and junction boxes on stucco or cedar shingle are sometimes reusable too. Hard drives are a mixed case: the footage on them may matter, but drives that have been spinning in a hot closet for years are not what we want new recordings sitting on. And cameras from other makers that were plugged into the old system may carry over to a current GeoVision recorder if they support ONVIF, which GeoVision says its GV-NVR works with across a wide range of third-party IP cameras. We test each piece and give you a written list of what stays, what goes and why.

Remote viewing done without opening the front door

The single most common weakness we expect in older Long Island systems is how the remote viewing was set up. Many installers simply forwarded ports from the router to the recorder so the owner could see the cameras from work or from Florida. That is the open door the botnet reports describe. GeoVision's current GV-Eye installation guide describes a different path through its GV-Relay service, which by default uses port 443 and moves to port 9443 if 443 is already taken by something else such as WebCam SSL or RTSP. We set remote viewing up on supported software with strong, unique passwords and nothing exposed that does not need to be, and we show you in the GV-Eye app, which the App Store lists as a free iOS app for live view, playback and snapshots on GeoVision devices, that it still works from outside the house.

Walk the property the way a stranger would

The most useful thing you can do before we arrive is walk your own property from the street to the back door at night and notice what a stranger would notice. Where could someone stand without being seen from the road? Which gate is unlocked? Which side of the house has no light at all? Where do delivery drivers leave packages? Where do cars park overnight? On the Island, the answers are usually the rear door, a side gate between houses, the driveway and the front step. Write them down. The camera list follows from those answers, not the other way around, and it saves paying for a camera on a wall nobody would ever approach.

Think about where the recorder should live

GeoVision recorders and PoE switches need a dry, ventilated, reasonably cool place with power and a connection to the router. In a slab ranch that might be a closet off the hall or a shelf in the garage; in a colonial, the basement next to the internet line is often ideal, unless that basement takes water. After the August 2024 storms, when the state's Department of Financial Services reported the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County and over 2,000 residents and business owners with flood damage, we stopped assuming a basement shelf is a safe place on low ground. If water has ever come into your basement, tell us, and we will put the recorder higher.

Check the neighbor's side of every view

Before you decide a camera should look down the side yard, stand where it would go and look. If the view takes in a neighbor's windows, pool or fenced yard, the camera may still be fine, but it needs privacy masking blocking those areas. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a, as one news explainer summarized it, makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy. In Shirley, police told CBS New York in a neighbor dispute that a backyard visible to the public was fair game, which shows how much depends on the exact view. We aim and mask with you so the camera watches your property and the public approach, not the house next door.

GeoVision Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

A GeoVision camera is the same camera in Bethpage or Bridgehampton. What changes is the route its cable takes, the wall it is fixed to, the weather it faces and who has to agree before we drill. These are the property types the census, local histories and news reports describe on the Island, and how each changes the install.

Slab Ranches and Capes

The Levittown model and its many relatives in Hicksville, Massapequa and beyond: no basement, heating pipes possibly still in the slab, attics that may have been split by later dormers and additions. Cable goes up and over, never down.

Colonials and Split-Levels

Two-story and split-level houses from the 1950s through the 1970s, many with a basement or half-basement and a garage. More routes for cable, more corners to cover, and often an existing alarm or camera system to work around.

North Shore Estates and Large Lots

Gold Coast properties, where more than 500 mansions were built from the early 1890s, plus the subdivisions carved from them. Long driveways, gates, outbuildings and distances that can exceed what a single copper run can carry.

South Shore and Waterfront Homes

Wood-frame and cedar-shingle houses near canals and bays, docks and boat lifts, salt air and storm exposure. Corrosion-resistant hardware, sealed connections and cameras that look at the water side as well as the street.

Seasonal and East End Homes

Houses that sit empty for months, where remote viewing, recording reliability and power backup matter more than anywhere else, because nobody is there to notice a camera going dark.

Condos, HOAs and Apartment Complexes

Shared buildings and garden complexes, including new rental buildings near LIRR hubs. Board approval, insurance certificates, common-area coverage and clear rules about who can see the footage.

Slab houses: getting cable from the attic to every corner

In a slab ranch the attic is the backbone of the camera system. We enter it, find the top plates of the exterior walls, and plan each camera so its cable drops the shortest distance to its mount. On many ranches the cleanest camera position is just under the soffit at a corner, with the cable coming straight out of the attic through a sealed hole in the soffit or the fascia, never left hanging on the siding. Where a camera has to sit lower, for example beside a back door, we fish the cable down inside the exterior wall cavity if it is open, or run it in a neat exterior raceway painted to match if it is blocked by fire blocking or insulation. What we never do is drill into the slab. The architecture history of Levittown describes hot-water heating pipes laid in those floors, and even where a system has been replaced, the pipes or their remains may still be there. Expanded houses add another wrinkle: a dormer or a second-story addition can split the attic into sections, so one corner of the house may only be reachable from a different hatch, or not at all. We look before we quote.

Colonials and split-levels: two routes, more corners

The two-story colonials and split-levels that make up much of the Island's 1950s, 1960s and 1970s housing usually give us both a basement and an attic. That makes cable easier, but the houses have more corners, more doors and often a garage that needs its own coverage. In a split-level the half-levels mean the floor of one room is the ceiling of another, and the shortest route may run through a closet rather than a wall. Many of these houses also carry the remains of earlier security systems: alarm wire to every window, a coax cable from an old analog camera, perhaps a GeoVision recorder from the 2010s. We test and reuse what makes sense, label what we leave, and take out only what you ask us to remove.

Estates and long lots: when copper runs out

A Cat6 run for a PoE camera has a practical limit of about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from the switch to the camera. Waterfront and storm exposure

South Shore houses near canals and bays live with salt air and storms, and the hardware has to match. Seasonal homes and the empty-house problem

An East End house that sits closed from October to May needs a camera system that keeps itself honest. The camera does no good if the recorder froze in December and nobody noticed until June. We put the recorder and switch on a battery backup, set the system so it can send an alert when a camera loses its picture, and walk the owner through checking camera health from the GV-Eye app. Power is the other risk. ABC7 reported that during Isaias in August 2020 almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million customers lost power at the peak. A battery backup will not run a system through a multi-day outage, but it rides through short drops and lets the recorder shut down cleanly instead of crashing, which protects the drive and the settings.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • State licensing. New York General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems; the statute exempts passive, non-detecting devices. We work under NYS license #12000287431 either way, because most business camera systems end up tied to alarms or detection.
  • County licensing. Suffolk County Code Chapter 563 requires a county license for regulated trades, with restricted licenses for limited areas of work, and does not apply in towns or villages that run their own licensing. Nassau's Office of Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement contractors and requires fingerprinting. Ask any installer which licenses they hold for your address.
  • Permits. Sources disagree. One installer says standard camera installs in Nassau and Suffolk typically need no municipal permit; another source points to state code permit rules for low-voltage work and says most Nassau towns and villages enforce permits; the Town of Hempstead code requires a permit for work that must conform to the Uniform Code. We check with the town or village for the exact address when the job involves new wiring through fire-rated walls or new electrical work.
  • Workplace cameras. New York Labor Law 203-c bars video in restrooms, changing rooms and similar spaces. We do not install cameras in those areas and we mask views that come close to them.
  • Audio. New York consent rules for audio recording mean we leave microphones off on business cameras unless you have a clear, lawful reason and notice in place.
  • Exits and fire-rated walls. Cable through fire-rated walls or ceilings has to be firestopped, and cameras or brackets must not block exit signs or paths. We check both before we drill.
  • Police registries. The Suffolk County Police camera registry lets businesses and residents register where their cameras are, and the department says it does not get access to the cameras. Your Questions Answered

GeoVision Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Old GeoVision equipment and security

Which GeoVision flaws does CISA say attackers are using right now, and what can they do with them?

The two flaws are CVE-2024-6047 and CVE-2024-11120. CISA added both to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and BeyondMachines reported that they let unauthenticated remote attackers run arbitrary system commands on affected end-of-life GeoVision devices. Security Affairs described CVE-2024-11120 as a pre-authentication command injection with a severity score of 9.8 out of 10, first seen exploited by a botnet that the Shadowserver Foundation observed, and BleepingComputer reported that the botnet was installing Mirai malware. In plain terms, an exposed device can be taken over without its password and used to attack other systems. If yours is on the list, it needs to come off the internet now.

The recorder in our Massapequa basement says GeoVision on the front. How do we know if it is one of the affected models?

Read the model label, usually on the back or the underside, and compare it with the models named in the reports: GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3, and GV-LX4C V2 and V3. If you cannot find the label or the box is in a tight spot, send us a photo of the front and back panels and we will identify it. A model that is not on that list is not automatically safe; any device that is past end of life or whose admin page is reachable from outside the house deserves a look. We check the model, the firmware version and the router settings together, because the risk comes from all three.

Can we just unplug our old GeoVision unit from the internet and keep recording locally?

As a short-term measure, yes, and it is the first step the published guidance recommends. Cutting the device off from the internet removes the path attackers use, and many recorders keep recording cameras on a local network without it. The trade-offs are that you lose remote viewing, an end-of-life unit will never receive fixes, and it is still running unsupported software on your network. We treat isolation as a bridge to replacement, not a permanent answer, and we can set up a supported recorder so you get remote viewing back safely.

Mixing cameras and brands

We want to keep a few non-GeoVision cameras. Why does the GeoVision recorder refuse to add them through ONVIF?

GeoVision lists this among its own support questions, so it happens often. ONVIF is a common standard, but cameras implement it differently. In the field, the usual causes are that ONVIF is switched off on the camera, the camera wants a separate ONVIF user and password, the camera's clock is far out of step with the recorder, or the camera's firmware supports only part of the standard. GeoVision also publishes a method for testing third-party cameras with GV-VMS. We check each camera's settings, update firmware where the maker supports it, test it on the recorder, and tell you plainly if a particular camera will not cooperate.

Do GeoVision's NVR and hybrid DVR speak ONVIF, so a mixed system is possible at all?

GeoVision's product page says GV-NVR works with a wide range of third-party IP cameras via ONVIF, and the company's support FAQ addresses ONVIF compliance for both GV-NVR and GV-Hybrid DVR. In practice, that means a mixed system is possible, but compatibility is decided camera by camera rather than brand by brand. Basic live view and recording usually work; features like motion analytics or two-way audio may not carry across. We test each camera before we promise anything and list which features work on which camera in the handover notes.

Remote viewing and storage

How many cameras can we watch at once from our phones when we are away?

It depends on the recorder model, the software version and your internet upload speed, and GeoVision answers the channel question model by model in its support FAQ rather than with one number. On a Long Island home system the practical limit is usually the upload speed of the home's internet, not the software, especially if several family members are viewing at once. We set the recorder to send a lighter stream to phones so more cameras load smoothly, test it from outside the house on mobile data, and tell you what to expect before you leave for the winter.

Does GeoVision care which hard drives go in the recorder?

GeoVision maintains a list of tested hard drives for its systems and answers that question in its FAQ, which tells you the company does care. Recorders write video around the clock, and drives built for that kind of continuous writing last longer than ordinary desktop drives. We check the drive against GeoVision's tested list for your model, size it for the number of cameras and the days of recording you want, and set the recorder to warn you when a drive starts to fail. In a hot garage or a closet with no air, we also look at where the recorder sits, because heat shortens drive life.

Our system has not been updated in years. How do you bring GV-System or GV-VMS up to date safely?

GeoVision publishes upgrade steps for GV-System in its support FAQ, and the order matters. We first confirm the hardware is still supported, because an end-of-life unit cannot be fixed by an update. Then we back up the configuration and export any footage you need, check that the new version supports every camera on the system, install the update, and test each channel, recording schedule and remote connection afterward. If an update would drop support for a camera you rely on, we tell you before we start, not after.

Wired, wireless and the property

Is a hard-wired GeoVision system really harder to beat than Wi-Fi cameras on Long Island?

Against jamming, yes. CBS New York reported a Long Island man who believes thieves jammed his Wi-Fi cameras before stealing a personal watercraft from his driveway, and News 12 reported police advice to hardwire rather than rely on Wi-Fi. A PoE camera on a Cat6 cable sends its video down the wire, so a radio jammer does not touch it, and it takes its power from the same cable, so there is no battery to die. Wired cameras can still be covered, moved or damaged, which is why we mount them out of easy reach and set alerts for lost picture.

How many GeoVision cameras does a typical Nassau house actually need?

For most Nassau ranches, Capes and colonials, four to six cameras cover the essentials: the front door and walk, the driveway, the back door and rear yard, and one or two side gates or blind corners. Larger houses, corner lots and properties with a detached garage, pool or long driveway need more. The reported break-ins that came in from dark rear yards are why we never skimp on the back of the house. We walk the property with you and count cameras from the approaches, not from a package size.

Our dock and boathouse are well beyond the reach of a normal cable. Can GeoVision cover them?

Yes, with the right backbone. Copper Cat6 for PoE cameras is limited to about 100 meters per run, so a dock or boathouse beyond that gets either fiber from the house with a small switch at the far end, or a second recorder in the outbuilding linked back to the main one. The Sands Point waterfront project another installer describes used a 32-channel recorder, fiber to a boathouse recorder and marine-grade housings on the water-facing cameras. We plan the route, coordinate any trenching, and specify sealed, corrosion-resistant hardware for the cameras facing the water.

Someone covered our cameras with tape before breaking in. How do you stop that from working next time?

Daily Voice reported exactly that in North Valley Stream in May 2026: cameras repositioned and lenses taped while the family was away. Three things help. Mount cameras higher and out of reach of a person standing on the ground or a trash can. Overlap views so one camera sees the next camera's mounting spot, which means anyone tampering is recorded doing it. And turn on tamper or video-loss alerts so the owner's phone gets a warning the moment a picture goes dark. Central Islip's widely reported doorbell camera destroyed with a knife is another reason we prefer higher, harder-to-reach cameras over doorbell-height units for primary coverage.

FAQ

Long Island GeoVision Camera Installation FAQ

What does a GeoVision camera system cost to put in on Long Island?
+

It depends on the number and type of cameras, the recorder size, the cable routes your house allows and the county the address is in. We publish separate Nassau and Suffolk price lists and a Long Island price page where you can build a system and see the total. Your written quote uses only lines from the list for your county, and nothing is added on the day unless you agree to it.

Do you put GeoVision systems into businesses as well as homes on the Island?
+

Yes. We install and take over GeoVision systems in shops, delis, restaurants, gas stations, offices, warehouses, flex buildings, schools, churches and HOAs across Nassau and Suffolk. Business jobs are planned camera by camera with the owner, scheduled around opening hours, and usually include a certificate of insurance for the landlord or managing agent.

Will my town or village on Long Island want a permit before cameras go up?
+

For most simple residential camera jobs, permits do not come up, but sources disagree and rules vary by town and village. The Town of Hempstead requires a permit for work that must meet the Uniform Code, and some sources say most Nassau towns and villages enforce low-voltage permit rules. When a job involves new electrical circuits, poles, fire-rated walls or a commercial fit-out, we check with the town or village for your exact address before starting.

Do you travel all the way out to the North Fork and the Hamptons?
+

Yes. We cover Nassau and all of Suffolk, including the North Fork, the South Fork and the seasonal communities of the East End. For distant addresses we plan the visit carefully so the cable, cameras and recorder are all on the van, and for seasonal homes we can schedule work while the house is open and set up remote checks for the months it is closed.

What should a Nassau or Suffolk owner check before hiring someone to install cameras?
+

Ask which licenses the installer holds for your address, including the New York Department of State security license and any county license, and ask for proof of insurance. Ask whether the quote lists every camera, the recorder model, storage and cable routes in writing. Ask how remote viewing will be set up and whether anything will be opened to the internet. And ask what happens to any old equipment, especially older GeoVision units that may be end of life.

Is a licensed installer legally required for security cameras in New York State?
+

New York General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, with an exemption for passive, non-detecting devices. A camera system with motion detection or alarm ties is more likely to fall under it. Counties add their own rules for some trades. We work under NYS license #12000287431 so the question does not arise.

How long does a typical GeoVision install take at a Long Island house?
+

A four to six camera system on a house with open attic access is usually a single working day, including cable, mounting, recorder setup and the phone walkthrough. Larger houses, estates with outbuildings, sealed attic sections and takeovers of old systems take longer, and we say so on the quote. We book the visit, or a phone review of your photos, within 2 hours of your request.

Do we have to put up signs saying cameras are recording?
+

For a private home we did not find a New York rule requiring camera signs, though many owners post them because a visible notice discourages people. Businesses and workplaces should take care: cameras may never cover restrooms or changing areas under Labor Law 203-c, and audio recording raises consent questions. If you have employees or tenants, we suggest checking with your attorney about notice, and we can place signs where you want them.

Does it matter whether an installer is certified by GeoVision?
+

What matters most is whether the installer knows the current GeoVision products, can tell supported equipment from end-of-life equipment, and sets up recording and remote access safely. Ask any installer how they would handle an older GeoVision recorder, how they configure GV-Eye remote viewing, and how they test third-party cameras over ONVIF. Clear answers to those questions tell you more than a logo.

Can a GeoVision recorder take over the cameras from the system we already have?
+

Often, yes. GeoVision says its GV-NVR works with a wide range of third-party IP cameras through ONVIF, and GV-VMS records GeoVision and third-party IP devices. Older analog cameras on coax are a different case and may need replacing or a hybrid recorder. We test each existing camera on the new recorder before quoting so you know which ones carry over.

Should we register our cameras with the Suffolk County Police?
+

That is your choice. The Suffolk County Police camera registry records where your cameras are so detectives can ask you to check footage after an incident; the department says it does not get access to the cameras, and retrieval is at the owner's convenience. Suffolk also runs an Emergency Remote Camera Access program that you can opt into or out of at any time. Some Nassau villages, such as Garden City, run their own voluntary registries.

Will our GeoVision cameras keep recording when the power goes out after a storm?
+

Only if the recorder and the PoE switch are on a battery backup, and only for as long as the battery lasts. A backup rides through short drops and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in a long outage, which protects the drive and settings. Isaias left almost half of PSEG's customers in the dark at its peak, so for seasonal homes and waterfront houses we treat battery backup as a strong recommendation, and we place it above any flood level.

Can you aim cameras so they don't record into the neighbor's yard?
+

Yes. We set each camera's view with you on site and use the recorder's privacy masking to black out neighbors' windows, pools and fenced yards that fall inside a view. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a bars video surveillance where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy, so masking is part of every install where houses sit close together, which on much of Long Island means most of them.
