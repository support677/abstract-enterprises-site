The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Properties Need NVR Installation

Most advice about network video recorders assumes the recorder is safe wherever you put it, and that it only needs to keep video until somebody notices a problem. Ulster County breaks both assumptions. The county is drained by fast creeks coming off the Catskills and the Shawangunks — the Esopus, the Stony Clove, the Rondout, the Wallkill — and its oldest commercial district in Kingston sits on a tidal waterfront. At the same time, the businesses that now rely on recording most heavily are increasingly the ones with a regulator watching how long the footage lasts. A recorder here has to be both dry and patient.

Water first. When Hurricane Irene crossed the county at the end of August 2011, almost every part of Ulster received more than six inches of rain and Slide Mountain recorded 11.53 inches. The Upper Esopus gauge at Coldbrook crested at 23.34 feet, 1.4 feet above its previous record, and record flood levels were reported on the Stony Clove in Phoenicia and the Rondout at Rosendale. The county’s emergency management director told Hudson Valley One that the Esopus crested 12 feet above flood stage, the Rondout nine and the Wallkill six; more than 80 roads were impassable and the Coldbrook Bridge in Shandaken was swept away. Recorders in those valleys were, and still are, very often in the cellar — the one place in a building guaranteed to meet the water first.

Kingston’s waterfront adds a second, slower kind of water. The city’s Flooding Task Force studied severe flooding on the Rondout and Hudson River shoreline after Hurricanes Irene and Sandy and Tropical Storm Lee; during Sandy, the tidal surge in the Hudson came through the exterior basement door of the Rondout II Lighthouse and flooded its basement. City planning documents for the Rondout shoreline give a 1-percent-annual-chance flood elevation of 8.2 feet and project up to 36 inches of sea-level rise by 2060, and in 2024 the city won a federal grant to raise East Strand and North Street against frequent tidal flooding. For a shop, restaurant or apartment building on the Strand, the question is not whether the recorder should leave the basement, but how high it must go.

Then the calendar. New York’s adult-use cannabis rules require a licensed dispensary to keep all recordings for at least 60 days, to keep the video system running for at least eight hours in a power outage, and to alert the licensee within five minutes when the security system fails. A Hudson Valley One guide to the county’s dispensaries listed five of them in Ulster — on Route 28 in the Town of Ulster, in New Paltz, two in Kingston and one in Stone Ridge — and every one of them runs a recorder that must meet those numbers. Kingston’s own police street cameras, by comparison, overwrite their video every 14 to 21 days, according to the department. The difference between three weeks and two months of video is the difference between one drive and several, and between a recorder bought off a shelf and one specified on paper.

Ulster facts that shape every recorder plan

  • Irene set records. Coldbrook on the Upper Esopus crested at 23.34 ft on August 28, 2011, 2.76 ft above its 2005 crest; the Stony Clove at Phoenicia and the Rondout at Rosendale both reached record flood levels.
  • Kingston’s waterfront floods from the river, not just the creeks. Sandy’s surge flooded the Rondout II Lighthouse basement; the city’s shoreline plan uses an 8.2 ft base flood elevation and up to 36 in of sea-level rise by 2060.
  • Sixty days is a legal minimum for dispensaries. 9 NYCRR 125.3 sets 60-day retention, eight hours of outage operation and a five-minute failure alert for adult-use retail; the medical cannabis rules in 9 NYCRR 113.14 carry the same three numbers.
  • Old buildings dominate. About one Ulster home in four — 21,829 of them — was built in 1939 or earlier, by Census-based figures reported by Stacker, so stone and brick cellars are the norm, not the exception.
  • A million square feet of former IBM space is being re-let. iPark 87, the 200-plus-acre former IBM Kingston campus, now holds Ulster BOCES and Archtop Fiber on its East Campus, with more tenants sought.
  • The county licenses electrical work. Ulster County Code Chapter 185 defines electrical work to include wiring for signalling, communication, alarm and data transmission, which matters when a recorder needs a new circuit.

Owners who call about the recorder usually want the cameras looked at too; our Ulster residential camera team handles camera layouts, and businesses that sell regulated products often pair the recorder with door access control so the door log and the video tell the same story.

Ulster Recorder Wet, Full or Silent?

Three calls come in from Ulster more than any other: the recorder sat in water after a storm, the recorder is full and only holds a fraction of the days the owner expected, or the recorder stopped and nobody knew. Ring (845) 640-3835 and we book the next open window; in most cases a recorder is writing video again within one to two hours of the technician arriving, with any drive, power or relocation work finished then or on a second visit. If cameras rather than the recorder are the problem, our Ulster camera repair crew takes the job.

Before anyone arrives: what to do and what not to do

  1. If the recorder got wet, leave it off. Do not power up a recorder that has stood in water to “see if it works”; switching it on with moisture inside is the surest way to lose the drive. Unplug it at the outlet only if you can reach the plug without standing in water.
  2. Note what you need to keep. Write down the date and time of anything you may need on video. If the drive can be recovered, we go straight to those hours first.
  3. Check how many days it is actually holding. On a working recorder, open playback and scroll back to the earliest recording. If that is far fewer days than you expected, the drive is too small for the cameras’ settings, or a drive has dropped out.
  4. Look for a drive error. Most recorders list each drive as normal, unformatted or faulty. A faulty drive in a two-drive recorder can halve its retention without any other warning.
Ulster Towns

Eight Ulster Places, Eight Different Recorder Rooms

The right room for a recorder in a Rondout storefront would be the wrong one in a Woodstock studio or an Ellenville mill building. These are the places we plan for most often and what each one changes about the box that keeps the video.

The Rondout and East Strand, Kingston

Tidal water, a mapped flood elevation and old brick buildings with low cellars. Recorders go to the first floor or above, never the basement, and the switch that feeds the cameras is mounted high on the wall rather than on the floor of a utility room.

Uptown Kingston and the Stockade

Dense 18th- and 19th-century buildings in a city historic district where outside changes visible from the street are reviewed. Recorders sit in back offices or upstairs storerooms; cable goes up inside, and anything new on a facade is planned with the district in mind.

Town of Ulster and Route 9W

Big-box retail, car lots, offices and the iPark 87 campus. Larger camera counts, longer runs and tenants who share buildings, so recorder choice turns on channel count, rack space and who controls the network closet.

New Paltz, Main Street and Route 299

A college village with more than 7,000 SUNY students, a dense Main Street of food and retail, and one of the county’s dispensaries. Recorders here often serve several businesses in one building and must be kept away from tenants who share the basement.

Saugerties Village and Route 32

Partition Street shops, older houses and the Esopus running through the village to the Hudson. Village police logs show commercial alarm calls at local stores; a recorder that holds enough days matters most when a break-in is found days later.

Woodstock and the Route 212 corridor

Galleries, studios, music rooms and houses tucked into wooded hillsides. Dust from kilns and woodshops and the need to show artwork, not visitors’ faces, shape where the recorder goes and how its cameras are masked.

Ellenville and the Route 209 valley

A canal-era village of about 4,200 people at the junction of Route 52 and US 209, between the Shawangunk Ridge and the Catskills, with old factory and resort buildings. Long, multi-storey masonry buildings mean long cable paths and careful recorder placement.

Phoenicia, Mount Tremper and Route 28

Creek-side hamlets where Irene set flood records, and where mountains still leave mobile dead spots along Routes 28 and 212. Recorders stay well above any floor that has taken water and are set to alert by whatever connection the building has.

Building Specialists

NVR Placement for Ulster County’s Architecture

Ulster’s buildings span four centuries, and each type hides a different threat to the recorder. These are the six we meet most, with the room we choose in each.

Stone houses and fieldstone cellars

Dutch and Huguenot-era stone houses and later farmhouses with fieldstone or bluestone foundations. Cellars stay cool but damp, walls sweat in summer, and some take water in spring. The recorder goes upstairs; at most a switch stays below, raised on the wall.

Kingston brick commercial blocks

Two- to four-storey brick buildings in Uptown and on Broadway, with shops below and apartments or offices above. Back offices and upper storerooms make good recorder rooms; we use existing chases and stairwells to bring cable up rather than across the street front.

Waterfront buildings on the Rondout

Brick and frame buildings near the Strand with low basements close to tidal water. We treat the basement as a no-equipment zone, set the recorder at least a full storey above it and put the camera switch on the same floor as the recorder when we can.

Mill and factory buildings

Long masonry buildings with concrete or heavy timber floors, from Ellenville’s knife-making past to converted industrial space around Kingston. Cable paths are long, floors are hard to drill, and a switch per floor with a single uplink to the recorder is usually the tidy answer.

Former corporate campuses

iPark 87 and other large commercial sites where a tenant leases one suite in a building the landlord runs. The recorder belongs in the tenant’s own lockable space, and any use of the landlord’s riser or network closet is agreed in writing first.

Studios, galleries and workshops

Woodstock and Saugerties studios, kiln rooms, woodshops and gallery spaces. Dust and heat are the enemies here, so the recorder sits in a closed office or house room away from the work, never on a bench where sawdust or clay dust can reach its fan.

How we fit a recorder into these buildings

  • Height before convenience. In any building that has taken water, the recorder’s shelf is set above the highest mark the owner remembers, and higher still near the Rondout’s mapped flood elevation. A wall-mounted bracket or a short rack beats a floor.
  • Air, not a sealed box. Recorders and drives make heat. A closed cabinet needs vents or a fan; a closet needs its door undercut or louvred. In damp rooms we add a small dehumidifier near the equipment rather than sealing it in.
  • A switch can stay where the cables end. When camera cables already run to a cellar, we leave a small switch there, raised on the wall, and run one network cable or fibre up to the recorder’s new home. Only the switch is then at risk, and it is cheap to replace.
  • Masonry is drilled once. Through stone or brick we core one sleeve per wall and pull every cable through it, sealed against water and insects, rather than drilling a fresh hole for each camera.
  • Every floor gets a label. In long or tall buildings each switch, patch point and cable is labelled to match the recorder’s channel map, so a future technician can trace a camera without opening walls.
  • Dust stays out. Near workshops and kilns the recorder gets a filtered location or a cabinet with a dust filter, and we note on the handover sheet when the filter should be cleaned.
Regulated Retention

When the State Sets the Retention: Recorders for Ulster Dispensaries

Most camera owners choose how long to keep video. A licensed cannabis dispensary does not. Section 125.3 of the state’s adult-use cannabis regulations spells out what the video system has to do, and nearly every requirement lands on the recorder rather than the cameras. We read the rule line by line before specifying a dispensary system, and the table below shows how each line becomes a recorder decision. It is our reading of the published regulation, not legal advice; your licence documents and the Office of Cannabis Management have the final word.

What 9 NYCRR 125.3 saysWhat it means for the recorder
All recordings retained for at least 60 daysDrive space is calculated for 60 days of every camera at the settings actually used, plus headroom. On most dispensary systems that means two or more drives, and a recorder with enough bays to hold them.
Longer retention while an investigation or proceeding is pendingThe recorder must be able to lock or export specific footage so it is not overwritten when the 60-day cycle reaches it. We set this up and show staff how to use it.
Cameras on safes, vaults, sales areas, entries, exits, each point of sale and any parking lot; all areas except where consumers consumeCamera count is set by the floor plan, not by budget, and the recorder’s channel count follows. A small shop can still need sixteen channels.
Able to produce a clear colour still photo from any camera, live or recordedCameras recorded at a resolution and bit rate that stand up to a still frame, and a recorder whose playback and export can save a still on demand.
Remote access or login credentials for immediate viewing by the Office on request; unaltered copies on requestA dedicated viewer account, a secure route for remote viewing that does not expose the recorder to the open internet, and an export method that produces untouched originals.
Failure notification to the licensee’s representative within five minutes, by phone, email or textRecorder health alerts — video loss, drive error, recorder offline — switched on, tested and routed to named people, plus an outside watchdog that notices if the recorder itself goes silent.
System operational for at least eight hours in a power outageBattery or generator support for the recorder, every PoE switch, the cameras they power and the network gear that carries alerts, sized and tested for eight hours.
Storage media secured against tampering or theftThe recorder in a locked room or locked cabinet, with its own camera on it, and admin rights limited to named managers.

The eight-hour rule changes the power plan more than owners expect. A recorder on its own draws little; sixteen cameras with infrared lighting, two PoE switches and a modem draw a great deal more, and a typical desktop battery unit that would carry a home recorder for twenty minutes will not come close. For dispensaries we total the actual load and choose between a larger battery system and a generator with a short battery bridge, then prove it with a timed test before opening day. The same thinking applies to medical cannabis sites: 9 NYCRR 113.14, the medical rule, uses the same 60-day, eight-hour and five-minute figures.

Rules change, and nothing here replaces your licence documents. Our part is technical: retention, power, alerts and access set to the published numbers and written down so an inspector can see how each is met. Businesses bound by insurer or contract retention terms get the same treatment.

Compliance callouts for Ulster business owners

  • State low-voltage licensing. Video surveillance installation in New York falls under the Department of State’s security and fire alarm installer licensing; our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.
  • County electrical licensing. Ulster County Code Chapter 185 governs electrical licensing in the county and defines electrical work to include wiring for signalling, communication, alarm and data transmission. New outlets, circuits and generator connections for a recorder are done by a licensed electrician with the local permit.
  • Kingston’s historic districts. In the Stockade, Rondout–West Strand, Chestnut Street and Fair Street districts, changes to a building’s outside appearance visible from a street or alley need the city’s preservation review; interior recorder work does not.

  • Tax credit. The state’s Commercial Security Tax Credit offers eligible small retailers with 50 or fewer employees up to $3,000 per location toward security equipment, including cameras; ask your accountant whether a recorder project qualifies.
Your Questions Answered

NVR Installation in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the recorder questions Ulster owners put to us that we do not hear elsewhere in the valley. Each answer gives the short version first.

Water, damp and the recorder’s shelf

Does the sea-level rise Kingston is planning for change where a Rondout business should put its recorder today?

For the recorder itself, only a little; for the cable and the room around it, yes. City planning documents for the Rondout shoreline use a 1-percent-annual-chance flood elevation of 8.2 feet and expect up to 36 inches of sea-level rise by 2060, and the city has already won federal money to raise East Strand and North Street against frequent tidal flooding. A recorder is replaced every several years as drives age and models change, so the next box can always go somewhere new. The cable paths, wall penetrations, equipment shelf and outlet it plugs into tend to stay for decades.

So we plan the fixed parts for the water the city expects, not just the water the building has seen. The equipment room is chosen a full storey above the basement where possible; the outlet the electrician adds goes high on the wall; cable routes come up through the building rather than along the cellar floor; and any switch that has to stay low is the only part at risk. When the recorder is replaced in five or eight years, its successor drops into the same dry spot without new holes in old brick.

Can footage be recovered from a recorder whose drive sat in floodwater?

Sometimes, if nobody switches it on wet. A hard drive is sealed against dust but not designed to be submerged, and river and creek water carry silt and contaminants. The damage that ends recovery is usually done when power is applied while water is still inside the recorder or the drive. The first instruction we give after any flood is therefore to leave the recorder off, and to unplug it only if that can be done safely.

When we arrive, the drive comes out of the recorder and is kept in a sealed bag. If the footage matters — for an insurance claim, a police report or a licence obligation — the drive goes to a specialist data recovery lab rather than being dried and tried on site; their cleanroom process gives the best chance, though no one can promise success. Meanwhile we install a replacement recorder, rebuild the channel map from our notes or the camera labels, and put the new box somewhere the next flood cannot reach, so recording resumes the same day.

Does a damp fieldstone or bluestone cellar hurt a recorder even if it never floods?

Yes, slowly. Stone foundations wick moisture, and many old Ulster cellars sit at high humidity for much of the summer. Warm, humid air meeting cool equipment can condense on circuit boards and connectors; over months that brings corrosion on the network ports, the power connector and the drive’s contacts, and mould on dust inside the case. The symptoms are intermittent: cameras dropping and returning, a drive that occasionally disappears, a recorder that reboots on humid days.

The cure is the same as for flooding — move the recorder up into the heated, drier part of the house — and where the cellar is the only option for a switch, mount it high on an inside wall, keep it off the stone and run a small dehumidifier in the room. When we inspect a recorder that has lived in a damp cellar, we check the ports and connectors for green or white corrosion and replace patch cords that show it; corroded contacts are a common cause of flickering cameras that owners blame on the cameras themselves.

Retention the state sets for you

Does the state’s 60-day rule for dispensaries mean continuous video or just motion clips?

The regulation says all recordings must be kept for at least 60 days; it does not set a frame rate or say motion-only recording is acceptable. We read it cautiously. Areas the rule names — safes, vaults, sales areas, entries, exits and each point of sale — are recorded continuously, because an inspector or investigator asking what happened at a register at a given minute should find video, not a gap where nobody moved. Quiet areas may use motion-triggered recording with a few seconds before and after, if your compliance adviser agrees.

Whichever choice you make, it has to be built into the drive calculation. Continuous recording at good quality on sixteen cameras uses far more space than motion clips, and the recorder starts writing over its oldest video the moment the drives are full. We size the drives for your actual settings, then check after the first two weeks how much space each day is using, and confirm in writing that the recorder will reach 60 days with room to spare. If it will not, we change the settings or add a drive before the shop opens, not after an inspection.

What does giving the Office of Cannabis Management “remote access” to our recorder look like in practice?

A viewer login, not your admin password, and a safe way to reach the recorder. The rule asks the licensee to make video available for immediate viewing by the Office on request, by remote access or login credentials, and to provide unaltered copies on request. We create a dedicated account that can watch live and play back recordings but cannot change settings, delete video or add users, and we write down how it is handed over and withdrawn.

The route matters as much as the account. A recorder opened to the internet through a port on the router is exactly what attackers scan for. Instead we use the recorder maker’s secure relay service or a VPN, so viewing works without exposing the recorder. For copies, we show staff how to export original files in the recorder’s native format along with the maker’s player, which keeps the video unaltered, and how to note the date, time and camera of each export. The aim is that any manager on duty can answer a request without calling us.

The cannabis rules say keep footage longer if an investigation is pending — how do we hold one incident past the 60 days?

Lock it on the recorder and export a copy. Most professional recorders can protect a time range from being overwritten, often called locking or tagging, and every recorder can export a clip to a USB drive or a computer. The rule asks you to keep the relevant recording until the matter is closed or the investigating body says it is no longer needed, so a protected range on the recorder alone is not enough: it uses space that the 60-day cycle needs, and it can be lost if the drive fails.

The method we set up is simple. The manager marks the incident on the recorder, exports the full camera views covering it, saves the files to two places — a USB drive kept in the safe and a secure computer or cloud folder — and writes a one-line log entry saying what was exported and why. When the matter closes, the lock is released. We leave a printed card in the recorder room with those steps, because the people who need them are rarely the people who were at the installation.

Big old buildings and campuses

We lease a suite on a large campus like iPark 87 — can we run our own recorder, or must we use the landlord’s?

Usually you can run your own, and for most tenants you should. Your cameras watch your space, your stock and your staff; the footage is yours to manage and, if you are regulated, yours to answer for. A recorder in your own lockable room, on your own network, keeps it that way. What you cannot do without the landlord is drill through shared walls, use the building’s risers or network closets, or mount cameras on common areas and the outside of the building.

On a campus the size of the former IBM site, with more than a million square feet across many buildings, the landlord will normally have its own cameras on roads, lots and entrances. We ask for the property manager’s rules early, agree in writing where our cable may run, and keep the tenant’s recorder separate from the landlord’s system. If the two parties later want to share certain views — a loading dock you both use, say — that can be done with a limited viewer account on either side rather than one shared admin password.

Coring through an old mill’s concrete floors is slow and costly — how few holes can a three-storey camera system get by with?

Usually one per floor: a switch on each storey and a single link between them, not a bundle of camera cables through every slab. Former mills and factory buildings, of the kind found in Kingston and in Ellenville’s old manufacturing district, often have thick concrete or heavy timber floors. Coring through them is slow, sometimes needs the structural engineer’s blessing, and every hole is a fire-stopping job. One core per floor, or an existing shaft, is far better than one per camera.

So each floor gets a small PoE switch in a closet or high on a wall, the cameras on that floor run to it, and a single network cable — or fibre where the distance is long or the run passes near heavy electrical equipment — carries everything to the recorder. Each penetration is sealed with a listed fire-stop product. The recorder sits on whichever floor is driest and most secure, and the per-floor switches are labelled on the channel map so the building’s next technician can follow the path.

Studios, galleries and shared streets

Our pottery or woodworking studio is dusty — where should the recorder go?

Anywhere except the studio. Recorders cool themselves with a small fan that pulls air across the drive and circuit boards; in a woodshop it pulls sawdust, in a pottery it pulls fine clay and glaze dust, and near a kiln it also sits in heat. Within months the vents clog, the drive runs hotter, and the recorder starts dropping cameras or rebooting. Many Woodstock and Saugerties studios are outbuildings or converted garages, which makes the temptation to put the recorder on the workbench even stronger.

The better plan is to record the studio from the house or an office: studio cameras run back to the house over cable or fibre, or to a small sealed switch in the studio that links to the house. If the recorder must live in the same building as the work, it goes in a separate closed room or a filtered cabinet with the filter on a cleaning schedule. We note that schedule on the handover sheet, because a clogged filter is a silent failure.

Can the recorder black out part of a camera’s view, such as a neighbour’s window across a narrow Uptown street?

Yes. Almost every current recorder and camera supports privacy masks: blocks drawn over part of the image that are blacked out in live view and in the recording itself. In Uptown Kingston, Saugerties Village and New Paltz the streets are narrow and apartments sit over shops, so a camera aimed at a shop door can easily take in an upstairs window opposite. A mask over that window means it is never recorded, which is both good manners and good practice.

Masks have limits. They are fixed to the image, so if the camera is moved the mask must be redrawn, and a mask also blocks anything that happens behind it. We set them after the camera is aimed, check them in recorded playback and not just live view, and list each one on the handover sheet. For galleries and studios the same feature hides a desk, a screen or a changing area while leaving the artwork and doors in view.

Rules that are specific to Ulster

Does Ulster County’s electrical licensing law reach the low-voltage camera cable?

The county’s law is broad on paper, so we plan as if it might. Chapter 185 of the Ulster County Code, the county’s electrical licensing law, defines electrical work to include wiring for signalling, communication, alarm and data transmission. Our camera and network cabling is done under a New York State low-voltage license, and the parts of a recorder job that are unmistakably electrical — a new outlet, a new circuit, a generator inlet — are done by a licensed electrician with the local permit and inspection.

Where the line falls for low-voltage work can depend on the town or village building department and the scope of the job, and we are not lawyers. On larger commercial projects we ask the local code office before work starts and keep their answer in the job file. For homeowners, the practical point is simple: if a recorder needs power where there is none, the outlet comes from an electrician first, and we take it from there.

Does putting a recorder and new cable into a building in one of Kingston’s historic districts need the preservation commission’s approval?

The recorder itself, no; what shows outside, possibly. Kingston’s Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews material changes to the outside appearance of buildings in its districts — the Stockade, Rondout–West Strand, Chestnut Street and Fair Street — when they are visible from a street or alley. A recorder in a back office, and cable run inside walls and floors, changes nothing anyone outside can see. Ordinary maintenance that keeps the same design and materials is also exempt.

The parts that may need review are outside: new conduit up a facade, a camera on a front wall, a cable entering through visible brick. We plan those first, using rear and side walls, existing openings and interior routes wherever possible, and where something new will show, the owner applies before work starts. On most Stockade and Rondout jobs the recorder work goes ahead at once while any exterior camera waits for the commission.

Linking the recorder to what is already there

Can our existing alarm panel tell the recorder when someone opens the door after hours?

Usually, yes. Most professional recorders have alarm inputs on the back: small terminals that react when a contact closes or opens. An alarm panel with a spare programmable output can be wired to one of those inputs, so when the system is armed and a door opens, the recorder records that door’s camera at full quality, marks the event on the timeline and can send a still to a manager’s phone. Some newer recorders and alarm systems can do the same over the network instead of with a wire.

This turns a night’s worth of video into a list of moments worth checking. It is especially useful in Ulster’s seasonal and part-time businesses, where nobody may look at the recorder for days. We coordinate with the alarm company, because their panel programming is theirs to change, and we test each input with the panel armed before we leave. If the alarm is monitored, we also make sure our work does not change what the monitoring centre receives.

Equipment

Types of NVR Equipment We Install

An Ulster recorder is chosen from the retention target and the room it will live in, not from a catalogue page. These are the building blocks we use most and where each one earns its place in the county.

Two-bay and four-bay recorders

Recorders that hold two to four surveillance drives. Extra bays are the simplest way to reach long retention, and a recorder with spare bays can grow when a business adds cameras without replacing the box.

Mirrored and parity drive arrays

Two or more drives set so that one can fail without losing video. For a business that must prove 60 days, a single failed drive should not mean a 30-day hole; arrays cost drive space but buy that certainty.

Rack-mount recorders and servers

For large retail, campus tenants and multi-building sites: 32 channels or more, hot-swap drive bays, dual power supplies on some models and rack ears for a proper network closet.

Wall brackets and small racks

Our standard answer to flood risk. A wall-mounted shelf or a short wall rack gets the recorder, switch and battery off the floor, out of reach of water and of a mop bucket, and lets air move around them.

Per-floor PoE switches

Small managed switches that power and connect the cameras on one floor of a tall or long building, sending everything to the recorder over a single uplink. They make old masonry buildings practical to wire.

Battery systems sized in hours

Battery units chosen by the hours they must run, not by the box size. For dispensaries we work back from eight hours of the full load; for other businesses from the outages the owner has lived through.

Alarm input and output wiring

Short runs from alarm panels, door contacts and motion detectors to the recorder’s alarm terminals, so events are marked on the timeline and recorded at full quality.

Filtered and ventilated cabinets

Lockable cabinets with fans and dust filters for recorders that must share a building with a workshop, a kiln, a commercial kitchen or simply a busy back room.

A recorder in the building compared with cloud cameras and alarm-company packages

Cloud cameras send video to the maker’s servers and charge a monthly fee per camera; alarm companies such as ADT and the self-install brands Ring and SimpliSafe sell camera plans tied to their own apps and subscriptions. For a single house with a couple of cameras and a fast connection, those can be enough. For an Ulster business that must keep 60 days of sixteen cameras, they become expensive quickly, and every camera depends on an upload connection that rural Ulster does not always provide.

A recorder in the building keeps every frame locally, internet or not, at the cost of having to protect the box itself from water, theft and failure — the subject of most of this page.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About NVR Installation in Ulster County

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for this topic from our build environment, so we have not quoted it. Instead, here are seven claims that turn up again and again in AI answers, cost guides from Angi and HomeAdvisor, installer blogs and forum threads when people ask about recorders — tested against what we find in Ulster County.

“Put the recorder in the basement — it’s cool, quiet and out of the way”

This is the most common placement advice in recorder guides, and in much of the country it is reasonable. Basements are cool, and a recorder’s fan noise and blinking lights are out of sight there. It also puts the box near where cable often enters a house.

In Ulster it ignores the county’s flood history. Irene put record water into the Stony Clove at Phoenicia and the Rondout at Rosendale, the Esopus crested twelve feet above flood stage according to the county’s emergency director, and Kingston’s waterfront takes tidal water as well as creek water. Many stone cellars that never flood are still damp enough to corrode connectors over a few summers.

Our rule is the reverse: the recorder goes in the driest, most secure room in the heated part of the building, as high as practical, and only a cheap switch is allowed below. Cool matters less than dry; a recorder is perfectly happy in an ordinary room-temperature closet with a little ventilation.

“Thirty days of recording is the industry standard”

Thirty days is a common default in recorder marketing and in many answers about how much storage to buy. It is a convenient round number, and for many homes it is plenty: if something happens, the owner usually knows within days.

It is not a standard anyone in Ulster is bound by, in either direction. A licensed cannabis dispensary must keep at least 60 days under state rules, twice that figure. Kingston’s police department, by its own account, keeps its street-camera video for 14 to 21 days before it is erased. A business that only discovers a theft at a monthly stock count needs more than thirty; a holiday rental may need less.

So we never start from a default. We ask how quickly you would notice a problem, who might ask for video and how long after, and whether a rule or contract sets a number. Then we size the drives for that, add headroom, and check the real figure after two weeks of recording.

“Motion recording saves space and is all a business needs”

Motion-triggered recording does save a great deal of space, and modern detection that looks for people and vehicles has made it much more reliable than it was. For many homes and quiet premises it is the right choice.

For a regulated business it can create exactly the gap an inspector or investigator will ask about. The cannabis rules require coverage of every entry, exit and point of sale and retention of all recordings; they do not say a register may go unrecorded when no motion is detected. Detection can also miss slow movement, people partly hidden by shelving, or events in poor light.

We therefore record named areas continuously in regulated businesses, use motion recording for quiet spaces where the compliance adviser agrees, and size the drives for that mix. Owners of unregulated shops choose, but we show them the difference on their own cameras before they decide.

“Any NVR can give an inspector or detective what they need”

Every recorder can play back and export video, so the claim sounds safe. In practice the differences show up at the worst moment: when someone needs a clean copy of a specific few minutes, fast, without the original being altered.

The cannabis rules set the bar clearly: a clear colour still photo from any camera, live or recorded; immediate viewing on request; unaltered copies on request. Low-resolution sub-streams, heavy compression or a recorder that only exports to a proprietary file nobody can open fail that bar, and so do staff who have never tried an export.

We record at quality that holds up to a still frame, test exports to USB and to a computer during handover, include the maker’s player with the files, and leave a printed export card in the recorder room. The same preparation helps any business that might need to hand video to police or an insurer.

“Cloud backup makes it irrelevant where the recorder sits”

Cloud backup is valuable, and we often set up off-site copies of key clips. The argument goes that if the video is in the cloud, a flooded or stolen recorder does not matter.

Most small systems do not upload everything. They upload motion clips, snapshots or nothing at all unless configured, and a full upload of sixteen continuous cameras would swamp most rural Ulster connections. In mountain hamlets along Routes 28 and 212, where coverage has long been patchy, the upload may be the weakest link in the whole system.

The recorder remains the only complete copy on almost every Ulster job we see. That is why its location, power and protection matter, and why we treat off-site copies as a safety net for the most important moments rather than as a replacement for keeping the recorder dry.

“You can’t add cameras or equipment to a building in a historic district”

This one turns up in forum answers and occasionally in AI summaries about old towns. It reflects a real concern: historic districts do review changes to how buildings look.

Kingston’s rules are narrower than the claim. The Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews material changes to the outside appearance of a building in its districts when they are visible from a public street or alley; ordinary maintenance that keeps the same design and materials is exempt. A recorder in a back room, cable inside walls and cameras indoors are not what the commission reviews.

So historic buildings in the Stockade and the Rondout can and do have full recorder systems. The planning work is about the outside: using rear and side walls, existing openings and interior routes, and applying before anything visible goes on the front.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

An Ulster recorder job runs in seven steps. The retention number and the flood line are settled before equipment is chosen, and the recorder is proven to hold its days before we call the job finished.

1. Settle the retention number

We agree how many days of video the recorder must hold and why: a licence rule, an insurer or contract term, or simply how long it takes you to notice a problem. That figure goes at the top of the quote.

2. Find the high, dry room

We ask where water has reached, look for stains and tide lines, and choose a room above them that is heated, lockable and near the network. Damp cellars and dusty workshops are ruled out for the recorder.

3. Walk the cable paths floor by floor

Each camera position is traced back to the recorder or to a switch on its floor, noting masonry walls, concrete slabs and historic facades, so the number of penetrations is as small as possible.

4. Calculate drives and power in writing

Camera count and recording settings give the drive space; the load of recorder, switches, cameras and network gear gives the battery size for the hours required. Both calculations are attached to the quote.

5. Build and label on the bench

Drives installed and tested, firmware updated, admin and viewer accounts created, clock locked to a time source, alerts routed to named people, masks drawn and every channel labelled to match the cable map.

6. Install, seal and mount high

Recorder and battery go on a wall shelf or rack, switches go high on walls, each masonry penetration is sealed and fire-stopped, and alarm inputs are wired and tested with the panel armed.

7. Prove the days, the power and the alerts

We cut power and time the battery, trigger a failure alert and time its arrival, run an export, and return after two weeks of recording to confirm the recorder is on track to hold the agreed number of days.

Field Notes: Look for the Tide Line First

When I walk into an Ulster building to plan a recorder, the first thing I look for is not the router or the camera positions. It is the tide line: the faint stain on a cellar wall, the swollen bottom of a door frame, the shelf that has been raised on blocks. Owners near the creeks and the Rondout often forget to mention that the building has taken water, but the walls remember. Wherever that line is, the recorder goes well above it.

The second thing I ask is how many days the video has to last, and who says so. For most homes the owner decides; for a dispensary the state has already decided, and for some businesses an insurer or a contract has. That number goes at the top of my notes, because every later choice — drives, recorder size, battery, settings — follows from it.

Before I leave, I play back the earliest recording on the recorder and note the date. Two weeks later I check again and work out how much space each day is really using. If the recorder will not reach the promised days with margin, we fix it then, not when someone needs footage from seven weeks ago. — Anwar Timothy, NYS low-voltage license #12000287431

FAQ

Ulster County NVR Installation FAQ

Can you audit how many days of video our current recorder actually keeps?
+

Yes, without replacing anything. We open the recorder's playback to find the oldest recording still on the drives, check every drive's health and capacity, note each camera's resolution, frame rate, bit rate and recording mode, and work out how much space a typical day uses. The result is a short written report: how many days you have today, how many you would have if a drive failed, and what changes — settings, an extra drive, a larger recorder — would reach the number you need. It is billed on the upgrade and repair line.

Why are Ulster County prices 1.53 times the Brooklyn base?
+

Every area we serve has a published factor applied to the same base price list. Ulster's is 1.53, the highest along with Dutchess, because of travel time up the Thruway and across a large, mountainous county, and because Ulster jobs more often involve old masonry buildings, long cable paths and longer-retention systems. The factor is applied to the recorder work and the per-line cable prices; our hourly service rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum is not multiplied.

Our building still has coax cameras from the 1990s — can a network recorder use them?
+

Not directly. A network video recorder expects network cameras on network cable. Old analog cameras on coax need either a hybrid recorder that accepts both kinds, or encoders that convert each coax camera to a network signal. Often the better answer is to reuse the coax itself: adapters can carry power and network over existing coax to a new network camera, which saves pulling new cable through stone or brick walls. We look at the cable's condition and length before recommending one route.

We are switching internet providers — what has to be redone on the recorder?
+

Recording itself is not affected; the recorder keeps saving video with or without internet. What usually breaks is remote viewing and alerts: a new router hands out different addresses, may block the recorder's connection to the maker's relay service, and changes the network name the recorder may rely on. After the switch, the recorder needs a fixed address on the new router, its relay or VPN connection checked, and a test alert sent. If you tell us the changeover date, we can do this remotely or on a short visit.

Can the recorder be fixed to the wall instead of sitting on a shelf?
+

Yes, and in flood-prone Ulster buildings we prefer it. Most recorders can sit on a wall-mounted shelf or in a short wall rack, along with the camera switch and the battery unit. That keeps them off the floor, away from water and cleaning buckets, and lets air move around them. The bracket has to be anchored into studs or masonry for the combined weight of recorder, drives and battery, and the cables are dressed so the recorder can still be slid out for a drive change.

Do you install recorders for cannabis cultivators and processors, not just dispensaries?
+

Yes. Their security requirements sit in different parts of the state's cannabis regulations from the retail rule quoted on this page, so we start from the requirements in your licence documents and the regulation that applies to your licence type. The recorder work is similar: retention calculated in writing, power support for the required hours, failure alerts, a viewer account for the regulator and storage secured against tampering. Large cultivation buildings add long cable runs and often several recorders.

Does a dispensary's recorder have to be in a locked room?
+

The state rule says the recording media must be secured in a way that protects the recordings from tampering or theft; it does not name a specific room. A locked room or a locked equipment cabinet is the usual way to meet that, with a camera covering the recorder itself and admin passwords limited to named managers. We also disable unused front-panel ports where the recorder allows it and keep a written list of who holds which account, so you can show an inspector how the storage is protected.

What should we write down about the recorder for our insurance agent?
+

The make, model and serial number of the recorder and each drive; where it is installed and how high above the floor; how many days of video it holds and how that was checked; what battery or generator supports it; and who can export footage. Photos of the installation help. After a flood or a break-in, that sheet shows what the equipment was and that it was set up sensibly. We give you most of it on our handover sheet, and we can add the serial numbers if your agent asks for them.
