Security camera installation in Westchester Square starts with an old village that never stopped being a crossroads. English settlers from the New Haven Colony laid it out around 1654, the Dutch called it Oostdorp, and it served as the seat of the Town of Westchester until the area joined New York City in 1895. Today the triangle where Westchester, East Tremont and Lane avenues meet is a transit hub, with the elevated 6 overhead, eight bus routes stopping nearby and, by the business improvement district's own count, more than 300 storefronts along East Tremont Avenue, the square itself, Williamsbridge Road and Zerega and Westchester avenues. Behind those corridors sit the blocks that grew up after the subway arrived in 1920: frame two- and three-family houses on Zerega Avenue, Italianate rowhouses with bay windows, six-unit buildings and a few co-ops. Shoppers told the BID they feel safe on the square by day far more often than at night, the el keeps Westchester Avenue in shade, and recent News 12 reports from Saint Peters Avenue and from a lot behind Westchester Avenue were the kind where a camera in the right place decides whether there is anything to show the 45th Precinct. We plan each camera around one door or one patch of ground, who owns the wall it goes on and who will watch it, and every part of the job is written on a quote before anything is ordered.

The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Square Properties Need Security Cameras Aimed One Doorway at a Time

Westchester Square is small on a map and old on paper. Wikipedia draws it between East Tremont Avenue and Silver Street to the north, Blondell Avenue and Westchester Creek to the east, Waterbury Avenue to the south and Castle Hill Avenue to the west, with East Tremont Avenue, Westchester Avenue and Williamsbridge Road as its main roads. It sits in Community District 10, is split between ZIP codes 10461 and 10462, and is covered by the 45th Precinct, whose station house is at 2877 Barkley Avenue. The land was bought by Thomas Pell from the sachem Ann-hock, also known as Wampage, and the village that settlers from the New Haven Colony built there became the county seat in 1683 and the center of the Town of Westchester until annexation in 1895. Much of the later farmland belonged to the Ferris family; Benjamin Ferris owned a large share of it in 1829.

The neighborhood people walk through today is mostly a product of 1920. That year the IRT Pelham Line, now the 6, opened stations at Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue and Zerega Avenue, and speculative builders filled the old estates with one- and two-family houses while shops lined the corridors. Irish and Italian families followed the new housing. The Historic Districts Council describes the result as a dense neighborhood of small houses with corridors of shopping and commercial activity, and notes that some of the relatively large single-family houses from the earlier estate era survive. NeighborhoodScout says much of the housing was built no later than 1939, a good share between 1940 and 1969, and that most of it is rented. City Planning describes the area as low- and mid-density and generally zoned R6. Listing guides add Italianate rowhouses with bay windows, six-unit buildings and some co-ops, and StreetEasy records on Zerega Avenue show what a typical side street holds: a two-family at 1443 Zerega built in 1901, a three-family at 1370 Zerega built in 1910, a frame two-family at 1654 Zerega and a three-family at 1704 Zerega, both from 1910.

That building stock decides almost everything about a camera job here. A frame house from 1910 has a cellar, wood walls, an attic and usually more than one household, so the questions are who sees which camera and how cable gets from the basement to the eaves without a wire across the clapboard. A rowhouse has no side yard, so cable to the back goes through the house. A six-unit building has a street door, a vestibule, mailboxes, a cellar and a roof door that the owner is responsible for, and tenants who should be told what is recorded. A store under the el has a landlord who owns the facade, a ceiling that may or may not be easy to open, a register, a stockroom and a back door that nobody watches. And a few buildings around the old village green are designated landmarks, where any camera on the outside goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

The reason people call is just as local. In its needs assessment presented to Community Board 10, the Westchester Square BID reported that 77 percent of consumers it surveyed felt safe on the corridors by day and only 38 percent at night, that evening lighting was dim, and that the elevated structure blocks natural light on Westchester Avenue. In a 2018 DOT workshop about the square, residents said lighting under the elevated 6 was lacking and asked for police or security presence after school hours. News 12 reported in 2026 on a gunman who approached two men inside 1472 Saint Peters Avenue just before 11 p.m., on a 57-year-old man who was robbed after getting out of his car in the rear of 2276 Westchester Avenue, and on an off-duty officer who was attacked on St. Peter's Avenue near Tratman Avenue after surveillance video showed him walking past a supermarket just after 4 a.m. CBS New York reported that a phone store in Westchester Square was robbed of four phones and $500 in cash. None of that tells us what any one shop or house needs. It does explain why so many calls from here begin with a back door, a dark sidewalk or a lot behind a building.

Westchester Square in the record

  • A shopping district with a camera history. Assembly Member Michael Benedetto's office says it funded cameras for Westchester Square in 2009, and Bronx Times reported that former BID executive director Lisa Sorin worked with New York State to have nine surveillance cameras installed in the BID's most trafficked areas.
  • A prosecutor's camera map. The Bronx District Attorney's Community Assisted Mapping System, unveiled at the end of 2018 with the Bronx Chamber and the borough's BIDs, adds a registered camera's location to a database so prosecutors can find nearby cameras when they investigate. In 2019 a sign in Westchester Square told passersby that video surveillance was in use.
  • A precinct with a property-crime load. Wikipedia lists the 45th Precinct's 2022 totals as 108 burglaries, 609 grand larcenies and 323 grand larcenies of autos, among other crimes. These are precinct-wide figures, not counts for the square alone.
  • More than 300 storefronts. The BID inventoried 318 storefronts across four corridors and found 94 percent occupied; about half of the 105 merchants it surveyed had five or fewer employees.
  • A station that already has cameras. News 12 reported that the Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue station upgrades included fire alarms, security cameras and new elevators. Those cameras watch the station, not the shops below it.
  • Two individual landmarks on the green. The St. Peter's Church, Chapel and Cemetery complex at 2500 Westchester Avenue was designated in 1976, and the Van Schaick Free Reading Room, now the Huntington Free Library, at 9 Westchester Square was designated in 1994. We found no LPC historic district covering the surrounding blocks.

Who Else Is Watching

BID Cameras, the 45th Precinct and the DA's CAMS Map: What They Cover and What They Leave to You

Westchester Square has more public and program cameras than many Bronx shopping streets, and that is exactly why owners ask whether they need their own. The honest answer starts with what each of those systems was built to do.

The BID's cameras in the busiest spots

The Bronx Times reported that the Westchester Square BID's former executive director worked with the state to install nine surveillance cameras in the district's most trafficked areas, and in 2015 that the BID had paid for the first phase of a camera project, four of the first fifteen cameras, while it waited on a state grant and worked with the 45th Precinct. Assembly Member Benedetto's office separately records funding cameras for Westchester Square in 2009. Cameras placed in the most trafficked areas are, by design, watching intersections, the plaza and the busiest stretches of sidewalk. They are not aimed at a particular register, a particular stockroom door or a particular car parked behind a building on a side street, and a shopkeeper cannot log into them to check last night.

Real-time police cameras are a different program

Owners sometimes mix up the square's cameras with the NYPD Argus cameras in the neighboring Throggs Neck BID. Assembly Member Benedetto's office says it secured a $320,000 grant for those Argus cameras, and Yahoo News reported that they are monitored in real time by the 45th Precinct and police headquarters. That is a specific installation in a specific district. We found nothing in our sources saying the cameras around Westchester Square are watched live the same way, and we would not tell any owner to plan as if they were.

CAMS: a map of who to ask

The Bronx District Attorney built the Community Assisted Mapping System with the Bronx Chamber of Commerce and the borough's BIDs and unveiled it at the end of 2018. As Welcome2TheBronx described it, when a home or business owner signs their cameras up, the location goes into a database that feeds an interactive map; when prosecutors investigate a crime, they enter an address and find nearby cameras that may hold evidence. In 2019 a sign in Westchester Square told passersby that video surveillance was in use. Registration does not give anyone your password or your recordings. It tells investigators which doors to knock on. That makes it useful only if your camera actually recorded something clear, with the right time on it, and if someone at your property can find and export the clip when asked.

The station's own cameras

News 12 reported that the upgrades at the Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue station included security cameras along with fire alarms and elevators that made the station fully accessible. Those cameras belong to the transit system and are placed for the platforms, stairs and fare areas. A store under the el benefits from busy foot traffic to and from the station, but nothing about the station's cameras covers the inside of that store.

What this means for a shop or a house

Put all of this together and the gap is clear. Public and program cameras watch the public places people pass through. Your door, your counter, your cellar stairs, your rear lot and your front steps are covered only if you cover them. The useful way to plan is to assume no other camera sees your door, place your own cameras for faces and hands at short range, keep the clock synced, and then, if you choose, register on CAMS so that your good footage can be found.

Under the El

Westchester Avenue Storefronts in the Shade of the 6

The Pelham Line runs elevated over Westchester Avenue, and the BID's own assessment says the structure blocks natural light on that corridor. It named two stretches as problem areas: Westchester Avenue from St. Peter's to Rowland Street, and Williamsbridge Road from Eastchester Road to Poplar Street. DOT's 2018 workshop heard the same thing from residents about lighting under the elevated 6. For a camera, that combination of daytime shade, mixed light at night and a bright street seen through a dark doorway is the main technical problem, more than resolution or brand.

The doorway problem

A camera inside a shop looking out at the door sees a dim interior and, beyond the glass, a sidewalk that may be brighter or darker than the shop depending on the hour. When the sidewalk is brighter, a person walking in turns into a dark outline. When the shop is brighter than the street at night, the glass reflects the inside of the store back into the lens. We choose door cameras with strong wide dynamic range, set them back from the glass and slightly to one side so reflections fall out of frame, and aim them at the spot just inside the door where every customer's face is lit by the shop's own lights. That spot is where an identification usually comes from.

Shade by day, mixed light by night

Outdoor cameras under the el see something different every hour: shadow from the steel, stripes of sunlight between the tracks, shop signs, streetlights, headlights and the light from passing trains. Cameras that switch to black-and-white infrared too early lose detail in the shade; cameras that never switch lose everything after closing. We set the day-night switch for the real light at that door rather than leaving the factory setting, and we test it after dark before we leave.

Steel overhead and steady pictures

Mounts go into solid masonry or a structural part of the storefront, never a sign band or an awning frame that moves when trucks and trains pass. A camera that shakes produces smeared video at exactly the moments you need it. Nothing we install goes on the el columns, the subway structure or the public sidewalk; the camera stays on the building you rent or own.

The plaza corner

In 2018 Community Board 10 approved DOT's plan for a plaza at the southwest corner of Westchester Avenue and East Tremont Avenue, replacing a triangular island and slipway and shortening the walk to the 6. DOT counted 44 crashes at that intersection from 2012 to 2016. The Bronx Times reported that the BID handles the plaza's maintenance, security and programming, and that some opponents worried about loitering. Shops facing the plaza often want their outside camera to take in the seating. It can, as part of the view from your own door, but the BID is the body that looks after the plaza, and a camera aimed squarely at people sitting at tables is a different thing from a camera aimed at your entrance.

After dark on the corridors

The gap between the BID's daytime and nighttime safety numbers, 77 percent and 38 percent, is the most useful single fact for a shop owner here. It says the hours after the sun goes down are when customers, workers and the last person locking up feel exposed. A store camera plan for the square should therefore treat closing time as its own event: a camera on the gate and the sidewalk in front of it, light on the spot where the gate is pulled down and locked, and a view of the back door for the staff member who leaves that way.

The House Blocks

Frame Two- and Three-Families on Zerega Avenue and the Side Streets

Walk a block off the corridors and Westchester Square becomes a neighborhood of houses. The StreetEasy records on Zerega Avenue are typical: a 1901 two-family, two three-families from 1910, a frame two-family from the same year. Most hold an owner and one or two tenant households, or two or three tenant households under an owner who lives somewhere else. A camera plan for one of these houses has to answer three questions before it answers any technical one: who lives on each floor, who should see which camera, and which parts of the outside belong to the house rather than to the neighbors.

The front: steps, door and curb

On a frame house set back a short distance from the sidewalk, the front usually carries a stoop or a few steps, a main door and sometimes a second door for a lower apartment. A doorbell or door camera handles faces at close range at the main door. A second camera high on the front, under the eave or the porch roof, covers the steps, the walk to the gate and the stretch of curb where the household's car is parked. That second camera is the one that shows the person who came up the walk before they reached the door, and the one that shows which way they went.

The side gap

Many of these houses stand a few feet from the next one, with a narrow passage leading to the yard and the cellar door. A camera at the front corner looking down that passage covers anyone heading for the back. The neighbor's side windows often face the same passage, so we draw privacy masks over them in both the live view and the recording, and show the owner what is blacked out before we leave.

The back yard and the cellar door

Yards behind the frame houses are small and touch other yards on two or three sides. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. So the rear camera is aimed down at your own yard, your back door and your cellar entrance, mounted only as high as it needs to be, and masked over any neighboring yard still in the frame.

Cable through a wood house

Wood-frame houses of this age often make cable easier than people expect. The cellar ceiling is usually open, the attic gives access to the eaves, and closets stacked above each other provide a vertical path. Old plaster on lath cracks if it is drilled carelessly, so holes inside the house go in closets and corners. Outside, mounts sit on a block that spreads the load on clapboard or shingle so the siding is not crushed, and every opening to the outside is sloped, sleeved and sealed.

Two or three households, separate views

One recorder in the cellar can serve everyone in a three-family house without anyone seeing more than they should. The owner sees all cameras. Each tenant household sees the street door, the steps, the sidewalk and its own entrance. Each adult has a personal login, so access can be removed the day someone moves out without changing anyone else's password. We give the owner a written list of each camera and what it sees, which tenants can be shown before the cameras go live.

The recorder's place in the cellar

The Bronx Times reported that Daisy's Juice Bar & Café in Westchester Square closed for five months after flooding. A recorder on a cellar floor is the first thing water reaches. In a frame house we put the recorder, the switch and a battery unit on a shelf well off the floor, away from the boiler, and where the cellar has taken water before, we suggest a spot on the first floor instead and run the cable up to it.

Small Buildings

Six-Unit Buildings, Rowhouses and Co-ops Around the Square

Between the corridors and the house blocks sit the buildings that need cameras for more than one household at a time: Italianate rowhouses with bay windows, some divided into apartments; six-unit buildings, a very common size in this part of the Bronx; small walk-ups above the shops; and a handful of co-ops, such as the one StreetEasy lists at 2181 Starling Avenue. The incident News 12 reported inside 1472 Saint Peters Avenue just before 11 p.m. is a reminder that the inside of a building's entrance is where many things happen and where a resident has the least control.

The entrance sequence

A small building's entrance usually runs street door, vestibule, inner door, mailboxes, then the stairs. The first camera goes inside, looking back at the street door from high in the hall, so it records the face of anyone who comes through. Where there is a vestibule with mailboxes, a second camera covers the mailboxes and the inner door. Those two cameras answer most of the questions owners and tenants ask afterward: who came in, who followed someone through the door, and who touched the packages.

Cellar, roof and rear

The doors nobody watches are the ones that matter after the entrance: the cellar door, the door to the rear yard and the roof door. A camera at the bottom of the cellar stairs and one at the top of the roof stair record anyone using those routes. In a building with a rear yard where tenants put out trash, a camera over the back door covers that too, aimed at the door and the path rather than into anyone's windows.

Telling the tenants

Brick Underground's advice for renters is that installing any equipment in a building's common areas, including the hallway outside one's own apartment, needs the landlord's permission. The other side of that coin is that the owner decides on common-area cameras and should tell tenants, in writing, where they are and what they cover. One installer's blog says a city law, which it calls Local Law 50, requires residential buildings with common-area cameras to notify residents. We could not confirm that law in a primary source, so we do not rely on it; we simply recommend that every owner post a notice and give tenants the list of cameras, because it prevents far more arguments than it causes.

Co-op boards

In a co-op, cameras in the lobby, halls, cellar and grounds are the board's decision and usually go through the managing agent. A shareholder who wants a camera at their own apartment door needs the board's approval for anything outside the apartment. Before we quote a board, we ask who will be able to watch live video, who can export clips, how long video is kept and how shareholders will be told; those four answers shape the system as much as the floor plan does.

Where cable runs in an older small building

Most of these buildings predate any idea of low-voltage wiring. Cable to the upper floors uses an existing chase where one exists, follows the stair in a painted raceway or conduit where it does not, and every penetration through a floor or rated wall is fire-stopped. The recorder lives in a locked spot, ideally away from the cellar floor, with a battery unit so a short outage does not leave a gap in the recording.

The Old Village Green

Cameras Near St. Peter's, the Huntington Free Library and Owen F. Dolen Park

The oldest part of Westchester Square is still laid out like a village. Owen F. Dolen Park, the former village green, is bordered by Westchester Avenue, Lane Avenue and Williamsbridge Road; it was named in 1926, renovated starting in 2011 and rededicated in June 2013. Around it stand the buildings that carry the neighborhood's history, and two of them are New York City landmarks. For a camera job, the difference between a designated landmark and a building that is merely old matters a great deal.

St. Peter's Church, Chapel and Cemetery

The St. Peter's parish dates to 1693. The church at 2500 Westchester Avenue was designed by Leopold Eidlitz and completed in the 1850s, rebuilt after a fire in 1878 by Cyrus L. W. Eidlitz, and the chapel dates to 1867 and 1868. The complex was designated a city landmark in 1976 and placed on the National Register in 1983. Anything fixed to the exterior of a designated landmark is the kind of work LPC reviews. We are not the church's installer and do not claim to be; we describe the rule because neighbors and organizations near the green ask about it.

The Huntington Free Library and its lot

The Van Schaick Free Reading Room at 9 Westchester Square was built in 1882 and 1883 to designs by Frederick Clarke Withers, with an addition from 1890 to 1892 by William Anderson, and was endowed by Collis P. Huntington. It became an individual landmark in 1994 and is surrounded by a green iron fence. LPC held a public hearing on the site in September 2023, and the City Planning Commission's report on the project explains that the existing annex and connecting corridor, neither part of the landmark designation, will be demolished to make room for a new branch library. That is a clear local example of a single lot where some parts are protected and some are not, which is exactly why we ask an owner to confirm with LPC and the designation report which part of a building a camera would touch.

What the LPC camera guidance says

LPC's Permit Guidebook includes a section on installing security cameras under its rules, and shows that staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria, including dome cameras finished to match the building. One industry summary of those rules says LPC allows no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings and three per 25 feet on large buildings, and generally discourages cameras on a primary facade unless there is no other option. We treat that summary as a starting point and check the current rules and the specific building before submitting anything.

Old but not designated

The Historic Districts Council chose Westchester Square as one of its Six to Celebrate in 2018 and lists other historic sites around the square, including the former Bronx Savings Bank, now Apple Bank, at 12 Westchester Square, built in 1942 to designs by Cross & Cross. The Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue station is on the State and National Registers. A Revolutionary War marker stands near the White Castle at 2900 East Tremont Avenue. Recognition by an advocacy group or listing on the National Register is not the same as city landmark designation, and we found no LPC historic district covering the neighborhood's residential blocks. For an ordinary house or store here, the people whose permission matters are the owner, the landlord and the board, not LPC, unless that particular building has been designated.

How cameras look on old fronts

Even where no permit is involved, a camera on a 19th-century house or a stone commercial front looks better small and quiet. We favor compact domes and turrets in a color close to the wall, mount them under eaves, in porch ceilings or in door reveals rather than in the middle of a facade, and run cable inside whenever we can so the front carries the camera and nothing else.

Behind the Buildings

Rear Lots, Parking Pads and the Walk From the Car

The News 12 report from the rear of 2276 Westchester Avenue described a man who had just gotten out of his car when he was approached. Many of the mixed-use buildings along the corridors have a small lot or a row of spaces behind them, used by store staff, tenants upstairs and sometimes the owner. Lots like these are where people are most exposed: getting out of a car, unloading, walking to a back door, often after dark.

Watch the approach, not just the cars

A camera that shows a whole lot from a high corner shows that something happened. A camera aimed at the way people walk into the lot, from the street, the alley or the gap between buildings, shows who came in. On a small lot, the best pair is usually one camera at face height covering the entrance and the path to the back door, and one wider view across the parked cars. Light on the entrance and the back door matters more than any camera setting.

The back door of the shop

Many stores on the square take deliveries and throw out trash through a rear door that opens onto a lot or alley. That door needs a camera outside, looking at whoever is waiting before it opens, and one inside looking back at the door. Staff who leave through the back at closing should be able to glance at the outside view on a screen by the door before they step out.

Parking pads beside the house

Some of the frame houses have a parking pad or a short driveway beside them. A camera on the corner of the house near the front, aimed back along the pad toward the car's doors, records anyone who walks up to the car. One aimed from the back toward the street mainly sees headlights. We aim for the approach.

Cars on the street

Public cameras, landmarks and boundaries

We are thinking of signing our cameras up with the Bronx DA's CAMS map. What does that registration hand over, and what stays under our control?

As the program was described when the District Attorney unveiled it with the Bronx Chamber and the borough's BIDs, registering puts your camera's location into a database behind an interactive map, so prosecutors investigating a crime can look up an address and see which nearby cameras might hold evidence. The recordings stay on your recorder, under your logins, and you decide how to respond to a request. For it to be worth anything, your clock has to be right and someone at the property has to know how to export a clip.

The Rules in Plain Words

New York Rules That Shape a Westchester Square Camera Job

Whether the job is a store under the el, a three-family on Zerega Avenue or a six-unit building, the same few rules come up. This is a plain summary of how we work within them, not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Who may install

New York requires a license from the Department of State to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and that license covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft or movement. General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in that business, or hold themselves out as able to do it, without a license. The Department of State's application also says installing "passive," non-detecting devices does not require licensure, and sources do not all describe the boundary the same way. We work under NYS license #12000287431 with insurance in place, and we suggest asking any installer for their number.

Who decides what goes where

The owner decides on the building. A tenant needs the landlord's permission for equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside their own door, as Brick Underground's guidance puts it. In a co-op, the board decides on the lobby, halls and grounds. A store tenant needs the landlord's agreement for anything on the facade. And a designated landmark's exterior goes through LPC.

Neighbors' yards and windows

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. On the small lots behind the frame houses and rowhouses here, yard cameras watch your own yard, and masks cover the rest. Cameras are also kept from looking into anyone's home through a window, and we mask any window that falls in a frame.

Sound

Talking through a doorbell to a visitor is a conversation you are part of. A camera quietly recording what people say in a hallway or on a sidewalk is something else. We leave continuous audio off on hallway, shared-space and outdoor cameras.

Telling the people on camera

Owners of buildings with common-area cameras should tell tenants in writing what is covered and post a notice. A vendor blog says a city law requires this; we could not confirm it, so we recommend it as good practice rather than citing it as law. Businesses do well to post a notice at the door too.

Who can see the footage

Brick Underground reported that a landlord is not obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant. That cuts both ways: whoever controls the recorder controls the video. For a three-family house or a small building, we set up access so each household sees what it should and the owner can export clips quickly when a tenant asks for help.

What moves a Westchester Square quote

  • The cable path. An open cellar ceiling or a drop ceiling is quick; plaster, pressed metal, stone and finished cellars take longer.
  • The wall material. Clapboard, brick, block and stone each need different anchors and sealing.
  • Height and reach. An eave on a three-story frame house or a rear wall over a lot may need taller ladders.
  • Count and type. The number of cameras, and whether any need low-light, narrow-view or vandal-resistant housings.
  • The recorder and drive. Channels and storage sized to how many days of video you want kept.
  • Access setup. Separate logins and views for owners, tenants, staff or a board.
  • Paperwork. Time to prepare drawings or photos for a landlord, a board or an LPC submission.
  • Lighting. A motion or dusk-to-dawn light where the camera needs it.

Each of these appears on its own line in the written quote, so you can see exactly what you are paying for and remove what you do not want. Estimates are free and requests are booked within 2 hours.

FAQ

Westchester Square Security Camera Installation FAQ

If our setup-only visit at a Westchester Square shop runs past the first hour, how is the extra time charged?
+

The setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour on our Bronx rate, and time after that is billed in 30-minute blocks. If the technician sees that the work will run past the first hour, for example because a recorder has to be reset and every camera re-added, they tell you on site before continuing so you can decide. Anything that turns into new mounting or cabling is moved onto a written quote instead of being added by the hour.

Our store rents the ground floor of a building on East Tremont Avenue. Can the written quote be split so the landlord pays for the outside cameras and we pay for the inside ones?
+

Yes. Because every camera, mount, cable run and recorder is already on its own line, we can issue the quote in two parts, one for the facade and sidewalk cameras the landlord controls and one for the interior cameras the store controls. That also makes it easy to keep two separate recorders if you want the store's video and the building's video apart. We still need the landlord's written agreement before anything goes on the outside.

Does the Westchester Square BID set rules for cameras on storefronts inside the district?
+

We did not find any BID rule about cameras on individual storefronts in our sources. The BID has worked on cameras for the district's busiest areas and with the 45th Precinct, and it looks after the plaza, but what goes on your facade is set by your lease, your landlord and, for a designated landmark, LPC. If your lease or the BID mentions anything about signage or facade changes, send it with your photos and we will plan around it.

Does the 2026 city grant money awarded to the Westchester Square BID pay for cameras at individual stores?
+

Not as it was reported. The Bronx Times described the 2026 Avenue NYC grants that the Westchester Square BID shares with two other groups as paying for program managers, needs assessments and training, and listed the BID among those receiving small BID support grants. Nothing in that report says the money buys cameras for individual shops. Ask the BID directly if you think a program applies to your store.

Can AESS sign our cameras up for the DA's CAMS map, or do we have to do it ourselves?
+

Registration is the owner's decision and is done in the owner's name, so we do not submit it for you. What we do is give you what the program asks about: a written list of each camera, where it is, what it sees and how long video is kept, and we show someone at your property how to find and export a clip with its original time stamp, which is what makes a registered camera useful when prosecutors ask.

Does work on a landmark building, or on the same lot as one, add anything to the quote?
+

It can add one line: the time to prepare photos, a camera plan and product details for an LPC submission, and to adjust the plan if staff ask for changes. The cameras and cabling are priced the same way as on any building, though LPC guidance favors small cameras finished to match the wall and positions off the primary facade, which can change the mounting method. We tell you up front which parts depend on LPC and do not order anything until the approval route is clear.

Our 19th-century house near the square was divided into apartments long ago and has wires from several owners. How do you work out which old wires are still in use before adding cameras?
+

For low-voltage wiring such as old bell, phone, alarm and camera cable, we trace each run with a tone generator and probe and a tester, label what is live, and leave dead runs alone or remove them where they are exposed. We do not touch household electrical wiring; if a new outlet is needed for the recorder, that is an electrician's job and we say so on the quote. New camera cable follows its own path and is labeled at both ends.

Our building faces Owen F. Dolen Park. Do we need anyone's permission to have a camera that sees part of the park?
+

A camera mounted on your own building and aimed at your entrance and sidewalk can show the edge of the park in the background without anyone's permission beyond the building owner's. What you cannot do is fix a camera to the park's fences, lamps or trees. Keep the view on your door and steps rather than on the paths and benches, and mask any part of the frame that looks into a neighbor's window.

Can a storefront install be scheduled around Fair @ The Square or other BID events on the plaza?
+

Yes. Tell us the dates of any event that fills the sidewalk in front of your store and we will book the outside work for another day. Interior work in the ceiling or back room can often go ahead during busy days because it stays inside, and we can start early or work after closing when ladders would block customers.

What do we receive at the end of a Westchester Square install?
+

Personal logins for each person who should see cameras, with views limited to what each one needs, the phone app set up on their devices, a written list of every camera and what it covers, the recorder's location and how long it keeps video, and a short lesson in finding and exporting a clip. Remote accounts from any previous installer are removed, and the owner keeps the main administrator login.

Will we have a written record of where every camera cable runs after the job?
+

Yes. Each cable is labeled at the recorder or switch and at the camera with a plain name, and the handover sheet lists which port feeds which camera and the general route it takes, for example up the rear closet to the attic or across the cellar ceiling to the front wall. That way a future repair, a new owner or a landlord's question can be answered without guessing.
