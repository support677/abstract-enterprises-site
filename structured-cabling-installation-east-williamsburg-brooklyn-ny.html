The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

East Williamsburg is an old place with a new argument about its name. In 1835 this ground became the Third District of the Village of Williamsburgh, and before that it was part of the Dutch settlement of Boswijck, which is why streets such as Devoe, Meserole and Vandervoort carry the names of early landowners. By the 1920s, CityNeighborhoods notes, it had become one of New York's densest industrial zones. Wikipedia points out that much of the area is still called Bushwick, Williamsburg or Greenpoint, and that the term East Williamsburg has been falling out of use since the 1990s, and the guides draw its edges differently: one runs it from Flushing Avenue to the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway and east to the Queens line, another defines it largely by the industrial park. For a cabling job none of that changes the price, which is our Brooklyn rate whatever your lease calls the block. What it does explain is why so many buildings here were designed for machines, freight and steam, and why getting a modern network into them takes planning.

The single most useful line on any map of the neighborhood is Morgan Avenue. Corcoran's guide describes it as the divider: east of Morgan toward Newtown Creek is zoned explicitly for industry as part of the East Williamsburg Industrial Park, while the blocks to the west hold most of the commercial and residential buildings. That zoning is enforced in spirit as well as on paper. City Limits reported that a 2003 study found roughly 500,000 square feet of industrial space in the area had been illegally converted to homes in just a few years, and that when a developer tries to convert space in the zone from industrial to residential, the city will oppose it. So the east side stays a place of warehouses, food plants, workshops and, increasingly, offices and studios carved out of factory floors. The west side mixes walk-up tenements, loft buildings that went residential long ago, and storefront rows along Graham Avenue and Grand Street. A cabling plan for a warehouse on Morgan Avenue and one for a loft office two blocks west may share nothing but the cable.

The conversions keep coming, and each one is a cabling project. Bushwick Daily reported that The Paper Mill at 456 Johnson Avenue, a paper mill built in 1931, was planned as 35 commercial office units after a gut renovation, and listed 455 Jefferson, 95 Evergreen and 215 Moore Street among other creative offices on the way in East Williamsburg and Bushwick. Brooklyn Paper reported that Netflix's 170,000-square-foot facility at 333 Johnson Avenue would hold six sound stages along with editing and meeting rooms. A commercial listing for a small lot at 16 Harrison Place mentions that companies like Netflix and Rivian have recently landed in the neighborhood. PincusCo reported a proposal for a fourteen-story building at 410 Morgan Avenue with about 92,958 square feet of light manufacturing space and 35,793 square feet of offices, with its zoning application filed on June 16, 2026. Every one of those spaces needs a network room, a backbone between floors, and horizontal runs to desks, cameras and access points, and every one of them is being built inside or beside walls that were poured or laid for a different century's work.

Then there is the question of how internet reaches the building at all. When NYCEDC launched its Connect IBZ program, it said the North Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg Industrial Business Zones were among the city's "digital deserts," and the network it backed was designed as a fiber core with a fixed-wireless edge, so that customers receive service wirelessly from network hubs through rooftop-mounted equipment. In practice that means some East Williamsburg buildings get their connection from a dish on the roof rather than a fiber or coax line in the basement, and the cabling job begins with a run from the roof down to the network room. In the neighboring district, the city's 2020 settlement with Verizon put Brooklyn Community District 4, Bushwick, on a priority list where a resident's request for paid Fios was supposed to be met generally within seven days; most of East Williamsburg sits in Community Board 1 instead, so whether that rule touches your address is something to check, not assume. ISP Reports lists Spectrum cable, Starry fixed wireless and T-Mobile fixed-wireless home internet for nearby Bushwick, and a filing Starry made with the SEC describes its system as relying on a building's existing wiring, including old twisted-pair wiring. The provider options in one building can be completely different from the one across the street, and the cabling has to fit whichever one you have.

The last reason is reliability. Bushwick Daily once quoted a resident complaining that internet had been down for nearly 24 hours in Bushwick and East Williamsburg. Greenpointers reported in February 2026 that hundreds of Williamsburg residents lost power after a manhole fire at 101 Montrose Avenue. StreetEasy lists 200 Morgan Avenue, a general warehouse, in hurricane evacuation Zone 1. None of those facts is unusual for a neighborhood beside a creek and an industrial waterfront, but together they decide where a sensible business puts its network equipment, whether that equipment needs battery backup, and whether a second internet line on a different technology is worth the cost. We raise all three on the survey, because a clean cable plant is not much use if the only switch is in a basement that floods or the only connection goes dark with the power.

Six East Williamsburg facts that reshape a cabling plan before a single drop is pulled

  • The industrial park is protected. East of Morgan Avenue, the city opposes industrial-to-residential conversions, according to City Limits. Warehouses, workshops and food plants stay, and their networks are built around loading docks, machinery and long, open floors.
  • Old factories are becoming offices. The 1931 paper mill at 456 Johnson Avenue was planned for 35 office units, and Bushwick Daily named 455 Jefferson, 95 Evergreen and 215 Moore Street among other creative offices coming to the area. Multi-tenant offices mean shared risers, separate tenant networks and a building owner who sets the rules.
  • Broadband can come from the roof. NYCEDC called the North Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg industrial zones "digital deserts" and backed a network that delivers service through rooftop-mounted equipment. That turns the roof into the first stop on the cable route.
  • Some service reuses the building's oldest wire. Starry's SEC filing describes its system as relying on existing building wiring, including old twisted pair. Whether that old copper is good enough is something a tester answers, not a guess.
  • Flood maps matter. StreetEasy places 200 Morgan Avenue, a warehouse, in hurricane evacuation Zone 1. Before a rack goes in any basement near the creek, we ask whether there is a better room above grade.
  • Power and service both fail sometimes. The February 2026 manhole fire on Montrose Avenue and the day-long outage Bushwick Daily described are reasons to plan battery backup and, for some businesses, a second provider on a second path.

Need Repair in East Williamsburg? Dead Ports After a Move-In, a Closet Full of Mystery Cable, or Drops That Quit When the Forklift Rolls By

The calls that cannot wait usually start with a new tenant. A company signs a lease on a floor in a converted factory, moves its desks in, plugs into the jacks already on the walls, and finds that half of them are dead, a few connect at a fraction of the speed they should, and nobody at the landlord's office knows where the other ends go. That is not unusual in a building that has had a dozen tenants in thirty years. Each one may have added cable, abandoned cable, or re-used cable for something else, and the labels, if there were any, describe a floor plan that no longer exists. A Yelp review in an East Williamsburg search for Ethernet wiring opens by saying the customer's home network wiring had been installed improperly, and we hear that story from businesses as often as from residents.

The second kind of urgent call comes from working floors. A data drop that runs along the floor to a packing station, a scale or a label printer gets crushed under a pallet jack, or a cable tie-wrapped to a sprinkler pipe works loose and sags into a truck's path. The third is the closet itself: a switch with no battery backup that rebooted during a power flicker and came back with its settings gone, or a patch panel so tangled that unplugging one cable takes down three desks. For all three, the first thing we do is find out which runs are actually good. A tone generator and probe tell us which cable at the panel is which jack on the floor, a certifier tells us whether each link still meets its rating, and from there you get a short list in writing of what can be re-terminated, what has to be re-pulled, and what is better abandoned and removed. Structured Cabling for East Williamsburg's Architecture

Six kinds of space account for almost every cabling call we get from East Williamsburg. They differ in what the floors, ceilings and walls are made of, how service gets into the building, and who besides you has a say over every hole. Those three things set the plan before anyone talks about cable categories.

The factory floor turned office suite

Old mills and plants carved into many office units, like the 1931 paper mill at 456 Johnson Avenue: big windows, open ceilings, shared shafts and a landlord who controls the risers.

The working warehouse east of Morgan

Single-tenant industrial buildings such as the 22,100-square-foot warehouse at 154 Morgan Avenue: long spans, high ceilings, loading doors and equipment on the floor.

The loft building with residents

Three-story factory buildings that became apartments, like the 51-unit building at 59 Bogart Street from 1931, where cabling serves homes, home offices and the building's own systems.

The avenue store building

Shops and restaurants on Graham Avenue, Grand Street and Morgan Avenue with offices or apartments above, where the network lives behind a counter and the ceiling belongs to someone else's floor.

The studio and the venue

Production spaces like the facility at 333 Johnson Avenue and concert halls like Brooklyn Steel on Frost Street, with editing rooms, ticketing, cameras and crowds.

The apartment and the walk-up

Three-story walk-ups west of Morgan and newer residential buildings around Cooper Park, where the job is a home network, a home office or a few runs a renter is allowed to make.

The factory floor turned office suite: many tenants, one set of shafts

The office conversions are where most of the neighborhood's new cabling demand comes from. A building like the Paper Mill on Johnson Avenue, planned for 35 office units, becomes a small town of separate businesses sharing one roof, one basement and a handful of vertical routes between floors. Each tenant wants its own network, its own internet service and its own locked equipment space, and the building owner wants all of that to happen without twenty contractors drilling twenty sets of holes through the same slab. The best results come when the owner sets up a shared backbone, a set of pathways or conduits from a main room to each floor, and every tenant's cabling plugs into it. When that has not happened, a single tenant's job has to start by asking the building where it is allowed to cross a floor, and the answer can add a day to the schedule.

Inside the suite, the open-plan look that makes these spaces attractive is the hardest part to cable cleanly. There is often no drop ceiling to hide cable above and no stud wall to fish it down. Cable travels along exposed timber beams or concrete ceilings on J-hooks or in a painted raceway, and drops to desks either down a column, inside a furniture whip, or up through a floor box where the owner allows coring the slab. We draw the route on a floor plan with you before quoting, because in a space this visible, the route is part of the design. The cable color, the hook spacing and the path along a beam line are decisions you will look at every day, and they are much cheaper to settle on paper than to move afterward.

The working warehouse east of Morgan: distance, dust and machinery

Industrial buildings in the park are big. LoopNet listed 32,300 square feet of industrial space at 134 Morgan Avenue in May 2026, and Commercial Observer reported Urban Umbrella taking all 22,100 square feet of the warehouse at 154 Morgan Avenue. In buildings that size, the first cabling question is distance. A copper Ethernet link has a length limit, so in a long building the network room has to be central or there has to be a second closet with a fiber or copper link back to the first. The office, the loading dock and the far corner of the floor may each want cameras, a scanner station, a printer or an access point, and putting all of their cable home to a closet in one corner can push the far runs past what the standard allows. We measure the actual route, up the wall, along the ceiling, around the columns and back down, because that route is always longer than a straight line on the floor plan.

The second question is what the cable has to survive. Warehouse ceilings carry sprinkler pipes, heaters, lights and sometimes cranes or conveyor supports, and nothing of ours should hang from any of them. Cable dropping to a workstation on the floor goes down a column inside conduit, not loose down a wall where a pallet can catch it. Near loading doors, where winter air and dust come straight in, we use enclosures and outdoor-rated cable where a camera or card reader sits on the exterior side. And because East Williamsburg's industrial park includes food businesses, EWVIDCO's 162 Cook Street building, for instance, is described as near Wonton Foods and Boar's Head, we ask whether any area has washdown or sanitation rules before choosing boxes and routes. A food plant can need sealed fittings where an office needs only a faceplate.

The loft building with residents: homes, home offices and the building's own systems

West of the park and around Morgantown, a number of factory buildings went residential decades ago. The building at 59 Bogart Street, built in 1931 with 51 units on three stories, is one example; Commercial Observer reported its sale for $30 million. Wikipedia notes that at the McKibbin Street Lofts the remaining floors were submitted for inclusion under the 2010 Loft Law, and that the Loft Board revoked registration for several basement units. In buildings like these, cabling serves two clients. Residents want a wired desk, a wired TV, an access point in the middle of a long loft and a clean path from wherever the provider's box landed. The owner wants the building's shared systems, a camera network, door access, a laundry room card reader, cabled to a closet that tenants cannot reach.

A loft apartment is usually one long room with brick walls on two sides and a heavy timber or concrete ceiling, which is exactly the space where Wi-Fi from a single router fades at the far end. A wired access point at the middle of the ceiling, fed by one cable from the router's corner, often does more than any extender. The run along a beam or a brick wall is visible, so we route it where it disappears into the room's lines. For the owner's side of the building, we keep shared-system cable out of residents' units entirely, running it in hallways, stair shafts and basements to a locked cabinet, so a tenant's renovation never cuts a camera line. If a building is still working through Loft Board legalization, we also ask whether the owner's architect has a plan for the common areas, because cable pulled this year should not end up inside a wall that the legalization work has to open next year.

The avenue store building: a counter, a back room and somebody else's ceiling

Graham Avenue, Grand Street, Metropolitan Avenue and the stretch of Morgan Avenue near the L station are lined with buildings that put a business on the ground floor and offices or apartments above. New York YIMBY described 101 Morgan Avenue, between Ingraham Street and Johnson Avenue, as a two-story commercial building with retail below and offices above. A storefront network is small, often a card terminal or two, a register, a back-office computer, a printer, a camera recorder and Wi-Fi for staff or customers, but it has to fit into very little space and survive the business's daily routine. The rack is usually a small wall cabinet in a back room or under a counter, and we keep it locked and ventilated, away from the sink, the mop and the delivery stack.

The ceiling of a storefront is the floor of the apartment or office above, and the landlord usually controls both. Cable stays in the ceiling space of your own unit or in the basement, and any opening into shared space is something we ask the owner about first. Restaurants and cafes need cable routed away from kitchen heat and grease, and sometimes a separate network for card terminals so a customer's phone on the guest Wi-Fi never shares a network with payments. The Graham Avenue and Grand Street business districts announced a partnership in 2025 to support the two corridors, and the state's $20 million Downtown Revitalization Initiative award for East Williamsburg is aimed at its commercial corridors, so we expect more storefront fit-outs and renovations in the next few years. A storefront that renovates is the right moment to put cable inside walls instead of along them.

The studio and the venue: big files, big crowds and short windows

East Williamsburg has become a place where things get filmed, edited and performed. Brooklyn Paper reported that the Netflix facility at 333 Johnson Avenue would include six sound stages along with editing and meeting rooms. Brooklyn Steel, at 319 Frost Street, is housed in a repurposed steel fabrication shop with a movable stage system, three full-service bars and a mezzanine. Haute Living reported that Brooklyn Pacha opens on November 6 and 7, 2026, in the former Great Hall at 140 Stewart Avenue, with an indoor room for about 2,500 people. We do not name any of these as customers. What they show is the kind of space the neighborhood now holds: rooms where editing workstations push large media files across a network all day, and halls where ticket scanning, bars, cameras and staff radios all depend on wired infrastructure that has to work on a night when thousands of phones are in the room.

Both kinds of space put the backbone first. An editing suite wants short, high-grade links between workstations and a central storage room, and often fiber between floors or between buildings on the same lot. A venue wants wired connections for every point-of-sale terminal and scanner instead of trusting Wi-Fi in a crowd, plus cable to cameras at entrances, bars and exits. Both also have short work windows. A studio may have a production schedule that rules out drilling on certain days, and a venue may only be available between load-out and the next afternoon. We plan the pull in stages so each stage leaves the space fully usable, and we confirm the schedule in writing so nobody is surprised by a lift in the middle of a rehearsal.

The apartment and the walk-up: a home network, a home office and what a renter may touch

Around the industrial core, East Williamsburg still has the residential blocks that housed its factory workers. New York YIMBY described the structures near 101 Morgan Avenue as mainly three-story walk-up tenements, and newer buildings such as Cooper Park Commons at 89 Maspeth Avenue, an eighteen-story, fully affordable building with 311 homes, have gone up nearby. Residential cabling here is usually modest: a wired line from the provider's box to a desk, a cable to a TV, an access point in the middle of the apartment, or a cable to a camera at a private door. The work is careful rather than large, because in an older walk-up the walls may be plaster on lath or brick, and the provider's box may have landed in the worst possible corner.

Most residents rent, and that changes what we can do. A Yelp review in a Brooklyn search for Ethernet cable describes an installer running two Ethernet cables under the molding from one room to another, and that kind of low-impact route, along baseboards, behind trim, in a paintable cover or through a closet, is often the right answer in a rental. We do not open walls in a rented apartment without the owner's agreement. In public and affordable housing, including the Williamsburg Houses, Cooper Park Houses and other developments in and around the neighborhood, common areas and shared building systems belong to the housing operator, and we work only inside a resident's own unit or where the operator has hired us. If you are not sure which side of that line a job falls on, ask us before booking; we will tell you plainly.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Half Hour of an East Williamsburg Cabling Survey

These notes describe how our technicians survey buildings like East Williamsburg's. They describe our practice, not a story about any single address.

Start where the service comes in, not where the desks are. The technician asks to see the provider's equipment before looking at the office. In this neighborhood that can be a cable box in a basement, a panel on a ground-floor wall by the loading door, a splitter in a hallway or a dish and a weatherproof box on the roof. That point fixes one end of every route, and it often decides where the rack should go.

Look up, then look for the sprinkler pipe. On an open factory floor, the ceiling tells you what kind of pathway is possible: heavy timber beams take hooks screwed into wood, a concrete ceiling needs anchors set into the slab, and a steel deck needs beam clamps. The sprinkler mains and branch lines show where cable must not go, because nothing we install hangs from, ties to or rests on fire protection piping.

Find every way between floors. In multi-story buildings the technician walks the stairs and shafts looking for existing sleeves, abandoned conduits, old freight elevator machine rooms and chases that earlier tenants used. Re-using a sound existing route is cheaper and less disruptive than coring a new hole through a slab.

Count the walls that cannot be crossed casually. Fire-rated walls around stairs and shafts, walls between tenants and the building's outer walls each need a different kind of opening, and every opening through a rated wall has to be sealed again. The survey marks each one on the plan.

Ask about water and power. Has the basement ever flooded? Where does the building's power come in, and has the space lost power in the last few years? The answers decide whether the rack goes downstairs at all and how much battery backup it needs.

Leave with a drawing. Before leaving, the technician sketches the main route with you, so the written quote describes a path you have already seen and agreed to.

Pathways

From the Demarc to the Desk: How Cable Travels Through East Williamsburg Buildings

A structured cabling system has the same parts everywhere: an entrance where service arrives, a room where the main equipment lives, backbone cable between that room and any other closets, horizontal cable from a closet to each outlet, and the outlets themselves. What makes East Williamsburg different is the material every one of those parts has to pass through. Here is how each kind of construction in the neighborhood changes the work.

Heavy timber. Many of the older factories and lofts were built with thick timber posts and beams and wooden floor decks. Timber is friendly to cable in one way: hooks and supports screw directly into it, and a small hole through a wooden floor for a cable is quick to drill. It is unfriendly in another: those beams are the building's structure, and the owner, the architect and often the building code care about every hole in them. We do not drill through structural beams to save a few feet of cable. Cable runs along the beams or under the floor deck between them, and where it has to pass from one floor to the next, it goes through an approved opening that is sealed afterward.

Reinforced concrete. Later factories and warehouses often have concrete columns, beams and slabs. Anchors for J-hooks and supports are set into the concrete with a hammer drill, and every hole is placed away from where the slab's steel is likely to be. When a cable has to pass through a slab, a core drill cuts a clean round sleeve, and any core through a slab or a structural wall is something the building owner approves first, because drilling into the wrong spot can hit reinforcing steel or a buried conduit.

Brick and block walls. Brick bearing walls and concrete block partitions are everywhere in the neighborhood, from walk-ups on the west side to warehouse offices in the park. They do not let cable be fished down inside them the way a stud wall does. Cable to an outlet on a brick or block wall either drops in surface raceway or conduit, or arrives from the floor or ceiling directly behind furniture. Where the cable has to pass through the wall, we core a sleeve rather than hammering a ragged hole, and we put the sleeve where the next contractor can re-use it.

Steel deck and steel framing. Some newer additions and many warehouse roofs sit on steel joists and corrugated deck. Cable supports clamp to the steel rather than screwing into it, and any opening in a deck that separates floors is sealed like any other floor penetration. Steel buildings also tend to have long, clear spans, which makes them easy to pathway but easy to overrun, because the cable has so far to go.

Drywall partitions inside old shells. When a factory floor becomes offices, the new walls between suites and meeting rooms are usually drywall on metal studs. These are the easiest walls to hide cable in, and the only ones where an outlet can sit flush with nothing visible on the surface. They are also the walls most likely to be fire-rated between tenants, so a technician checks the drawings or asks the owner before treating one as an ordinary partition.

Every hole gets closed again

Every time cable passes through a fire-rated wall or a floor, it makes a hole in a barrier that was put there to slow fire and smoke. Those openings are sealed with firestop material made for the purpose, sized to the opening and the cable bundle, so the barrier works again once the job is done. In a multi-tenant building that is not only good practice; it is usually a condition of the owner allowing the work at all. We seal each penetration as we go, not at the end of the job when it is easy to miss one, and we note them on the drawing so the next person to add cable knows where the sleeves are.

Cable that shares a ceiling with heat, light and power

Old industrial ceilings are crowded. Electrical conduit, lighting, unit heaters, ductwork and sprinkler pipe all compete for the same space. Data cable is kept apart from electrical lines and away from the heat of unit heaters and lamps, supported on its own hooks or tray, and never laid on top of ducts or lights. Where a ceiling space is used to move air for heating or cooling, the cable has to be rated for that space, and we check which ceilings in your building are being used that way before choosing the cable. All of this sounds fussy until you remember that the cable is meant to last through several generations of the equipment plugged into it.

Rules and Licenses

The 2025 Permit Change, Alarm Licensing and Landlord Approval: What Applies to an East Williamsburg Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and this is not legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before work starts.

Data cabling and the electrical permit. Local Law 128 of 2024 added a section to the city's Administrative Code, §28-105.4.7, which took effect on December 21, 2025. It says an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits. A hiring guide for low-voltage work summarizes the condition attached to that exemption: the work must be performed by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. For most data cabling, that means no electrical permit filing, but it does not mean anyone with a spool of cable is doing legal work.

Cameras, access control and alarms. A 2026 roadmap on New York low-voltage licensing draws the line this way: pure structured cabling under 50 volts is generally exempt from state licensing, while burglar alarms, fire alarms, CCTV and access control require a state license under Article 6-D. AESS holds New York State license #12000287431. When a cabling job includes camera or door-access runs, the same licensed company is responsible for those systems, rather than a cabling crew pulling wire for someone else to connect later.

Fire alarm wiring is its own world. One NYC cabling guide notes that fire alarm wiring falls outside the low-voltage exemption and requires specialized licensing and additional permits. If your building's renovation includes fire alarm work, that work is filed and installed under its own rules. We keep data cable separate from fire alarm wiring in every pathway and never use fire alarm cable routes for network cable.

The building owner's rules. In multi-tenant factory buildings, the owner usually controls the roof, the basement, shafts and any opening between floors, and may have a contractor agreement covering insurance, working hours and how penetrations are sealed. Most landlords want a certificate of insurance naming them and their managing agent before any crew arrives, and we send ours ahead of time. If your lease requires the landlord's approval for alterations, get it in writing before booking, and tell the landlord exactly which openings the cable route needs; our survey drawing is usually enough for that conversation.

Industrial zoning does not change the cable. The Industrial Business Zone governs how a building may be used, not how its network is wired. A legal office inside the park gets the same cabling as an office anywhere else. What the zone does affect is the neighbors: your cable may share a ceiling with a workshop or a food business, which is one more reason to keep the pathway tidy and well supported.

Where internet enters the building, the room for the rack, the ceilings, your cross streets, floor and square footage, and how many desks, cameras, access points and screens need cable.

2. Walk the pathways

A technician walks from the service entrance or roof to every work area with you or the super, noting timber, concrete, brick, block, existing conduit and fire-rated walls.

3. Written design and quote

Rack location, cable type, drop count, pathway method and every penetration, priced at the Brooklyn rate with payment terms, on paper before anything is ordered.

4. Settle access and paperwork

Building hours, freight elevator or dock, roof and basement access, landlord approval and the certificate of insurance, all confirmed before the crew is booked.

5. Build the pathway

Sleeves cored through block and brick, J-hooks or conduit installed, and every opening through a rated wall or floor sealed with firestop as the work goes.

6. Pull, terminate and label

Cable pulled from staged boxes, terminated on patch panels and jacks, and labeled at both ends to match a printed port map.

7. Certify every link

Each run tested with a certifier; any link that fails is re-terminated or re-pulled before the crew leaves.

8. Hand over and walk through

Port map and test results handed over, rack dressed and identified, the area cleaned, and a walk-through with you or your IT provider.

FAQ

East Williamsburg Structured Cabling FAQ

What photos help you scope an East Williamsburg cabling job before anyone visits?
+

Send a photo of where internet enters the building, the spot you want for the rack with its outlet in view, the ceiling of the main work area looking straight up, and anything already on the walls such as old jacks or raceway. Add your cross streets, floor, rough square footage and a count of devices that need cable, and we can usually tell you what kind of pathway the building will need before the survey.

Our landlord wants a certificate of insurance before any contractor starts. Can you send one?
+

Yes. Send us the exact names and address the landlord or managing agent wants on the certificate, and we send it before the crew is scheduled. Many East Williamsburg building owners also have a contractor agreement covering hours and penetrations, and we are glad to review it with you ahead of time.

Our lease lists the address as Bushwick but the building is in the industrial park. Does that change the rate?
+

No. Whether your lease says Bushwick, Williamsburg or East Williamsburg, the job is billed at our Brooklyn rate. What affects the quote is the building itself: its construction, the distance cable has to travel and how many openings the route needs.

Who arranges access to the roof and basement in a multi-tenant building?
+

Usually the building owner or super controls both, so we ask you to put us in touch with them before the survey. We confirm the access times, any escort requirement and where the provider's equipment sits, and we put the agreed access plan in the written quote so the crew is not left waiting at a locked door.

Can we buy our own switches and access points and have you install them?
+

Yes. We install customer-supplied equipment at labor-only rates in the written quote. Tell us the models before the visit so we can check that switches can power your cameras and access points and that the battery unit is large enough for the load.

Can the conference-room screen be mounted on the same visit as the cabling?
+

Yes. Mounting a screen up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs is $179.99 on our Brooklyn list, brick adds $65 and an in-wall power and cable kit is $169, with the bracket extra unless you supply a rated one. The data cable to the screen is part of the cabling quote, so both are finished together.

How messy is the work, and what happens to the dust from coring?
+

Coring and anchoring make dust, so we cover desks and equipment near each hole, use vacuum attachments where the drill allows, and clean each area before moving on. Ceiling tiles that come down are put back, scrap cable and boxes leave with the crew, and the space is swept before the walk-through.

Does the super or a building rep need to be there while the crew works?
+

Only if the building requires it. Many owners want someone present for roof, basement or shaft work, and some want to see each penetration before it is sealed. We find out during scheduling and plan the day around that person's availability.

How far ahead of a move-in date should we reach out?
+

As early as you have a floor plan and a lease. We book the first conversation within 2 hours of your request, but the survey, the landlord's approval and any equipment orders all take time, and a building that needs new openings between floors takes longest. Tell us your move-in date on the first call and we will tell you honestly whether it is realistic.

Will you work with the IT company that already manages our computers?
+

Yes, and it usually makes the job smoother. We agree with your IT provider on the rack layout, port counts and equipment before the visit, label everything to the scheme they prefer, and hand them the port map and test results at the end so they can take over the configuration.

What if the crew finds something unexpected in an old factory ceiling, like crumbling insulation?
+

The crew stops work in that area and tells you and the building owner right away. Old industrial buildings can contain materials that should only be handled by specialists, and we do not disturb anything suspect. We re-route around it if a safe path exists, or wait until the owner has it assessed.

Can you remove dead cable that earlier tenants left in the ceiling?
+

Yes. After tracing and testing, cable that serves nothing can be cut out and taken away, which frees space in crowded ceilings and keeps the next contractor from mistaking it for live cable. We list what will be removed in the quote, and anything that belongs to the building or another tenant stays unless the owner approves its removal.
