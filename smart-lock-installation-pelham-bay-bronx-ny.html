Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Built Around the Commute

Pelham Bay was built because the train came, and it still runs on the train’s timetable. That terminal is the Pelham Bay Park station at Bruckner Boulevard and Westchester Avenue, which opened on December 20, 1920 as the end of the line and is still the northern terminus of the 6. Buhre Avenue station opened the same day. Ask most households here how their weekday works and the answer starts with which train or which bus they catch.

The houses the trains brought are mostly freestanding. City Planning counted detached homes as about 72 percent of the residential stock in the rezoning area, which runs from Pelham Parkway South down to Middletown Road and from the Hutchinson River Parkway across to the Bruckner Expressway. Semi-detached houses made up about 17 percent of residential structures, attached houses about 6.6 percent and multifamily buildings about 4.5 percent of residential lots, with the apartment buildings lining the wider avenues. The City Council adopted the rezoning on March 22, 2006, mapping much of the inner blocks as R5A, a district that allows only detached one- and two-family homes, precisely to protect the large detached houses residents feared would be knocked down for denser buildings.

Put those two facts together and you have the job we are usually called to Pelham Bay for. A detached house has more doors than an apartment — the front door, a side or back door, often a door up from the garage or basement — and on a weekday nobody is behind any of them. The city comptroller’s neighborhood economic profile for this community district put the typical commute at 45.5 minutes in the 2012–2016 survey years, up from 41 minutes, with roughly as many residents getting to work by public transit as by car. Add the walk to the station or the express bus at either end and a working couple is out of the house for ten or eleven hours. During those hours the house still receives people: a cleaner, a plumber, a dog walker, a parent dropping off a grandchild, a child back from school. The question is how they get in, and whether you know when they leave.

That is what a smart lock is for in this neighborhood. Not as a gadget on the front door, but as the thing that replaces the key under the flowerpot, the copy given to the contractor three jobs ago and the 4 p.m. phone call asking “did you lock the back?” We set each visitor up with their own code that works only in their hours, have the bolt throw itself behind them, and tune the alerts so the phone in your pocket on the train tells you something useful: that the house was opened by the person you expected, at the time you expected, and that it is locked again.

Pelham Bay is also an established neighborhood, with a longstanding Italian American community and a sizable Greek one; the Greek American Institute and the Catholic schools associated with St. Theresa are part of its fabric. What we measure and test on a Pelham Bay door

  • The bore that is already there: diameter and backset of the existing deadbolt hole. Common smart deadbolts expect a 2-1/8 inch bore and a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset; a door that has worn four locks may be oversized, off-centre or patched.
  • How thick the slab is: we put calipers on the door because makers list a minimum and maximum. Solid old front doors sometimes exceed the top of that range, while a thin back or cellar door can fall below it, and either result narrows the models we can offer or adds a spacer or longer-bolt kit.
  • What sits in front of the door: an aluminium storm door or an iron gate changes where the keypad can be reached and whether a deep inside unit leaves room for the storm door’s own handle.
  • Glass within reach: sidelights and glazed panels next to the lock decide whether a thumb-turn could be reached through a broken pane, and therefore which inside hardware makes sense.
  • Self-latching: we let the door swing shut from a few inches open. If it will not latch on its own, the strike or hinges are corrected before any motor is asked to do the work a carpenter should.
  • Signal at the slab: a phone on the door itself shows whether the router reaches it; brick walls, foil-faced insulation and a router in the cellar all count against it.

Owners redoing a two-family or a store-and-apartment building often bring the lock into a larger entry project. The Empty House

The House Between Trains: Running a Pelham Bay Door While Everyone Is Out

Most smart lock marketing pictures someone standing at the door. In Pelham Bay the more useful picture is the door with nobody at it. A household that leaves for the 6 or the BxM8 in the morning and comes back after dark spends most of its waking weekday hours away from home. The lock’s real work happens in those hours, and it can be planned as carefully as a train timetable.

We start every Pelham Bay walkthrough by writing out the house’s weekday on one sheet: who leaves when, who comes in while the house is empty, which door they use and how long they stay. The plan for the locks falls out of that sheet. Below is the kind of schedule we end up with for a working couple with one child at school and a parent who visits, with the settings each line calls for. Your times will differ; the logic will not.

Three habits make that schedule work. First, one person per code. If the cleaner and the dog walker share a code, the log cannot tell you which of them came, and taking one away takes both. Second, give each code the narrowest window that still fits. A Tuesday-morning cleaner does not need a Saturday-night code, and if hers is ever overheard it is useless outside her hours. Third, put the visitors on the door you want them to use. A contractor who comes and goes from the truck all day is better on the back or side door with an auto-lock, leaving the front door untouched and its log clean.

The alerts deserve as much thought as the codes. A family that turns on every notification gets twenty or thirty a day and stops reading them by the end of the first week, which defeats the point. For a commuting household we usually leave on four kinds: a code used outside its schedule or by a user you flagged, a door left open, repeated wrong codes or a tamper warning, and low battery. Arrivals of people you expect go into the log without buzzing your phone, except the ones you actually want to hear about, such as a child getting home. You can always open the app and read the day’s history on the ride home.

Finally, decide what happens when the plan breaks. A train delayed for an hour, a cleaner who comes on Wednesday instead, a child locked out because the battery died. We set an administrator on two phones so one of you can always change a schedule from wherever you are, leave a mechanical key with someone nearby, and show you how to issue a one-off code in under a minute. A plan that only works on a good day is not a plan.

What the commute changes about the hardware

  • Remote access matters more than usual. A Bluetooth-only lock with no bridge cannot be checked or changed from the train. In a house that is empty all day we fit a Wi-Fi lock, a bridge or a Matter-over-Thread setup so the lock is reachable from anywhere.
  • Door sensing is worth having. A lock that knows whether the door is open, not only whether the bolt is out, can tell you a contractor left the side door ajar, and can refuse to throw its bolt into an open doorway.
  • Battery life gets used up faster. Many visitors and many alerts mean many motor cycles and radio wake-ups. We tell you what to expect and set low-battery warnings to go to two phones.

For a house that is empty all day, a camera that shows who used the code is the natural partner to the lock. Our camera crews for Pelham Bay houses fit a doorway camera at the same visit when you want one.

Rules a Pelham Bay business door has to meet

  • Free exit without a key: the inside of any door people leave by opens with a lever, thumb-turn or push bar, without a key, a code or special knowledge. A keypad on the outside never changes that.
  • Accessible hardware: where a public entrance has to meet accessibility standards, operable parts should work without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist and sit between 34 and 48 inches above the floor.
  • Electric strikes on egress doors: fail-secure strikes keep a door locked from outside during a power cut while the inside lever still opens it; magnetic locks raise more questions and are a matter for the building and its code consultant, not a shop-owner decision on the day.
  • Apartments above the store: if the building has three or more apartments it is a multiple dwelling, and the tenants’ street door has its own rules, separate from the shop door.
  • Entry data: a log of staff codes is business data. Decide who can read it and for how long it is kept before the first employee is enrolled.

Shops on these avenues that move to keyless doors often add a camera on the register and the door at the same time. Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Pelham Bay: Questions Answered

These are the questions Pelham Bay owners and shopkeepers ask once the lock is chosen and they start thinking about living with it. They are grouped by what they are really about. Price, booking and practical housekeeping questions are in the FAQ further down.

Alerts, schedules and the working day

Our phones buzz every time anyone opens the door — can the lock tell us only about the openings that matter?

Yes, and it is the first thing we change for commuting households. Most lock apps let you choose notifications per user and per event rather than all or nothing. A sensible set for a house that is empty all day is: an alert when a particular user arrives (a child home from school), an alert when any code is used outside its schedule, an alert for repeated wrong codes or tampering, an alert if a door stays open longer than a few minutes, and a low-battery warning. Everything else — your own comings and goings, the grandparent who drops by — goes quietly into the log.

Some apps also offer a daily summary, or let you see the whole day’s history in one list. Where yours does not, a two-minute look at the log on the ride home does the same job. What you should not do is leave every alert on and train yourself to ignore them; the one that matters will arrive in a pile of ones that do not.

Our cleaner’s Tuesday code started and stopped an hour off after the clocks changed — why would that happen?

Scheduled codes run on the lock’s own clock, and that clock is set by the app, a bridge or a hub when the lock connects. If the lock’s time zone was never set correctly, or the lock has not synced since daylight saving time began or ended, its idea of “9 a.m.” can be an hour away from yours. A lock that has been offline for weeks can drift for the same reason.

The fix is usually simple: open the lock settings, confirm the time zone is Eastern, and let the lock sync through its bridge or your phone. We set and check the time zone at handover and note it on the handover card. If schedules keep drifting after that, the lock is losing its connection, which is a signal problem worth fixing for its own sake.

Our children’s after-school code is set for weekdays — what happens on snow days and school holidays?

A weekday schedule does not know the school calendar. On a snow day or a holiday the children’s code still works from the start of its window, which is usually fine, but it may not work outside it, which is not. If your children’s code runs only from 2:30 to 6 on weekdays, a child at home on a snow day who steps out to a friend’s house at noon could be locked out on return.

There are three ways round it. Give children an unscheduled code or fingerprint and rely on arrival alerts instead of time windows; widen the window to cover the school day, accepting a little less restriction; or keep the schedule and plan to change it from the app on the morning of a closure. Most families with children at PS 71 or PS 14 end up choosing the first, because a locked-out child is a bigger problem than a code that works at lunchtime.

We spend several weeks a year with family abroad — can we add and delete codes from overseas, or only on our home network?

If the lock is reachable over the internet — a Wi-Fi lock, a lock with a Wi-Fi bridge, or a Matter lock with a border router at home — you can manage codes from anywhere your phone has data. The phone talks to the maker’s service or to your home hub, which passes the change to the lock. A Bluetooth-only lock with no bridge can be changed only by someone standing near the door.

Before a long trip, check two things: that the lock shows as online in the app away from your home Wi-Fi (switch your phone to mobile data at the curb and try it), and that someone nearby holds a mechanical key in case the lock goes offline while you are away. We test both at handover for any household that tells us it travels.

Security questions people actually ask

Could someone standing outside with a radio gadget trick a smart lock into opening?

The real-world risk sits mainly with “hands-free” or “auto-unlock” features that open the door because your phone or fob is nearby. In May 2022 the security firm NCC Group published a Bluetooth Low Energy relay attack: equipment that forwards the radio conversation between a phone and a lock so the lock believes the phone is next to it. NCC reported successful exploits against a Tesla Model 3 and Y and against the Kwikset/Weiser Kevo smart lock, and said the problem was not an ordinary bug a patch could fix.

Its advice for users was to switch off passive unlock features that do not require an explicit action, such as tapping a button in the app. That is our default too. On a Pelham Bay house that sits empty all day we leave proximity auto-unlock off unless you specifically want it, and rely on codes, fingerprints or a deliberate tap in the app. Keypad codes and fingerprints are not affected by a relay of this kind.

The whole family logs in to the lock app with one shared email and password — what is wrong with that?

Three things. Anyone who knows that password has full control of the door, including the ability to delete everyone else’s access. The log cannot tell you which family member made a change, because they all appear as the same account. And if the password leaks from some other site where it was reused, a stranger has your front door.

The better arrangement is one owner account with two-factor sign-in turned on, a second full administrator on a different person’s phone so you are never locked out of the settings, and everyone else invited as an ordinary user or given a code. We move households onto that arrangement at installation, and when we take over an existing lock we start by changing the shared password.

The keypad locks when anyone touches the logo — could a passer-by or a child lock someone out by mistake?

Many keypad deadbolts have a one-touch locking feature: tap the logo or a lock button outside and the bolt throws. It is convenient when you leave in a hurry for the train. It also means anyone at the door can lock it, which matters on a house where a relative inside has no key and no code, or on a door where a child plays on the stoop.

Locking someone in is not a risk, because the inside thumb-turn always opens the lock. Locking someone out is. If anyone in the house regularly steps outside without a code or phone, either give them a code or turn one-touch locking off and rely on auto-lock with a short delay instead. We go through this setting at handover rather than leaving it at the factory default.

At night we would like the door to stay shut even to someone who has a valid code — is that possible?

Many smart deadbolts have a privacy, vacation or “lock-out” mode, set from the inside or the app, that disables keypad codes until it is turned off. It suits a household that gives out several visitor codes during the day and wants none of them to work overnight, or a family going away that wants every code silenced at once rather than deleted one by one.

Two cautions. Check whether the mode also blocks the mechanical key or the app on your model, because some do and some do not. And make sure everyone who might come home late knows it may be on. Schedules can do much the same job automatically by ending every visitor code at a set hour, which is often the simpler choice.

Phones, deliveries and records

Our family is half iPhone and half Android — will one lock work well for everyone?

Usually, but it decides which lock you should buy. Brand apps such as those from the big lock makers run on both iPhone and Android, so anyone can be invited as a user. The friction comes with phone-wallet keys and home platforms: Apple’s home key works only on Apple devices, and a lock set up only in Apple Home or only in Google Home leaves the other half of the family on the outside.

A Matter lock can be shared with more than one ecosystem at once, so an iPhone household member and an Android one can both see it in their own app, though not every feature appears in every app. For a mixed household we usually keep the lock maker’s app as the main control, add Matter or a platform for convenience, and make sure nobody depends on a phone-only feature for getting in.

Should we sign up for one of the delivery programs that let a courier open the garage while we are at work?

It depends on what is behind the garage door. Amazon’s Key In-Garage Delivery, for instance, works with a compatible smart garage opener: when the driver arrives they scan the package, Amazon checks the package and the driver’s location, the linked garage opens, the parcel goes inside and the door closes, with notifications at each step and an optional camera to watch. For a Pelham Bay house with a detached garage at the back of the lot and nothing valuable inside, that can end parcels left on the stoop.

Where the garage is tucked under the house with a door up into the living space, the garage is really part of the house. In that case the door between the garage and the house should be locked by its own smart deadbolt whenever the household is out, and set to relock if it is opened, so a delivery program can never become a way into the kitchen.

Can the lock’s entry history line up with our camera recordings so we can see who used each code?

Yes, if both systems keep the right time. The lock log records which user unlocked at what minute; the camera records what happened at the door. If both clocks are correct, finding the clip that matches a log entry takes seconds. Some platforms join the two automatically, showing the code user beside the video event; others need you to look the time up in each app.

For shops on Westchester and Crosby avenues this is often the main reason to go keyless: a dispute about who opened up late, or who was in the back after closing, is settled by the log and the footage together. We set both clocks, test the match at handover and, where the camera system supports it, link the lock so its events appear on the recording timeline.

Delivery riders collect orders at our restaurant’s side door all evening — should they get a code, or should the door stay staffed?

Keep riders out of the code list. Riders change from night to night and work for several platforms, so any code they hold is effectively public within a week, and a door they can open is a door into your kitchen and stockroom. The workable pattern is a side door that stays locked from outside, with staff opening it from inside when a rider knocks or rings, and a shelf or pass-through just inside for bagged orders.

Staff themselves can have their own codes for the same door, scheduled to their shifts, so the log shows who let deliveries out on a given night. If the volume justifies it, a video doorbell or intercom at the side door lets the person at the counter see the rider before opening. The inside of the door must always open freely for anyone leaving.

Does a smart lock add anything to what a Pelham Bay house appraises for?

We have not found any published data showing that a smart lock changes an appraisal in this neighborhood, and we would not suggest buying one for that reason. Appraisals here turn on the house, the lot, the condition and the sales around it. A lock is a small fixture by comparison and a buyer may well replace it.

What a smart lock does do at sale time is make the handover cleaner: you can delete every code, give the buyer the administrator role or factory-reset the lock, and hand over the mechanical keys, so the new owner starts with a door nobody else can open. If you are planning to sell soon, choose a mainstream model with long app support rather than a niche one a buyer may not recognize.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Pelham Bay

Ask a chatbot or skim the cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and LawnStarter and the same seven claims about smart locks keep coming back. We were unable to load Google’s AI Overview itself when this page was built, so each heading below quotes a claim made in that published material, and the paragraphs under it test the claim against the doors, commutes and households we actually meet in Pelham Bay.

“Smart lock installation costs around $330”

That is LawnStarter’s national average, with a typical range of $200 to $460 and labor of $50 to $110. It is a fair figure for a pre-drilled modern door in a suburb where the lock drops into the existing holes and pairs with a phone in the driveway.

On a Pelham Bay house the national number leaves out the parts that take time here: an oversized or patched bore from several earlier locks, a strike that has to be moved or reinforced, a storm door in the way, and the signal work to make the lock reachable from a train. It also leaves out the hour spent writing visitor schedules and tuning alerts, which is the part that makes the lock useful to a commuting family.

That is why we quote house doors flat after seeing them, and publish only the building-door figures that do not depend on the door’s history. The written quote is the bill.

“Installing a smart lock is a 30-minute DIY job”

Product reviews often say a smart deadbolt goes on with a screwdriver in half an hour, and on the right door it can. The screwdriver part is rarely what goes wrong. What goes wrong is a door that does not quite latch, so the motor strains and the battery drains; a bolt that catches on the strike in humid weather; or a lock that never reliably reaches the internet.

The other half of a good installation is setup. A family that installs its own lock usually ends up with one shared code, every alert switched on and no second administrator. None of that is visible on day one; all of it matters on the day something goes wrong while everyone is at work.

If you want to fit the lock yourself, do it, then check the latch with the door swung shut from a few inches open and test remote access on mobile data. If either fails, that is the part worth paying someone for.

“Auto-unlock makes your door open as you walk up”

Many locks offer it, and it is pleasant with full shopping bags from the avenue. It relies on the phone’s location and a Bluetooth handshake, and both can be wrong. Geofences fire late or early, and in 2022 NCC Group showed that Bluetooth proximity features on at least one smart lock could be fooled by a relay attack.

There is also a practical problem in a dense neighborhood: if your phone decides you have arrived while you are still at the corner, the door can sit unlocked until you reach it.

For a house that is empty all day we leave auto-unlock off by default and use auto-lock instead, which closes the door behind you without depending on where your phone thinks you are.

“Smart locks are easier to break into than regular locks”

The physical part of a good smart deadbolt is a deadbolt, graded under the same ANSI/BHMA residential standard as a mechanical one. What breaks most doors in practice is the frame and strike, not the cylinder, and that does not change with electronics.

The new risks are digital: a weak shared password, an old app no longer updated, a proximity feature left on. All three are setup choices, which is why we treat account setup as part of the installation.

Against that, a smart lock gives you things a mechanical lock never could: a record of every opening, a code you can cancel without changing the lock, and an alert when somebody tries wrong codes at two in the afternoon.

“Matter means any smart lock works with any phone”

Matter makes it possible for one lock to appear in more than one home platform, and Thread gives the lock a low-power mesh to talk over. It does not make every feature appear everywhere. Lock makers’ own guidance notes that feature support varies by app and that a Matter-over-Thread lock needs a Thread border router at home before it works.

In practice a mixed household often ends up with codes and schedules in the lock maker’s app and simple lock and unlock in the other platforms. That is fine as long as you know which app holds the full controls.

If someone tells you Matter means you will never need the maker’s app, ask them how you will set a Tuesday-only cleaner’s code without it.

“You can install a smart lock on any door”

Smart deadbolts are made for a standard range of door thickness and backset, and many Pelham Bay doors fall inside it. Plenty do not: older solid doors thicker than a lock’s listed range, secondary doors too thin to hold it, storm doors with too little room behind them, and doors whose old bore has been enlarged by a succession of locks.

Almost every door can take some form of keyless lock. The question is which form, and whether the door needs work first. That is what the walkthrough decides.

The one door type we will refuse is any door where the proposed lock would need a key or code to get out. Free exit from inside is not negotiable on any home or business door.

“A smart lock replaces your alarm system”

Marketing sometimes implies that a lock with alerts is a security system. It is not. A lock tells you about the lock: codes used, the bolt’s position and, on some models, whether the door is open. It says nothing about a window forced at the back of the house or a cellar door it is not fitted to.

In a detached house with several ground-floor windows and a garage, an alarm with contacts and motion detection covers the openings the lock never sees. The two work well together: some alarm platforms can arm or disarm when particular lock codes are used, so the cleaner’s code also disarms the system for her visit.

Our advice for an empty-all-day house is to treat the lock as the access system and the alarm as the detection system, and connect them where your equipment supports it.

Owners and Tenants

Pelham Bay Two-Families: Whose Door, Whose Codes, Whose Log

The R5A rules that cover Pelham Bay’s inner blocks allow two families in a detached house, and many houses here hold exactly that. The arrangement varies: an owner upstairs renting the ground floor, a retired couple downstairs with a married child above, a house let as two separate rentals. The lock plan has to follow who actually lives behind each door, and a few legal lines frame it. What follows is a summary to help you ask the right questions, not legal advice about your own lease.

Where the city’s rules draw the lines

  • Two families or three: New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building occupied by three or more families as a multiple dwelling. A two-family house is not one, so the multiple-dwelling rules on tenant-installed locks and on smart-access data do not reach it, while an avenue building with three or more apartments falls under them.
  • The owner’s duty to provide a lock: the city’s Housing Maintenance Code requires the owner of a dwelling to provide a key lock on each dwelling unit’s entrance door and at least one key; its peephole requirement does not apply to owner-occupied units in one- and two-family homes.
  • No lockouts, ever: changing a lock or deleting a code to keep a lawful occupant out is an illegal eviction under city law, whatever kind of building it is. Codes come off a tenant’s door when the tenancy has lawfully ended, not before.
  • Free exit: the city’s fire safety guidance tells residents that an apartment door must open from the inside without a key; we never fit a lock that needs a key or code to get out of any home.

How we usually set up an owner-occupied two-family

Each household gets its own account on its own door. The owner administers the shared entrance, if there is one, and the owner’s own unit; the tenant administers the tenant’s door or, if the owner keeps the account, has full use of a personal code and a mechanical key, and the owner agrees in writing not to read the tenant’s entry log. A shared front door gets one code per adult in both households, so either side can be changed without touching the other.

Where the two floors are family — parents and an adult child — we often put both units on one account with two administrators. That works until the day one floor is let to someone else, so we label each user clearly and keep the tenant-ready setup a five-minute change away.

The apartment buildings on the avenues

If you rent an apartment in a building with three or more units on Westchester or Crosby Avenue, the state’s Multiple Dwelling Law gives you the right to add your own lock to your door, in addition to the landlord’s, provided you give the landlord a duplicate key on request. A retrofit or keypad deadbolt can fit that rule if it keeps a key cylinder. Building owners who move a street door to app or fob entry fall under the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act and must publish a data retention and privacy policy. Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check Before Trusting a Door to Lock Itself

This is general practice from our installers rather than the story of one job. On a house that will be empty all day, auto-lock does the work a person used to do on the way out, so the door has to be able to close and latch reliably every time with nobody watching. Before we switch auto-lock on we close the door gently from a few inches open, then from half-way, then let it swing from fully open. If any of the three leaves the latch short of the strike, the bolt will fire into the frame and jam, or the lock will report locked on a door that is not.

Older Pelham Bay doors fail this test more often than new ones, usually because a hinge screw has pulled out of the jamb, the strike has drifted after years of slamming, or weather-stripping added later makes the door spring back. The fix is carpentry, not electronics: longer hinge screws into the framing, the strike moved or filed, the weather-strip trimmed. Only when the door passes all three swings do we turn auto-lock on and set its delay. It is the least visible part of the job and the one that decides whether you stop thinking about the door at all.

FAQ

Pelham Bay Smart Lock Installation FAQ

If the lock we bought online turns out to be wrong for our door, will you help us sort out a return or exchange?
+

Returns are between you and the shop that sold the lock, but we can make them easy. If you send the model before the walkthrough, we tell you whether it suits the door; if we find a problem on the day, we stop before opening the box so it can go back unused, and we tell you exactly which model or option would fit.

We are repainting the front door next month — should the smart lock go on before or after?
+

After, if you can wait. Painting around a fitted lock means masking or removing it, and fresh paint on the door edge and jamb can make a latch bind until it hardens. If the lock must go on first, we can fit it and you remove the two halves for painting, but leave the strike and latch area paint thin.

Do you also fit app-controlled padlocks for a backyard shed or a side gate?
+

Yes. Bluetooth padlocks and keypad padlocks suit sheds, gates and storage lockers where there is no power and no door to bore. They are simpler than a deadbolt and usually have no internet connection, so they do not report remotely; we can set them up with codes for the same people who use the house.

Is there a smart lock we can use without creating an online account with the manufacturer?
+

Yes. Some keypad deadbolts are programmed entirely at the lock, and some Matter locks can be run from a home platform you already use without a separate maker account. You give up remote changes from the maker’s app or some features, so we explain what you lose before you choose. For a house empty all day, most families still prefer remote access.

Our keypad came with a factory master or programming code — should it be changed?
+

Yes, always. Many keypad locks ship with a master or programming code printed in the manual or on a label, and anyone who knows the model can look it up. We change it at installation, keep it off the handover card and give it only to the administrator. If you fitted the lock yourself, check the manual and change it today.

How should we clean a keypad or fingerprint reader without damaging it?
+

Use a soft cloth lightly dampened with water, and dry it straight away. Avoid household sprays, solvents and abrasive pads, which can cloud a touchscreen or a fingerprint window and let moisture past the seals. Check the maker’s care instructions for your model, and wipe the keypad now and then so wear marks do not build up on the numbers you use.

Does a smart deadbolt need a different strike plate from the one already on our door frame?
+

Often, yes. Smart deadbolts come with their own strike, and its position and size may not match the old one. We fit the new strike where the bolt actually lands, fix it with long screws into the framing, and fill or cover any old mortise so the bolt has nothing to catch on. A motorized bolt needs a cleaner path than a hand-turned one.

How often should we change the codes on our door?
+

Change a code whenever you think someone outside its owner knows it, and delete visitor codes when the visitor no longer needs them. Household codes do not need changing on a calendar if each person keeps their own and they are not written by the door. A seasonal look through the user list in the app does more good than routine changes.
