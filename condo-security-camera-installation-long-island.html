Why Long Island Is Different

Long Island Condos Spread Out, So Their Cameras Have to Travel

A Manhattan condo board thinks about cameras floor by floor. A Long Island condo board thinks about them acre by acre. East of the city line, the typical condominium is a cluster of two- and three-story buildings set around parking courts, with a clubhouse, a pool, a row of mailbox kiosks and, in many communities, a gatehouse at the end of a private entrance road. The common elements are mostly outdoors, and the distances between them are measured in hundreds of feet. That one fact changes almost every decision in a condo security camera project here: where the recorder lives, how the cable gets from a pool fence to a clubhouse closet, what the cameras can see at night across an unlit lot, and what the board ends up paying.

Newsday's reporting on a new condominium planned beside the Mineola LIRR station put the contrast in the developer's own words: its concrete and metal construction, he said, "will stand out from existing condo communities made up of two- to three-story buildings with wood construction" (Newsday, April 2026). That is a fair description of most of the condo stock we see in Nassau and Suffolk, and it explains why camera work here looks more like campus work than building work. Wood-frame walls are easy to fish cable through; the open ground between buildings is not. A camera at the far end of a parking court may be 300 feet from the nearest place a recorder can safely sit.

The second difference is who is watching. Some Long Island communities have a staffed gatehouse; real estate listings for gated developments along Route 110 in Melville describe guard booths at the entrance as a selling point (ActiveRain, 2017). Most smaller condos have no staff at all, only a managing agent who visits and a landscaping crew on Tuesdays. In a staffed community the gate camera feeds a monitor in the booth and the recordings back up what the officer saw. In an unstaffed one, the cameras are the only witness, and the recordings matter only if someone can find the right clip after the fact. We design for whichever of those your community actually is.

The third difference is law. Long Island condos are governed by the same state Condominium Act as city condos, but none of the New York City local laws that city buildings deal with apply out here. A board in Garden City or Smithtown answers to its declaration and bylaws, to New York State statutes, and to its town or village, not to the city's Housing Maintenance Code or its tenant data privacy law. We set out what that means, with sources, further down this page. For the full picture of condo security on the Island, including gates, fobs, intercoms and amenity access, see our condo security systems page for Long Island; this page stays on cameras.

Long Island condo facts that shape a camera plan

  • Nassau alone has two cities, three towns and 64 incorporated villages, plus more than 60 hamlets, according to the state's county profile. Which one your condo sits in decides who issues any permit and, in some villages, which police department answers a call.
  • The Long Island Index mapped 882 co-op and condo properties across the two counties, alongside 1,456 rental buildings, in its 2016 rental mapping report (Long Island Index, 2016). Owner communities are a minority of the Island's multifamily stock, and many are large complexes rather than single buildings.
  • Long Beach is the only place on Long Island with oceanfront high-rises, a 2021 State Senate release noted while describing buildings "sandblasted every day between sand and the ocean breeze" (NY State Senate, 2021). Outdoor cameras there need salt-rated housings and sealed connections.
  • Tropical Storm Isaias affected about 420,000 PSEG Long Island customers in August 2020 (PSEG Long Island, Aug. 5, 2020). A recorder without battery backup stops recording exactly when a community most wants footage.
  • A string of burglaries hit a Yaphank condo community in 2015: at least five break-ins and three attempts in about two weeks, and one resident's own camera caught the suspect (CBS New York, Aug. 25, 2015). Owner cameras captured what the community's common-area coverage did not.
  • Suffolk County police have asked residents and businesses to register their cameras so detectives know where footage might exist after a crime (Officer.com, Aug. 28, 2019).
Nassau vs Suffolk

Condo Cameras in Nassau County and Suffolk County Are Not the Same Job

Boards often treat "Long Island" as one market. For a camera installer it is two, with a line at the county border that shows up in the price, the building types and the logistics.

Nassau: denser, older, closer to the train

Nassau condos tend to sit on smaller parcels in established villages. We see garden-style complexes from the 1970s and 1980s with analog coax still running through the attics, newer elevator condos within walking distance of LIRR stations in places like Mineola, Westbury and Hicksville, and townhouse communities squeezed between commercial strips on the North Shore. Parking is tighter, lots back onto public streets, and commuter parking spills into condo lots near stations. A Nassau camera plan usually spends more of its budget on lot edges and building doors and less on long runs to remote amenities. Nassau's four-camera house-brand system is $999 on our Nassau camera price page, the starting point for the whole Island. Boards that want the broader Nassau picture can read our Nassau County condo security systems page. A Nassau condo camera page of its own is in preparation.

Suffolk: bigger communities, longer drives, more open ground

Suffolk has the Island's large planned communities: gated developments with a clubhouse at the center and hundreds of attached homes around it, active-adult communities in Brookhaven and Riverhead, and seasonal condos on the North and South Forks. A 2021 overview of gated communities on eastern Long Island listed developments of 512 homes in Manorville and 653 in St. James, the latter "with the majority being condos" (55places, 2021). Communities that size mean long cable runs, wireless bridges and pole-mounted cameras on entrance roads. Suffolk's four-camera system is $1,099 on our Suffolk camera price page; the difference covers drive time east of the Nassau line. The county view of condo security lives on our Suffolk County condo security systems page, and a Suffolk condo camera page is in preparation.

Who answers when the footage is needed

The responding agency differs by location too. In Nassau, some incorporated villages run their own police departments while others are patrolled by the county police (Nassau County list of police departments). In Suffolk, the county police department has run a camera registration effort that asks owners to tell it where cameras are, so officers canvassing after a crime know whom to ask (Officer.com, 2019, cited above). Neither changes how the cameras are installed, but both affect how a board's footage policy should handle police requests, which we cover below.

Camera Placement

Where Cameras Go in a Long Island Condo Community

We plan a Long Island condo from the public road inward, because that is the path every visitor, delivery driver and intruder takes. Not every community has every space below; a twelve-unit townhouse condo in Bellmore and a 600-home gated community in Brookhaven share only a few of them.

The entrance road and gatehouse

If there is one camera a Long Island condo should have, it is at the entrance. Every car that comes in passes that point, so a camera there turns "someone broke into cars last night" into a list of the vehicles that entered between midnight and four. In gated communities we usually place two cameras on the inbound lane: an overview that shows the car, the gate arm and the booth, and a narrow-angle camera aimed low at the bumper to record plates. Where there is no gate, a camera at the stop sign where the community road meets the public street does the same job. Plate cameras need the car to slow down, so we look for the speed bump, the gate or the stop line before choosing a pole.

Clubhouse doors and lobby

The clubhouse is the community's shared living room, and its doors are where after-hours entry, guest disputes and damage questions start. One camera on each exterior door and one inside the lobby facing the entry usually answers who came in and when. Clubhouses are also the most sensible place for the recorder: heated, dry, locked, with internet service already in the management office.

Pools and pool gates

Pool cameras cover the gate, the deck and the equipment shed, never the changing rooms or restrooms. Boards most often want them to settle guest-limit arguments, after-hours swimming and vandalism to furniture. Because the pool is often the farthest common element from any building, it is where long cable runs and wireless bridges come in.

Mailbox kiosks and package shelters

Many Long Island townhouse condos use cluster mailbox units at the end of a street or beside the clubhouse rather than mailrooms inside buildings. A camera here should show the faces of people at the boxes and the vehicle they arrived in, without pointing at the windows of the units across the street. A short pole or the kiosk's own roof framing usually gives the right height.

Parking courts, carports and garages

Car break-ins and hit-and-run damage are the most common incidents boards bring to us. Open lots need full-color or well-lit infrared cameras at each entrance to the parking court, plus wide views along the rows. Carport roofs make good mounting points. LIRR-area elevator condos add underground garages, which need the same ramp and door coverage as a city garage.

Maintenance yard, dumpster enclosures and storage

The maintenance garage, salt and equipment storage, the dumpster enclosures and any RV or boat lot are low-traffic, high-cost areas. A single camera at each enclosure gate is usually enough.

Building doors and interior halls

In garden-style condos each building has its own entry and stairs. Covering every entry can double a system's size, so we start with the buildings nearest the road and the lots, and add others where incidents happen. Interior corridor cameras are less common out here than in the city, but where a building has an interior hall, the camera looks along it, not at unit doors.

Places a Long Island condo camera should never point

  • Into any unit, patio, deck, rear yard or window that belongs to an owner.
  • Pool changing rooms, clubhouse restrooms, showers and locker areas.
  • Staff break rooms and any space where maintenance staff change clothes.
  • Neighboring properties beyond the community's boundary, including homes across a public road.

We will not install a camera aimed at any of these, whoever asks. The legal background is in the privacy section below; the practical reason is that a single badly aimed camera can turn a security project into an owner dispute.

Equipment & Distance

Condo Security Camera Systems Built for Long Island Distances

The cameras on a Long Island condo look like the cameras on a city building. What differs is everything between them and the recorder. A network camera cable has a practical limit of about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, before the signal and the power it carries need help. In a tower that limit rarely matters. On a condo campus it decides the design.

Camera styles that suit outdoor condo common areas

Turret cameras handle building doors, clubhouse lobbies and mailbox kiosks. Vandal-rated domes go where a camera is in reach, such as a pool equipment shed or a carport post. Long-reach bullets and varifocal cameras cover entrance roads and the far ends of parking courts. Full-color low-light models earn their keep in lots, because a car's color and a jacket's color matter more to the police than a gray infrared shape. Pan-tilt-zoom cameras have a place on a large green or a long entrance drive, but only alongside fixed cameras, never instead of them. We cover how each choice affects price in the pricing section.

Recorder location and storage

The recorder goes where it is dry, ventilated, secure and close to the most cameras: usually the clubhouse office, sometimes a maintenance garage with a conditioned closet, occasionally the gatehouse itself in communities where the booth is staffed and heated. Storage is sized to the retention period the board picks; New York condo guidance commonly cites 30 to 90 days (FirstService Residential, 2024). Outdoor cameras watching trees and traffic generate more data than indoor ones, so lot and road cameras usually record on motion with a short continuous buffer.

Plate reading at the gate

License plate cameras are the most requested and most misunderstood item on Long Island condo wish lists. A plate camera is a narrow-view camera with a fast shutter and its own infrared, aimed where cars slow to under about 15 miles per hour and where the plate is within a set distance. Plate-recognition software, when the board wants it, can turn those images into a searchable list. It works at a gate or a stop line. It does not work on a camera mounted for a wide view of the whole entrance road.

Privacy & New York State Law

Condo Camera Privacy on Long Island: State Law, Not City Rules

We are a licensed low-voltage contractor, not attorneys, and nothing on this page is legal advice. Each condominium should have its own counsel review a camera plan against its declaration, bylaws, rules and any master association documents. What follows are the public sources that most often come up when Long Island boards plan cameras.

The Condominium Act covers Long Island the same way it covers the city

New York's Condominium Act is state law. Its definition of common elements includes the "yards, gardens, recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces" along with halls, lobbies and entrances (Real Property Law §339-e), which on a Long Island campus describes most of the property. The same act requires the bylaws to cover the board's powers and how rules for the common elements are adopted (§339-v). That is where a board's authority to place cameras on lawns, lots and pool decks comes from.

Homeowners associations layered over condos

Many Long Island communities are not a single condominium. A master homeowners association may own the entrance road, the clubhouse and the pool, while separate condo associations own the buildings. The state's rules for HOA offering plans require the plan to describe the common areas, including "the land, buildings, lots, parking facilities, recreational facilities" (13 NYCRR §22.3). Before cameras go up, counsel should confirm which entity owns each place a camera will be mounted, because that entity is the one that signs and pays.

Audio is the line not to cross

Video of shared outdoor areas is widely used. Audio is different. New York's eavesdropping statute covers the "mechanical overhearing of a conversation" without the consent of at least one party (Penal Law §250.00), and eavesdropping is a class E felony (§250.05). A pool deck or clubhouse lobby microphone records conversations between people who agreed to nothing. Our condo installations ship with audio off, and we enable it only on written approval from the board's counsel.

Changing rooms, restrooms and staff spaces

The unlawful surveillance statute makes it a felony to record a person without knowledge or consent, for no legitimate purpose, "in a bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" (Penal Law §250.45). Pool houses and clubhouse locker areas are exactly those rooms. FirstService Residential's New York guidance also points to the Labor Law's bar on video of employees in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas (FirstService Residential, cited above), which covers the maintenance staff's room in the community garage.

Patios, decks and rear yards

Long Island condos raise a question tower buildings rarely do: the patio or deck behind a townhouse may be a limited common element reserved to one owner, even though the association maintains it. A Newman Ferrara attorney writing in CooperatorNews warned that a camera whose view "peers into a neighboring unit" invites a privacy claim, because "the law recognizes the interior of a neighbor's unit as subject to considerable privacy safeguards" (CooperatorNews, Q&A: Camera Placement). We aim lot and lawn cameras so private patios, decks and windows fall outside the frame, or mask those areas in the recorder where the view cannot be changed.

Owner-installed cameras

The same CooperatorNews piece notes that condominiums "typically prohibit unit owners from unilaterally altering" common areas and that "a request for board consent is advisable." On Long Island that comes up with video doorbells on shared porches and floodlight cameras on siding the association maintains. The board's rule decides; we install owner devices on common elements only with the board's written approval.

City laws that do not reach Nassau or Suffolk

Boards reading online advice often run into New York City rules. Two come up most. The city's tenant data privacy law, Local Law 63 of 2021, regulates "smart access" systems in class A multiple dwellings in New York City (intro.nyc), and the entrance and intercom requirements in section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law apply to New York City buildings (Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a), while towns and villages on Long Island fall under the separate Multiple Residence Law (Multiple Residence Law §3). A condo in Hauppauge or Massapequa is outside both. Statewide rules, such as the Penal Law sections above and the state's licensing of security installers, still apply everywhere.

Who may legally install the system

New York State licenses security system installers through the Department of State, and its rules include closed-circuit and video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion (19 NYCRR Part 195). Our license number is NYS #12000287431, and boards should check any bidder's number before signing.

Footage Policy

Who Sees the Recordings in a Long Island Condo Community

On Long Island the hardest footage questions usually come from distance and turnover. The managing agent may be in an office twenty miles away. Board members serve two-year terms and move to Florida. The gate officer works for a guard contractor that changes every few years. A footage policy has to survive all of that, and the camera system has to be set up so the policy can actually be followed.

FirstService Residential's New York guidance recommends that buildings "maintain a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage," and that residents not receive footage "without prior approval from the board and legal counsel" (FirstService Residential, 2024, cited above). The setup that supports that kind of policy looks like this:

  • A named account for the managing agent with live view, playback and export, used remotely through the manufacturer's app.
  • A gatehouse account that shows live view of the entrance and nearby cameras only, with no export rights, so a guard contractor's staff never walk off with a clip.
  • A board account at whatever level the board itself decides, often playback only.
  • A superintendent or maintenance account limited to the cameras on equipment and storage areas.
  • Health alerts to the managing agent when a camera drops offline or the recorder stops writing to its drive, so nobody finds out on the morning after an incident.

Police requests in Nassau and Suffolk

Requests may come from the Nassau County police, a village department, the Suffolk County police or an East End town department, depending on where the community sits. However the request arrives, it should go to the managing agent and, where the policy says so, to counsel. Whether to hand over a clip voluntarily or wait for a subpoena is the condominium's legal decision. We do not release recordings to anyone ourselves; our job is to make sure the agent can find and export a clip, with its date, time and camera name intact, in a few minutes.

Signs at the entrance

A sign at the community entrance and at the clubhouse door stating that common areas are recorded tells visitors, contractors and residents the same thing the board's notice told owners. Wording and placement are the board's call; we can mount signs on the same poles as the cameras.

Board Approval

Getting Cameras Approved by a Long Island Condo or HOA Board

Long Island boards tend to move on a seasonal rhythm. Many hold their annual meeting in spring, approve capital work over the summer, and lose a quorum in winter when snowbird members head south. A camera project that misses the summer window often waits a year. We build our proposals so the board can decide in one meeting.

What the board packet contains

  1. A site plan, not a floor plan. An aerial or survey of the property with each camera numbered and its view drawn, plus every cable route, conduit and wireless link.
  2. One line per camera saying what it is for, for example "visitor lane, plates" or "pool gate and deck." If nobody can write that line, the camera is dropped.
  3. Options in phases, typically entrance and clubhouse first, then pool and lots, then individual buildings, each priced separately with a recorder sized for all of them.
  4. Which entity pays for what where a master association and one or more condo associations share the property.
  5. The recording plan and running costs: retention in days, drive size, any subscriptions, the warranty period and what a longer plan would cost.
  6. A privacy summary: audio off, no cameras in changing rooms or restrooms, private patios and windows masked or out of frame.

Reserves, operating budget or assessment

The Condominium Act lets the bylaws provide for reserves for capital expenditures (Real Property Law §339-v, cited above). Many Long Island communities fund a full camera system from reserves the same way they fund paving or roofs, while a single added camera or a replacement recorder usually comes from operating funds. That choice belongs to the board and its accountant. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Telling owners, including the ones who are away

Owner notice works better when it shows the plan. We give the managing agent a one-page summary with the site plan, the list of covered areas, a statement that audio is off and who can view recordings. In communities with many seasonal residents, sending it by email as well as posting it in the clubhouse saves the board a round of calls from owners who see a trench in April and wonder what it is.

Working around community rules

Gated communities set contractor hours, vehicle passes and quiet periods around pool season and holidays. We follow them, book visits early in the day, and issue the insurance certificate naming the condominium, the master association and the managing agent once the contract is awarded. Trenching is scheduled around irrigation, lawn treatments and the landscaper's mowing day.

What moves a Long Island condo camera quote

  • Distance. Runs longer than 100 feet, buried conduit, fiber or a wireless bridge are the largest variable on most Long Island proposals. Each is shown and priced separately before anything is ordered.
  • Trenching and restoration. Digging across lawns, boring under a driveway and restoring sod are priced per run, and utility markouts are requested before any digging.
  • Poles. Cameras on existing lot light poles depend on constant power at the pole; new camera poles add material and labor. Electrical work is done by the community's electrician.
  • Concealed versus exposed cable. Inside finished clubhouse rooms, concealed wiring is quoted as an extra; in attics, carports and garages, exposed runs are usually fine.
  • Reusable cable. Old coax in garden-style buildings can often carry new high-definition cameras. If a run might fail its test, we tell you before the order and give the replacement price.
  • Plate cameras and analytics. Dedicated plate cameras at the gate add to the price and are listed as their own line.

Cameras the association already owns

If the community bought its own cameras, our price covers installation labor only. Send us the models and a marked-up site plan or aerial, and we quote by phone or text; the work goes on the schedule once the deposit is paid. We do not do walk-throughs for owner-supplied equipment, and the equipment's performance stays with whoever sold it.

Community Types

Camera Plans for Each Kind of Long Island Condominium

A Long Island board asking for "condo cameras" could be running a 24-unit garden complex next to a shopping center or a gated community the size of a small village. The plan, the cable method and the price all follow from which one it is.

Garden-style condo complexes

Two- and three-story wood-frame buildings around shared parking, often built in the 1970s and 1980s. Attic and crawlspace cable paths make building doors easy; the lots and the gaps between buildings are where coverage and cable effort go.

Gated planned communities

A gatehouse, an entrance road, a clubhouse and pool at the center, and hundreds of attached homes around them. Gate and plate cameras, wireless or fiber links to the clubhouse, and a recorder sized for 32 channels or more.

55-plus and active-adult communities

Clubhouse-centered life, busy amenity calendars and many owners away in winter. Coverage leans toward the clubhouse, walking paths, the pool and the entrance, with remote viewing for the managing agent during the months the board is scattered.

Townhouse condos with private lanes

Rows of attached homes with their own garages on association-owned lanes. Cameras at the lane entrances, the mailbox kiosk and guest parking, aimed so driveways and garage doors of individual owners are not the subject.

Transit-oriented elevator condos

Newer concrete buildings near LIRR stations with lobbies, elevators, garages and roof amenities. These are the Long Island condos that look most like city buildings, with lobby, garage ramp, package room and elevator coverage.

Waterfront and seasonal condos

Ocean, bay and harbor buildings on the South Shore and North Shore, and seasonal condos on the East End. Salt-rated hardware, equipment placed above flood-prone ground floors, and remote viewing for owners who are not there half the year.

Mixed-use village condos, with shops on the ground floor and residences above, appear in downtowns such as Patchogue, Rockville Centre and Port Jefferson. They need separate camera groups and permissions for the residential association and each commercial owner, set up so neither side sees more than the declaration says it should.

Across Long Island

Condo Surveillance Cameras From the Queens Line to the East End

The hardware is the same from Elmont to Southold, but the communities, the conditions and the usual complaints change as you drive east. This is what we plan for in each part of the Island.

North Shore of Nassau

Great Neck, Port Washington, Roslyn, Glen Cove and Oyster Bay have older condo buildings on hilly lots, newer waterfront developments such as the Garvies Point project in Glen Cove, which Newsday reported would include 1,110 condos and apartments when complete, and townhouse condos tucked off Northern Boulevard. Mineola, Garden City, Westbury, Hicksville and Carle Place are where most of the Island's new transit-oriented condos and apartments are going up around LIRR stations. These elevator buildings need garage, lobby, package room and elevator coverage, and their lots fill with commuter cars that do not belong. Older garden complexes in the same towns often still run analog cameras on coax, a good candidate for an upgrade on existing cable.

South Shore of Nassau

Long Beach, Lido Beach, Oceanside, Freeport, Merrick and Massapequa put condos close to tidal water. Long Beach has the Island's only oceanfront high-rises, where salt spray and wind are constant; inland South Shore complexes deal with flooding in low lots. Recorders and switches go above the ground floor wherever possible, and outdoor connections are sealed rather than taped.

Western Suffolk and the Route 110 corridor

Huntington, Melville, Dix Hills, Commack and Smithtown hold many of the Island's gated communities and larger condo developments, several with staffed gatehouses near the Long Island Expressway. Here the gate camera, the visitor lane and the guard's monitor are usually the heart of the system, with long links back to the clubhouse.

Central Suffolk and Brookhaven

Ronkonkoma, Hauppauge, Lake Grove, Coram, Middle Island and Yaphank mix garden condos, active-adult communities and new transit-oriented development around the Ronkonkoma station. The communities are large and the distances long, so this is where wireless bridges, outdoor switches and pole cameras come up most. The East End

Riverhead, the North Fork, the Hamptons and Montauk have seasonal condos and active-adult communities with owners away for months. Cameras here are judged by whether an absent owner or managing agent can check the property after a storm, and whether the recorder rides out a winter power failure. East End jobs take the longest drive from our Brooklyn office, and we schedule them as early in the day as the community allows.

How It Works

How a Long Island Condo Camera Installation Runs

Adding a camera at a mailbox kiosk can take a single visit. A full community system can take a summer from first call to handover, mostly waiting for board meetings and trench schedules. Every project follows the same seven steps.

1

Request and booking

Reach us by phone, text or the form above. The walk goes on the calendar within 2 hours of your request, seven days a week, early in the day, with a two-hour window for our arrival.

2

Property walk

We walk the entrance, clubhouse, pool, lots, kiosks and storage areas, measure the distances, check poles and power, test any old cable, and note where conduit or a wireless link is needed.

3

Site plan and options

An aerial photo of the property marked up with camera views and cable paths, and phases the board can approve one at a time, each with its own number, recording days and yearly costs.

4

Approval and contract

Once the board, or every association with a stake in the property, votes as its documents require, the managing agent signs and pays the deposit. Ordering and the insurance certificate follow.

5

Markouts and owner notice

Utility markouts are requested for any trench, and the agent sends owners the summary and work dates, including those away for the season.

6

Installation

Conduit, cable, links, cameras and recorder go in during the community's contractor hours. Views are checked against patios and windows, audio is turned off and retention is set.

7

Handover

Separate logins for the agent, the gate booth, the board and the maintenance staff, a training session, written export steps and a labeled aerial. The association holds every password.

Two points protect the board. Equipment is ordered only after approval and the deposit, and once ordered it cannot be returned, so anything that could add cost, such as an old coax run that may fail testing or a trench that may hit rock or roots, is put in writing before the order. And the community's lawn and paving are restored as part of the job, not left for the landscaper to discover.

Existing Systems

Repairing, Upgrading or Replacing a Condo Camera System on Long Island

Plenty of Long Island communities already have cameras. The trouble is usually that they were added one at a time over fifteen years by three different vendors, on two recorders nobody can log in to, with half the outdoor cameras fogged or dead.

When a repair is enough

A failed drive, a dead power supply, a camera knocked out of aim by a ladder or a snowplow, or water in an outdoor junction box can usually be fixed without starting over. General camera repair across the Island is covered on our security camera repair page for Long Island; on condo jobs we pair the repair with a quick check of every other camera so the board knows what else is close to failing.

When the cable is good but the picture is not

Garden-style complexes wired with coax in the 1990s and 2000s can often keep that cable. A modern high-definition recorder and new cameras on the existing coax give a far sharper image than the old analog system, at a fraction of the cost of new cable, and the board avoids opening attic hatches in every building again.

When the system has to go

Recorders with no software updates, cable that fails throughout, or cameras placed where nothing happens are reasons to replace. We phase the replacement so the entrance and clubhouse stay covered while the rest changes over.

Taking control back from a developer or former vendor

In newer communities the developer often bought the first camera system, and the admin account may still belong to the sponsor or its installer long after turnover to the owners. We recover or reset those accounts, re-register everything to the association, and document it, so the next change of managing agent does not lock the board out again.

Ongoing maintenance

Outdoor cameras on Long Island collect pollen in spring, salt in winter and spider webs all summer. A periodic visit to clean lenses, check views, confirm the recorder is writing to its drives and update firmware keeps the system useful. Analog and coax-based systems are also covered on our CCTV installation page for Long Island.

Weather & Power

Storms, Salt and Outages: Long Island Conditions That Shape the Hardware

Long Island condo cameras live outdoors more than city cameras do, and the Island's weather finds every weak point. These are the conditions we design around.

Power failures

When Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, PSEG Long Island said the storm affected about 420,000 customers, with roughly 160,000 still out late that night (PSEG Long Island, cited above). Outages like that are when owners most want to know what is happening at the property. We put the recorder, network switch and any midpoint switches on battery backup sized for the load, and in communities with a clubhouse generator we connect the camera equipment to a circuit the generator already serves, through the community's electrician.

Lightning and surges on long runs

Long copper runs between buildings and up light poles pick up surges from nearby strikes. Surge protection at both ends of outdoor runs, proper grounding, and fiber between buildings where the run is long all reduce the chance that one storm takes out a switch and every camera on it.

Salt spray and coastal wind

Within sight of the ocean or the bay, standard mounts rust and connectors corrode. We use weather-rated housings, stainless hardware, sealed cable entries and drip loops, and we avoid pointing cameras straight into the prevailing wind where rain and spray collect on the lens.

Snow, plows and ice

Low cameras on lot edges and pool fences get hit by plow blades and buried by piled snow. Mounting heights, pole guards and placement behind curbs come from where the plow contractor actually pushes the snow, which the superintendent usually knows better than anyone.

Trees, deer and headlights

Mature trees are part of what owners love about many Long Island condos, and they are also what sets off motion alerts all night and blocks views by June. We set detection zones that ignore branches, prefer person and vehicle detection over raw motion, and check leaf-on views when the walk happens in winter.

Dark lots

Many condo lots are lit for walking, not for cameras. Full-color low-light cameras work well where there is some light; where a lot is truly dark, infrared cameras placed away from parked car bumpers, which bounce infrared back into the lens, give a cleaner image.

Network & Access Security

Keeping a Condo Community's Camera Network Locked Down

A Long Island condo camera system usually touches more networks and more outsiders than a city building's. The clubhouse internet service may also feed guest Wi-Fi for residents at the pool. The guard contractor brings its own tablet to the booth. The landscaping company's foreman asks to see the lot camera after a mower hits a car. Each of those is a door into the system unless it is planned for.

  • Cameras on their own network. The camera system runs on a separate segment from the clubhouse guest Wi-Fi, the management office computers and any smart TVs in the community room.
  • Nothing opened to the outside. The clubhouse router forwards no ports to the recorder; phones and laptops reach it through the manufacturer's encrypted app relay instead.
  • Every default password changed on cameras, recorder, switches and wireless bridges, with the list held by the association.
  • Accounts tied to roles, not people's personal emails, so a board member who moves away or a guard company that loses the contract loses access the same day.
  • Wireless bridges encrypted and hidden, so the link from the gatehouse to the clubhouse does not appear as an open network to anyone parked nearby.
  • Firmware updated at handover and checked on maintenance visits.

Where a managing agent or the community's IT provider has its own network rules, we follow them and coordinate before installation day, so the camera system is not the one device on the property that ignores the standard.

When something happens on the property

After a break-in, a damaged car or a vandalized pool gate, the managing agent's first job is to save the clip before the recorder writes over it. Note the time window and the place as precisely as possible, export the clip with its date, time and camera name, and log who asked for it and why under the board's policy. Then write down any gap: a camera that was dark, fogged, blocked by a branch or simply missing. That list is the best guide to the community's next camera decision. If the agent cannot find or export the clip, or finds the recorder was not recording, we can help recover what the drives hold and fix the cause on a service visit.

Real Questions

Questions Long Island Condo Boards Ask Us About Cameras

Our guard writes visitor plates on a clipboard. Can the gate cameras keep that record instead?

Yes, and more reliably. A plate camera at the visitor lane, aimed where cars stop at the booth, records every plate with a time stamp, and an overview camera shows the car and driver's side. The guard still checks visitors in; the cameras replace the handwriting and the clipboard that gets lost. If the board wants a searchable plate list, recognition software can be added to the same camera.

Our gatehouse is staffed only in the evening. What should the gate cameras do during the day?

Record every car either way. When the booth is empty, the overview and plate cameras become the only record of who came in, so they run on the same schedule all day. Some boards also send an alert to the managing agent when the gate arm is raised manually or held open during unstaffed hours. When the officer arrives, the booth monitor shows the same views, so nothing changes in how the cameras record.

Our landscaping and snow removal crews sometimes damage cars and curbs. Can cameras settle those claims?

They often do. A plow blade that clips a parked car at 4 a.m. or a mower that throws a stone into a windshield shows up on lot and lane cameras if those cameras cover the paths the crews actually drive. Before the season, ask the superintendent where the plow and mower routes run and make sure the views include them. The recording then goes to the contractor's insurer through the managing agent, under the board's footage policy.

Our community backs onto a nature preserve and a golf course. Should cameras cover the back property line?

Usually only where there is a path, a gap in the fence or a history of trespass. A camera every hundred feet along a wooded boundary records mostly deer and branches. A camera at each known footpath or fence gap, aimed along the line rather than into the neighboring land, covers the real entry points and keeps the preserve or course out of the recordings.

Many owners leave for the winter. What should the cameras watch while those units sit empty?

The common elements that lead to the empty units: the lane or parking court entrances, building entries and walkways. Cameras do not look into private units or yards, but an empty-season pattern, such as a car that keeps returning at night, shows up clearly on entrance cameras. Many boards also set alerts to the managing agent for movement in quiet areas after midnight during the off-season.

Our community has a master HOA and several condo associations. Who controls the shared entrance cameras?

Usually the entity that owns the entrance, which is often the master association. We can run all cameras on one recorder with separate permission groups, so each condo association sees its own buildings and the master association sees the gate and amenities, or install separate recorders if the documents require it. Counsel should confirm ownership before the contract is signed.

Can a camera on a parking lot light pole take its power from the pole?

Only if the pole has constant power. Many lot lights are switched by a photocell or timer, so the power disappears at dawn. Providing constant power at a pole is electrical work, which the community's electrician handles; we don't do electrical work. Often a better answer is to run network cable to the pole and power the camera from the switch, which needs no electrician at all.

Contractors and neighbors dump debris in our dumpsters at night. Will a camera at the enclosure catch them?

A camera aimed at the enclosure gate, with enough resolution to read a truck's lettering or plate as it backs in, catches most of it. Pair it with a sign. Boards often find that a visible camera and a posted notice end the problem within weeks, and the recordings back up any charge-back to the owner whose contractor did the dumping.

Kids from outside the community use our pool at night in summer. What camera setup actually stops it?

A camera on the pool gate that shows faces as people come through, a second camera covering the fence line they climb, and a sign at the gate. Deterrence cameras with a light and a recorded voice message can be aimed at the deck after closing hours, kept away from nearby bedroom windows. Cameras record and discourage; the fence, the lock and the pool rules still do the stopping.

Our condo sits beside the LIRR tracks. Will passing trains blur the cameras?

Vibration can soften the image on a camera mounted on a long arm or a thin pole, especially at high zoom. We mount cameras near the tracks on solid masonry or short, rigid brackets, choose wider lenses that hide small movements, and turn on image stabilization where the camera offers it. Headlights from trains at night are handled the same way as car headlights, by aiming across the light rather than into it.

Our townhouses each have their own garage. Can the association's cameras cover the private lanes between them?

If the lanes are common elements, yes. We place cameras at the lane entrances and along the lane itself, aimed down its length so passing cars and people are recorded while individual garage interiors and front doors are not the focus. Owners can see the field of view on the site plan before anything is installed.

The clubhouse gym has no attendant. Can a camera show who was there when a machine was damaged?

One ceiling camera inside the gym door, looking into the room, shows who entered and when. If the clubhouse uses key fobs, the camera time stamp lines up with the fob log. The camera never covers the locker rooms or showers next door.

Reality Check

What Online Answers Get Wrong About Long Island Condo Cameras

Board members research before they call. Much of what comes back from search results, chatbots and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr is written for single-family houses or for New York City towers. Here is how common claims hold up on a Long Island condo property.

"A solar cellular camera solves the far corner of the property"

It can, for a while. Solar and cellular cameras are useful for a construction phase or a seasonal boat lot. As permanent coverage for a pool gate or an entrance, they struggle in December's short days, record less than a wired camera, and add a monthly data bill per camera.

For anything the community will rely on year after year, conduit or a wireless bridge to the recorder is the better investment.

"Condo camera rules are the same everywhere in New York"

Statewide laws are, and they matter most: the Penal Law's eavesdropping and unlawful surveillance sections and the state licensing of installers. But much of the condo camera advice online is written for New York City buildings and cites city laws that do not apply in Nassau or Suffolk.

Long Island boards should read their own declaration, bylaws and any master association documents first.

"One PTZ camera on the clubhouse roof can watch the whole community"

A pan-tilt-zoom camera looks at one place at a time. While it is zoomed on the pool, it is not recording the gate. On a campus, fixed cameras at entrances and doors do the reliable work, and a PTZ is an extra for a large open green.

"Cost guides tell you what a condo camera system costs"

National cost guides quote prices for a few cameras on a house. They cannot account for a 400-foot run to a pool, a trench across a lawn or a wireless link to a gatehouse, which are often the largest items on a Long Island condo proposal.

Our published price is the four-camera starting point for each county; everything beyond that is priced from the property walk, in writing.

"Lot light poles already have power, so pole cameras are simple"

Many lot lights switch off at dawn by photocell or timer. A camera on that circuit goes dark every morning. Constant power at a pole is electrician work, which is why we often power pole cameras over their network cable instead.

"Any 4K camera can read license plates at the entrance"

Resolution is not the problem; motion blur and headlight glare are. Reading plates at night takes a dedicated camera with a fast shutter, aimed where cars slow down, within a set distance. A wide 4K view of the entrance road will show that a car passed, not its plate.

"Register the cameras with police and they can watch the community"

The Suffolk County police camera registration effort described by Officer.com is a way to tell detectives where cameras exist so they can ask for footage after a crime; the article does not describe live police access to registered private cameras. Registering does not hand anyone the keys to the system.

Any release of recordings remains the board's decision under its footage policy.

From the Field

Field Notes From Condo Camera Work on Long Island

Since 2002 we have put security systems into every kind of property, and Long Island condo communities keep teaching us the same handful of lessons. They are now part of every first walk.

The gate camera was aimed at the headlights. Original installers often point the entrance camera straight down the road at oncoming traffic. At night it records glare. Turning it to look at cars from the side as they stop at the gate, with a separate plate camera aimed low, fixes it.

The recorder was in the pool house. We find recorders in pump rooms, unheated sheds and gatehouses with space heaters. Chlorine fumes, damp and freezing winters kill drives. Moving the recorder to the clubhouse office, and bringing the pool cameras in over a link, usually solves more than the camera upgrade itself.

The summer view was not the winter view. Walks done in February show open sightlines that vanish once the trees leaf out. We ask the superintendent where the foliage fills in and place cameras accordingly.

Nobody knew it had stopped recording. The most common failure is a recorder that quietly stopped writing to its drive months earlier. Health alerts to the managing agent are now part of every handover.

The site plan prevented the arguments. Communities that circulated the plan, with each camera's view drawn on an aerial, rarely heard owner complaints. Where cameras simply appeared, owners asked who was watching their patio.

Long Island Condo Security Camera FAQ

Why does a Suffolk condo camera system start higher than one in Nassau?
+

Our four-camera house-brand system with recorder is $999 installed in Nassau County and $1,099 in Suffolk County, matching our two county camera price pages. The difference covers the longer drive to Suffolk communities, many of which are an hour or more east of our crews. Cameras beyond the first four are priced per camera after the property walk.

Do New York City's camera, intercom and tenant data rules apply to Long Island condominiums?
+

No. Laws such as the city's Local Law 63 of 2021 on smart access systems and the Multiple Dwelling Law's entrance requirements apply to buildings in New York City, not to condos in Nassau or Suffolk towns and villages. State laws do apply everywhere, including the Penal Law sections on eavesdropping and unlawful surveillance. The condo's own declaration and bylaws govern where cameras go; counsel should confirm.

Should our condo register its cameras with the Suffolk County police?
+

That is the board's choice. Suffolk County police have asked camera owners to register so detectives know where footage may exist after a crime; registration as described is a list of locations, not live police access. If the board registers, its footage policy should still say who handles police requests and when counsel is involved.

Do pool cameras replace a lifeguard or pool attendant?
+

No. Cameras record what happened and can deter after-hours swimming, but nobody watches a recording in time to help a swimmer in trouble. Pool safety staffing and rules are a separate decision for the board and its insurer. We cover the gate, the deck and the equipment area, never the changing rooms or restrooms.

Can a camera inside the gatehouse booth record the officer at work?
+

Video of the booth interior is commonly used to protect the officer and document visitor check-ins, and we can install one with audio turned off. The guard contractor should be told in writing, the camera should never cover a restroom or changing area, and the board's counsel should review the plan, since the officers are the contractor's employees.

Should the clubhouse lobby TV show the live camera views?
+

Most boards decide against it. A public screen shows anyone in the clubhouse who is coming and going at the gate and the pool, which can conflict with the footage policy. Where a community wants a display, we usually limit it to the entrance and parking views, or put it in the management office instead.

Do we need a camera at every building entrance in a garden-style complex?
+

Usually not at first. Covering the community's entrances, parking courts and the buildings nearest the road catches most of what happens, and building doors are added where incidents actually occur. A recorder sized for the full plan lets the board add entrance cameras building by building in later budget years.

How far east on Long Island do you install condo cameras?
+

All the way. We work in every part of Nassau and Suffolk, including Riverhead, the North Fork, the Hamptons and Montauk. East End jobs are scheduled early in the day, and the Suffolk price page applies to them.

Will adding cameras at the gatehouse interfere with our gate arm or call box?
+

No. Cameras are mounted and wired separately from the gate operator, the arm and the visitor call box, and they do not draw power from the gate controller. We coordinate with the community's gate service company if a camera has to share a pole or an enclosure with its equipment, so warranty and service responsibilities stay clear.

Can cameras watch our community's private beach, dock or boat slips?
+

Yes, where those areas are common elements. We use salt-rated cameras on sealed mounts and aim them at the access path, gate or dock entrance rather than at boats owned by individuals or neighboring properties across the water. Power and cable routes near the water are planned above flood level where possible.

How long does the walkthrough of a multi-building condo community take?

For a small garden complex, about an hour. For a gated community with a clubhouse, pool and several parking courts, plan on two to three hours with the superintendent, since we measure every run and check poles, conduit and old cable. The written site plan and options follow after the walk.

Is a camera on the tennis and pickleball courts worth the cost?
+

Often, if the courts already have fence damage, after-hours use or guest disputes. One camera at the court gate showing who enters is usually enough, and it can often share a cable route or wireless link with the pool cameras nearby.
