The Neighborhood

Why Upper West Side Properties Need Smart Lock Installation: Roofs, Yards, Gates and Glass

On the Upper West Side, the front door is rarely the only door that matters. The neighborhood was built in two great waves: rows of brownstones and limestone houses on the side streets from the 1880s and 1890s, then tall apartment houses on Broadway, West End Avenue and Riverside Drive after the IRT subway opened its Broadway stations in 1904. Wikipedia's history of the Ansonia records that the new line “spurred the development of high-rise apartment buildings on the Upper West Side along Broadway.” The result is a district where a family can live in a garden duplex with its own door under the stoop and a second door onto the yard, where a top-floor apartment shares a landing with the stair up to the roof, and where entire buildings such as the Apthorp and the Belnord are entered through arches into a private courtyard before anyone reaches an elevator.

Each of those layouts adds a way in that a smart lock on the hallway door does nothing about. In December 2019 West Side Rag passed on a warning from the 20th Precinct about seven residential burglaries between Central Park West and Broadway, from 82nd to 85th Street, in nine days. Police said the people responsible used “either a back door, fire escape or roof” and had been “crossing rooftops, moving from building to building.” The precinct commander's advice was about locks on fire-escape windows and patio sliding doors, cameras covering every access point, and a free building assessment from the precinct's Crime Prevention Officer. None of it was about the apartment's front door.

The obvious response — lock everything — runs straight into New York's rules. The Multiple Dwelling Law says a roof bulkhead door “shall never be self-locking and shall be fastened on the inside by movable bolts, hooks or a lock which does not require a key to open from the inside of the dwelling.” The city's rule on entrance doors goes further: bulkhead doors and scuttles “shall have no key locks and shall not be locked by a key at any time.” HPD says it is illegal to put double-cylinder locks on apartment doors and that key-locked gates on fire-escape windows are prohibited. In larger buildings converted after 1968, doors from the street, a court, a yard or the cellar must close and lock on their own and still open from the inside without a key. So the answer is never a smart lock on every opening. It is a smart lock on the doors people actually use, and the right latch, sensor or frame repair on the rest.

The people who live here make the planning matter. The NYU Furman Center's 2026 profile of Manhattan Community District 7 counts 230,436 residents and 130,471 homes. The homeownership rate is 35.6 percent, above the 32.7 percent citywide, which means a large share of West Side doors belong to co-op shareholders and condo owners who answer to a board as well as to themselves. Nearly 23 percent of residents are 65 or older and 43.6 percent of households are one person living alone. Census Reporter's 2024 profile of the district puts the share who worked at home at 23 percent, with a wide margin of error. A home that is empty all day is a different problem from a home where someone is in the back bedroom on video calls while a dog walker lets themselves in at the front.

The commercial blocks bring their own version of the same lesson. In August 2026 two men in ski masks used a large rock to break into a taqueria on Amsterdam Avenue between 84th and 85th Streets, smashed a restaurant window ten blocks south, and got into a coffee shop between 74th and 75th Streets by breaking the window above its front door, West Side Rag reported. The front door's lock was not the weak point; the glass above it was. And on Broadway between 59th and 110th Streets the Rag counted 80 vacant storefronts in October 2025 — every one of them a door whose next tenant will want to know who still holds a key or a code.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Upper West Side's Architecture

The same block can hold four different kinds of front door. These are the building types we meet most often west of the park, and how each one changes the job.

Side-Street Brownstones and Row Houses

Built in rows on the side streets, many now split into a garden duplex, a parlor floor-through and smaller apartments above. A single house can have a street door, a vestibule door, an under-stoop door, apartment doors on each landing and a yard door at the back — and in a converted house with a resident owner, several of those belong to different people. The LPC counts nine historic districts on the Upper West Side, so the street face of many of these houses is under landmark rules even when the apartment doors inside are not.

Courtyard Apartment Houses

The Apthorp fills the block between Broadway, West End Avenue, 78th and 79th Streets; its wrought-iron gates lead through arches to an internal courtyard with a garden, driveway and the entrances to the apartments. The Belnord's arches on 86th Street lead to a courtyard too, and its sales pages describe six separate residential lobbies. In buildings like these the gate, the lobby and the elevator all sit between the street and your door, and none of them is yours to change.

Pre-War Elevator Buildings on the Avenues

The tall apartment houses of West End Avenue, Riverside Drive and Central Park West, the Apthorp's twelve stories among them, many of them co-ops with classic-six layouts, a kitchen door to a service hall and, at the top, a few apartments on setbacks with terraces or a landing shared with the roof stair. House rules usually set the corridor hardware and may limit when work can be done.

Avenue Walk-Ups and Old-Law Tenements

Five- and six-story walk-ups on Amsterdam and Columbus, often with a shop on the ground floor. Doors are steel-clad or painted wood, transoms over apartment doors are common, and the city's old-law tenement rules require those transoms to be sealed where fire-rated apartment doors are needed. Fire escapes run across the back or the front, with windows that must open without a key.

Riverside Boulevard and Lincoln Square Towers

Riverside Park South records nineteen buildings eventually erected on the former New York Central 60th Street Yard; the first towers along Riverside Boulevard were finished at the turn of the century. These are doorman buildings with building-wide lock and key policies, rated corridor doors and, often, an in-house locksmith or a management office that controls apartment entry hardware.

SRO Buildings and Rooming Houses

HPD's Shared Housing Roadmap names the single-room-occupancy hotels of the Upper West Side as part of the city's history of shared housing. Tenants in rooming units hold their rooms under written or oral agreements, with locks on their own doors and shared bathrooms or kitchens. Locks here raise legal and fire-safety questions that do not apply anywhere else.

Cottage Co-ops on a Private Lane

Pomander Walk, between Broadway and West End Avenue from 94th to 95th Street, is a cooperative of 27 small buildings from 1921; the Walk itself is a narrow courtyard between two rows of eight buildings, with a locked gate at each end. Lane gates, house doors and the households who share them make a small access system of their own.

Mitchell-Lama and Public Housing

The Furman Center counts 12 Mitchell-Lama properties with 2,075 units and 22 public housing properties with 5,927 units in the district. Apartment entry hardware in these buildings is set by the managing agent or the housing authority, so we start with their rules and work only within them.

How the method changes from door to door

  • Brownstone apartment doors: often thinner, older wood doors that have been cut for three generations of locks. We map every old bore before choosing where the new one goes, and use a reinforcing plate where the stile is weak.
  • Garden duplex under-stoop doors: exposed to rain and leaves blown into the areaway, so we prefer a keypad with a good weather rating, or an inside-only retrofit if the door is the household's second entrance rather than its main one.
  • Yard and terrace doors: the lock must open from inside without a key; we often pair a simple thumb-turn deadbolt with a door contact instead of a smart lock, and spend the effort on the strike and frame.
  • Pre-war co-op corridor doors: heavy, sometimes rated, with a mortise lock to the building's standard. An inside-only unit on a single-cylinder deadbolt, or a smart deadbolt that keeps the corridor look, usually satisfies the house rules.
  • Walk-up doors with transoms: we check whether the transom is sealed, wired glass or still openable before promising that the new lock improves anything; a transom that opens is a way around the lock.

Rules that touch a West Side keyless door

  • Roof bulkheads: the Multiple Dwelling Law says a bulkhead door is never self-locking and is fastened on the inside with movable bolts, hooks or a lock that needs no key from inside; the city's entrance-door rule says bulkhead doors and scuttles have no key locks at all. We do not put a keyed or code-only lock on a roof door. Alarms and contacts that report the door opening are a different matter.
  • Street, court, yard and cellar entrances: in a class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments, every entrance from the street, a court, a yard or the cellar has to be automatically self-closing and self-locking, and every such door has to open readily from inside without keys.
  • Apartment doors: the Housing Maintenance Code makes the owner provide a heavy-duty latch set and deadbolt with a thumb-turn inside, plus a chain guard; HPD says double-cylinder locks on apartment doors are illegal. The Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant add a lock of their own, with a duplicate key to the owner on request.
  • Transoms in old-law tenements: in old-law tenements of four or more stories, where one-hour apartment doors are required, transoms onto the public hall are sealed and fire-retarded, and other transoms are wired glass permanently sealed shut.
  • Fire escapes: the Fire Code says fire escapes and the access to them are kept free of obstructions, and HPD prohibits key-locked gates on fire-escape windows. A lock on a fire-escape window that needs a key or a code is not an option.
  • Electromagnetic locks: NYC Building Code section 1010.1.9.9 allows magnetic locks on certain egress doors only with built-in release hardware that cuts power directly and unlocks on power loss. Most shop doors here are better served by a strike.
  • Landmarks: in the nine historic districts, exterior work generally needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit even without a building permit, while replacing ordinary door hardware such as knobs and hinges does not — except ornate historic hardware on special doors.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Upper West Side: Questions Answered

Owners, shareholders, renters and shopkeepers west of the park tend to ask us the same handful of things, so the answers are sorted below by the part of the home or business they concern. When a statute, a city rule or a published source decides the matter, it is named, so you can check it for yourself; this is practical guidance, not legal advice.

Roofs, yards and the top of the house

We live on the top floor beside the stair to the roof, and the burglars in the West 80s crossed the rooftops — what up there can legally be locked?

Less than most people expect, and more than nothing. The roof bulkhead door itself is off limits for a keyed or code lock: the Multiple Dwelling Law says it is never self-locking and is fastened inside with movable bolts, hooks or a lock that needs no key from inside, and the city's entrance-door rule says bulkhead doors have no key locks at all. That rule exists so that people can get out onto the roof in a fire, and it is why the 2019 team the 20th Precinct described could come down through buildings once they were up top. What you can change is your own side of that landing. Your apartment door is the one that matters most, and a strong smart deadbolt with a good strike and long frame screws belongs there. If you have a terrace or a window onto a roof, its lock can be improved as long as it still opens from inside without a key. And the board can add a door contact or an alarm to the bulkhead so that someone is told when it opens, which deters roof-crossers without trapping anyone.

Our garden duplex has a door under the stoop and another into the building's hall — which one should carry the smart lock?

The one the household uses every day, and in a garden duplex that is usually the under-stoop door, because it lets you in without passing through the building at all. That door also faces the weather and the areaway, so we choose a keypad deadbolt with a solid weather rating, or a fingerprint model if the household prefers not to share codes. The door into the building's hall then becomes the secondary door. It still needs a proper deadbolt, since anyone who gets into the building can reach it, but it can often stay mechanical, or take an inside-only retrofit if you want to see in the app that it is locked. The mistake we see is the reverse: a smart lock on the hall door because it is the “front door” on the lease, while the under-stoop door, which everyone actually uses, keeps a twenty-year-old cylinder that half the neighborhood once had a copy of.

Old doors, transoms and the glass around them

Our walk-up apartment door still has a glass transom over it — does that matter once a smart lock goes on?

Yes, because a lock only protects the opening it is fitted to. A transom that can still be pushed open or broken is a way around the best deadbolt on the door below. In old-law tenements of four or more stories, the city's Housing Maintenance Code already deals with this for fire reasons: where one-hour apartment doors are required, transoms onto the public hall are to be sealed and fire-retarded, and other hall transoms are glazed with wire glass and permanently sealed shut. If yours is sealed, fire-rated and fixed, it is not a security gap and the smart lock does its job. If it still opens, or still has ordinary glass, that is a condition for the owner to correct, and we would rather tell you before installation than after. We do not seal or alter transoms ourselves on a rental door, because the door assembly is the owner's responsibility; on a co-op door the board's rules decide who does it. What we can do is install the lock, note the transom on the written survey and suggest a door contact if you want to know when the door opens.

Gates, courtyards, lanes and rooms

Our building is entered through a gated arch, then a courtyard, then a lobby — which of those doors can a shareholder actually change?

Only the last one: your own apartment door, and even that within the house rules. In courtyard buildings like the Apthorp, where wrought-iron gates in the arches lead to a courtyard with a garden, a driveway and the entrances to the apartments, the gate, the courtyard and each lobby are common property run by the board or the condominium. Their access system, whether staffed, keyed or on fobs, is the building's decision and its contract. That is not a reason to skip the unit lock; it is a reason to plan it with the building's layers in mind. With staff at the gate and a lobby beyond it, your door's job is less about strangers off the street and more about people who are already inside: contractors working on other floors, staff of other households, visitors who were let through. So we put weight on personal codes, a log of who opened the door and an auto-lock, and less on gadgets aimed at the street. If the board is reviewing the gate or lobby system, its managing agent can talk to us separately.

Our walkway has a locked gate at each end — can both gates take keypads without trapping anyone inside the lane?

Yes, if the gates are treated as exits as well as entrances. A lane with a gate at each end, like the one Pomander Walk is known for, has to let everyone inside leave freely in either direction, at any hour, without a key, a code or a phone. That rules out double-sided keypad locks and anything that needs a credential from inside. The usual answer is a keypad or reader on the street side of each gate, an electric strike or a gate lock that holds the gate shut, and a lever, push bar or exit button on the inside that releases it with one simple motion. Both gates can share one list of codes, so a household adds its dog walker once and both ends work. Power and the controller sit on the property, out of reach from the sidewalk. Because a lane like this is usually owned in common, the co-op or association decides the system and who administers it; individual households then manage their own users within it. On a landmark property the reader's position on the gate or the masonry also becomes a Landmarks Preservation Commission question.

I rent a room in one of the old SRO buildings — can I add a smart lock to my room door?

Not on your own say-so, and this is one of the few places where we will not proceed until the building's position is clear. HPD's Shared Housing Roadmap explains that tenants in rooming units live in their own rooms with locks on their doors and share bathrooms or kitchens, so your room should already lock, and if it does not, that is a condition to take up with the owner. Adding a second lock is different from adding one to an ordinary apartment: the Multiple Dwelling Law section that lets tenants add their own lock excludes hotels, and many West Side SROs began as residential hotels. The same HPD report warns that smart locks do not have master keys that management or the Fire Department can override in an emergency, and proposes that future rooming-unit locks have FDNY-approved fail-safe release. So the honest answer is: ask the owner in writing, and if the reply is unclear, the Goddard Riverside Law Project, which represented SRO tenants in a 2024 West Side Rag story, helps tenants with housing questions. If the owner agrees in writing, we fit a lock that keeps a working key and opens from inside in one motion.

Co-op rules and fire-escape windows

Our co-op only lets residents do alterations in the summer — does changing a lock count as an alteration?

It depends on the wording of your house rules, and it is worth asking before you book rather than after. Brick Underground notes that some buildings only allow renovations in the summer and that most limit work to 9 to 5 on weekdays. Those rules are written with kitchens and bathrooms in mind, and many managing agents treat a lock swap on an existing bore as repair or maintenance rather than an alteration. A new bore through the door, a change to the corridor face, or any wiring to a strike in the frame is more likely to count. Our practice is simple: we send you a short description of exactly what will be done, with photos of both faces of the door, and you send it to the managing agent with a one-line question. If the answer is “summer only”, an inside-only retrofit on the existing deadbolt often qualifies as no change at all, and the full smart deadbolt can follow when the window opens. Either way we keep to the building's weekday hours.

Can the security gate on our fire-escape window be opened with a code instead of a key?

No, and a good installer will not offer to make it so. A fire-escape window is a way out of the apartment in a fire, often in smoke and in the dark, and HPD states plainly that key-locked gates on fire-escape windows are prohibited. The Fire Code adds that fire escapes and the access to them are kept free of obstructions at all times. A keypad, a fingerprint reader or a phone-operated latch on that gate would recreate the same problem as a key: someone who is panicking, a child, a guest or a firefighter coming in from outside would need knowledge they may not have. What is allowed is a gate designed to be opened from inside by hand, without a key or a code, and a window lock that works. If the fire escape worries you because of the 2019 rooftop burglaries, the useful additions are a contact on the window that reports when it opens, a camera aimed along the escape where the building permits it, and the habit the 20th Precinct urged at the time: keep the window locked whenever you are out. We fit the contact; we do not put any code on the gate.

Shops, practices and towers

Burglars on Amsterdam Avenue got into a coffee shop through the glass above its door — what should happen to the fanlight over ours?

It means looking at the glass as well as the lock. In the August 2026 break-ins West Side Rag described, one business was entered after a large rock went through the window above the front door, another had a window smashed but held, and a third was broken into with the same rock. A smart lock with staff codes and a log is still worth having; it tells you who opened up, it stops lost keys from being a problem and it locks itself at closing. But it cannot stop someone climbing through a fanlight. For a storefront we look at the whole opening: the glass above and beside the door, whether a roll-down gate is used, where the alarm's contacts and glass-break detectors are, and how strong the frame is around the strike. Often the right package is a keypad lock on the door plus a glass-break detector and a contact, with the alarm and the lock reporting to the same phone. We quote the lock at the published Manhattan rates and refer the glazing to the right trade.

Our practice suite opens off the lobby of a Central Park West building — can the door let patients into the waiting room on schedule without opening the treatment rooms?

Yes, by treating the suite as two zones. The suite entrance can run on a schedule, unlocked during reception hours if someone is at the front desk, or on a buzz-in or a keypad if nobody is, and locked automatically at the end of the day. The inner door from the waiting area to the treatment rooms or offices then gets its own lock with staff codes, so a patient who arrived early, or someone who wandered in from the lobby, cannot walk through. Both doors must still open from inside with one motion. Because the suite is in a residential building, the board or managing agent usually controls the lobby hours and may have rules about what hardware can face the lobby, so we confirm those before ordering anything. Staff codes go in the log by name, which helps if records ever have to show who was in the suite and when. For a one-door suite, a keypad lever with a schedule and a privacy mode is often enough.

In our Riverside Boulevard tower only the building's locksmith may touch apartment doors — is a smart lock still possible?

Usually, but on the building's terms. Doorman towers along Riverside Boulevard often have a management policy that only the in-house locksmith, or a contractor it approves, may change apartment entry hardware, partly because corridor doors are rated and partly because staff need emergency access. We work in one of three ways. We can supply and fully program a lock the building's locksmith then fits, so the door work stays with them and the codes, phone keys and administrator setup stay with us. We can fit it ourselves after the management office approves the model and the method in writing. Or, where neither is allowed, we can install an inside-only retrofit on your existing deadbolt, which in many buildings does not count as changing the door at all. Whatever route, the building's emergency key arrangement keeps working, and the hallway side keeps to the standard the building sets. Ask the office for its policy in writing; it saves a wasted visit.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Upper West Side

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so the claims below are the ones that cost guides, product pages and home-improvement sites publish — the material AI summaries are built from. Each holds for a suburban house; each needs correcting west of the park.

“Put a smart lock on every exterior door”

Whole-house guides tell homeowners to make every door keyless so the app can lock the lot at bedtime. In a suburban house with a front door, a side door and a garage door, that makes sense.

On the Upper West Side, several of the “exterior” doors are ones New York forbids you to key-lock. The roof bulkhead may not carry a key lock at all. Yard and court doors in larger post-1968 buildings must close and lock by themselves yet open from inside without a key. HPD prohibits key-locked gates on fire-escape windows.

So the West Side version is a smart lock on the door people actually use, a thumb-turn lock and a contact on the doors that must stay free to exit, and no lock at all on the bulkhead beyond what the law allows. That plan is safer, and it is legal.

“Fit a smart lock and the burglary risk is handled”

Product pages lead with security: tamper alarms, auto-lock, no keys to lose. All true, as far as the door goes.

The West 80s burglary team that the 20th Precinct warned about in 2019 came in by back doors, fire escapes and roofs. The coffee shop on Amsterdam Avenue in 2026 was entered through the window above its door. In November 2024 the New York Post reported that a burglar in a 39-floor doorman building on West End Avenue smashed through an apartment door and broke the jamb. None of those would have been stopped by a better keypad.

A smart lock improves control: who has access, when they used it, and whether the door is locked now. Security comes from the whole opening — the frame, the strike, the glass, the other doors — and we price those honestly alongside the lock.

“Fitting one is a quick do-it-yourself chore”

A Bob Vila review of smart locks found that its testers got by with a single Phillips screwdriver in roughly thirty minutes. On a new door that came pre-bored for a standard deadbolt, that is believable.

A West Side apartment door may have been cut for three or four earlier locks, painted twenty times and hung in a frame that has settled with the building. A brownstone under-stoop door may be swollen with damp. A co-op may need the work described to the managing agent first, and may only allow it on weekdays between nine and five.

The thirty minutes is still in there. It just comes after the survey, the paperwork and the time it takes to make an old door close cleanly against its strike.

“About $300 is the going rate to have one put in”

The figure that keeps appearing comes from Fixr, whose January 2025 cost guide gives $250 to $400 for having a smart lock put in, roughly $300 typical, and $50 to $100 an hour for a handyman. It is a reasonable national benchmark for one house door.

Manhattan work carries a 1.25 area rate on our published figures, and a West Side apartment door is quoted flat at a free walkthrough because the door, the earlier locks and the building's rules decide the work. Building doors have published numbers: a keypad or fob reader with an electric strike is $1,875–$3,125 here.

The national number is a sensible check on whether anyone is overcharging for a plain door. It is not a price for a garden duplex with three entrances or a courtyard gate shared by forty households.

“Tenants never need anyone’s permission for a retrofit”

Renter-focused articles often say an inside-only retrofit needs no permission because it changes nothing outside. In many apartments that is close to true.

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law does let tenants add their own lock and asks them to give the owner a duplicate key on request, but it excludes hotels, and HPD's Shared Housing Roadmap says the city has interpreted its codes so that residents of shared apartments cannot have locks on bedroom doors. It also warns that smart locks lack a master key that management or FDNY can override. Co-op shareholders answer to house rules as well.

So a renter in an ordinary apartment is usually fine with a retrofit, a shareholder should tell the managing agent, a room in a share should not get a lock at all, and a room in an SRO needs the owner's written agreement first.

“Just use the building's Wi-Fi or a hub anywhere in the home”

Setup guides assume the router is a few rooms away and the signal reaches the door. August's own support page says its bridge belongs within 10 to 15 feet of the lock.

Pre-war West Side apartments put the entrance at the end of a long gallery or foyer, often behind masonry and plaster, while the router sits in a back bedroom by the cable outlet. Garden duplexes put the under-stoop door a floor below the living space. Signals that look fine on a phone in the kitchen can drop at the door.

We test at the door before choosing the lock, and if the signal is weak we plan for a bridge in the foyer, a Thread border router or a lock that keeps its codes locally so it works whatever the network does.

“Any smart lock will fit a standard door”

Fit checkers ask for a door thickness and a backset and assume the rest is standard. Most new doors are.

Here the doors run from heavy pre-war mortise doors to thin, much-cut brownstone doors and steel-clad doors whose fire labels limit drilling. August itself says its retrofit suits single-cylinder deadbolts only.

We measure, photograph and check labels at the walkthrough, then name a lock that fits. If the honest answer is that a smart lock is the wrong tool for a particular door, we say so before anything is ordered.

Door Survey

The Whole-Home Door Survey, Opening by Opening

Fitting the lock is the short part of a West Side visit. The survey that comes first is what decides whether it was worth fitting. This is the order we walk a home in, and what we write down at each opening. You keep the sheet whether or not you go ahead.

1. The door everyone uses

We start with the door the household actually comes home through, which is not always the one named on the lease. In a garden duplex it is usually under the stoop; in a parlor floor-through it may be the building's hall door; in a pre-war co-op it is the corridor door off the elevator. We note the door's construction, thickness, any fire label, every existing bore and plate, and whether it closes and latches on its own when released from half open. This is where the smart lock almost always goes.

2. The second door

Classic sixes often have a kitchen door to a service hall, combined apartments can keep two doors on the corridor, and duplexes have one door per floor. For each we ask how often it is used, by whom, and whether it is required as an exit. Some get an inside-only retrofit so the app shows them locked; most just need a sound deadbolt that is actually thrown.

3. The yard, court or garden door

Back doors onto shared yards are where the 2019 West 80s pattern began for some households. We check that the door opens from inside without a key, as the rules require in many buildings, and then look at the strike, the frame and the glass. A contact that reports the door opening, and an alert if it is left open after dark, usually does more here than a keypad.

4. Terraces and anything near the roof

Setback terraces, roof decks and the landing beside the stair to the bulkhead get the same test: can a person from a neighbouring roof reach this opening, and does it still release freely from inside? We improve terrace door locks within that limit and write a separate note for the board about the bulkhead, since the law keeps key locks off it.

5. Windows onto fire escapes

We do not lock fire-escape windows with keys or codes, and HPD prohibits key-locked gates on them. We do note whether the window locks work, whether an approved gate is present, and whether a contact or a camera aimed at the escape would help.

6. Transoms and glass beside the door

A transom over an apartment door, a sidelight beside a brownstone door or a fanlight over a shop door can make a lock irrelevant. We note whether each is sealed, wired, fixed or openable, and whose responsibility it is to deal with it.

7. Who holds what

Finally we list every person and every system that can open any of these doors: household members, the owner's duplicate key, the super's emergency key, a dog walker, a cleaner, a home attendant, the building's fob or app. That list becomes the code plan, and anything on it that no longer belongs there is the first thing we remove.

Terms

A Short Glossary for West Side Door Work

  • Bulkhead: the small structure on the roof that houses the top of the stair and the roof door. New York law keeps key locks off bulkhead doors so people can always get out.
  • Scuttle: a roof hatch reached by a ladder, treated like a bulkhead door under the same rules.
  • Garden duplex: an apartment occupying the garden and parlor floors of a brownstone, often with its own entrance under the stoop and a door to the yard.
  • Floor-through: an apartment taking a whole floor of a row house from front to back.
  • Areaway: the sunken space in front of a row house, below sidewalk level, onto which the under-stoop door opens.
  • Old-law tenement: a tenement built under the rules in force before 1901; the Housing Maintenance Code has special fire rules for them, including sealed transoms.
  • Transom: the glazed panel over a door. Over an apartment door in an old building it may need to be sealed shut.
  • Rooming unit: a room let on its own, as in an SRO building, with its own lock and shared bathrooms or kitchens.
  • Setback terrace: an outdoor space on the stepped-back upper floors of a pre-war apartment house.
  • Inside-only retrofit: a motor housing that replaces just the indoor thumb-turn of a deadbolt you already have; the corridor face and the key stay as they were.
  • Strike: the metal box and faceplate set into the jamb where the bolt lands; after a century of settling, it is often where an old door fails first.

Can the keypad at our practice be wiped down with disinfectant between patients?
+

Most sealed keypads and fingerprint readers tolerate gentle cleaning, but makers differ on which products are safe, so we check the specific model’s cleaning guidance before ordering. Our door has heavy acoustic seals so a musician can practise — will a motorised bolt still throw?
+

It can, once the door closes fully against its seals without being pushed. Do you fit door sensors on the yard, terrace and roof-stair doors at the same visit?

Yes, if you want them. Contacts that report to the same app as the smart lock, or to an alarm panel, are often the right answer for doors that must open freely from inside. We list them as separate lines on the quote, so you can decide door by door.
