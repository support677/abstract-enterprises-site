The 171 red-brick buildings Metropolitan Life put up between 1939 and 1942 are now two condominiums, North and South, and their entrances run on a condominium-owned fob system and, since 2024–25, an app-based video intercom that another company installed across the whole complex. The Neighborhood

Why Parkchester Properties Need Access Control

Parkchester began as a single landlord’s idea. In 1938 the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company bought 129 acres from the New York Catholic Protectory and announced a self-contained apartment community with its own shops, its own central heating plant and landscaped open space, building on only about 27 percent of the land; Richmond H. Shreve, whose firm designed the Empire State Building, drew the plans (Parkchester Preservation Management history). Wikipedia dates construction from 1939 to 1942 and counts 171 four-sided brick buildings, either eight or thirteen stories, with 12,271 apartments (Wikipedia: Parkchester, Bronx). The same name now covers the streets around it as well: Wikipedia draws the neighborhood from East Tremont Avenue on the north to Castle Hill Avenue on the east, Westchester Avenue on the south, East 177th Street and the Cross Bronx Expressway on the southwest and White Plains Road on the west.

For thirty years one owner held every door. That changed in stages. MetLife sold the complex in 1968 to a syndicate headed by Harry B. Helmsley; the North Quadrant became the Parkchester North Condominium in the early 1970s (management’s timeline marks 1972, Wikipedia says about a third of the complex converted in 1974), and the South, East and West Quadrants became the Parkchester South Condominium in 1986. In 1998 a partnership called Parkchester Preservation Company bought all 6,362 unsold sponsor apartments, which sat in every building in all four quadrants, and took over the legal role of sponsor (management timeline). Parkchester Preservation Management describes itself as the exclusive on-site rental agent for those sponsor-owned apartments. The result is a mix you cannot see from the sidewalk: in one stairwell there may be an owner who has lived there since the 1980s, a renter of the sponsor, a renter of a private investor and an owner who has just moved in.

That mix is why access control in Parkchester works differently from anywhere else we serve. The Parkchester South Condominium says it oversees nearly 8,000 apartments in the east, west and south quadrants (PSC home page). Its building-key page describes the controllers, sub-controllers and door readers, and the exterior doors they control, as a common element maintained by the condominium, with key fobs bought and issued only by the condominium for a fee the board sets, currently $11 (PSC Building Keys). Its facility-services page goes further and lists hallways, the apartment door, windows and most locks among the common elements, maintained by a unionized staff that includes locksmiths (PSC Facility Services). In the North, the condominium’s own rules say the bottom lock cylinder on each apartment door belongs to the condominium, that a second cylinder may be added or changed, and that all unit door keys must be filed with the Management Office (PNC Rules and Regulations).

On top of the fobs sits the newest layer. Comelit, the intercom maker, writes that a modernization plan for Parkchester’s intercoms began in September 2024 and that within nine months all 171 buildings had touch-screen entrance panels and network gateways, with each resident given a lifetime app license, installed by a firm called Total Security; residents without smartphones receive the call on an ordinary phone and press *9 to open the door (Comelit case study). The residents’ intercom help site adds that the fob system was not changed and is separate from the intercom, and that only registered residents whose email or phone number is on file with management can be added (Parkchester intercom FAQ). None of that system is ours, and we do not service it.

Residents have been loud about doors. In August 2024 a man kicked a large hole through an apartment door in a building about a block from the 6 train and climbed in while the woman who lived there hid in her bathroom; he was arrested. Around 100 neighbors rallied on September 21, 2024, and planned a second rally for October 13, asking for lighting, cameras and stronger doors; one longtime resident told the Bronx Times she had not realized until then that her door was hollow, and the residents’ private Facebook group was cataloguing faulty lobby door locks and missing packages alongside elevator outages (Bronx Times, October 7, 2024). In 2025 News 12 reported a couple, both using wheelchairs, who said they had been unable to buzz in guests or medication deliveries for months while management said the system was working (News 12, February 5, 2025), and later a new emergency call point near Virginia and McGraw avenues (News 12, July 22, 2025).

Read together, those sources set the limits of this page. A private installer cannot fix a condominium’s lobby door because a resident asks, cannot swap an apartment door the condominium owns, and should not sell anyone a way around a building-wide fob system. What we can do is narrower and, we think, more useful: tell each caller exactly where their own authority ends, do the work that sits inside it, help owners and boards put a written request or a bid package together, and serve the large part of Parkchester that is not inside the complex at all. Wikipedia notes that the surrounding area is dominated by multi-unit buildings unrelated to the complex, with 78.4 percent of housing units renter-occupied, and that the retail backbones run along Metropolitan Avenue, East Tremont Avenue, Unionport Road and White Plains Road, with more shops on Starling and McGraw avenues.

Repairs

Shop Reader Down or an Approved Deadbolt Jammed in Parkchester?

Two kinds of Parkchester call reach us when something has failed, and they get opposite answers.

If the failure is a condominium building entrance, its fob reader, the Comelit panel, the lobby door that will not latch, or your own apartment door in a building where the condominium owns the door, the fastest fix is the condominium, not us. In the South the Service Department handles repairs and the condominium says private arrangements with its staff are prohibited (PSC Facility Services); in the North, complaints about building service go in writing to the Board of Managers, the managing agent or the General Manager (PNC rule 22). If the intercom does not ring your phone, the residents’ help site says the usual cause is that your number or email is not on file with management. Write down the date, the door and what happened, and keep a copy: a dated, written record is what boards, managers and, if it comes to that, city agencies act on.

If the failure is yours, a keypad deadbolt in an approved position inside your unit, a reader on your shop’s back door on Metropolitan Avenue, an office door in a medical suite, or the street door of a privately owned building on Starling Avenue or in Stratton Park, call or text (646) 490-0629. Same-day visits are usually possible. Service is billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), and we work on systems we did not install, provided the person calling has the right to authorize the work.

Who Owns the Door

Whose Door Is It? A Door-by-Door Map of Parkchester

Every Parkchester access question we answer starts with the same exercise: walk from the sidewalk to the couch and name the owner of each opening on the way. The answer changes four or five times in thirty feet, and it is different in the North and in the South, so we write it down rather than rely on memory.

The New York Condominium Act frames it. A condominium is made of units, owned by individuals, and common elements, owned by all unit owners together and run by the Board of Managers. Courts reading that law in a case involving Parkchester North have repeated its rule that each unit owner is deemed the person in control of the unit owned, while the board controls the common elements (Smith v. Parkchester North, via vLex). Where exactly the line falls between unit and common element is set by each condominium’s declaration and bylaws, and Parkchester’s two condominiums publish more of their line than most.

The table below is our working map, built only from what the two condominiums publish. It is not legal advice and it does not replace the declaration, the bylaws or a letter from the managing agent; where a published rule is silent, the table says so, and the right step is to ask the office in writing before anyone buys hardware.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Parkchester’s Architecture

Six kinds of property make up the Parkchester we serve: two heights of complex building, the terrace units, the retail space MetLife built in, and the private buildings on and beyond the edges. The rules for touching a door differ for each.

The eight-story Parkchester building

The most common building in the complex: a four-sided brick block with a single elevator, according to Wikipedia. Entry is the condominium’s fob door and Comelit panel. For a unit owner here, the work we can do is inside the apartment and, in the North, in the second lock position, after approval.

The thirteen-story Parkchester building

Taller, with two elevators side by side (Wikipedia). More apartments share one entrance, which is why residents notice lobby-door failures quickly. Same ownership rules as the eight-story blocks: the entrance, lobby and corridors are the condominium’s.

Terrace apartments

Some ground floors have terrace-level units, marked with a T, whose living rooms open through an extra screened door to a concrete patio (Wikipedia). A patio door is the one apartment opening that faces the outside directly. Its status is not spelled out in the published rules, so the first step is a written question to management.

Retail buildings on Metropolitan Avenue and the ovals

Management’s history describes a shopping center of more than 500,000 square feet largely built into the residential structures near Hugh J. Grant Circle. Shop doors belong to the retail landlord’s premises and the tenant’s lease, not to the condominiums, so tenants can order work with landlord consent.

Walk-ups and elevator buildings outside the complex

Wikipedia notes that the area around the complex is dominated by multi-unit buildings unrelated to it. These have ordinary private owners and the usual city rules for multiple dwellings, so the full range of street-door, vestibule and cellar-door work applies.

Stratton Park and the edges

West of White Plains Road, Wikipedia describes Stratton Park, bounded by the Amtrak Northeast Corridor and East 177th Street, in ZIP 10460, whose residents consider themselves part of Parkchester. It is outside the complex, so owners there decide for themselves.

How a Parkchester job changes because of who owns the door

  • We ask for the quadrant before the address. North and South publish different rules, so the same request can get a different answer one street apart.
  • We ask whether you own or rent. Renters of a unit owner deal with that owner; renters of the sponsor deal with Parkchester Preservation Management; owners deal with their condominium.
  • We get approval in writing before ordering hardware. The approval names the model, the position and who holds the spare key.
  • We keep everything we install off the common elements unless the condominium has hired us. No cable in corridors, nothing on the outside face of an apartment door, nothing on the building entrance.
  • We do not interfere with the fob or intercom systems. No splicing, no repeaters, no attempts to make a personal device open a building door.
  • We leave a record of what was installed, where the spare key is filed and how to reverse the work if the unit is sold.

Unit Owners

What a Unit Owner Can Change, and How to Ask for the Rest

Most Parkchester owners who call us want one thing: a stronger, smarter apartment door after reading about the 2024 break-in. The published rules shape the answer. In the North, the bottom cylinder is the condominium’s and stays; the second cylinder may be added or changed, and every unit door key, including the key to a new second lock, must be filed with the Management Office (PNC rule 19). That leaves room for a good deadbolt, keyed or keypad, in the second position, provided management accepts the model. In the South, the condominium lists the apartment door and most locks as common elements and says its own staff, including locksmiths, can do non-common work for a fee through the Service Department (PSC Facility Services). There, the first call is to the Service Department, and we step in only for what it confirms in writing is the owner’s to change.

A keypad deadbolt in the second position does two useful things in a building like this. It removes the hunt for keys when you come home with shopping from Metropolitan Avenue, and it lets you give a relative, an aide or a dog-sitter a code that you can cancel without changing a cylinder. It does not make a hollow door solid. If you are worried about the door itself, the right request is to the condominium, because in the South the door is theirs and in the North its replacement is a matter for the declaration and the board.

Inside the apartment, almost everything is the owner’s. That is where we fit locks on interior doors, a lockable home-office door, door contacts that report when the entrance opens, and an indoor camera aimed at the inside of your own entrance. The North’s rule 2 bars personal items on the outside of apartment doors, so we do not mount doorbell cameras or keypads on the corridor face of a door unless the condominium has approved it in writing; an indoor camera that sees the door from inside does not touch the common element at all.

If an owner or household member has a disability and needs a physical change, such as a lever handle, an automatic opener or a different lock, the route is a reasonable-accommodation request. Parkchester’s published policy says an accommodation may include a physical change to a unit or a common area, that requests can be made in writing or orally to Resident Services at 2000 East Tremont Avenue, and that a decision follows within ten business days of complete paperwork (Parkchester Reasonable Accommodation Policy). If the condominium approves a change and asks the resident to arrange the work, we can quote it and install it to their conditions.

Five steps for a request that gets a clear answer

  • Write to the right office. North: the Board of Managers, managing agent or General Manager. South: the Service Department for repairs, Resident Services for accommodations.
  • Name the door, the position and the product. “Keypad deadbolt in the upper lock position of apartment 5C” gets an answer; “a smart lock” does not.
  • Attach the data sheet and our certificate of insurance. We supply both.
  • Say how the spare key reaches management. In the North a filed key is required.
  • Ask for the answer in writing and keep it with your unit papers; the next owner will need it.

Investor Owners

Investor Owners and Landlords of Condominium Units

A large share of Parkchester apartments are rented by owners who do not live there: the sponsor, and private investors who own anything from one unit to dozens. The South’s key policy has a whole access group for them. A non-resident owner’s agents and employees can be issued fobs that open, around the clock, only the buildings where that owner holds title; the policy’s own example is an owner with 25 apartments in ten buildings and three keys (PSC Building Keys, Access Group II). The North requires the board’s prior written consent before a unit owner leases or renews a lease (PNC rules 32 and 33) and makes unit owners responsible for their tenants’ acts.

What that leaves for a landlord of condominium units is the turnover between tenants. A mechanical second lock means a cylinder change, or a filed rekey, every time a tenant leaves. An approved keypad deadbolt in the same position lets the owner retire the departing tenant’s code the day the keys come back, issue a new one at move-in, and give a contractor a code for a week of painting. Using one approved model in every unit keeps the paperwork simple: one data sheet to management, one way of filing spare keys, one procedure for the staff.

We set these up as a short written procedure rather than as an app on the owner’s phone alone: which code belongs to which tenant, when it was issued, who deleted it and when, and where the filed key is. That matters if a former tenant disputes a lockout, and it matters to the condominium when it asks how the owner controls access to a unit it is responsible for watching over.

Boards and Managing Agents

When the Condominium or Its Managing Agent Is the Client

The condominiums are large organizations with their own staff, systems and contractors. The building-entry system in the South is described in detail on its website, the intercom upgrade was carried out by another firm, and the South says it runs the fourth largest high-pressure steam plant in the metropolitan area for both condominiums and over 75 commercial enterprises (PSC Facility Services). An outside installer is one of several possible bidders on a project, not a substitute for the condominium’s own people.

When a board or managing agent asks us to bid, we start by asking for the existing systems’ documentation: controller model and software, how fobs are formatted, where the controllers sit, and who supports the intercom. We do not promise that a new door will join the existing fob system until we have seen that documentation and the system’s owner has agreed. Where joining is not possible, we say so and price a separate, clearly labeled system for that door instead.

Typical common-element doors that boards put out to bid in buildings like these are storage and bicycle rooms, management and security offices, staff-only service doors and doors to mechanical spaces. Every one of them has to keep free exit from the inside and, where it is on a required exit route, release as the NYC Building Code requires (NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.8–9). Entry logs from a residential building also carry privacy duties under the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which covers smart access systems in multiple dwellings (HPD: Tenant Data Privacy Law); in a condominium, how those duties apply is a question for the board’s counsel, and we build retention settings to whatever they decide.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Parkchester

Parkchester’s shops sit in two kinds of place: the retail space MetLife built into the complex, and the private buildings on the streets around it. Listings name Olshan Properties as owner of the Parkchester retail district on Metropolitan Avenue (LoopNet listing); the condominiums say they heat over 75 commercial enterprises. Rules that shape Parkchester entry work

  • The condominium documents. Declaration, bylaws and rules decide what is a unit and what is a common element; in the North, rule 19 governs the two lock cylinders and rule 2 bars items on the outside of apartment doors (PNC rules).
  • The South’s key program. Four access groups (residents, non-resident owners and agents, caregivers, contractors and agents), each with its own registration, photo and fee rules (PSC Building Keys).
  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. Requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercom in larger multiple dwellings; in the complex that duty sits with the condominiums, outside it with each building’s owner (MDL §50-a).
  • NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9. Electrically locked doors on an exit route must release on fire alarm and power loss and from a manual control (Building Code).
  • Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021). Limits what data smart access systems in multiple dwellings collect and how long it is kept (HPD).
  • The shop lease. Alterations to doors and frames in retail space normally need the landlord’s written consent, and the landlord will want keys or an override for emergencies.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Parkchester: Questions Answered

These are the questions Parkchester owners, renters, landlords and shopkeepers bring us, grouped by who is asking. Each answer starts with who controls the door, because in Parkchester that decides everything else.

Apartment Doors and Locks Inside the Complex

The bottom lock on our Parkchester North apartment door belongs to the condominium. What can go in the upper position, and who keeps a key to it?

Under rule 19 of the North’s Rules and Regulations, owners and tenants may not change the bottom cylinder, may add or change a second cylinder, and must file all unit door keys with the Management Office (PNC rules). In practice the upper position can take a good keyed deadbolt or a keypad deadbolt with a key override, as long as management accepts the model and receives a key.

We bring the product sheet to you first so you can send it to management with a short note naming the apartment and the position. Once approval comes back in writing, the installation itself is ordinary locksmith-grade work: a correctly sized bore, a strike reinforced into the frame, a test that the bolt throws fully, and the filed key handed to management with a receipt.

If you rent from a unit owner, the request goes through that owner, who answers to the board for what happens in the unit.

After the 2024 break-in we found out our apartment door may be hollow. Can we hire someone to replace it or bolt a steel plate to it?

In Parkchester South, not on your own: the condominium lists the apartment door as a common element (PSC Facility Services). In the North, the published rules cover the locks but not the door slab, so whether an owner may replace or reinforce the door is a question for the declaration and the Board of Managers. Either way, a contractor who swaps or plates a door without written approval exposes the owner to a violation and to being told to put it back.

The useful step is a written request. Residents who rallied in 2024 asked management to replace or reinforce doors at the sponsor’s expense, and their council member’s office was reported as saying $500,000 in capital improvements for the two condominiums would come with the Metro-North package (Bronx Times). A dated letter from you, naming the apartment and the concern, puts your door on that record.

What you can do now without touching the door: an approved deadbolt in the upper position in the North, a door contact that alerts your phone, and an indoor camera that sees the entrance from inside the unit. None of those makes the door stronger, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Can we mount a video doorbell or a camera on the corridor side of our apartment door?

Not without written permission. The North’s rule 2 says no personal property or decorative items on the outside of apartment doors, with FDNY “tot finder” stickers as the only stated exception (PNC rule 2), and the corridor is a common element in both condominiums. A doorbell camera also records neighbors in a shared hallway, which is the kind of thing a board will want to decide once for everybody.

The approach that stays inside your own space is an indoor camera aimed at the back of your entrance door, or a door contact that tells your phone when the door opens. If the board does approve a corridor-side device, ask for the approval to name the model and the mounting, and we will install exactly that.

Our ground-floor terrace apartment has a screened patio door. Is that door ours to lock however we like?

Do not assume so. Wikipedia describes the terrace-level T apartments as having an extra screened door from the living room to a concrete patio, but neither condominium’s published rules say whether that door, the patio or the opening is part of the unit. A terrace faces the grounds directly, so it is exactly the opening a board is likely to treat as common or limited-common.

Ask management in writing what is yours: the screen door, the door behind it, the patio, the fence if there is one. If the answer gives you the interior door, we can fit a keyed or keypad lock and a contact on it; if it gives you nothing, the request goes back to the condominium.

The Building Entrance, Fobs and the Intercom

The lobby door in my building does not latch. If I pay, will a private installer come and fix it?

Not a reputable one. The entrance door and its fob hardware are condominium common elements; a private repair would be unauthorized work on someone else’s property, could break the fob system’s monitoring, and would leave you liable if it failed.

What works is a written report to the condominium, with the building, the date and what the door does, kept on file. Residents organized through the Parkchester Watch Group have used exactly that kind of record, and the Bronx Times reported that faulty lobby door locks were among the things residents were documenting in 2024 (Bronx Times). If the written route gets no answer, a copy to the Board of Managers, rather than only to the front desk, is the next step the published rules point to.

The new intercom rings an old landline that nobody answers. Who changes where it calls?

Management. The residents’ intercom help site says the system calls the home and mobile numbers on file with Parkchester’s management office, that a changed phone number has to be updated there, and that the app works only once your email is on file and the welcome message has arrived (Parkchester intercom FAQ). It lists separate management numbers for the North and for the South or the sponsor’s rental office.

We cannot reprogram it: the panels, gateways and resident records belong to the condominium’s system, and Comelit says resident contact details are stored locally in each building’s gateway on a closed network.

My mother lives alone in Parkchester South and cannot get down to the lobby to let her home aide in. What are her options?

The South’s key program has a group for exactly this. A resident who is elderly or disabled and lives alone can ask, by a letter countersigned by the caregiver, for a fob issued to the caregiver; the caregiver registers in person with photo identification, the fob can be limited to set days and hours and given an expiry date, and the caregiver may not establish residency (PSC Building Keys, Access Group III). That is safer than lending her own fob, which the policy says must not be swapped among household members.

Inside the apartment, a keypad deadbolt in an approved position can give the aide a personal code that your mother, or you, can cancel the day the agency changes staff. If she needs something more physical, such as an automatic opener, that goes through the reasonable-accommodation process described on this page.

Owners Who Rent Out Parkchester Apartments

I rent out four apartments in three Parkchester South buildings. How do my handyman and I get into those buildings when we do not live there?

Through the South’s Access Group II. A non-resident owner sends a letter listing the people who need access and the buildings where the owner holds title; each person gets a fob in their own name that opens those buildings around the clock, and the group is updated as the owner buys or sells, without reissuing fobs. The policy adds that the fob may be used only on the owner’s business (PSC Building Keys).

Keep a simple list: each fob holder, the date issued, and the day you told the condominium they left your employment. It is the same record you would want for apartment keys.

Between tenants, is it cheaper to rekey the upper lock every time or to put in a keypad deadbolt once?

If you turn a unit over every year or two, a single approved keypad deadbolt usually pays for itself within a few changes, because retiring a code costs nothing while each rekey is a new visit. If the same tenant stays for many years, a good mechanical lock is simpler and has no batteries to forget.

Whichever you choose, the North still requires a filed key, and a keypad lock still has one. What the keypad changes is that the departing tenant’s way in can be ended the moment you get the apartment back, and a painter or exterminator can be given a code that stops on Friday.

Our tenant fitted a smart lock without asking us. What should we do before the condominium notices?

Find out which position it is in and whether management has a key. In the North, a tenant who changed the bottom cylinder has broken rule 19 and the condominium’s cylinder has to go back; a lock in the upper position may be acceptable if management approves the model and receives a key. In the South, any lock change should have gone through the Service Department.

Then put it right in writing: tell the tenant what the lease and the condominium require, ask management whether the device can stay, and either file the key or have it removed. We can swap an unapproved lock for the condominium’s standard or for an approved model, and return the tenant’s device to them.

Shops, Offices and the Buildings Around the Complex

Our clinic on Metropolitan Avenue has a back door into a corridor shared with the residential building. Who decides what goes on that door?

Start with your lease and the floor plan. The shop or office side of the door belongs to your premises under the lease with the retail landlord; the corridor beyond it may be part of the residential building, which in the complex is a condominium common element. If the door is on a required exit route for either side, the Building Code decides how it must release.

In practice that means two written consents: the retail landlord for any work on your door and frame, and, if anything mounts on the corridor side or the door is an exit for residents, the condominium as well. We draw the door, mark which side each device is on and send the package to both.

Our building on Starling Avenue is not part of the complex. Do the Parkchester condominium rules apply to it?

No. The condominium rules, fob program and intercom apply only to the complex. A privately owned building on Starling Avenue, Unionport Road, White Plains Road or in Stratton Park follows its owner’s decisions and the city’s rules for multiple dwellings, such as the self-closing, self-locking door and intercom requirements of Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a (MDL §50-a).

That makes these buildings the simplest Parkchester work we do: the owner or managing agent decides, we survey the doors, and the system is designed around the building’s residents and shops rather than around someone else’s platform.

The Metro-North station planned for East Tremont Avenue will bring new buildings and more people. Should an owner nearby plan the entry system around that now?

It is worth planning, not rushing. The city says the rezoning around the new Bronx stations was approved in August 2024 and that the MTA expects the stations to open in 2027 (NYC Planning); Wikipedia places the Parkchester/Van Nest station on East Tremont Avenue just east of Unionport Road and reports that up to 7,000 homes could follow in the three neighborhoods rezoned. If you already plan to replace an old street-door system in the next few years, choosing one that can add credentials and readers without rewiring keeps options open.

What we would not do is buy hardware for foot traffic that has not arrived. Build the system for the residents and tenants you have, with cable and capacity for one or two more doors, and revisit it once the station and the new buildings are real.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Parkchester

Google’s AI Overview and related-question boxes were unreachable from the environment we researched in, so nothing below quotes them. Instead, here are seven pieces of access control advice that circulate widely online and in sales pitches, each checked against what Parkchester’s own documents and local reporting say.

“Any locksmith or installer can upgrade your building’s entry for you”

In most private buildings, an installer hired by the owner can. In Parkchester, the building entrances, their readers and controllers, and the intercom panels belong to the condominiums, which say so on their own websites and run them with their own staff and contractors.

A resident cannot hire anyone to change those doors, and a contractor who agrees to is doing unauthorized work on someone else’s property. The honest service an outside installer can offer here is advice on how to ask, and work on the doors that are actually yours.

When a website or chatbot promises a lobby upgrade “for your apartment building” without asking who owns it, ask that question back before you pay anything.

“Smart locks are renter-friendly: install one and take it with you”

Some smart locks fit over an existing deadbolt and come off cleanly, and that advice works in many rentals. In the North, the bottom cylinder is the condominium’s, a change to the second cylinder still requires a filed key, and a renter of a unit owner answers to that owner.

In the South, the condominium treats the door and most locks as common elements. A lock that comes off cleanly is still a lock the condominium did not approve while it was on.

The renter-friendly route is the owner’s written consent, then management’s, then the installation.

“A video doorbell is the easiest security upgrade”

On a private house, often true. In an apartment corridor it means fixing a device to a common-element door and recording neighbors in a shared space, and the North’s rules bar personal items on the outside of apartment doors.

At the 2024 rally one resident’s sign read that she should not have to rely on her neighbor’s doorbell camera, which shows both how common they already are and how much residents expect the building, not individual tenants, to provide.

Inside the unit, a camera facing the back of your own door gives you the record of who came in without touching the corridor.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $5,000 per door”

Cost guides from home-services sites and installers publish ranges like that, and they describe a reader, an electric strike, a controller and wiring on a commercial door. An approved deadbolt in an apartment door is a much smaller job, and a condominium-owned entrance is not a job a resident can buy at all.

Our own published Bronx prices, on this page, start at $1,500 to $2,500 for a single wired door with a reader or keypad and strike; service visits are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum.

For apartment work in the complex we quote item by item after management approves the model, so you do not pay for wiring a battery lock does not need.

“Building security is the police department’s job”

Parkchester has two: the NYPD’s 43rd Precinct, which is the city police for the area, and the complex’s own Parkchester Police, formerly the Department of Public Safety, whose officers are special patrolmen with limited peace-officer powers on the property, according to Wikipedia.

Neither maintains doors. Locks, closers and readers are the property owner’s job: the condominiums for their common elements, landlords for their buildings, owners for their units.

Comelit says each new entrance panel has a security button that calls the Parkchester Police concierge stations, and News 12 reported a public emergency call point near Virginia and McGraw avenues in 2025 (News 12). Those reach help faster; they do not secure a door.

“New entry systems fix packages going missing”

A better door helps only if it closes and latches every time. Residents told the Bronx Times in 2024 that they were documenting missing packages alongside faulty lobby door locks, which is the usual combination: the door, not the technology, is the gap.

The North’s rules require supplies and packages to be delivered through the building’s front entrance, so in the complex any change to delivery handling is the condominium’s to make.

Shops and private buildings around the complex can solve it themselves with a package room on a separate credential or lockers, as long as the street door is fixed first.

“An app-based intercom means you no longer need fobs”

Parkchester shows the opposite. The residents’ help site says the fob system was not changed by the intercom upgrade and is separate from it: the intercom lets you see and admit visitors, the fob is how residents get in.

App-based entry also depends on the resident’s details being on file. The intercom FAQ’s most common problems are an email address management does not have and a phone number that changed.

For shops and private buildings we usually recommend the same split: a credential residents or staff carry, plus a video call station for visitors.

Field Notes: The Approval Comes Before the Drill

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular apartment. In a condominium, the most expensive mistake is a correct installation on the wrong door. A lock can be fitted perfectly and still have to come out because nobody asked whether the cylinder was the owner’s to change, or because management never received a key.

So on Parkchester calls we reverse the usual order. The first visit, often only a phone call and two photos, is about paperwork: which position, which model, which office, who signs. We send the owner a one-page request to forward. Only when the answer comes back in writing do we order hardware and book the installation, and the key that has to be filed leaves with us in an envelope addressed to the Management Office.

It is slower by a week or two. It also means nobody gets a violation letter about something we installed.

Is it legal to put a camera inside our apartment that faces our own entrance door?
+

A camera inside your own unit, aimed at the inside of your entrance, does not touch the condominium’s corridor or door, so the condominium rules we have read do not address it. The people inside the apartment are a different matter: tell everyone who lives there, avoid bedrooms and bathrooms, and if you rent the unit out, do not leave a camera running in a tenant’s home. We set the camera to record the door, not the room.

What happens if the board or management says no to our lock request?
+

Then we do not install it, and you keep the written answer. Ask whether a different model or position would be acceptable; boards often refuse a specific product rather than the idea. If the request was about a disability, it can be resubmitted as a reasonable-accommodation request, which Parkchester’s published policy says will be answered in writing, with reasons if denied, within ten business days of complete paperwork.

Can a home-office door inside the apartment have its own code, separate from the entrance?
+

Yes. Interior doors are part of the unit, so a keypad lever or deadbolt on a home-office or file-room door is the owner’s choice. We fit models that always open from inside without a code or key, so nobody can be trapped, and give the office its own codes so a cleaner or relative can use the front door without opening your files.

We rent our unit out with an approved keypad deadbolt. Should we, as owners, be able to see when our tenant comes and goes?
+

No, and many lock apps let you limit it. An entry history is useful to the person who lives there and, briefly, at changeover, to confirm an old code is gone; it is not a tool for watching a tenant. We set the owner’s account to manage codes only, give the tenant the history for their own codes, and write down who can see what. If the condominium ever asks how the unit’s access is controlled, that written arrangement is the answer.

We rent from Parkchester Preservation Management. Can we change our lock or add one?
+

Ask your landlord first, in writing. Parkchester Preservation Management describes itself as the exclusive on-site rental agent for the sponsor-owned apartments, so it stands in the owner’s place for your unit and answers to the condominium for the door. We will install only what the landlord approves in writing.

Will Parkchester Police respond when the door system in our Metropolitan Avenue shop reports a break-in?
+

Do not plan on it. Parkchester Police are special patrolmen employed for the complex, with authority limited to their area and working hours, according to Wikipedia, and we know of no arrangement for them to receive alarms from shops. Send door alarms to your own monitoring company or to your phone, and call 911 for a crime in progress; the NYPD’s 43rd Precinct covers the area. Give your landlord and your monitoring company the same list of keyholders, so whoever is called can get in.

Do you serve the Stratton Park blocks west of White Plains Road and the streets toward Castle Hill?
+

Yes. Wikipedia describes Stratton Park, between White Plains Road and the Amtrak Northeast Corridor, as a part of Parkchester whose residents consider themselves Parkchester, and the shops of Starling Avenue and Castle Hill Avenue are a few blocks east. Those buildings are outside the complex, so their owners order work directly, at the same Bronx prices and from the same Parkchester line.
