The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need TV Installation

Hanging a television is a twenty-minute job on a modern wall, and almost no wall in Carroll Gardens is a modern wall. The neighborhood was built out mostly in the second half of the nineteenth century as rows of brownstone and brick houses, and the city later protected a core of it as the Carroll Gardens Historic District in 1973. What survives inside those houses is the problem and the charm for anyone holding a drill. Interior partitions are typically plaster on wood lath, sometimes close to an inch thick once three coats and a century of paint are counted. Walls shared with the next house are masonry, with plaster applied directly to the brick. Parlors have chimney breasts, many now purely decorative, with marble or slate mantels that owners would rather not scratch. Ceilings on the parlor floor are tall enough that a person standing at the mantel looks up at a mirror, not a screen. None of that stops a TV going on the wall. It decides which fixing, which add-on and which way the wires can travel.

The second thing that shapes the work is who owns the walls. Brick Underground's 2023 survey of the neighborhood's market described a housing stock made of single-family brownstones and brownstones divided into small condos and co-ops, and its examples show the range: an eight-unit co-op from 1910 on Luquer Street, three condos in a turn-of-the-century brownstone on President Street, an eight-unit 1920 limestone on Clinton Street, an eight-unit four-story building from 1900 on Court Street, and newer condos such as the six-story building on Huntington Street from 2000, a seventeen-unit building at 14 President Street from 2018 and thirteen units on Degraw Street from 2019. In a single-family house, the owner decides and the only question is the wall. In a three-condo conversion with no managing agent, the owner of each unit decides about their own rooms, but the condo documents may treat certain walls differently. In an eight-unit co-op, a board and an alteration policy usually sit between you and the drill. And in the many houses where an owner lives on one floor and rents out the others, the tenant upstairs and the landlord downstairs need to agree before anybody arrives.

The third thing is how people here watch television. In the brownstones we see, the parlor floor is often the formal room, with the fireplace as its centerpiece, and the TV ends up either over that mantel or banished to a back room or the garden floor. The garden floor, half a level below the sidewalk behind the front garden, tends to become the family room or a separate rental apartment, with lower ceilings, smaller front windows and a kitchen opening to the rear yard. Upper floors are bedrooms and studies. Each floor asks for a different screen height and a different cable plan, and in a house where the internet comes in at the cellar, the further the TV is from the cellar, the more planning the signal needs. FCC figures published for Carroll Gardens in October 2026 show Spectrum's cable network reaching 99.87 percent of locations and Verizon's fiber reaching 92.48 percent, so most homes can choose between the two. Fios was new here not so long ago: Patch reported in 2011 that Verizon was laying fiber in Carroll Gardens, Cobble Hill and Boerum Hill, and that apartment buildings would need landlord coordination. Either way, the provider brings the line to the house; getting the picture and the network to the screen on the third floor is where we come in.

The fourth thing is the street. Carroll Gardens is compact, roughly the blocks between Degraw Street and the Gowanus Expressway approach to the south, and between the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway trench along Hicks Street and the Gowanus Canal side, with Court Street and Smith Street as its two shopping streets. Parking near the house is never guaranteed, the stoops are steep, and a parlor floor is a full flight above the sidewalk. A 65-inch box carried up a brownstone stoop, through a vestibule with two sets of doors and around a newel post at the top is a different day's work from a lobby and an elevator. We ask about the stoop, the vestibule doors and the stair turns on the phone, and we suggest keeping the TV in its original box until we arrive, because a boxed screen can be stood on its edge safely and an unboxed one cannot.

Same-Day Repair in Carroll Gardens

Need Repair in Carroll Gardens? A Screen Sagging Off Old Plaster, a Mantel Mount That Was Never Right, or a Bracket the Last Owner Left Behind

The urgent TV calls from brownstone neighborhoods have a pattern, and Carroll Gardens fits it. The commonest is a bracket on a lath-and-plaster partition that was fixed with anchors meant for drywall. Plaster is hard on the face and softer behind it, and an anchor that only grips the plaster slowly crushes the material around it. It holds on the day, holds for a season, and then the screen starts to tilt forward at the top or drop on one side. Full-motion arms make it happen faster, because every time someone pulls the screen out to watch from the kitchen, the arm levers its weight on the top fixings. The second pattern is a TV over a mantel that went up on short plugs in the chimney breast's plaster without ever reaching the brick. On the Brownstoner thread about exactly this kind of wall, one owner wrote that short anchors in his shelves failed after a few weeks, while long shields set through the plaster into brick had held for about forty years. The difference is depth into sound masonry, not the size of the screw head.

The third pattern is inheritance. Carroll Gardens houses change hands, floors get rented to new tenants, and the old TV's bracket stays behind, often with a cord disappearing into a hole above it and coming out again by the baseboard. That cord inside the wall is the part we worry about most. Flexible power cords are not meant to be buried in a wall cavity, where they can be pinched by a fixing or overheat in a bundle, and the proper fix is a kit with its own rated in-wall cable and a recessed outlet behind the screen, or the wire back on the surface in a cover. If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back up. If two people can lift it down safely, do that, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. Re-hanging a screen on a properly fixed bracket is priced as a new mount on that wall, so on plaster it is $224.99 for a TV up to 55 inches, and on brick $244.99, with the take-down added if we are removing one first. If we find the trouble is in the wall itself, a crack running through the plaster or a loose brick in the chimney breast, we say so plainly so the owner can bring in a plasterer or a mason before anything goes back up.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Carroll Gardens' Architecture

Six kinds of building cover nearly every TV call in Carroll Gardens. They differ in three ways that matter far more than the brand of the TV: what the walls are made of, how far the screen has to be carried, and who besides you gets a say in what is drilled.

The single-family brownstone

Four or so floors for one household: a formal parlor with a mantel, a garden-floor family room, bedrooms above. Plaster on lath inside, brick party walls on both sides.

The house with a rental floor

An owner on one or two floors and a tenant on another, often the garden floor. Two households, one set of walls, and a landlord who may hear the drill.

The three-condo conversion

A turn-of-the-century brownstone split into a few condos, like the President Street example Brick Underground listed, usually with no managing agent and walls shared between owners.

The small pre-war co-op

Eight units in a 1900 to 1920 building on Luquer, Court or Clinton Street, with a board, house rules and plaster walls on brick or clay tile.

The limestone and the wider row house

Later houses and apartment buildings with limestone fronts, such as the 1920 eight-unit limestone on Clinton Street, where the walls are similar but the rooms are laid out differently.

The newer condo

Buildings from 2000 onward on Huntington, President and Degraw Streets, with drywall partitions, bigger windows and an association that wants to know what is opened.

The single-family brownstone: four floors, four different TV problems

A whole brownstone for one household is the most common job we see in neighborhoods like this one, and it is rarely one TV. The parlor gets the big screen, usually over the mantel or on the long party wall opposite the windows. The garden floor gets a family-room TV, often where the children or a guest watch. The main bedroom gets a smaller screen facing the bed, and the top-floor study sometimes gets a monitor-sized TV for news while working. Each floor has a different wall and a different height. The parlor, with ceilings that are often well over ten feet, can take a screen a little higher than a modern living room without feeling strange, as long as the center of the picture stays close to the eye line of someone seated, which the Data Wire Solutions guide for brownstones puts at about 42 to 44 inches from the floor in a living room. A bedroom screen watched from bed sits higher, around 48 to 54 inches to the center, with a slight downward tilt. The garden floor, with its lower ceiling, rarely has room to go high at all.

In a single-family house the walls belong to you, so the only questions are physical. The party walls are brick under the plaster, the interior partitions are framed and lathed, and the chimney breasts are masonry boxes containing flues. We price each by what it is: $45 for plaster on framing, $65 for brick, $130 more if the screen is above a fireplace. If the whole house is being done, we plan the cable routes floor by floor at the first visit, because in a four-story house the internet equipment is usually in one place, often the cellar or the garden floor, and every TV above it needs either a strong Wi-Fi signal or a network line. Thick brick and plaster are not kind to Wi-Fi, and a TV that streams perfectly on the garden floor can buffer two floors up. We tell you where we expect that to happen before you discover it during a match.

The house with a rental floor: agree the wall before the drill comes out

A great many Carroll Gardens houses have an owner on the upper floors and a tenant on the garden floor, or the other way round. For a TV job that means two households are involved even when only one is paying. If you rent, your landlord may live directly above or below the wall you want, and may decide whether a bracket goes up at all. Brick Underground's 2022 column on landlords and wall hanging found most leases do not ban hanging things outright, but it quoted a broker advising tenants to ask before installing anything that leaves more damage than a nail, and named TV brackets specifically. We suggest a short text to the owner before booking: which wall, what size of TV, how many holes and where the wires will go. Owners who are asked rarely refuse. Owners who first hear about it from the hammer drill sometimes do.

If you are the owner setting up the rental floor, there is a good argument for putting the TV bracket up yourself before the tenant moves in. One bracket in the right place, on a wall you chose, with the wires in a cover or a kit, is better than three tenants each drilling their own holes over the years. The garden floor is usually the rental, and its TV wall is often the party wall, which means brick under the plaster and a $65 line on our list. Its front windows are small and low behind the garden, its back opens to the yard, and its ceiling is lower than the parlor's, so a fixed or slightly tilting bracket at seated height usually works best. We can make the job out to the owner and leave the bracket paperwork with whoever lives there.

The three-condo conversion: three owners, shared walls, no managing agent

Brownstones split into two, three or four condos are common here, and Brick Underground's 2023 piece gave a President Street example of exactly that. The TV question in these buildings is usually not structural but practical: who has to agree? A condo owner generally controls the inside of their own unit, but the wall between two units, or the wall a unit shares with a common stair, may be treated differently in the condo's declaration and bylaws. We are installers, not lawyers, so we do not interpret those documents. What we can tell you is what the work involves, in plain terms you can send to the other owners: a bracket fixed into the brick or framing on your side of the wall, a pilot hole hidden behind it, and either a surface cord cover or an in-wall kit that opens two small rectangles in your side of the plaster or drywall and nothing else.

The other practical point is sound. A brownstone divided horizontally puts one owner's TV wall above or below another owner's bedroom, and in a vertical split the party wall between units is shared. Drilling is brief, a few minutes of hammer drill for a masonry bracket, but a soundbar or subwoofer on a hollow cabinet can pass bass into the floor below every evening for years. A soundbar mounted on the wall under the screen, $129 on our list, keeps more of the sound in the room than one sitting on a console or the floor, and we set bass and night modes during setup at the volume you actually use.

The small pre-war co-op: a board, house rules and walls that fool a scanner

The eight-unit co-ops in Carroll Gardens, in buildings from the first two decades of the twentieth century on streets like Luquer, Court and Clinton, have more in common with a small apartment house than with a brownstone. There is a board, there may be a managing agent, and there are usually house rules on contractor hours and insurance. Hanging a TV is minor work in most buildings of this kind, but opening a wall for an in-wall power kit can be treated as an alteration, and some boards draw that line firmly. We ask you to check before booking, and if the board wants a certificate of insurance, we send it to the managing agent before the visit.

The walls in these buildings vary more than in a brownstone. Some interior partitions are plaster on wood lath, some plaster on metal mesh, and some, in buildings of this age, are hollow clay tile or gypsum block under plaster. A scanner reads metal mesh as metal everywhere and reads tile as solid, and neither reading tells the truth. Hollow tile is the wall that catches out handymen: it looks solid, but its face is thin and a standard sleeve anchor can break through into the void. That is why we confirm with a single pilot hole behind the bracket plate, read the dust and the feel of the bit, and choose the anchor for what is really there. A tile or mesh wall is priced by the material behind the plaster, and we tell you the line before we continue.

The limestone and the wider row house: different fronts, similar insides

Not every house here is brownstone. Limestone fronts, like the eight-unit 1920 building on Clinton Street in Brick Underground's list, and wider brick row houses appear across the neighborhood, often a little later than the earliest brownstone rows. From the inside, a TV installer sees much the same thing: plaster partitions, masonry party walls, and in the larger buildings a mix of framed and masonry interior walls. What changes is the layout. Wider houses and small apartment buildings tend to have more doors and windows on the long walls, which leaves fewer clear stretches for a big screen, and the best TV wall is sometimes a short wall between two doorways, where a full-motion arm would block a door when pulled out. We plan the screen and bracket for the space you actually have, and if a fixed or tilting bracket on a shorter wall suits the room better than an arm on a long one, we say so.

The newer condo: drywall, glass and an association that reads the details

The buildings finished since 2000 at the neighborhood's edges, like the Huntington Street condo from 2000 and the President and Degraw Street buildings from 2018 and 2019 in Brick Underground's list, are built very differently from the houses around them. Interior partitions are usually drywall, and in buildings of this type they are often on light-gauge steel studs rather than wood, which matters because a lag bolt that bites into wood strips straight out of thin steel. We find out which you have with the same scan and pilot hole we use everywhere, and if the studs are steel we tell you what that changes and what it costs before the visit is confirmed.

Associations in these buildings tend to care about two things: whether a wall shared with a corridor or a neighbor is being opened, and whether the contractor is insured. An in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list, opens two small holes in the drywall between studs, one behind the TV and one low on the wall, and carries power in the kit's own rated cable to a recessed outlet; nothing is connected to the building's wiring. Most associations accept that inside a unit's own wall. If yours does not, a paintable cord cover at $59 needs only a few small screws, and it can come off cleanly if you sell.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Carroll Gardens Parlor Before Anything Touches the Plaster

These notes describe how our technicians approach a room in a house like the ones in Carroll Gardens. They are our working practice, not a story about any one address.

Find the party walls first. Standing in the parlor, the technician works out which long wall is shared with the next house and which is the stair or hall partition. The party wall is brick under the plaster and will hold a heavy screen well; the partition is lath on framing and needs the fixings to find the wood. The chimney breast, if there is one, sits on a party wall and is its own case.

Look at the mantel, then above it. A marble surround is noted and covered before anyone climbs a ladder. Above the shelf, we look for the faint outline of old patches, a sign that someone has drilled there before, and for hairline cracks that run across the breast, which tell us where the plaster has already let go of its base.

Scan, knock, and keep the test to one hole. On original plaster we do not want a row of exploratory holes. The scanner and a knock along the wall at screen height narrow it down; then a single small pilot hole goes exactly where the bracket plate will cover it. White powder followed by wood means lath on framing. White powder followed by red or orange grit means brick. A sudden drop into nothing means a void, and the fixing plan changes.

Tape it and sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen. You sit where you watch, on the sofa or in the bed, and we check the height, the glare from the front windows and whether a door or a radiator gets in the way. Moving tape costs nothing.

Drill once, clean as we go. The noisy part comes in one short burst. Dust is caught at the hole and vacuumed before the bracket goes up, because in a house this age the paint on the wall is old enough that nobody should be sweeping its dust around the room.

Mantels and Walls

Over the Mantel or on the Party Wall: What Holds a TV in a Carroll Gardens House and What Each Wall Costs

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs. In a Carroll Gardens house, drywall on wood studs is the exception, so the wall lines below are the part of the price to understand. Each exists because the wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time, and each is applied once per wall you choose.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). Thin wooden strips nailed across the studs, then two or three coats of plaster, then a century of paint. It is strong when pressed and weak when pulled. A screw into lath alone, or a plastic plug in plaster alone, can feel tight and still fail months later. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and lath into the framing behind it, through a pilot hole drilled slowly so the face does not crack outward. Brownstone framing was not laid out on modern spacing, so we find each member rather than measuring from a corner, and the bracket's slots let us catch two of them even when they are not where a modern stud would be.

Plaster on brick (the brick line, +$65). On party walls, the plaster is usually applied straight onto the brick. There are no studs to find, which the owners on the Brownstoner thread said in so many words. This is a very good wall for a TV, provided the anchor goes through the plaster and well into sound brick. The StreetEasy interview with a masonry specialist made the key point about older buildings: mortar is usually easier to drill than brick, but in an old wall it may be crumbling and too weak for something as heavy as a TV, in which case the fixing belongs in the brick itself, away from its edges. CE Pro put it more bluntly: "mortar is not glue for your TV." We use a hammer drill, masonry anchors sized for the bracket, and on softer old brick more fixings at a lower load each.

Above a fireplace (+$130, added to the wall line). A chimney breast is a box of masonry built around one or more flues, and it is the most popular TV spot in a Carroll Gardens parlor. The add-on covers protecting the mantel shelf and hearth, working from a ladder over a projecting mantel, drilling into a breast whose depth we cannot see, and setting a screen high enough to clear the shelf but not so high that it strains the neck. It is added to the wall material, so a breast faced in plaster over brick is $179.99 plus $65 plus $130, or $374.99, before any soundbar or wiring. More on the specific risks in the next section.

Drywall over old plaster. Renovated houses often have new drywall laid over or in place of original plaster. Behind it may be lath, brick or new framing, and in a gut renovation sometimes steel. The pilot hole tells us, and the wall is priced by what is behind the drywall, not by its surface.

Fireplaces: heat, height and the flue you cannot see

Three things decide whether a TV above a Carroll Gardens mantel is a good idea. The first is heat, and it depends on whether you still light the fire. Many parlor fireplaces here are decorative, sealed long ago, and give off no heat at all. Others are used a few evenings a winter. The Data Wire Solutions guide advises against mounting above a fireplace if the wall surface gets hotter than 100 degrees Fahrenheit during use, noting that most TV manuals list maximum operating temperatures around 104 degrees, and suggests checking the wall with an infrared thermometer after the fire has been burning for at least an hour. CE Pro adds that a wooden mantel shelf helps by deflecting rising heat, so the space above it is often cooler than people expect. If you burn wood, measure before you book, and if the wall runs hot, choose another wall.

The second is the flue. The masonry above the mantel is not solid all the way through; there is a smoke chamber and a flue behind it. CE Pro warns that anchors driven into the smoke chamber can open a path for smoke and carbon monoxide into the room when the fireplace is in use. That is why we keep anchors short of any cavity, drill only as deep as the fixing needs, and on a working fireplace place the bracket's fixings in the solid brick at the sides of the breast where possible rather than in its center. On a fireplace that has been sealed, the risk of smoke is gone, but the cavity is still there, and an anchor that breaks into it holds nothing.

The third is height. A parlor mantel shelf can sit at chest height, and a screen that clears it will be well above a seated viewer's eyes. One owner on the Brownstoner thread warned that a mount over a mantel is high and "invites a sore neck." A tilting bracket angles the picture down toward the sofa, which helps a little, and a pull-down mantel mount lets the screen come down to eye level for watching and go back up afterward. On a narrow parlor where the sofa sits only a few feet from the fireplace, the better answer is often the long party wall instead, and we show you both positions with tape before you decide.

Hiding the wires the way the electrical code allows

After "put it on the wall," the request we hear most is "make the wires disappear," and in an old house there are right and wrong ways. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out near the floor. A flexible cord inside a wall can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it bunches, and cannot be inspected, which is why the Data Wire Solutions guide, citing the National Electrical Code, recommends a recessed receptacle or a listed in-wall power kit instead. The right way, on a framed wall, is that kit: a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, the kit's own rated cable inside the wall, and a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit.

The kit needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits lath partitions and drywall but not a brick party wall, a chimney breast or a wall where fire-blocking or a horizontal brace sits between the two holes. For those, the choice is a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, which runs neatly down the wall and takes the wall color, or a TV placed directly above a console so the cables drop behind the furniture. In a restored parlor with a picture rail and a cornice, the cover can usually run tight to a door casing or in the corner where it disappears from the seating area. A brand-new hard-wired outlet behind the screen is different work altogether: in New York City, electrical work requires a license from the Department of Buildings, and we leave that to a licensed electrician.

Renters, Owners and Old Paint

Landlords Upstairs, Boards, Deposits and Lead Paint: Mounting a TV When the House Is Older Than the Rules

Because so many Carroll Gardens households rent a floor of a house or own a share of a small building, permission comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and what we have learned doing the work, so you can ask the right question before booking.

Start with the lease. In Brick Underground's November 2022 column on landlords and wall hanging, the attorney Steven Kirkpatrick said it is "not common for a landlord to ban you from hanging items," and that the first place to look is the lease's alteration provision. A broker in the same piece advised asking permission before installing anything that does more damage than a nail, naming TV brackets. If your lease restricts fixtures, ask in writing and keep the reply. A landlord who knows the installer is licensed and insured, and that no cord will be pushed through the wall, is usually easy to persuade.

Know what counts as damage. The same column quoted a property manager saying a hole larger than a nail counts as damage, and that a tenant who removes a mounted TV and leaves real damage must repair it or pay for it. A typical bracket leaves four to six holes, each wider than a nail, and an in-wall kit leaves two neat rectangles. All of it can be patched and painted. The column's advice was to patch before moving out, ask for an inspection and photograph the repair, and it noted that a landlord must give an itemized list of any deposit deductions and return the rest within two weeks.

Or do not drill. If the lease is strict or you expect to move soon, a TV on its own stand on a good console is a perfectly sound choice. On our list that is $119.99, including connecting the devices, and a soundbar placed in front of it is $79. You get the same tidy cables and setup, and nothing to patch.

Lead paint is part of the conversation in a house this old. The city's Health Department guidance for building owners says Local Law 1 covers rental buildings built before 1960, and in some cases up to 1978, and that owners must find out whether a child aged five or younger lives in, or routinely spends ten or more hours a week in, each apartment. Federal rules require professionals to follow lead-safe practices for work that disturbs lead paint. Drilling a handful of bracket holes disturbs only a few square inches of paint, but it still makes dust. We put down drop cloths, catch the dust at the hole, vacuum before the bracket goes up and leave the floor clean. If a young child lives in the apartment, tell us when you book, and tell your landlord, who has duties of their own under the law; if the paint on the TV wall is already peeling or the plaster is breaking up, that is a repair for the owner before any TV goes on it.

Rules that apply to Carroll Gardens business screens

  • Keep screens out of walking paths. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, wall-mounted objects with a leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may not stick out more than 4 inches into a circulation path. In a narrow restaurant aisle or a waiting room, that rules out a pulled-out full-motion arm at head height; we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. Hidden power behind a business screen runs through an in-wall kit with its own rated cable, or in a cord cover on the surface. A new circuit or hard-wired outlet is electrical work for a licensed electrician.
  • Exits and life safety stay clear. Screens do not go where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and cables stay off the floor of any route people use to leave.
  • Outside the building is a different matter. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook governs what is attached to the outside of buildings in the historic district. Interior screens do not need its approval, but anything on a facade, a storefront exterior or a visible roof is its business, and we do not put screens or dishes outside a district building without the owner confirming the approvals.
  • Insurance paperwork first. Commercial landlords and managed buildings often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner before work starts. We send it ahead of the visit.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the situations Carroll Gardens owners, tenants and shopkeepers describe when they call. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Parlors, mantels and original plaster

We restored our parlor's plaster ourselves and would hate to see a line of exploratory holes. How do you find something solid behind it without them?

By narrowing it down before drilling anything. On the Brownstoner thread about this exact wall, the owner noted that a stud finder works poorly on plaster, and that is our experience too, so the scanner is only the first clue. We knock along the wall at screen height, look at where the door casings and the baseboard joints fall, and work out from the room's layout whether the wall is a framed partition or a brick party wall. Then one small pilot hole goes exactly where the bracket plate will cover it. If that hole lands on framing or brick, the bracket goes up around it; if it lands in a void, the next hole is planned from what the first one told us and is also kept behind the plate. You should not see a single hole once the TV is up.

We still burn wood in the parlor a few evenings a winter. Could an anchor in the chimney breast let smoke into the room later?

It can if it goes into the wrong place, which is why we plan the fixings around it. Behind the face of a chimney breast is a smoke chamber and a flue, and CE Pro warns that anchors penetrating the smoke chamber can let smoke and carbon monoxide into the living space when the fire is lit. On a working fireplace we place the bracket's fixings in the solid brick toward the sides of the breast, drill only as deep as the anchor needs, and stop at once if the bit breaks into a cavity. Before booking, check the wall above the mantel with an infrared thermometer after an hour of burning; the Data Wire Solutions guide uses 100 degrees Fahrenheit as the limit. If it runs hotter, the party wall is the better place for the screen.

The sofa in our parlor can only sit about eight feet from the fireplace wall. What size of screen suits that distance?

Around 55 inches is a comfortable fit at eight feet. The rule of thumb CE Pro quotes from MantelMount is to divide the screen size by 0.55 to get a viewing distance, which puts a 55-inch TV at about 100 inches, roughly eight feet. A much larger screen at that distance, mounted above a mantel, means a lot of looking up. That matters for the price too: our $179.99 starting price covers TVs up to 55 inches, and for anything larger we confirm the total from the model number before you book.

Garden floors and upper floors

Our garden-floor front room sits half below the sidewalk, and daylight comes in high through the windows facing the front garden. Which wall works for the TV?

Usually a side wall, at a right angle to the windows, rather than the wall facing them or the wall they are set into. Light entering high from a front garden falls at a steep angle, and a screen on the wall opposite those windows catches it as glare for much of the day; a screen between the windows puts you looking into the light. On a garden floor the side walls are often the party walls, brick under plaster, which hold a TV very well and are the $65 line on our list. The ceilings are lower than the parlor's, so a fixed or slightly tilting bracket with the screen at seated height is usually right. We check the glare with tape on the wall at the time of day you watch most.

Verizon left our Fios equipment in the cellar, and the bedroom TV will be three floors up. Does that TV have to rely on Wi-Fi?

No, but it often ends up doing so, and in a brownstone that is a gamble. Brick party walls and thick plaster floors weaken Wi-Fi more than drywall does, so a smart TV three floors above the router can buffer on a busy evening. There are three ways around it. The provider can move or add equipment, which is their service, not ours. A mesh Wi-Fi system with a unit on the TV's floor often solves it without new wire. And a network cable can be run to the TV, through a closet, alongside a riser or in a cover. We look at the house, tell you which is realistic, and quote any cable run before booking.

There are three cable plates on our parlor walls from different decades, and we have no idea which one is live. Does the TV have to go near one of them?

Only if you watch a provider's box that needs that line. Many households here stream only, and then the TV needs nothing but power and Wi-Fi or a network line. If you do need the box, we find the live plate before choosing the TV wall, not after, using a tone probe on the lines at the plates and in the cellar. If the only live line is on the wrong wall, the box can sit near it and the picture can travel to the TV, and we show you that cost before you commit to either wall. If you only want the setup sorted out, our setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour.

Conversions, co-ops and rentals

Our eight-unit co-op treats opening any wall as an alteration. What can hide the TV's wires without going through the board's alteration process?

A paintable cord cover is the usual answer. It is fixed to the surface with a few small screws, carries the power and HDMI cables from the screen down to the outlet or console, takes the wall color, and comes off with only small holes to fill. On our list it is $59. Many boards treat that the same as hanging a picture, but we cannot promise yours does, so check the house rules or ask the managing agent first. The other route is to place the TV directly above a console, so the cables drop straight behind the furniture with nothing on the wall but the bracket. An in-wall kit, at $169, is the neatest of all, but it is the option that opens the wall.

We are one of three condo owners in a converted brownstone and want the TV on the wall we share with the next unit. What should we tell the other owners?

Tell them exactly what will happen on your side of the wall: a bracket fixed into the brick or framing, four to six holes hidden behind it, and either a cord cover or an in-wall kit with two small openings in your plaster only, nothing going through to their side. Mention that the drilling takes a few minutes, give the date and the two-hour arrival window, and say whether the soundbar will be wall-mounted, since that keeps more bass off the shared structure. Whether the condo documents give the other owners a formal say is a question for those documents and perhaps a lawyer, not for us, but in a three-unit building a clear note before the visit avoids most disagreements.

We rent the parlor floor, and our landlord on the top floor says yes to a TV but no to anything that shows after we leave. What does that rule out?

It rules out very little, provided you plan the move-out from the start. Bracket holes are hidden while the TV is up and can be filled and painted when you go, and a cord cover leaves only a few small screw holes. What tends to show afterward is an in-wall kit's two openings, which need a proper patch, and a mantel or marble surround damaged by careless work, which is why we protect it. If even filled holes are too much for your landlord, a TV on its stand at $119.99, with the soundbar placed for $79, leaves the walls untouched. Get your landlord's answer in writing either way.

Shops, restaurants and studios

Our Smith Street dining room has exposed brick that was sandblasted years ago, and the mortar between the bricks is soft. Can a screen still go on it?

Usually yes, as long as the fixings go into the bricks and not the joints. Our studio near the Smith and Ninth Streets station wants a screen facing the mats for instruction videos. What should we think about before choosing the wall?

Keep it off the wall opposite the windows and away from mirrors, which bounce glare into the class. And route the cables up and away rather than across the floor, where people step and stretch. If the instructor controls the screen from a phone or laptop, we set up the casting or wireless connection during the visit and test it with the device you actually use.
