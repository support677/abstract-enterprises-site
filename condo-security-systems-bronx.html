The Neighborhood

Why Bronx Condo Buildings Need Condo Security Systems Planned for Campuses, Garages and Rented Units

Ask most New Yorkers to picture a condominium and they imagine a glass tower with a doorman. The Bronx breaks that picture. Its single largest block of condo homes sits in brick buildings put up before the Second World War and converted from rentals decades later. Its Riverdale condos share streets and boards' associations with co-ops. Its east-side condos include garden-style complexes, townhouse rows and gated waterfront communities that look more like a suburb than the city. And in the South Bronx, where the cranes are busiest, most of what is going up is rental, so the condos that do exist there are small and scattered. Condo building security in the borough has to fit all of those shapes, and the system that suits one rarely suits the next.

What those buildings share is the legal frame. Under New York's Condominium Act, unless a building's declaration says otherwise, the common elements include "halls, corridors, lobbies, stairs, stairways, fire escapes, and entrances and exits of the building," along with "basements, cellars, yards, gardens, recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces" and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" (Real Property Law §339-e). Read that list with a security contractor's eye and it covers almost every place a condo association puts a reader, a lock, a panel or a lens: the front entrance, the cellar, the courtyard, the parking lot, the bike room. That is why, in most Bronx condominiums, the board of managers is the client for the building's security and the common charges pay for it.

The same statute leaves the details to each building. Section 339-v requires the bylaws to state how the board is elected, what it may decide on its own, whether it may hire a managing agent, and how owners adopt "decisions binding on all unit owners," and lets them set up reserves for repairs and replacements. In a 40-unit Riverdale condo, that usually means a five-person board, a managing agent who collects bids and a reserve line for the lobby. In a multi-building complex it can mean several layers: a board for each condominium section, a shared management company and sometimes an umbrella body that controls the grounds, the garages and the security staff. Before we scope anything large, we ask who in that chain signs, because a proposal addressed to the wrong body is a proposal that sits in a drawer.

Parkchester: the borough's largest condo community

No discussion of Bronx condo security gets far without Parkchester. Metropolitan Life built it as a planned community, showing a scale model at the 1939 World's Fair, with 51 groups of buildings planned for 12,000 families. Today the complex is made up of 171 brick buildings of eight or thirteen stories arranged around the two-acre Aileen B. Ryan Oval. In 1974 roughly a third of it was converted to condominiums, and the rest, now Parkchester South Condominium, converted in 1986 (Parkchester history). The complex also has its own special patrol officers, licensed by the NYPD, which shapes how security work there is organized: a building-level intercom or camera project sits inside a campus that already has staff walking it.

The residents' own reporting shows where the gaps are. In October 2024 the Bronx Times described residents rallying for the second time in a month for "more lighting, cameras and increased law enforcement engagement," after a break-in in which a resident found that her apartment door "is hollow and quite easy to break." One protester's sign read, "I should not have to rely on my neighbor's Ring Doorbell" (Bronx Times, Emily Swanson). A few months later the Parkchester Times ran a Parkchester South resident's appeal about package theft that urged neighbors to download "security footage before it auto-deletes" and to "only buzz in people you know or deliveries you are expecting" (Parkchester Times, June 18, 2025).

Those two articles read like a scope of work. Lighting is not our trade, but every camera we install depends on it, and a board that funds cameras before fixing dark paths gets grainy footage of nothing. Footage that auto-deletes before an owner can ask for it is a retention and request-process problem, not a camera problem. And "only buzz in people you know" is advice residents give each other when the entrance itself can't tell a courier from a stranger. Each of those is something a condominium association can fix with the right entry system, a sensible credential policy and a recorder whose settings someone has actually written down.

Riverdale: elevator condos in a co-op neighborhood

Riverdale's condominiums are mostly mid- and high-rise elevator buildings on the slopes above the Hudson, mixed in among a larger stock of co-ops. Owners there tend to organize. The Riverdale Press reported in July 2024 that the local association of cooperatives counts "more than 100 co-operatives and condominiums" among its members and runs workshops on "code compliance, legal parameters, green energy and local law 11" (The Riverdale Press). For a security contractor that means a Riverdale condo board usually arrives informed: it has compared notes with neighboring buildings, it has a managing agent with a vendor list, and it wants a proposal that lines up against two others.

Riverdale condo projects also share a calendar with heavy building work. Facade programs, roof replacements and elevator modernizations are common capital items in these buildings, and each one touches security equipment: a sidewalk shed blocks the camera that covered the entrance, an elevator overhaul is the moment to add a cab reader, a lobby renovation is the moment to change the panel. We ask boards for their capital calendar up front so the security work lands next to the project that disturbs it, not a year before.

The east Bronx: garden complexes, townhouse rows and gated waterfront communities

East of the Bronx River the condo stock spreads out. Marketproof's neighborhood guide notes that in Throgs Neck "condo developments have arrived on Schurz Avenue, as well as gated co-op communities with single family homes" (Marketproof, Throggs Neck), and describes Pelham Bay as a mix where "tree-lined homes" sit beside "newer apartments," with the 6 train "spurring on apartment building development" next to Pelham Bay Park (Marketproof, Pelham Bay). Condo associations out here often manage something closer to a campus than a building: several low-rise buildings, a shared parking lot, a gate, a mail area and a perimeter fence.

That changes the security design. A garden-style condo complex can have a dozen entrances and no lobby at all. The parking lot matters as much as the front door. A vehicle gate needs a reader or remote receiver, a call box for guests and a camera that can see a plate. Near the water, outdoor equipment takes salt air and wind that a Grand Concourse lobby never sees. A system planned for a single high-rise does a poor job here; what works is a single credential list that spans every door and gate, with readers rated for outdoor mounting and cameras placed for the lot as well as the buildings.

The South Bronx: plenty of construction, few condos

The south end of the borough is where new housing is rising fastest, and it is easy to assume new condos come with it. The record says otherwise. NYCEDC describes how, "in 2009, this 30-block stretch along the Harlem River was rezoned to allow for residential and commercial development," and the first phase of the waterfront project that followed calls for "approximately 540 units of 100% permanently affordable housing" (NYCEDC, Lower Concourse). 6sqft's roundup of notable new Bronx buildings lists Mott Haven projects such as a seven-tower waterfront complex and a 12-story building on Bruckner Boulevard, all of them rentals (6sqft).

So the condominiums we meet in Mott Haven, Port Morris and around the lower Concourse tend to be small: a converted loft, a handful of new units over a storefront, a boutique building whose owners form the whole board. Their security needs are compact but sharp. The entrance usually faces a busy commercial street, the ground floor is often a business, and the association has no staff at all. A video panel that rings phones, a reader on the cellar or bike room and two or three well-placed cameras do most of the work, and the board needs one clear price rather than a menu.

Rented units change the math

Bronx condo boards deal with a lot of residents who are not owners. Citywide, the NYC Comptroller counts "318,000" occupied condominium apartments and reports that "18% of condo units citywide are owned by LLC's," noting that some owners "subsequently vacated and are renting out their units" (NYC Comptroller, March 2024). The Comptroller's report gives no Bronx-only figure, and we don't invent one, but in converted complexes where investors bought units at conversion, renters are a familiar part of the resident mix. Every one of those households needs entry, a directory listing and a fob, and every lease that ends is a credential somebody has to switch off. A condo security system that can't keep up with turnover leaks access month by month.

Bronx condo facts that shape the work

  • 171 buildings, 8 or 13 stories: the Parkchester complex, originally planned for 12,000 families (Wikipedia, Parkchester).
  • 1974, then 1986: Parkchester went condo in two stages, about a third of the complex first and the section now called Parkchester South twelve years later (Wikipedia, Parkchester).
  • 100+ co-operatives and condominiums: the membership of Riverdale's building owners' association in 2024 (The Riverdale Press).
  • 30 blocks, 2009: the Lower Concourse rezoning along the Harlem River (NYCEDC); the new buildings that followed are largely rentals (6sqft).
  • 18%: the share of New York City condo units owned by LLCs, a rough signal of how many units are rented rather than owner-occupied (NYC Comptroller).
  • Eight apartments, built or converted after 1968: the point at which state law expects a self-locking entrance with a working intercom (MDL §50-a).

Associations that start with the intercom almost always end up adding the parking lot gate or the package room to the same proposal, because once residents see one entrance working properly, the next weak point becomes obvious.

Condo intercom replacement, upgrades and repair

The entry intercom is the system every resident touches and the one most Bronx condo boards call about first. In prewar converted buildings it is often an audio system with a speaker panel, a riser running up the stair hall and a handset in each apartment, patched over the years with parts from three different makers. In Riverdale elevator condos it may be a newer video panel with a concierge connection. In small South Bronx buildings it is frequently an app-based panel the developer chose. We repair all of them, and when repair stops making sense we carry out a full condominium intercom replacement: audio to video, wired handsets to monitors or phone answering, a four-button plate to a scrolling directory, or a single entrance to several entrances on one system.

The money question comes before the hardware question. Is this a repair the agent can approve from the operating budget, or a capital replacement that belongs in the reserve plan and possibly in front of the owners? We write condo intercom proposals so a board can tell. The failing component gets its own repair line. The replacement scope lists every entrance and every panel, says what happens in each apartment (new monitor, existing handset kept, phone or app answering only), shows the riser or network path, names the door release hardware and puts any maker's subscription on a separate line with its yearly cost. That layout drops straight into an RFP and lets a board compare three bids without translating them. Installation detail lives on our Bronx intercom installation page, and faults in an existing system are covered on intercom repair in the Bronx. Boards planning a full replacement can also read our condo intercom replacement guide on budgeting, bid comparison and owner notices.

Buzzer systems in walk-up and garden condos

Low-rise condos across the east Bronx often still run on a plain buzzer: a button plate at each entrance, a talk unit in each apartment and a strike on the door. In a garden complex with eight or ten entrances, a single dead buzzer usually affects one line of apartments, and the fault can sit in the station, the cable, the transformer in the cellar or the strike. We trace it on the service call, replace what failed and test the release from every apartment on that line. Prices by component are on our door buzzer repair in the Bronx page; boards that want to start fresh on a small building can look at door buzzer installation in the Bronx.

Condo access control: fobs, cards and phone credentials

A Bronx condo's controlled openings add up fast. Beyond the lobby there is the cellar, the laundry room, the bike or storage room, the trash room, the roof, the community room, a garage door or two and, in campus-style complexes, every building entrance on the grounds. We install readers, electric strikes, magnetic locks, door contacts and request-to-exit devices, then the controller and software that hold the credential list and the entry log. The main benefit for an association is control at turnover: when an owner sells or a renter's lease ends, their fob stops working that day and nobody re-keys a cylinder. Hardware and pricing are covered on our page for access control installation in the Bronx, and condo owners around the Oval can see local context on access control in Parkchester.

One credential list across many buildings

The Bronx's large condo complexes raise a problem a single tower never does: a resident of one building should usually open their own entrance, the shared laundry and the parking lot, but not the lobby of the building across the courtyard. Staff, patrol officers and contractors need wider access on set schedules. We set this up as credential groups on one networked system rather than a separate box in every building, so the management office edits one list and the entry log shows every door on the campus in one place. Where buildings are too far apart for cable, the controllers talk over the network, and we plan that path during the walk-through.

Package rooms and delivery access

Package theft is the complaint we hear most often from Bronx condo residents, and the fix is almost always about the entrance rather than the camera. A carrier who can't get in leaves the box at the door; a carrier buzzed in by a random resident leaves it in an open lobby. A package room with its own reader and a camera on its door, or a delivery credential that opens only the vestibule during set hours, closes both gaps. We install the reader, the lock and the camera and set the delivery schedule if the platform supports one. Parcel lockers come from locker companies; where a board has one or wants one, we wire and integrate the doors and intercom around it rather than selling the locker itself.

Community rooms, laundry rooms and other shared spaces

Bronx condos have fewer rooftop pools and more laundry rooms, community rooms, playgrounds and storage areas. Each shared space raises the same board questions: who may use it, when, and with whom. A reader on a schedule answers the first two; a camera on the door settles the arguments about the third. Community rooms rented out for parties get a temporary credential for the host that expires at the end of the booking, and laundry rooms can close overnight without anyone holding a key.

Cameras: covered briefly here

Video for a Bronx condo usually starts at the doors people actually use: main and side entrances, mail areas, the lot and its gate, garage doors, then laundry, storage and trash rooms, and the elevators only if the board votes for it. We record to equipment locked inside the building, keep footage for exactly the number of days the board writes into its policy, and issue separate logins to each person the board approves. A dedicated page on condo security camera installation in the Bronx is coming to this series; for now, recorder sizing and camera placement in multifamily buildings are explained on our page about apartment building security camera installation in the Bronx.

Elevator access control

Elevator condos in Riverdale and the taller converted buildings sometimes want the cars to stop residents from riding to floors they don't live on, or to keep the roof and the garage level for credentialed users only. We mount the reader in the cab and install the relay interface that tells the controller which buttons a credential may light; the elevator maintenance company stays responsible for the controller itself, and we schedule our visit alongside theirs. Details are on elevator access control installation in the Bronx.

Parking lots, garages and vehicle gates

Parking is a bigger security question in the Bronx than in most of the city, because so many condo complexes have surface lots, garages or both. A vehicle gate takes a reader or a long-range tag receiver, a call box so guests can reach a resident, and a camera placed to read plates at the speed cars actually move. Garage pedestrian doors and the doors from the garage into each building go on the same credential list as the front entrances, so a lost fob is switched off everywhere at once. The gate operator and its motor belong to the gate company; we connect our readers and cameras to it and coordinate visits when both trades are needed.

Alarms for owners and ground-floor businesses

Intrusion alarms are mostly a unit-level purchase in Bronx condos. The association protects its common doors with access control and video, while an owner of a ground-floor home, a commercial condo or a storage unit may want sensors, a keypad and a monitored panel of their own. We install those for owners and connect them to central station monitoring; setup is described on alarm installation in the Bronx.

Fire alarm work, only within our scope

Fire alarms come up on nearly every condo access job, because magnetic locks on exit paths generally have to release when the building alarm activates. Fire alarm systems themselves are a separate, engineered trade in New York City that starts with sealed drawings and an FDNY filing and is priced building by building. We take on fire alarm work only within our licensing scope; fire alarm installation in the Bronx walks through the approval path.

Cable, network and power behind it all

Video panels, readers and cameras ride on Cat6, PoE switches and a riser path, and in prewar Bronx buildings that path is the hard part: thick masonry, plaster stair halls, and cellars where three generations of wiring share the same ceiling. We run, label and document the security side so the next technician can follow it, and we reuse existing conduits and sleeves wherever they are sound. Bigger cabling scopes sit on our structured cabling installation in the Bronx page. We don't do electrical work: if a controller, panel or lock power supply needs a new 120-volt outlet or circuit, the association's electrician supplies it.

Taking over a system nobody can log into

Converted complexes and newer boutique condos share one problem: the original installer is gone and nobody on the current board has the administrator password. In a converted building the system may date from a management company that no longer runs the property; in a new building, the developer's integrator may have opened the cloud account in an employee's name. We start with a service call that inventories what is installed at each door and in each closet, identifies which parts are cloud-managed and who holds those accounts, resets local equipment where the maker allows it, and gives the board a written list of what to keep, what to replace and what is under subscription. Where a platform requires the account holder's cooperation to transfer ownership, we tell the board exactly whom to ask.

Guests, caregivers, contractors and short stays

Visitors are a constant in any Bronx condominium: relatives staying for a week, a tenant on a two-year lease, a cleaner on Thursdays, a contractor gutting a kitchen. A board that can't see those credentials can't switch them off. We configure access so the office can hand out a fob or phone pass that opens chosen doors until a chosen date, and so owners can send a single-use code from the intercom app where the platform offers it. The house rules, and city law, decide whether short-term stays are allowed in a given building; that call belongs to the board and its counsel, and our settings simply carry out whatever they decide.

A one-hour self-check for Bronx condo boards

A board officer or superintendent can learn a lot in an hour on foot before calling anyone. Our free walk-throughs follow the same route:

  • Walk the perimeter: every entrance, gate, garage door, cellar door and fire exit. Which close and latch on their own, and which are propped, taped or broken?
  • Count the credentials: keys, fobs, gate remotes and app users, and who holds them (owners, renters, staff, patrol, contractors, the commercial tenant). When was the list last checked against the owner roll?
  • Test the front door like a stranger: call three apartments from the panel, watch a delivery arrive, and see what happens when nobody answers.
  • Pull a clip: ask whoever has the recorder login to find yesterday's footage of the main entrance. How long did it take, and how many days back does it go?
  • Open the equipment room: note where the controllers, network switches, recorder and door power supplies sit, whether anyone could walk in, whether it overheats in August, and whether a battery keeps it running in a blackout.
  • Look at the lighting: walk the lot and the paths after dark. A camera can only record what the lights show it.

Boards that run this list usually find their real exposure is narrow: one gate that never closes, one recorder that stopped writing months ago, one door closer that gave out in the winter. Those fixes cost far less than a full replacement and belong at the top of any plan.

Repairs, maintenance visits and support

Fixing a Bronx condo security fault begins with our $250 service call. Boards that prefer a planned check, say each spring or before the board turns over at the annual meeting, can schedule a maintenance visit that goes through panels, readers, gates and the recorder in one trip. Questions by phone about a system we put in cost nothing. What we do not offer is a managed security plan, a video watch service or a virtual doorman: our role is the equipment that staff or an outside provider relies on, and we will tie into a provider the board already pays.

Condominium Building Security for the Bronx's Condo Building Types

The same fob reader costs a different amount, takes a different schedule and needs different paperwork depending on whether it goes on a 1940s brick entrance in a converted complex, a Riverdale elevator lobby or a gate at the edge of a waterfront parking lot. These are the condo building types we plan around in the borough.

A

Converted prewar complexes

Brick buildings of eight to thirteen stories on shared grounds, converted from rentals to condominiums. Many entrances, a management office, campus staff and residents who have lived there for decades.

B

Riverdale elevator condos

Mid- and high-rise buildings on the hills above the Hudson. Staffed or part-staffed lobbies, garages, informed boards and managing agents who bid every job.

C

Garden-style condo complexes

Two- and three-story buildings around courtyards in the east Bronx. Separate entrances for each building, a shared lot and no lobby.

D

Townhouse and gated communities

Rows of attached homes with private front doors and a shared gate, common grounds and parking near the Throgs Neck and Pelham Bay waterfront.

E

Small new condo buildings

Five- to thirty-unit buildings on side streets in Pelham Bay, Throgs Neck and Morris Park. App intercoms, keyless locks and modest reserves.

F

Mixed-use and loft condos

Converted industrial buildings and new units over storefronts in Mott Haven and Port Morris, with a business on the ground floor and no staff.

Converted prewar complexes: many doors, long-time residents

A converted complex is a security job measured in buildings rather than doors. Each building has a front entrance, often a rear or courtyard entrance, a cellar door and a laundry or storage room, and the campus adds parking, a management office and paths between the buildings. The entry systems were usually specified when the property was still a rental, and many were replaced piecemeal afterward, so the same complex can hold four generations of intercom side by side. We start these jobs with a survey of every building's panel, riser and door hardware, and we price a standard that each building moves to as its old system fails or as the reserve allows. Residents in these complexes have often lived there for decades and include many older owners, so any new system needs an answering option that works without a smartphone and a directory large enough to read.

Riverdale elevator condos: staff, garages and bid packages

A Riverdale condo tower behaves like a small campus stacked vertically. A doorman or concierge may cover the lobby for part of the day, residents answer the panel on monitors or phones after hours, and the garage has its own vehicle door, pedestrian door and elevator landing. Work there is about sequencing: one entrance changed while the other stays live, elevator time booked through the agent, programming done when the desk is quiet. Boards in these buildings expect a scope that matches their RFP line for line and a single contact for scheduling. For the neighborhood itself, see intercom installation in Riverdale.

Garden-style complexes: a dozen entrances and no lobby

Garden condos spread their residents across low buildings, each with its own door to the street or courtyard. There is no lobby to staff and no single place for packages to land. The work is mostly repetition done carefully: a matching panel or buzzer plate at each entrance, strikes sized to doors that see heavy weather, and one controller that can see every reader. Underground cable between buildings is often the most expensive line item, so we look first for existing conduit and, where there is none, for a network path that avoids trenching.

Townhouse rows and gated communities near the water

In a townhouse or gated condo community, each owner's front door is usually part of their unit, while the gate, the shared drive, the parking and the grounds are common. That split decides who pays for what. The association's system is typically the gate reader, a call box that rings each home, cameras at the entrance and on the shared parking, and perhaps readers on a clubhouse or pool. Owners who want a video doorbell or a smart lock on their own door can hire us directly for that, as long as the community's rules allow it. Near the Throgs Neck and Pelham Bay shoreline we specify outdoor-rated, corrosion-resistant hardware for the gate and any exposed camera.

Small new condo buildings: watch the running costs

The newest Bronx condos tend to be compact buildings with an app-based intercom, keyless locks on the entrances and sometimes a subscription charge per door or per apartment. Their boards ask two questions: what will this cost every year, and what happens if the platform raises its price or drops support? We answer with a several-year cost for the system they have and for one alternative without a recurring fee where one exists, so the owners can decide with numbers rather than sales copy.

Mixed-use and loft condos in the South Bronx

Condos in Mott Haven and Port Morris often sit in or beside former industrial buildings, with heavy masonry, freight-size doors and a business on the ground floor. Readers and strikes have to suit oversized steel doors, cable paths have to work around brick that won't take a quick fish, and the residential credential list must stay separate from the commercial tenant's so a store employee can't wander upstairs.

Materials and methods by building type

  • Prewar brick and stone entrances (converted complexes, Riverdale): surface-mount panels or panels set into existing openings, and steel arms that hold cameras off carved stone instead of drilling it.
  • Plaster stair halls (prewar buildings): we meter the old intercom conductors floor by floor and pull fresh cable only on the runs that fail, which keeps work inside apartments to a minimum.
  • Aluminum and glass doors (newer lobbies): an electromagnetic lock or a strike hidden in the frame, with an exit sensor and, where a building alarm exists, a release tied to it.
  • Exterior gates and fences (garden and gated communities): outdoor-rated readers on posts, gooseneck stands for drivers and sealed junction boxes.
  • Steel service and garage doors (every type): heavy strikes or mag locks rated for the door weight, and a working closer confirmed first, since no reader can secure a door that hangs open.
  • Between buildings (campuses): existing conduit reused where sound, otherwise network links between controllers rather than new trenches.

Boards replacing an intercom across several buildings often add readers to the laundry and storage rooms in the same project, because the controller, cable and programming visit can be shared.

Keeping the business and the residents apart

A typical request from a Bronx mixed-use condo: the pharmacy or deli downstairs needs its staff in the cellar and at the trash area, and the residents want those same employees kept out of the upstairs halls. One controller handles both with two separate groups. Fobs issued to the business open only the doors its deed or a signed agreement covers, only during hours the board approves, and the log shows which group used which door, which ends most arguments about who left the cellar unlocked.

The commercial owner's own system

Shops and offices on the ground floor usually buy their own video and alarm. We are glad to do that work for the business owner too, but we keep it apart from the association: its own recorder, its own passwords, its own invoice. Feeding a store's cameras into the building recorder raises awkward questions about who gets to watch whom, and keeping the two systems fully separate avoids them.

Rules that apply

The state intercom requirement. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies to class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments. It calls for a self-closing, self-locking entrance door and an intercom allowing "voice communication between the occupant of each apartment and a person outside," with the door released "from the apartment." Older buildings fall under it when a majority of the residents ask in writing, and the section makes it a misdemeanor to "wilfully destroy, damage, or jam" that equipment. Converted Bronx complexes and most Riverdale condos have far more than eight homes, but how the law reads for a particular condominium is a question for the association's attorney; our job is to keep the equipment that satisfies it working.

Privacy rules for digital entry. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021 (codified as chapter 30 of the NYC Administrative Code), covers "smart access systems" in class A multiple dwellings, which include fob and card readers, entry apps and biometric devices. HPD explains that owners using them owe residents a written statement of what data is gathered and how long it is retained. Whether that duty reaches a condominium association with renters in the building is for its lawyer to say. What we do is leave off any data capture the board's policy doesn't require and point to the exact device or cloud account where entry records live.

Permits, power and paperwork. Low-voltage security wiring generally doesn't need an electrical permit in the city, apart from life-safety work, and any 120-volt circuit is the association's electrician's job, since our license is limited to low voltage. Our general liability certificate, naming the condominium and its managing agent as the paperwork requires, is issued once the job is awarded rather than sent with a bid.

Your Questions Answered

Condo Security Systems in the Bronx: Questions Answered

We grouped what Bronx boards, agents, supers and unit owners most often raise during site visits and calls under six headings. Each answer is the one we would give standing in your lobby.

Boards, budgets and bids

Our condo is one section of a big multi-building complex. Does our board decide on security, or someone above it?

That depends on how the complex was set up, and the governing documents answer it, not us. In some Bronx complexes each condominium section controls its own buildings while a shared management company or an umbrella body runs the grounds, garages and patrol. In others a single board covers everything. Practically, we ask for a one-page chart of who owns which doors before scoping: building entrances, courtyard gates, lots, laundries. A proposal then goes to the body that controls those openings, and anything shared between sections is shown on its own line so each board can see its share.

How should a Bronx condo board compare three security bids that don't match line for line?

Make the bids match before comparing prices. Write one list of every door, gate and camera position and ask each contractor to price against it. Then check five things side by side: what each apartment gets (monitor, existing handset or phone answering), whether the old cable is reused or replaced, what door hardware is included, which recurring platform fees exist and for how long, and what the association must provide, such as electrical outlets or escorts. A cheap bid that leaves out the door hardware or assumes every riser is good often ends up the most expensive one. Our own proposals follow that layout so the comparison is easy.

Can a large condo add cameras one building at a time without buying a new recorder for each?

Yes, if the first phase is designed for it. On campus-style properties we size the first recorder, or a small server, with room for the later buildings, and we connect each building back to it over the network. Each phase then adds cameras and a switch rather than a whole system. The alternative, a separate recorder in every building's cellar, multiplies the logins, the hard drives that can fail and the places someone has to go to pull a clip. Phasing that way also lets the board spread the spending across several budget years without ending up with mismatched systems.

Entrances and doors

Half of our entrance doors don't close and latch on their own. Is that an intercom problem or a door problem?

Usually a door problem that looks like an intercom problem. If a closer is worn, a hinge sags or the strike sits out of line, the door drifts open, the release seems unreliable and residents stop trusting the panel. We check every door's closing and latching before touching electronics, adjust or replace strikes as part of the job, and tell the board when a closer, a hinge or the door itself needs a door contractor or locksmith. A reader on a door that never shuts is money wasted, so this is often the cheapest first step in a Bronx condo security plan.

Our residents say their apartment doors feel flimsy. What can a security contractor actually do about that?

Be honest about the line between trades. Replacing or reinforcing apartment doors and frames belongs to a door contractor or locksmith, and who pays for a unit door depends on whether the declaration treats it as part of the unit. What we add is detection and evidence around those doors: a camera covering the corridor, a door contact or alarm on a unit for an owner who wants one, and a building entrance that keeps strangers out of the halls in the first place. Most corridor break-ins start with someone getting through the front door, so the entrance is where an association gets the most for its money.

How many entrances can one condo intercom system run in a garden-style complex?

Far more than most boards expect. A modern networked entry system treats every entrance panel as one more device on the same directory, so a complex with twelve building doors and a vehicle gate can share one resident list, one management login and one call log. The practical limits are cable or network reach between buildings and the cost of a panel at each door, not the software. On the walk-through we map how each entrance would connect back and price any door that needs a long run separately, so the board can see which entrances are cheap to add and which aren't.

Can the lobby panel ring residents who only have a landline?

Often, yes. Many entry systems can dial a regular phone number for each apartment as well as, or instead of, an app or an in-unit monitor, and residents press a key to open the door. That option matters in Bronx buildings where many owners are older or simply don't want a smartphone app. It may require a phone or cellular line at the panel, and some platforms charge for it, so our proposal states the answering methods each apartment will have and any recurring cost that comes with them.

Credentials, staff and deliveries

Can our private patrol officers and porters have their own credentials and a record of where they've been?

Yes. Staff and patrol credentials go in their own groups, each with the doors and hours that role needs, and every swipe lands in the entry log with a name and time. Some boards use that log as a simple tour record, checking that the back doors and cellars were actually visited overnight. A lost staff fob is switched off from the office at once, and a new one is issued without touching the residents' credentials. If the patrol uses its own radio or guard-tour system, the access log works alongside it rather than replacing it.

Where can a package room go in a walk-up condo with no spare room?

Usually in space the building already has: part of a cellar near the entrance, a disused storage closet, a vestibule that can take a second inner door, or a corner of a laundry room partitioned off. What matters is that the room is close to the entrance couriers use, can take a reader and a lock, and can be seen by a camera. In buildings with no space at all, a delivery credential that opens only the vestibule, combined with a camera there, still beats boxes left on the stoop. We look at the options on the walk-through and price the ones that fit.

Can the parking lot gate and the building doors work from the same fob?

In most cases, yes. The gate gets a reader on a post at driver height, or a long-range reader that sees a tag on the windshield, wired to the same controller as the building doors. Owners then carry one credential, and the office switches off a sold unit's access to the lot and the buildings in one step. Where the gate still runs on old remotes, we can usually add a reader alongside them and let the board phase the remotes out as residents switch over.

Video and lighting

An owner's package was taken from the lobby. How quickly do they need to ask for the video?

As soon as possible, and the board should publish the deadline. Recorders overwrite the oldest footage automatically when the drive fills, so a clip that exists today may be gone in a week or two, depending on the camera count, the resolution and the drive size. We set retention to the number of days the board's written policy names and tell the board what that number actually is. The policy should also say who an owner contacts, how quickly that person saves the clip and how it is shared, so requests don't wait for the next board meeting.

Should a condo fix its lighting before adding more cameras?

Usually, yes. Infrared cameras see in the dark at short range, but identifying a face or reading a plate across a parking lot or a path needs light. If the paths, lots and entrances are poorly lit, more cameras mostly produce more dark footage. We walk the property after dark when we can, mark the spots where lighting limits what a camera will capture, and pass that list to the board for its electrician or lighting contractor. Lighting and cameras planned together give a far better result than either alone.

Weather and the waterfront

Does salt air near the Throgs Neck shore shorten the life of outdoor readers and cameras?

It can. Shoreline air works on painted mounts, exposed screws, connectors and the hinges and strikes of gates. For condo communities near the water we specify outdoor-rated equipment with sealed housings, stainless or coated hardware where it is available, drip loops and sealed junction boxes, and we mount panels where an overhang protects them. A maintenance visit once a year to clean and check exposed equipment is cheap insurance against corrosion that otherwise shows up as an intermittent fault in the middle of winter.

Access and scheduling

A repair needs every apartment on the line, and several are rented out. How do we get in?

Through the owners, with enough notice. The association tells each owner the date, the two-hour arrival window and why their unit is needed, and the owner arranges access with their tenant or leaves a key with the management office. We ask for the list of affected units when the job is booked, because a wired intercom repair can't be closed until every apartment on the line has been tested. One or two units that can't be reached usually means a second visit, so the extra notice pays for itself.

Is there an added charge for condos at the far edges of the borough, like Riverdale or City Island?

No. The $250 service call is the same at any Bronx address, and building-wide proposals use the same pricing whether the building is in Riverdale, Mott Haven or out past Pelham Bay. Our office on East Fordham Road sits near the middle of the borough, which keeps travel reasonable in every direction.

Strikes, magnetic locks and gate relays

Doors decide the release hardware. A strike swaps out the keeper in the frame and lets the existing latch go, which fits the wood and steel entrance doors on most older Bronx buildings. An electromagnetic lock clamps a plate on the door and lets go when it loses power, the usual choice for glass storefront-style lobbies, and it needs an exit sensor plus a release tied to any fire alarm. A gate relay just signals the gate operator. We decide opening by opening after checking the frame and watching how people use the door.

Fobs, cards, phones and faces

Most Bronx associations stay with fobs: they cost little, survive years on a keyring and can be killed from the office in seconds. Older proximity fobs can be duplicated, so we recommend the encrypted kind for new systems. Phone passes are handy for owners and renters who live on their phones and can be sent before a move-in. Face terminals are available and appear in a few newer buildings, but because they store biometric data they bring the city privacy rules above into play. Whatever mix the board picks, we document what each credential type keeps on file and where.

When the power goes out

Think about the blackout before it happens. A mag lock with no backup falls open when the power drops, a strike-held door stays shut from the street side, and a dead intercom calls nobody. Batteries on the lock supplies and the entry panel buy time on the main entrances, and the board chooses ahead of time which doors should fail open and which should stay locked. On campuses we put that backup on the entrances the most residents use.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems in the Bronx

Ask an AI assistant or a national cost site like Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr about condo security and you get averages built on suburban houses and glass towers. Bronx condominiums rarely fit either picture. These are the claims we correct most often when we walk a property.

1. "A complex with its own security staff doesn't need building systems"

Summaries sometimes suggest that a large community with patrol officers or guards is covered. Staff and equipment do different jobs.

A patrol can't be at a dozen entrances at once, and it can't tell who opened a cellar door at 3 a.m. last Tuesday. Parkchester has its own licensed special patrol, and residents there still rallied for more cameras and lighting in 2024, according to the Bronx Times. Entrances that lock, credentials that can be switched off and recordings that last long enough to be useful are what give staff something to work with.

The best results we see come when a board plans the two together: patrol routes that check the doors the access log flags, and cameras placed where staff can't stand all night.

2. "Neighbors' doorbell cameras can cover the building"

Consumer doorbells are often presented as a cheap stand-in for building cameras. In a condo, that leaves the common areas covered by whichever owner bought a device, stored in whoever's account it is.

The association can't set retention, can't guarantee access to footage and can't rely on the camera still being there next month. One Parkchester protester's sign put it plainly: "I should not have to rely on my neighbor's Ring Doorbell."

Owner doorbells have a place on owners' own doors. The entrances, mail area, halls and lot belong to the association, and so should the cameras watching them.

3. "More cameras will stop package theft"

AI answers lean on cameras as the answer to theft. Cameras record a theft; the entrance decides whether it happens.

Most lobby package losses we look into start with a door that lets in anyone a resident buzzes or anyone walking in behind a resident. A delivery credential that opens only a vestibule or package room, a package room with its own reader, and a policy against buzzing in strangers prevent far more losses than another camera.

Cameras still matter, chiefly when someone can pull a clip before the recorder overwrites it. That is a settings and policy question we sort out with the board as part of the job.

4. "Condo security costs a set amount per door"

Cost guides like to give a per-door or per-unit figure. In the Bronx, the same door count can produce very different totals.

Twelve entrances in one tower share a riser and a closet; twelve entrances spread across a garden complex may each need a panel, a controller link and a run between buildings. A prewar riser that tests good saves days, while one that fails means new cable through occupied apartments. Gates add posts, call boxes and outdoor hardware that a lobby never needs.

So the only price we post in advance is the $250 service call for a repair. For anything bigger we walk the property for free and price it building by building and door by door, which is the only honest way to number a Bronx campus.

5. "New construction in the Bronx means modern condo systems everywhere"

Because the South Bronx is building quickly, summaries can imply the borough's condos are new and their systems current. Most of the new buildings there are rentals.

The bulk of Bronx condo homes are in older buildings converted from rentals, where entry systems date from the rental years or have been replaced one building at a time. Those associations face mixed generations of equipment, risers decades old and directories that never caught up with resales.

For them, the first useful step is a survey that shows what each building actually has, followed by a standard every building moves toward as the budget allows.

6. "Going all-app is the modern intercom choice"

App-only intercoms are often described as the obvious upgrade. For some buildings they are; for others they shut residents out.

Many Bronx condo owners are older, some have no smartphone, and some simply won't install an app to answer the door. A system that only rings phones through an app leaves those residents with no way to let a visitor in. Some app platforms also charge every month per door or per apartment.

We plan answering methods by apartment, often mixing monitors or landline dialing with app access, and we show any recurring fee over several years before the board decides.

7. "Security upgrades cut a condo's insurance bill"

Automated answers frequently promise lower premiums after cameras or access control go in. Insurers decide that policy by policy, and there is no general rule that they must.

We don't predict premiums. A board that wants to know should ask its broker; if the carrier wants an equipment list, we provide one.

Where a good system earns its keep is the morning after something goes wrong: the board can see which entrance was used, by which credential, at what time, and what the camera recorded. That is worth having whatever the premium does.

Field notes from our Bronx crew

In converted Bronx complexes, the first thing we look at is not the panel but the door. A closer that has given out or a strike knocked out of line leaves an entrance that drifts open, and residents blame the intercom. Fixing the door first is often the cheapest improvement on the whole property, and it makes everything installed afterward work as intended.

The second is the cellar. Older buildings often hide a row of transformers from systems long since removed, plus abandoned cable that may or may not still carry anything. We trace, label and remove what is dead before adding anything new, so the next technician isn't guessing.

The third is the directory. In buildings with years of resales and rentals behind them, the panel often still lists owners long gone and misses current residents. Cleaning that list, and agreeing with the agent who updates it at every closing and lease, solves more "the intercom doesn't work" calls than any new hardware.

FAQ

Bronx Condo Security Systems FAQ

Do you work on security in Parkchester's condominium buildings?
+

Yes. Parkchester's buildings are condominiums, and we take requests from boards, management and individual owners there like anywhere else in the Bronx. We are an independent contractor with no connection to the complex's management or patrol, and building-wide work needs the approval of whichever body controls those doors.

Can the entrance panel show visitor instructions in Spanish?
+

Many current entry panels support more than one display language, and some let the visitor choose. It depends on the model, so we confirm it for the specific panel in the proposal. Printed instructions beside the panel are an easy addition either way.

Can you mount cameras on our parking lot light poles?
+

Often, yes, using pole brackets and outdoor-rated cable or a wireless link back to the building. Power at the pole has to come from the association's electrician, because we don't do electrical work. We check the pole, the view and the lighting on the walk-through.

Will you coordinate with our guard service or patrol company?
+

Yes. We set up staff credentials and camera access for the people the board approves and can place a viewing station where the guards work. The guard company runs its own staff and procedures; we provide the equipment they use.

Do you take out the old equipment and patch the holes it leaves?
+

We remove abandoned panels, readers, cameras and dead cable that are part of our scope, and we cover openings with blank plates where that fits. Patching and painting walls is not our trade, so the association's painter or handyman finishes the surfaces.

Can we keep the fobs residents already have when the access system is upgraded?
+

Sometimes. If the new readers can read the existing format, residents keep their fobs and the change is invisible to them. If the old fobs are an easily copied type, we usually recommend switching to encrypted ones, and we plan the handout so nobody is left without a working credential.

What should we send ahead of the free walk-through?
+

The number of buildings, apartments and entrances, a photo of each entrance panel and of the equipment room, the names of any current platforms or installers, and the board's main complaints. That lets us bring the right questions and keeps the visit short.

If the building replaces the intercom, can an owner pay to upgrade the equipment in their own apartment?
+

Yes, where the board allows it. A common setup is a standard answering method paid by the association, with owners able to pay for a larger monitor or an extra station in their own home. We list the optional upgrade price in the proposal so owners can decide.

Can a board member meet the estimator after work?
+

Walk-throughs are scheduled between 8 AM and 8 PM, in two-hour arrival windows, so an early-evening slot is often possible. Daylight visits are better for checking outdoor camera views, so large campuses sometimes need a second short visit.

Can the managing agent get one proposal and one invoice for several buildings in a complex?
+

Yes. We can issue one document covering every building, with each building and each shared item on its own line, so costs can be split among condominium sections or budget lines as the governing documents require.

What happens if a panel or reader has to be special ordered?
+

We tell the board before the deposit that the item is special order and that shipping time is set by the manufacturer. The order goes in once the deposit clears, we share the arrival date when the maker gives it, and installation is booked once the equipment is in our hands.

Do you monitor alarms or cameras for condo owners?
+

Intrusion alarms we install for owners can be connected to central station monitoring through our provider. We don't run a video monitoring center or watch the association's cameras; the recorder and its logins stay with the people the board names.
