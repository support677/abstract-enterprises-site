The Neighborhood

Why Queens Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Door by Door

Queens is the borough of the house with three doors and seven key-holders. Drive down almost any block in Bayside, Middle Village, Hollis, Woodhaven or Whitestone and the pattern repeats: a detached or semi-detached house with a stoop or porch at the front, an aluminium storm door hung over the main door, a side door at the end of the driveway that the family actually uses every day, and a set of concrete steps leading down to a basement door at the back or under the porch. Each of those doors gets a different amount of traffic, faces different weather and is used by different people. A smart lock project here starts by deciding which doors deserve one and which should simply be locked properly and left alone.

The households behind those doors are rarely a couple and a dog. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts for Queens County report 2.72 persons per household, nearly half of all residents born outside the United States, and more than half of residents over five speaking a language other than English at home. In practice that means a parent or grandparent who reads Mandarin, Korean, Bengali or Spanish more comfortably than English, an older relative who has never owned a smartphone, adult children coming and going at all hours, and often a home attendant or a cousin who needs to get in on a schedule. A keyless system that only works through one English-language app on one person's phone fails most of the household. We plan the credentials around the people first: keypad codes for those who prefer numbers, fingerprints for those who forget codes, fobs for those who want something to hold, and a working mechanical key for everyone, always.

The basement is the second Queens-specific question, and it is a serious one. When the remnants of Hurricane Ida hit the city on September 1, 2021, the Queens Daily Eagle reported that 11 of the 12 people then confirmed dead in the city were from Queens, and that 10 of the dead had lived in basement apartments. The city's own health department data story on flooding later put Ida-related deaths at fourteen, most of them by drowning in unregulated basement apartments. Five years on, New York City Emergency Management still tells basement occupants to keep exits clear and know more than one way out, and to install a water sensor or flood alarm below grade. A basement door is therefore the one place in a Queens house where we are most conservative about electronics: the inside must open with a plain turn in total darkness, nothing about the lock can depend on power, and anything electronic on the outside should be chosen and positioned with standing water in mind.

Then there is the legal layer around those basement units. In 2024 the city enacted Local Law 126, a pilot program to bring existing basement and cellar apartments toward legal occupancy in a list of community districts that includes Queens Community District 2 — Long Island City, Sunnyside and Woodside. To qualify for a temporary residence authorization, a unit must have existed before April 20, 2024 and must, among other conditions, have at least one means of egress directly to the outdoors, plus smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and water sensors. A smart lock cannot make an unlawful apartment lawful, and we do not pretend otherwise. What we can do is make sure the hardware we put on that door never stands between the people downstairs and the street.

The rest of Queens adds its own door types. Garden co-ops in Jackson Heights, Glen Oaks and Bay Terrace put apartment doors on courtyards and walk-up stairs rather than on long interior corridors. Long Island City's glass towers, many of them built in the last fifteen years, often arrive with the building's own app-based entry already in place. And the borough's shopping streets — Main Street in Flushing, Steinway Street in Astoria, Jamaica Avenue, 82nd Street and Roosevelt Avenue in Jackson Heights — are full of family-run shops and restaurants where three or four relatives open up on different days and the back door onto the alley is as important as the front.

Door and hardware details we check on every Queens job

The storm door and the real door. Most Queens houses have an aluminium storm door hung on the outside of the main frame. It keeps weather off the main door and, in summer, lets air through a screen. Many storm doors carry their own small keyed latch, and families sometimes end up using that latch as their lock while the main deadbolt is left open behind it. That is backwards: a storm-door latch is not built to resist force. We put the smart lock on the main door, check that its keypad can be reached and read with the storm door held open, and confirm the storm door's closer and latch don't drag on the deadbolt's exterior housing.

Frames that have moved. Attached and semi-detached houses settle, and wooden frames swell in humid summers and shrink in dry winters. A motorised deadbolt is less forgiving of a misaligned strike than a hand-turned one: if the bolt touches the edge of the strike plate, the motor strains, the battery drains and the lock reports a jam. We test the bolt with the door pulled shut by hand, by its own weight and by the storm door closing behind it, and we move or enlarge the strike where needed so the bolt throws cleanly in every season.

Below-grade doors. A basement door at the bottom of exterior steps lives in the wettest spot on the property. Leaves block the drain at the foot of the stairwell, rain pools against the threshold and, in a cloudburst, water can rise above the lock. Yale's own support page, for example, describes its Smart Keypad as IP55 — dust protected and effective against water jetting — and says its lock and bridge are installed inside the home and are not intended for outdoor use. Nothing in that rating describes being submerged. So on a basement door we favour mechanical inside hardware, mount any keypad as high on the door or frame as is practical, and plan for the outside electronics to be replaced rather than trusted after a flood.

One turn to get out. Every lock we put on a residential door in Queens opens from inside with a thumb-turn or lever, no key, code or phone required. HPD's fire-safety guidance says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors, and on a house door — especially a basement door — we apply the same standard, because the danger it guards against is the same.

Compliance callouts for Queens business doors

  • Exit without a code. A door that serves as a way out of a shop or restaurant must open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. Outside keypads are fine; an inside keypad or key cylinder on an exit door is not. Electrified locks get a push-to-exit button or motion sensor on the inside.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure, door by door. When power drops, an electric strike or magnetic lock either releases or stays locked. Which one is right depends on whether the door is part of the exit route and what the fire code expects for that space, so we specify it per door, never by default.
  • Alley and delivery doors. A rear door that staff use as an emergency exit stays an exit. Keyless entry on the outside never changes the inside, and we will not fit anything that requires a code to leave.
  • Residents upstairs. Where a shop shares a building with apartments, the residential door carries the Housing Maintenance Code's requirements and, if tenants are given smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Act. Shop credentials stay off the residents' door.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Queens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Queens families and business owners put to us before a smart lock goes on a door, grouped by situation: households where several generations share the doors, basements and flood-prone entrances, garden co-ops and towers, house doors with storm doors, and shops. Price, scheduling and coverage questions are in the FAQ further down. Nothing here is legal advice; where a law or a city rule is mentioned we name it so you can read it yourself or ask your own lawyer.

Households that share the doors

Grandma doesn't carry a smartphone — how does she get in once our front door goes keyless?

Exactly the way she wants to. A smart lock doesn't have to involve a phone for every user; the phone is only needed by whoever administers the lock. For a relative who has never used an app, the usual choices are a keypad code, a fingerprint, a small fob on her keyring, or simply her existing key, which should keep working on any lock we fit to a Queens house door. We ask her which she prefers, not the grandchildren.

A few details make the difference for older users. A keypad with large, physical, backlit buttons is easier than a smooth touchscreen that needs the whole face woken first. A four- to six-digit code built from something she already knows — never her birth year, which is printed on too many documents — is easier to remember than a random string. A fingerprint enrolled twice, on two different fingers, avoids lockouts when one finger is cut or bandaged. And a lock that announces "locked" or flashes clearly removes the habit of going back down the stairs to check.

Before we leave, we have her open and lock the door herself, from outside and inside, with the method she has chosen, while we watch. If she struggles, we change the method on the spot rather than leaving a lock the household will quietly stop using.

Our home attendant comes three mornings a week — can her code work only on those days?

Yes, and it should. Most current smart locks and their apps let the administrator give a user a schedule: certain days, certain hours, or a start and end date. A home attendant who works Monday, Wednesday and Friday from 8 a.m. can have a code that opens the door only in that window, and a second, separate code can be created for the relief aide the agency sends when she is off. Each opening is logged under that person's name, which is useful when an agency asks you to confirm visit times.

Two practical points. First, schedules depend on the lock knowing the correct time; locks that stay connected to a bridge or hub keep accurate time, while a purely Bluetooth lock that nobody opens with the app for weeks can drift. We set up whichever connection keeps the schedule reliable. Second, give each caregiver their own code rather than sharing a family code. When the agency changes staff, you delete one code and nobody else is affected.

If the person being cared for has trouble with doors themselves, think about which door the aide uses. A side door off the driveway with a keypad is often easier for a visiting worker than a front door behind a storm door and a set of steps.

The lock's app is only in English — what are the options for parents who read Chinese, Korean or Spanish?

Check the app before you buy the lock, because the answer varies widely by brand and even by country. When we checked while preparing this page in October 2026, Apple's App Store listed the Schlage Home app in English, French and Spanish, and the Yale Access app sold for US and Canadian Yale locks in English only, while Yale's separate Yale Home app, offered outside North America, listed English plus 25 more languages. Phone settings don't translate an app that hasn't been translated by its maker.

For many Queens families the more useful answer is that the parents never need the app at all. The app belongs to whichever family member manages the lock; everyone else uses a keypad code, a fingerprint, a fob or a key, none of which involves reading a screen. Some keypad locks also speak setup prompts aloud — Yale's programming guides for several keypad models describe a voice-prompt language setting of English, Spanish or French.

What we do on the day is simple: the administrator's phone gets the app, each parent gets the method that needs no language at all, and we leave a one-page sheet with the steps written out so a family member can translate it into the language the parents read.

Dad has early dementia — can the lock tell us if he opens the front door at night without locking him in?

It can tell you, and it must never lock him in. The Alzheimer's Association's wandering checklist suggests warning bells above doors or a monitoring device that signals when a door is opened, and it is explicit that a person living with dementia should never be locked in at home. A connected smart lock fits the first part well: the app can send a notification when the door is unlocked from inside, or when it is opened between hours you choose, and a separate door contact can add an alert for the door simply being opened.

What we won't do is make the inside of the door harder to open. A keypad or key on the inside of a house door would trap him — and everyone else — in a fire, which is exactly why HPD calls double-cylinder locks illegal on apartment doors. The same checklist mentions placing deadbolts out of the line of sight; on a residential door that raises its own fire-egress questions, so talk that through with his doctors and the rest of the family rather than leaving it to a lock installer.

The practical set-up is a smart lock with a normal inside thumb-turn, notifications to two family members' phones, a chime or contact if you want a sound in the house, and a doorbell camera so the person who gets the alert can see the doorstep immediately.

Arthritis makes turning a key painful — which kind of smart lock is easiest to work?

One where nothing has to be gripped and twisted. With a motorised deadbolt, the bolt is thrown by the motor once a code, fingerprint or fob is accepted; the user still has to push or pull the door, but never turn a small key in a stiff cylinder. Locks that relock automatically after a set time also remove the step of turning anything on the way out.

Among input methods, a fingerprint reader asks the least of stiff hands, followed by a fob held near the reader. Keypads vary: large raised buttons are easier than a touchscreen that needs firm, accurate presses. Inside, check the thumb-turn — a long, wide paddle is far easier than a small round knob — and look for a lock with an inside button that locks the door without turning anything.

Just as important is the door itself. If the door needs a shove to latch because the frame has moved, a weak hand can't close it far enough for the bolt to throw. We adjust the strike and hinges so the door latches with a light push, which helps every user, not only the one with arthritis.

Basements, floods and the doors nobody watches

Our basement door at the bottom of the outside steps took water during Ida — will a smart lock survive there?

Not if it goes under. Smart lock electronics are designed for rain on a door, not for standing water over the lock. Yale's support page, for instance, rates its outdoor Smart Keypad IP55 — protected against dust and water jets — and says its smart lock and bridge belong inside the home. A basement stairwell where water pooled to knee height during Ida is outside what any of those ratings describe.

That doesn't rule out convenience on the basement door; it changes the priorities. Our usual approach is a high-quality mechanical deadbolt with a plain inside thumb-turn as the primary lock, and, if the family wants keyless entry there, a keypad unit mounted as high as the door and frame allow, accepted as a part that may need replacing after a serious flood. Where the main living entrance is upstairs, we often recommend putting the smart lock on that door instead and keeping the basement door simple.

Just as useful is fixing what made the stairwell flood: clearing the drain at the foot of the steps, and adding the water sensor NYC Emergency Management recommends for basements and ground floors. A lock that survives doesn't help if the room behind it doesn't.

If the basement floods at night and the power is out, can whoever is downstairs still get out?

They must be able to, and the hardware we fit is chosen so they can. NYC Emergency Management's flash-flood guidance tells basement residents to keep exits clear, know more than one way out and move to a higher floor when heavy rain is coming, rather than waiting for water to come in. Every one of those steps assumes the door opens instantly from inside, in the dark, for someone who may be frightened and wet.

So on any door that serves as a way out of a basement we follow three rules. The inside opens with a single thumb-turn or lever — no code, no key, no phone. The lock is a battery or mechanical deadbolt that stays fully operable by hand when the electricity is off; we do not put an electrified strike or magnetic lock on a residential basement door. And nothing extra — a slide bolt, a chain at head height, a second keyed lock — is added that someone unfamiliar with the door would have to find in the dark.

We also check the door's swing and the stairwell. A door that sticks when the frame is damp, or that opens onto steps cluttered with stored items, is a bigger risk than any lock. If yours sticks, that is the first thing we fix.

Burglars in Glen Oaks walked in through an unlocked basement door — is our basement door worth a smart lock, or is a door sensor enough?

It depends on how the door is used. QNS reported in July 2025 that three burglars entered a private house near 268th Street and 79th Avenue in Glen Oaks through an unlocked basement door on a June afternoon and took about $50,000 in jewellery. Three years earlier, ABC7 reported a pattern of daytime break-ins in Queens Village, Glen Oaks and New Hyde Park, each involving a side door or window being broken. The lesson of the first story is that the door people use least is the one left open; the lesson of the second is that a lock is only one layer.

If the basement door is used every day — by a relative who lives downstairs, or as the route to the laundry and the yard — a smart lock earns its place: it can report whether the door is locked, send a reminder at a set hour if it isn't, and give the downstairs occupant their own code. If the door is opened a few times a year, a good mechanical deadbolt plus a door contact tied to an alarm or a smart-home hub is usually the better value: it tells you the moment the door opens, whether or not someone remembered to lock it.

Many Queens families end up with both on the busiest below-grade door, and a contact alone on the others. Neither stops a broken basement window, which is where cameras and window contacts come in.

We have a family member living in the basement of our Woodside house — what do the city's basement rules say about that door?

Several things, and none of them is decided by the lock. The 2024 basement and cellar pilot program, Local Law 126, covers existing basement apartments in a list of community districts that includes Queens Community District 2, which takes in Woodside, Sunnyside and Long Island City. To be eligible for a temporary residence authorization, a unit must have existed before April 20, 2024, and the inspection criteria in the law include at least one means of egress directly to the outdoors and access to a public way. The authorization also comes with requirements for smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, water sensors and alarms, and later a sprinkler system. Owners have until April 20, 2029 to apply, and the Department of Buildings proposed implementing rules in late 2025.

Whether a relative's basement room counts as a separate apartment, and whether your house is covered, are questions for the Department of Buildings, HPD or your own lawyer or architect, not for us. What we can say is that the door to the outside is the means of egress the law is talking about, so the hardware on it has to open instantly from inside without a key or code, stay operable without power, and not be supplemented with bolts or chains that slow someone down.

Garden co-ops and Long Island City towers

Our garden co-op apartment's door opens straight onto the courtyard, not a hallway — does that change which smart lock fits?

Yes. Most smart lock advice for apartments assumes a door inside a heated corridor. In Glen Oaks Village, which describes itself as 2,904 garden-style apartments in 134 buildings, in the Bay Terrace sections in Bayside, and in parts of Jackson Heights, many unit doors face a courtyard, a garden path or a short exterior stair. That door gets rain, sun and winter cold on its outside face, and the lock's exterior half has to be rated for it.

In practice we look for three things. An exterior keypad or reader that its maker rates for outdoor use, rather than a model intended for interior corridors. A finish that matches what the co-op expects on its doors — most garden co-ops care a great deal about the uniform look of their courts. And an inside unit that copes with the temperature swing of a door that is cold on one face and warm on the other, which mostly means good batteries and a door that seals, so condensation doesn't form inside the housing.

Board approval and duplicate-key rules for co-ops are covered on our five-borough page; here the point is the weather. Ask your managing agent whether the exterior face of the unit door is the co-op's responsibility before buying anything, and bring us the answer.

Our Long Island City rental came with the building's app lock — can we connect it to Google Home or Apple Home?

Only as far as the building's platform allows. When a developer or landlord installs an entry system on unit doors, the hardware and the software account belong to the building, and integrations with consumer smart home apps are switched on or off by the platform's operator, not by the resident. Some platforms offer phone widgets or their own voice-assistant links; others offer nothing beyond their own app. Ask the building management or the platform's support pages what is enabled for your building.

What we won't do is open or modify a building-owned lock, add a retrofit unit over hardware the lease says belongs to the owner, or wire anything into the building's system. If you want automations at home, the safer route is to build them around devices you own — a doorbell camera or a door sensor inside the apartment — and leave the door hardware alone.

If the app-based entry is the only way into your apartment, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, matters to you: HPD's summary says an owner of a multiple dwelling may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to enter their own unit. That's an issue to raise with management, not something to solve with a drill.

House doors

Our house has an aluminium storm door with its own lock over the front door — which one should get the smart lock?

The main door, almost always. A storm door is a lightweight aluminium frame with glass or screen panels, hung outside the real door to protect it from weather. Its latch, even a keyed one, is designed to keep the storm door closed in wind, not to stop someone forcing their way in. The main door, with a deadbolt throwing into a solid frame, is the security barrier, and that is where a smart deadbolt belongs.

The storm door then becomes a usability question. Can a visitor or a relative reach and read the keypad with the storm door held open? Will the storm door's handle or closer hit the smart lock's exterior housing? Does the storm door's own lock get left locked, so that someone with a code for the main door is still stuck outside? We check all three and, where the storm-door latch is causing more trouble than it prevents, we suggest leaving it unlocked or swapping it for a latch with no key.

There are exceptions. Some families use a full-view security storm door as their real barrier, especially on a side door. In that case we talk through whether the main door or a reinforced storm door should carry the electronics, but we still keep the inside release on both doors simple.

Shops and restaurants

Our shop's keypad faces busy Jamaica Avenue — how do we stop passers-by watching staff punch in the code?

Start with where the keypad sits. A keypad mounted at eye level beside the door, in full view of the bus stop, invites shoulder-surfing. Mounting it on the frame at the side where the user's body naturally screens it, under a small hood, or recessed into the storefront return makes the code much harder to read from the sidewalk. We look at the sightlines from the street and the nearest bus shelter before choosing the spot.

Then reduce how much the code matters. A fob or a fingerprint can't be watched and repeated, so the staff who open most often can use those, with codes kept for occasional users. Codes that are individual rather than shared can be changed for one person without retraining everyone, and the app's log shows if a code is suddenly used at an hour nobody was working. Wiping the keypad face now and then also removes the worn-shiny buttons that give away which digits are in the code.

Finally, choose hardware built for a commercial street: a metal keypad housing rather than plastic, screws that need a special bit, and a cable route protected inside the frame. On Jamaica Avenue, as on any busy Queens strip, the keypad will be bumped, leaned on and rained on far more than one on a house.

Stylists rent chairs in our Jackson Heights salon — how do we give them after-hours access without handing out keys?

Give each stylist their own code with a schedule. In a chair-rental salon, each renter is effectively running a small business inside yours and may book clients early in the morning or after you close. A keypad deadbolt or a keypad with an electric strike on the front door lets you issue each renter a personal code, limited to the days and hours your agreement allows, and remove it in seconds when they move on.

Think about which door they use. If the salon has a rear door to a hallway shared with apartments upstairs, keep salon codes off any door the residents use; residents' doors are governed separately and their access shouldn't be mixed with your tenants' business hours. If there is a back room with stock, products or the till, consider a separate lock that only you and your manager can open.

Write the arrangement into your chair-rental agreement: which door, which hours, who is responsible for locking up, and that the code ends when the agreement ends. The lock then enforces what the paperwork already says.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Queens

A word on sources: the tools used to research this page had no access to Google's AI Overview, so the seven headings below are not its words. They are claims that appear again and again in lock marketing, review roundups and how-to articles, which answer engines pick up and repeat. Each is followed by what we see on real Queens doors.

“Everyone in the house just uses the app”

Smart lock marketing almost always shows one person opening the door with a phone. In a Queens household of three generations that picture leaves out the grandparent without a smartphone, the parent who reads Bengali but not English, the teenager whose phone is always at five percent, and the aide who shouldn't be given an app account at all.

The app is an administration tool. It should be on one or two phones belonging to the people who manage the lock. Everyone else is better served by a keypad code, a fingerprint, a fob or a key — and the best locks support several of those at once.

When we set up a Queens house, we write down each person and the method they will use before we choose the lock. That list decides the model far more than any feature chart.

“The app works in any language your phone is set to”

Phones translate their own menus, not third-party apps. An app shows only the languages its maker has translated it into. When we checked in October 2026, the App Store listed the Schlage Home app in English, French and Spanish and the US Yale Access app in English only; Yale's Home app, sold outside North America, listed 26 languages.

For a borough where, according to the Census Bureau, more than half of residents speak a language other than English at home, that matters. A parent who can't read the app can't approve a guest code, check the log or understand a low-battery warning.

Check the App Store or Google Play listing for the language you need before buying, and plan for the parents to use a method that doesn't involve reading at all.

“Smart locks are weatherproof, so any door is fine”

Many locks are rated for rain and temperature swings on an ordinary exterior door. Ratings like IP55 describe dust and water jets, though, not immersion. Yale's own support article rates its Smart Keypad IP55 and says the lock and bridge belong inside the home.

Queens has thousands of doors that sit below grade at the bottom of exterior steps, and Ida showed how fast those stairwells can fill. A lock sitting under a foot of water is outside every rating we have seen.

On those doors we keep the inside mechanical, mount any keypad high, treat outside electronics as replaceable, and often recommend a battery-free mechanical keypad instead.

“Auto-lock means you never have to think about locking up again”

Auto-lock is genuinely useful: the bolt throws on its own a set time after the door shuts. It is the best single answer to the forgotten side or basement door that QNS described in the Glen Oaks burglary of June 2025.

But auto-lock assumes the door is closed and latched. A door that rests a quarter-inch open because a frame has moved, or that a storm door holds slightly ajar, can report "locked" after the bolt has thrown into thin air, or jam and drain the battery trying.

Before relying on auto-lock we make sure the door latches from its own weight or closer, and we turn on the door-position feature where the lock has one, so the app knows the difference between locked-and-shut and bolt-out-door-open.

“A smart lock is all the security a house needs”

A good deadbolt that is actually locked is excellent protection for the door it is on. That is all it protects. The 2022 burglary pattern ABC7 reported in Queens Village and Glen Oaks involved side doors or windows being broken, which no lock prevents.

Smart locks earn their place by making it easy to keep every door locked and by telling you when one isn't. Windows at the back of the house, basement windows at ground level and garage side doors are separate problems.

That's why we often pair a smart lock job with a doorbell camera at the most-used entrance and alarm contacts on the doors and windows that can't be seen from the street.

“Put a keypad on the basement door and the apartment is good to go”

Online advice about renting out a basement sometimes treats a separate keypad entrance as the finishing touch. In New York City the door is the least of it. Local Law 126 of 2024 sets out a pilot path for existing basement apartments in specific community districts, including Queens Community District 2, with egress to the outdoors, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, water sensors and later sprinklers among the conditions.

A lock cannot make a unit legal, and an electronic lock that fails shut or needs a code to exit can make a basement more dangerous, not less.

If you have or are planning a basement unit, talk to the Department of Buildings, HPD or an architect first. We will fit door hardware that keeps the way out simple once you know what the space is allowed to be.

“A building's app lock works with any smart home system”

Some consumer locks are certified for several smart home ecosystems, and articles about them blur into articles about the entry systems new rental towers install. They are different things.

A building-run platform on unit doors, common in Long Island City's newer towers, is owned and configured by the building. Whether it links to Google Home, Apple Home or Alexa is the operator's decision, and a resident can't add it.

If your building's system is the only way into your apartment, the Tenant Data Privacy Act says your landlord can't require you to use it to get into your own unit. Raise that with management; don't try to modify the lock.

Below-Grade Doors

A Basement Door Checklist for Queens Houses

After Ida, the basement door deserves its own checklist. This is what we go through before recommending any lock for a below-grade entrance in Queens.

  • Is it a way out? If anyone sleeps, works or spends evenings in the basement, the door is an escape route. The inside must open with one turn, without a key, code, phone or power.
  • Where does water go? We look at the drain at the foot of the stairwell, the threshold height and any staining on the door from past water. A door that has been underwater gets mechanical hardware first.
  • Is there a second way out? NYC Emergency Management tells basement residents to know more than one way out. We don't design exits, but we point out when a basement appears to have only one.
  • Is there an alert? A water sensor below grade, as NYC Emergency Management recommends, and a door contact if the family wants to know when the basement door opens.
  • Who uses it? A relative's or tenant's private entrance gets its own lock and codes, separate from the family's doors upstairs.
  • What does the city say about the space? If the basement is lived in, the 2024 pilot program and the Department of Buildings decide what is required. We fit hardware that suits whatever the space is allowed to be.

Basement residents can also join the Notify NYC basement alert group, which the city describes as targeted warnings about life-threatening weather for people living below grade. It costs nothing and it is the warning that gives people time to use the door.

Connectivity

Getting a Signal to the Side Door and the Basement

In a Queens house, the router usually sits in the living room or upstairs, and the doors that matter most are as far from it as the house allows. A side door at the end of the driveway, a rear door through a kitchen extension and a basement door below a concrete slab can each be a dead spot.

  • Bluetooth only: fine for a door that only needs to open for people standing at it. No remote access and no alerts, but nothing to fail.
  • Bridge or hub near the door: a plug-in bridge or a hub placed within range relays the lock to your network. We test signal at the bridge position, not at the router.

Whatever the method, we finish the same way: phone on mobile data, standing on the sidewalk, lock and unlock each door remotely and confirm the alert arrives. Field Notes on Queens House Doors

The order our installers follow around a Queens house — a routine we use everywhere, not the story of one visit

We start at the door the family actually uses, which in Queens is often not the front door. We ask who came in that way today and how: a key, a hand on the storm door, a code at the side gate. That door gets the most attention, because it will be opened a thousand times a year by the most people.

Then the storm door. We open it, hold it, and see whether a hand can reach the main door's deadbolt and whether the storm door's own latch is being used as the lock.

Then the basement. We walk down the outside steps, look at the drain, the threshold and the bottom of the door for water marks, and open the door from inside with the lights off. If it sticks, has a chain or a slide bolt high up, or needs a key from inside, that is fixed before anything smart goes near it.

Last, the people. We sit with whoever will use each door, including the oldest and least technical member of the household, and choose their method with them. A lock that the grandmother can't open is a lock the family will stop using.

Queens Smart Lock Installation FAQ

We just closed on a house in Queens — should we re-key first or go straight to smart locks?
+

Go straight to smart locks on the doors you plan to convert, because the new locks come with new keys and nobody else's old copies will fit them. On the doors you are keeping mechanical, have the cylinders re-keyed or replaced, since you can't know how many keys the previous owners, tenants and contractors kept. We can do both in the same visit.

Will you leave written instructions for relatives who won't use the app?
+

Yes. We leave a one-page sheet for each door covering how to open it with a code, fingerprint, fob or key, how to lock it on the way out, what the lights or sounds mean, and who to call if it stops working. It is written in plain English so a family member can translate it for anyone who reads another language.

Do you bring the lock, or should we buy one before the visit?
+

Either works. We can supply the lock once the walkthrough has shown which models suit each door and each person, or fit one you have already bought. If you buy first, keep the box and receipt until we have checked the door thickness, backset, storm-door clearance and the app language, so it can go back if it is the wrong fit.

Who should hold the administrator account when a whole family shares the lock?
+

Two people who live in the house and are comfortable with apps, usually a parent and an adult child. One administrator alone is a risk: if that phone is lost or that person moves out, nobody can add users or change codes. Avoid making a relative who lives elsewhere the only administrator.

Will the lock warn us before its batteries run out?
+

Most smart locks flash or beep at the keypad and, if connected, send a low-battery notification to the administrators' phones, usually weeks before the lock stops working. We set those notifications to reach two people, show the household what the warning looks like, and recommend keeping a spare set of the correct batteries in the house.

Do you cover the Rockaways and Broad Channel as well as northern Queens?
+

Yes. Our Queens crews work across the borough, from Astoria and College Point to Far Rockaway, Breezy Point and Broad Channel. Appointments are booked in 2-hour arrival windows between 8 AM and 8 PM, seven days a week.

Can you put a keypad on the front-yard gate of a Queens house?
+

Often, yes. Metal front-yard and driveway gates can take a weatherproof keypad lock or a keypad with a gate-rated electric lock, depending on the gate's construction and whether power can reach it. We look at the gate, the post and the latch at the walkthrough and price it with the house doors.

What if the wood frame on our older house is too soft to hold the lock's strike?
+

We reinforce it. That can mean long screws through the strike into the framing behind the trim, a reinforcing strike plate, or filling and re-drilling stripped screw holes. Where the frame itself is rotted or split, we tell you before installing, because a carpenter's repair has to come first.

Can you match the new lock's finish to the other hardware on our house?
+

Usually. Most smart lock lines come in several finishes such as satin nickel, matte black, bronze and brass. Tell us the finish of your handles, hinges and storm door at the walkthrough, or send a photo, and we will suggest models that match or tell you if the lock you want isn't made in that finish.
