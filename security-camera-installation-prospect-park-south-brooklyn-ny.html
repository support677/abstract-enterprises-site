The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Park South Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Prospect Park South is a neighborhood of big freestanding houses, and a freestanding house has no shared walls to protect any side of it. The district runs from Church Avenue south to Beverley Road, with the BMT Brighton Line along its east side and its western edge between Stratford Road and Coney Island Avenue. Dean Alvord laid it out from 1899 and the houses went up over the next dozen years. The Prospect Park South Association counts about 200 homes. Wikipedia describes them as substantial, freestanding houses of more than 3,500 square feet, in styles from Colonial Revival and Queen Anne to Tudor Revival, Shingle style, a Swiss chalet and a Japanese-inspired house. A row house in Park Slope has two walls to watch. A house on Rugby Road or Buckingham Road has four, plus a lawn, a driveway, side yards and, often, a garage at the back.

The layout that makes the blocks beautiful also changes how cameras have to work. The Brooklyn Eagle notes that the houses were set back 39 feet from the sidewalk behind an eight-foot planting strip, and Alvord planted trees both at the curb and along the building line so that each house would seem to stand alone in a park. There are no front fences on most blocks and no gates. That means the first boundary a stranger crosses is the edge of an open lawn, and the front door is far enough away that a camera mounted beside it and aimed at the street will show a person on the sidewalk as a small figure in a wide frame. A camera plan here has to decide where on that long approach a face is recorded, and that is almost never at the curb.

The houses are also large inside, and the largest are very large. When 1305 Albemarle Road came on the market in 2022, Brownstoner described more than 11,000 square feet over four floors, a corner lot, a gated rear yard, a long driveway leading to a two-car garage with a charging station, and a staff apartment with its own outside entrance. Not every house is that size, but the pattern repeats across the district: several floors, a full basement, an attic, a side or kitchen door, a back door, and frequently a second household or a rental unit with its own way in. Each of those doors is an entrance a camera system has to account for, and each of those floors is distance a cable has to travel to reach one recorder.

The edges of the district are different again. The Sovereign at 55 Parade Place, built in 1927, is a six-story co-op of 73 apartments facing the Parade Ground, and walk-ups and apartment houses line Church Avenue, Caton Avenue and the blocks toward Coney Island Avenue. In those buildings the questions are lobbies, mail areas, basements and who owns the footage. A few blocks south of the district, in 2018, Bklyner reported that more than 100 packages had gone missing over about six months from one building near Cortelyou Road and Stratford Road; one resident told the outlet, "After some pressure, the building management installed a camera for the mail area." That is the kind of problem a lobby camera is for, and it is different from anything a freestanding house needs.

Crime in the 70th Precinct, which covers Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park, Midwood and Fiske Terrace, is falling, and we would rather you heard that from us than from a scare-sales pitch. Through November 8, 2026, NYPD CompStat counts 80 burglaries in the precinct this year against 117 a year earlier, a drop of 31.6 percent, and all seven major crimes together are down 25.2 percent. The reasons to put cameras on a house here are practical rather than fearful: the property is big and open, the owners are often away from the parts of it that matter, packages and cars sit in plain view at the end of long walks and driveways, and if something does happen, the footage has to be good enough to use.

The Open Lawn

Thirty-Nine Feet of Lawn and No Fence: Where a Prospect Park South Camera Can Actually Record a Face

Every camera makes a trade between how much it shows and how much detail it keeps. A wide lens shows the whole front yard and the sidewalk beyond it, and in exchange each person in the picture is made of very few pixels. A narrow lens, or a wide lens placed close to where people walk, shows less ground but enough detail to recognize someone. On a Brooklyn row house the stoop is a few steps from the sidewalk, so one camera can do both jobs. On a Prospect Park South house the sidewalk is roughly 39 feet from the building line and the door is often farther back again, behind a porch. One camera cannot do both jobs here, and most disappointing systems we see in the district are one camera trying.

Decide what each zone of the yard is for

We break the front of the lot into three zones. The sidewalk and curb are for context: who walked past, which way a car went, what time it was. The lawn and the front walk are for approach: someone left the sidewalk and came toward the house. The porch steps and the door are for identity: this is the face, these are the hands, this is the package being picked up. A system that works here records identity at the steps, approach across the lawn, and context at the street, using different cameras or different lenses for each. We write that down on the quote, so you know which camera is meant to give you a face and which is only there to tell you that someone came.

Put the identity camera where people slow down

People walk quickly across a lawn and slow down at steps, at a door and at a package. That is where a face is turned toward the house, lit by the porch light and close enough to fill the frame. On most houses in the district the best identity position is under the porch ceiling, aimed down the steps at about head height of someone climbing them, rather than high on the facade aimed at the street. It also keeps the camera small and out of sight from the sidewalk, which helps on a landmarked front. Where the porch wraps around a corner of the house, as many do here, a second porch camera may be needed to see the side steps or the length of the porch.

No fence means no line to alert on, so we draw one

Behind a fence, an alert can fire when someone opens the gate. On an open Prospect Park South lawn there is no gate and no edge except the planting strip and the line of the front walk. Modern cameras let us draw a virtual line or zone in the picture and alert only when a person crosses it, so the approach camera can stay quiet for dog walkers on the sidewalk and alert when someone steps onto the grass after midnight. We set those lines on site, at night, with the street trees moving in the wind and the streetlight doing what it does, because a zone that looks right at noon often fires all night once leaves start moving through the infrared light.

Street trees, building-line trees and the winter picture

Alvord's double row of trees, at the curb and along the building line, means many front cameras look through branches. In summer the leaves can hide the sidewalk entirely; in winter bare branches move across the picture and trigger motion alerts. We place approach cameras low enough to see under the canopy where possible, and we use person and vehicle detection rather than plain motion so swaying branches do not wake you up. We never fix a camera, a cable or a bracket to a street tree, and because NYC Parks requires a permit for work within 50 feet of a street tree, any plan that would need a trench across the front of the lot is routed another way or flagged for a permit before it is quoted.

The sidewalk view is still worth having

Context matters after the fact. A clip that shows a person walking north toward Church Avenue at 2:14 a.m. helps a detective connect a case even if it never shows a face. On a corner house, or on a lot where the house sits a little closer to the street, one wide camera that sees the sidewalk in both directions is often worth one of the front positions the Landmarks count allows. We will tell you honestly whether yours is one of those lots, or whether the front positions are better spent on the porch and the driveway.

Wood-frame Victorians: mount on solid blocking, never on trim

A clapboard or shingle wall is easy to drill and easy to damage. A camera screwed straight through shingles into nothing will loosen in the first winter and let water behind the siding. We mount outside cameras on a solid base, either into the framing behind the wall or onto a small mounting block sealed against the siding, and we keep off the turned posts, brackets, spindles and cornice trim that make these porches what they are. The Landmarks guidebook itself says equipment must not be attached on decorative surfaces, and on these houses that is also simply good practice. Cable comes through the wall in the same spot as the camera base, so nothing runs across the face of the house.

Tudor and masonry houses: mortar joints, not stone faces

Stucco, brick and stone change the drilling, not the plan. We anchor into mortar joints or plain masonry, which can be patched invisibly if a camera is ever moved, and avoid carved or decorative stone. On half-timbered fronts we avoid the timbers themselves. Wherever possible the cable goes straight through the wall behind the camera into the basement or the attic, because the guidebook asks for conduit to be concealed and allows only about ten inches of visible conduit where concealing it is not possible.

Showpiece houses: small, quiet and matched to the finish

On a house that people photograph from the sidewalk, the camera should be the last thing anyone notices. We favor small domes and compact turrets finished to match the surface they sit on, which is also the camera type the LPC guidebook illustrates. Under deep eaves and porch ceilings a small camera disappears; on a flat painted wall it does not. We send photos of each proposed position, marked up, before anything is mounted, so you see how the front will look before you approve it.

Large corner and estate houses: two frontages, long runs and more than one recorder location

A corner lot has two street faces, and the Landmarks count is measured per 25 feet of street frontage, so a corner house can carry more front cameras than a mid-block house, spread across both faces. Inside, the distances add up. Standard network cable is specified for runs of up to 100 meters, about 328 feet, from switch to camera. On a four-floor house with a garage at the end of a long driveway, a single recorder in the basement can reach most of the house but not always the far side of the garage and back yard comfortably. We measure the longest runs before quoting and, where needed, place a small switch in the garage or the attic so no camera sits at the ragged end of its cable.

Houses divided into apartments: shared doors and private ones

Many big houses in this part of Flatbush were split into apartments over the decades. On those houses the street door, the porch, the driveway and the yard are usually shared, and each apartment's own door is private. We cover the shared parts on the owner's system and leave apartment doors alone unless the tenant asks. Tenants can be given a view of the shared entrance on their own phone without seeing anything else. If two owners share a house, we can set one recorder with two administrators, or install two small systems, whichever ends fewer arguments.

Prewar co-ops and walk-ups: the board owns the cameras

The Sovereign, built in 1927, is a six-story co-op of 73 apartments; the walk-ups on the surrounding avenues are smaller, but the logic is the same. The corporation or the landlord owns the system, the managing agent usually holds the account, and the super is the person who gets called when a camera goes dark. We plan the street door, the lobby, the mail and package area, the elevator, the basement and any rear or service door, write down who may view and export footage, and set retention long enough that a board hearing about a theft two weeks late can still find it.

Church Avenue: the door, the counter and the stockroom

A Church Avenue shop is usually narrow and deep, with one door to the street and a back room or a basement. The camera that matters most is the one just inside the door, aimed back toward it at head height, because that is the one moment every customer faces the camera. Then the counter, framed tightly on hands, register and the customer side, and the stockroom or basement door, which is where losses that nobody notices for weeks tend to happen. The 70th Precinct Community Council heard in November 2025 that retail theft had risen; a clip of someone walking out with goods is only useful if the camera at the door shows their face on the way in.

Coney Island Avenue: wide fronts, cars and late hours

Coney Island Avenue businesses are often wider than Church Avenue shops and more often deal with cars: repair bays, parking in front, deliveries at the curb. We add a camera that sees the curb and the cars parked in front, set for plates in daylight and for headlights at night, and cover rolling gates and side lots where the business has them. City Planning's South of Prospect study is looking at rezoning along Coney Island and McDonald Avenues, and News 12 reported in May 2026 that it could mean thousands of new homes. If your building is likely to change, we plan cameras that move easily and a recorder you can take with you.

Around the stations: commuting peaks and empty nights

Near the Beverley Road and Church Avenue stations, foot traffic swings between rush-hour crowds and nearly empty streets after midnight. For a shop or an apartment house entrance close to a station, we set alerts for the quiet hours only and make sure the entrance camera has a clean view of the door with no backlight from the street at night. The Brighton Line runs through an open cut along the district's east side, and on lots that back toward it we aim rear cameras at the yard and the back door rather than out across the cut, which keeps the picture on your property and out of the trains' headlights.

Houses of worship and practices in houses

A mosque, a church or a practice in a house has visitors who are not family, at regular times, through one or two doors. The cameras for those doors are planned the way a shop's are: a face at the door coming in, and the paths to the rooms visitors use. Prayer halls, consulting rooms, restrooms and changing areas get no cameras, and we tell the institution in writing where every camera points, so it can tell its members or clients.

Historic District

Cameras on a Landmarked Prospect Park South Front: What the Commission's Guidebook Allows

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Prospect Park South Historic District in 1979, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983. For a camera job, the part that matters is Chapter 11 of the Commission's Permit Guidebook, which covers security cameras, light fixtures and intercoms. It is shorter and more practical than most owners expect, and it says plainly: "Staff can issue permits approving security cameras if installation meets the following criteria." In other words, a well-planned set of front cameras is a staff-level permit, not a public hearing.

The count: two per 25 feet of street frontage

For small residential and commercial buildings, the guidebook says "no more than two security cameras can be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage." For large residential, commercial and institutional buildings the limit is three per 25 feet. Most Prospect Park South houses are small residential buildings in this sense, even when they are large houses. The count is measured along the street, so a wide lot carries more front positions than a narrow one, and a corner lot has two frontages to work with. We measure the frontage before we design the front, so the plan never asks for more cameras than the house is allowed.

Spacing and appearance

The guidebook also asks that cameras "be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and its illustrations show "dome-type security cameras finished to match surrounding materials." On these houses that points to small domes or compact turrets, in white, black or a painted finish that matches the trim or the wall they sit on, placed in logical spots such as porch ceilings and eaves rather than scattered across the facade. A porch camera is often invisible from the sidewalk altogether, which is both what the guidebook prefers and what works best for faces.

Where the cable can go

The general criteria in the same chapter say equipment "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." Where conduit is needed, it "must be concealed by going through plain masonry," and if that is not possible, staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit." In practice we bring cable straight through the wall behind each camera, or up through the porch ceiling, into the basement or attic. That is cheaper to maintain anyway, because cable inside the house does not weather.

Sides, back and garage

The guidebook's camera counts are written in terms of street frontage. The sides and back of a freestanding house, and a garage at the back of the lot, are where most of a camera plan lives on these houses, and we design them with the same discretion: small cameras, logical spacing, no conduit across the walls. If any part of the plan is visible from the street on a corner lot, we treat it as front.

Replacing a camera that is already there

The guidebook notes that applications for "in-kind replacement of existing light fixtures, intercoms, security cameras, and mailboxes" do not need comparative elevation drawings or large-scale details. That makes swapping an old camera for a similar new one in the same spot simpler on paper. It does not say such replacements are exempt from a permit, so we treat them as a permit item and prepare the photos and cut sheets either way. Who files is your choice: many owners file themselves, and we supply the camera positions, spec sheets and marked-up photos the application needs.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Planning a Prospect Park South House From the Curb, Then the Cellar, Then the Attic

This is how we approach a house in the district, not the story of one particular job. We start at the curb, outside the planting strip, and look at the house the way a stranger would: where the front walk leads, where the driveway is, which side yard is dark, whether the porch hides the door. Then we walk the lawn to the porch, the porch to the side door, the driveway to the garage and around the back, noting where people would slow down and where a camera could see them do it. On a Prospect Park South lot that walk takes longer than the whole survey of a row house.

Then we go inside, and the basement comes first, because the basement is where these houses keep their wire. Old alarm cable, abandoned bell wire, a telephone block from decades ago and sometimes an earlier camera system are usually there, along with the paths up through the house: a chase beside the chimney, a stack of closets, a laundry chute or the space behind a back stair. After that the attic, which is where the eave and porch-roof cameras will draw their cable. Only once we know how cable gets from the attic to the basement do we decide where the recorder lives, and we come back after dark, or ask the owner for phone clips at night, to set the alert lines with the streetlights and trees doing what they do.

Security Camera Installation in Prospect Park South: Questions Answered

With 39 feet of lawn between the sidewalk and our porch, the camera by our door shows people at the curb as tiny figures. Should we zoom it in or add a second camera?

Add a second camera rather than zooming the first. If you zoom the door camera toward the curb, you lose the porch steps, which is the one place a face is reliably close and lit. Keep the door camera on the steps and the package spot, and give the street its own wide camera, mounted where the Landmarks count and the trees allow. On many lots the better answer is a third position partway: a camera at the corner of the porch looking along the front walk, which records people at a middle distance as they commit to walking up to the house.

We installed an electric-car charger at the end of our driveway. Can a camera tell us if someone unplugs the car or uses the charger?

Yes. We aim a camera so the charger, the cable and the car's charging port are all in frame, and set a detection zone around the charger only, so a person stepping into that box at night sends an alert while cars passing on the street do not. If the charger sits inside a garage, the camera goes inside too, pointed at the charger and the garage door. Brownstoner's 2022 listing for 1305 Albemarle Road describes exactly this arrangement, a long driveway leading to a two-car garage with a charging station, and it is becoming common on the larger lots.

Our staff apartment has its own side entrance, used by the person who lives there and by deliveries for the household. Should that door's camera alert us, them, or both?

Usually both, with different settings. The resident of that apartment should see their own door live and get alerts when someone arrives, so they are not surprised by a visitor. The household may want only a record, and perhaps alerts after a set hour. We create separate app accounts, give each one only the cameras it should see, and set alert schedules per account. We do not put cameras inside the apartment, and we write down where the side-door camera points so everyone knows what it records.

The trees at the curb and along the building line hide most of the street from June to October. Is a front camera aimed at the sidewalk worth one of our Landmarks positions?

Sometimes, and it depends on the gap. Stand on your porch in summer and look at the sidewalk: if you can see it between the trunks below the canopy, a camera mounted low on the porch can too, and it will be useful all year. If the view is solid leaves, the sidewalk camera will be a winter-only camera, and that front position is better used on the driveway entrance or the front walk. We take a test picture from each proposed spot before you decide, and we look at it in leaf if the job is in summer.

Our front is cedar shingle. Will mounting cameras on it let water in behind the shingles?

Not if the mount is done properly. Shingles shed water downward, so the danger is a camera base that traps water above it or a hole that is not sealed. We mount on a flat block fixed through the shingles into solid framing, flash or seal its top edge, and bring the cable through the wall behind the base so there is no open hole. Where a soffit or porch ceiling is available, we prefer it, because a camera tucked under an overhang barely sees rain at all.

Our house is four floors plus a basement. Is there a limit to how far a camera can be from the recorder?

Yes. Standard network cable is specified for up to 100 meters, about 328 feet, from switch to camera. Most cameras in a house this size fall well inside that, even running from the attic to a basement recorder. The ones that do not are usually at the far end of the garage or the back of a deep yard. For those we add a small switch closer to them, in the garage or the attic, fed by one cable from the recorder, so every camera stays inside its distance and gets full power.

Our back yard ends near the Brighton Line cut. Will train headlights at night keep setting off the rear camera?

They can if the camera looks toward the tracks. Headlights moving through a dark picture are a classic false alarm. We aim rear cameras back toward the house, the back door and the yard rather than out toward the cut, mask out any part of the picture that shows the tracks or the far side, and use person detection rather than simple motion. That keeps alerts to people in your yard, and it keeps the recording focused on your own property.

Our house has wide flared eaves with exposed rafters, like the Japanese-inspired house on Buckingham Road. Where can a camera go without spoiling them?

Under the eave, between rafters, on a small plate fixed to the underside of the roof boards rather than to the decorative rafter tails. From the street the camera disappears into the shadow of the overhang. Cable runs straight up into the attic or the eave space behind it, so nothing crosses the visible woodwork. On showpiece houses we photograph each proposed spot and mark it up for your approval before drilling, and we choose a camera color that matches the underside of the eave.

Our house faces the planted mall in the middle of Albemarle Road. Can our front camera see cars parked on the far side of it?

It can see that they are there, but not read them. A camera on your house looking across your lawn, the sidewalk, your side of the road, the mall and the far lane is looking a long way, and plates and faces on the far side will not be readable at night. If your concern is your own car, cover it where it parks, usually on your driveway. If it is the street outside the house, a wide camera on the porch gives useful context about which way cars and people went, and that is the honest limit of what it can do.

There are no fences on our block, so people cut across lawns during the day. Can the lawn camera ignore daytime traffic and alert only after we go to bed?

Yes. Every camera records all the time, but alerts can follow a schedule. We set the lawn camera to alert only during the hours you choose, for example from midnight to six, and only for people crossing the line we draw across the grass. During the day it keeps recording quietly, so a clip is there if you need it, without sending your phone every child walking a dog across the corner of the lawn.

Our side yard is a narrow strip between our house and the next one, and our neighbor's windows face it. How do we cover our side without looking into their house?

We aim the camera along the strip from front to back, at a slight downward angle, so it sees the ground and your wall rather than across to theirs, and we apply privacy masks that black out the neighbor's windows in the recording itself. You get a record of anyone walking down the side of your house, and your neighbor can see from the masked picture that nothing of theirs is being recorded. We are happy to show them the view if it helps keep the peace.

Driveways & Garages

The Long Driveway, the Rear Garage and the Back Door: Getting Cameras to the Far End of a Prospect Park South Lot

On a row house, the back of the property is a yard you can see from the kitchen. On a big freestanding lot here, the back can be the length of the house plus a deep yard away from the street, with a garage at the end of a driveway and a back door nobody uses after dark. That is where cars sit, where deliveries are sometimes left, and where someone can stand unseen from the street. It is also where cameras are hardest to reach, so it is where a plan needs the most thought.

Three cameras for a driveway, not one

We think of a driveway as three moments. The turn in from the street, where a car's plate and a driver's arrival are recorded; the parked car, where doors, windows and the charger are watched; and the garage door, where a person reaching the back of the lot is seen. A single camera at the house corner aimed down the driveway can show all three as a long, shallow picture, but none of them clearly. On a short driveway, two cameras may do. On a long one, the three moments get three cameras, each with a short, clear job.

How the garage gets its picture

A detached garage needs a cable from the house, and there are four ways to get it there. If there is already conduit from the house to the garage, for an old light or an opener, we pull through it. If the garage is close and the owner agrees, a cable can run along the fence or the house wall in a protected path. An overhead run, properly supported, can bridge a short gap. Trenching across the yard is the last choice, and we never plan a trench near the front of the lot without first checking it against the NYC Parks rule that work within 50 feet of a street tree needs a permit. Wireless bridges exist for gaps that cannot be cabled, and we use them where nothing else works, priced and explained on the quote.

Second doors are first targets

The front door of a Prospect Park South house is lit, visible from the street and usually solid. The side door off the driveway, the kitchen door to the yard and the steps down to the basement are none of those things. Each of them gets a camera that sees a face as someone reaches the handle, not just a figure in the yard, and each is set to alert during the hours that door is normally quiet. If a second household uses one of those doors as its front door, it is set up the way that household wants, on its own account.

The gated rear yard

Some lots have a gated back yard, like the one in the 1305 Albemarle Road listing. A gate is the one real boundary on many of these properties, which makes it the most useful place for an alert. A camera aimed at the gate from the house side records the face of anyone who opens it, and a zone drawn on the gate itself sends one alert when it moves after hours. Combined with a yard-wide camera, that gives you both the moment someone came in and what they did next.

Keeping the recorder close to the middle

The recorder is the heart of the system and, on a big house, its location decides how long the longest cables are. We look for a dry, ventilated spot near the middle of the house's footprint, with power and a path to the internet router: a basement utility room or a closet near the stairs is typical. We avoid putting it in the garage or anywhere it would sit in summer heat or winter cold, and we keep it out of plain sight so it does not leave with anyone who gets inside.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Prospect Park South

The claims below are the kind that come up again and again in AI-written answers and comparison sites when people ask about cameras for a house in this part of Brooklyn. Each is partly true somewhere. Here is how each one holds up on a freestanding Prospect Park South lot.

"Security camera installation in Brooklyn costs about $1,400."

That figure is real. HomeAdvisor puts the Brooklyn average for camera installation and repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and a low-to-high spread of $160 to $3,500. It mixes repairs with installs and one-camera jobs with whole systems, which is why the range is so wide.

An average built mostly from row houses and apartments does not describe a house with a lawn, a porch, two side yards, a driveway and a garage. The number of cameras is higher, the runs are longer, and the garage feed alone can be its own line on the quote. A district house can land well above that average without anyone overcharging.

Our four-camera packages start at $850 on the Brooklyn base rate, and everything beyond the package is written out line by line before you agree to anything. That is a better budget tool than an average, because it shows you which cameras cost what and lets you choose.

"A video doorbell is the best first camera for any house."

For an apartment door or a row house stoop, often yes. A doorbell records who rings the bell, which on those buildings is most of what happens at the front.

On a Prospect Park South house, the doorbell sits at the back of a deep porch, 39 feet or more from the sidewalk. It sees the courier who walks all the way up. It does not see the driveway, the side door, the garage, the yard or the length of the porch where packages are often left, and those are the places owners here ask about after something goes wrong.

A doorbell is a fine addition, and we install wired ones. As a first camera on these lots, the porch steps camera and the driveway camera usually do more.

"You cannot put cameras on a landmarked house."

You can. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook says that staff can issue permits approving security cameras when an installation meets its criteria, which means a well-planned front is a staff-level permit rather than a public hearing.

What the guidebook limits is the count and the look: no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small residential buildings, well spaced, dome-type cameras finished to match, nothing attached through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and conduit concealed or kept to about ten inches where it must show.

Most of a camera plan on a freestanding house sits at the sides, back and garage anyway. We prepare the front positions, photos and cut sheets an application needs, whether you file or we help you file.

"Wireless cameras are just as good as wired, and easier."

Easier to put up, yes. Just as good at the back of a four-floor house on a deep lot, rarely. Wi-Fi has to reach from wherever the router sits, through floors and walls, out to a garage or a yard that may be a long way from it, and battery cameras have to be taken down and charged.

A wired camera gets its power and connection on one cable, records continuously to a recorder in the house, and keeps working when the Wi-Fi is busy or the router reboots. On the long lots here, that difference shows up exactly where cameras matter most: the back of the property.

We use wireless where a cable truly cannot go, such as some garage gaps, and say so on the quote. Everything else is wired, through the basement, the attic and the walls.

"Crime is rising in Flatbush, so you need cameras now."

Not in the 70th Precinct by the latest numbers. NYPD CompStat for the week ending November 8, 2026 shows burglary down 31.6 percent for the year, robbery down 33.9 percent and the seven major crimes together down 25.2 percent.

There are honest caveats. Grand larceny auto rose over the latest 28 days, 9 cases against 8, and the precinct's community council heard in November 2025 that thefts had ticked up. Numbers move, and a quiet year for the precinct is not a guarantee for a particular block.

The real case for cameras here is not fear. It is that these properties are large and open, owners cannot see most of them from inside, and if something does happen, good footage is the thing that turns a report into an answer.

"Police can get into your cameras whenever they want."

Not through the doorbell apps, and not through a recorder you own. Ring's Community Requests, launched in September 2025, let verified agencies post a request on the Neighbors app, and Ring's own announcement says "your participation is completely optional" and "If you ignore the request, the agency will not know."

The city's business camera pilot, run on Fusus by Axon, is also voluntary; businesses that join choose to share. A homeowner with a recorder in the basement holds the only copy of the footage until they decide to hand some of it over.

We set every system up so you can export exactly the minutes a detective asks for, with the correct time stamp, and nothing else. That is the fastest way to help an investigation and the best way to keep the rest of your footage private.

"It is your house, so you can put cameras anywhere inside."

Not quite. New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law section 250.45, applies inside private homes too, and it covers places such as bathrooms, bedrooms and changing areas where people expect privacy. That matters in houses here that have guests, a rental apartment, a staff apartment or household help.

Sound is a separate rule. New York is a one-party consent state for audio under Penal Law section 250.05, which is why recording other people's conversations is a different legal question from recording video.

We keep cameras to entrances, halls and shared rooms, install video-only unless there is a clear lawful reason for audio, never point a camera into a second household's space, and recommend telling anyone who works in the house where the cameras are.

FAQ

Prospect Park South Security Camera FAQ

If police post a footage request in the Ring Neighbors app after something happens on our street, will they know whether we answered?
+

No. Ring's Community Requests, launched in September 2025, let verified agencies post a request with a location and time window, and Ring says participation is completely optional and that if you ignore a request the agency will not know. Agencies receive only the clips owners choose to send. Footage on a recorder in your house works the same way: it stays with you until you decide to share some of it, and we show you how to export just the minutes asked for.

Can the cable to our garage be trenched under the front lawn near the street trees?
+

We avoid it. NYC Parks says a permit is needed for any work within 50 feet of a street tree, and the trees at the curb are close to most front lawns here. A garage cable almost always has a better route: existing conduit, along the house or fence, or overhead for a short gap. If a trench truly is the only way, we flag the permit question on the quote before anything is planned around it.

We have a housekeeper and a nanny who work in the house. What should we know before adding indoor cameras?
+

Keep cameras out of bathrooms, bedrooms and anywhere people change, because New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law section 250.45, applies inside private homes. Keep to entrances, halls and shared rooms, and record video only, since recording conversations you are not part of raises separate audio rules under Penal Law section 250.05. We also recommend telling anyone who works in the house where the cameras are. It avoids misunderstandings and it is the fair thing to do.

Our plan has ten or twelve cameras instead of four. Will two weeks of recording still hold?
+

Not on the same drive. Storage fills in proportion to the number of cameras, their resolution and how much movement they record, so a dozen cameras need roughly three times the drive space of four for the same number of days. On a standard four-camera system two weeks is included, and one to four months costs from $180 to $450. For a larger system we size the drives to the retention you want and show the cost on the quote.

Does a light with a built-in camera count toward the two light fixtures the Landmarks guidebook allows at our door?
+

The guidebook counts light fixtures and intercom panels per door opening, with a maximum of two lights and one intercom panel, and counts cameras separately per 25 feet of street frontage. It does not address combination units. A floodlight or porch light with a camera in it could reasonably be read as both, so on district fronts we use separate small cameras and leave the light fixtures as they are.

Could a camera go on the brick piers at the end of our street to watch everyone coming in?
+

No. The brick entrance piers are part of the district's historic streetscape, not part of any one house, and we do not mount private cameras on public or shared structures, street trees or light poles. A camera on your own house that sees the sidewalk in both directions does the useful part of that job legally, and on a corner lot it can be one of your front positions.

Our porch ceiling is original narrow beadboard. Can a camera be mounted on it without damage?
+

Yes, with care. Beadboard is thin, so we locate the joist or nailer behind it and fix a small mounting plate through the board into solid wood, which keeps the camera steady and spreads the load. The cable goes straight up through the same spot into the porch roof space. If the camera is ever moved, the hole sits under the plate's footprint and is easy to fill and paint.

A storm brought a tree limb down on our car in the driveway. How do we get the clip to our insurance company?
+

Export it from the recorder or the phone app as soon as you can, before it is overwritten, covering a few minutes before and after the event. Save it to a USB drive or your phone, keep the original file unedited, and note the camera name and time. At handover we practice an export with you, so on the day you need it, it takes a few minutes rather than a call to us.

Does the Prospect Park South Association have to approve our cameras?
+

The approval that governs cameras on the front of a house in the historic district comes from the Landmarks Preservation Commission, through a staff-level permit when the installation meets the guidebook's criteria. If you live in a co-op or rent, your board or landlord also has a say. Many owners let their neighbors know before cameras go up, and on blocks without fences that courtesy goes a long way.

Can we get one kind of alert for a car pulling into the driveway and another for a person on the porch?
+

Yes. Modern cameras tell people from vehicles, and each camera has its own detection zones and schedule.
