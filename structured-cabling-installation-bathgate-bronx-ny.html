The Neighborhood

Why Bathgate Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Bathgate packs a twenty-acre industrial park, a school campus built inside a former warehouse, a commercial strip on Third Avenue and more than a century of housing into the blocks between the Cross Bronx Expressway and Claremont Parkway, and almost every one of those buildings was put up before anyone planned for a data network. That is the short version of why cabling here is rarely a matter of pulling a few wires. A network has to be designed around what each building already is.

Start with the name. Lehman College's history of Bronx neighborhoods explains that a Scottish immigrant named Bathgate worked land on the Morris estate early in the nineteenth century and later bought 140 acres of it. His descendants sold the property to the city in 1884, expecting a park with the family name; after a surveying dispute the city called it Crotona Park instead. Wikipedia's article on Crotona Park records that the area was once known as Bathgate Woods. What survived of the family name is the avenue, the playground at Fulton Avenue and East 173rd Street, and the neighborhood itself.

The buildings tell the rest. When the elevated line was extended along Third Avenue around the turn of the twentieth century, Lehman's history says, Bathgate filled with European immigrants drawn largely from lower Manhattan. The city's Department of Finance still records houses from that era: a one-family on Bathgate Avenue built in 1901, a walk-up cooperative on Bathgate Avenue from 1911, and a three-family on Webster Avenue from 1899. A photograph collection on Shorpy describes the old Bathgate Avenue market between 171st and 174th Streets as one of the most crowded places in the Bronx, with stores running continuously along both sides of the street. Buildings of that age have solid masonry, plaster on wood lath, chases that were made for steam pipes and gas lines, and no space set aside for anything like a network closet.

Then came the expressway. Lehman notes that the Cross Bronx was cut through just south of East Tremont Avenue in the 1950s, and by 1980, according to Wikipedia, the city had designated a former residential district of vacant land and buildings as the site of Bathgate Industrial Park. The park opened in 1982 as a joint project of the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey and the New York City Economic Development Corporation. Today it covers seven city blocks and about 454,000 square feet in eight buildings, used for light industry, distribution, offices and education. Its tenants include a generic drug manufacturer, food distributors and academic and vocational training centers. Those are the kinds of businesses that live or die by handheld scanners, label printers, temperature sensors, card readers, classroom projectors and dependable Wi-Fi across big open floors, which makes the park the heaviest user of structured cabling in the neighborhood.

The education side of the park is substantial. Building Design + Construction reported that a dilapidated warehouse there was turned into the Bathgate Educational Campus, a 140,000-square-foot building with four schools and 1,300 students, at a cost of $52 million. Wikipedia lists the Mott Hall Bronx High School and the Urban Assembly School for Applied Math and Science on the campus. Nearby, the SUNY Bronx Educational Opportunity Center operates at 1666 Bathgate Avenue. Classrooms, computer labs and training rooms need far more outlets per square foot than an ordinary office, and they need them in places that can survive furniture being moved every semester.

The newest layer is being built right now. PincusCo shows that Mega Contracting Group filed a new-building job in October 2023 for a 184,512-square-foot residential building at 1904 Bathgate Avenue, with later filings describing 213 apartments. Marketproof reported a March 2025 permit for a five-story building at 1899 Bathgate Avenue with apartments over about 4,700 square feet of commercial space. New York YIMBY has covered a planned four-story, eight-unit building at 2088 Bathgate Avenue, and Openigloo lists 1880 Bathgate Avenue, an eight-floor building with 379 units completed in 2018. Every one of those buildings needs a telecom room, a backbone to each floor and outlets in every home and store, and the cheapest moment to get that right is before the drywall closes.

Connectivity in the area is uneven in a way that matters for building owners. BestNeighborhood's summary of FCC data for Tremont, the wider area that photographer Pat Arnow's essay treats Bathgate as a section of, shows Optimum cable at nearly full availability and Verizon fiber at about 93 percent, but availability on a map is not the same as a fiber line inside a particular building; the provider still needs a path from the street to each apartment. At a May 2026 announcement of the city's Neighborhood Internet initiative, HPD Commissioner Dina Levy said that forty percent of households in the Bronx do not have home internet access. For an owner, the practical question is whether the building has a clean entry point, a riser and a closet that a provider, or a city program, can actually use.

We want to be clear about what we could not find. Our searches turned up no Reddit threads, Yelp reviews or local news stories about network cabling in Bathgate specifically, and we could not see Google's People Also Ask boxes or AI Overview from our build environment. The questions on this page come from contractor FAQ pages, a Verizon customer forum and the conditions the public record describes, and we say where each fact comes from.

From the Provider's Box to the Desk: Finding the Starting Point in a Bathgate Building

Every structured cabling plan begins at the demarcation point, the place where the internet or phone provider's equipment ends and the building's own wiring begins. Everything on the street side belongs to the provider. Everything on the building side, including the cable to each floor, each suite and each outlet, belongs to the owner or the tenant who pays for it. In Bathgate that starting point moves around a great deal depending on the age and use of the building, and finding it is the first thing we do on a walk-through.

Who controls which part

The provider owns its fiber or coax up to its own terminal, and we do not cut, move or open that equipment. If the terminal is in the wrong place, the provider moves it. Past that terminal, ownership depends on the building. In a multi-tenant industrial building the landlord typically controls the shared room, the corridors and the ceiling space outside each suite, and a tenant's cable crosses that space only with the landlord's agreement. In an office condominium, the condominium's board and managing agent normally control the riser and common corridors. In a rental apartment building, the owner controls everything outside the apartments. We find out who has authority over each piece before the drop schedule is written, because a route that crosses someone else's space without permission is not a route at all.

Extending the demarc instead of moving the office

A common Bathgate situation is a provider line that ends in a cellar or a ground-floor room while the business that needs it sits two or three floors up, or at the far end of a long industrial building. The fix is a demarc extension: one tested cable, or a fiber pair for longer distances, from the provider's terminal to a cabinet in the tenant's own space. That gives the tenant a clean, labeled starting point under its own lock, and it means the provider's technician only ever has to deal with their own box. On the price list it appears as a demarc extension from $350 per run.

Fiber availability is not the same as fiber in your building

A Verizon community forum thread describes a familiar problem: a customer in an older apartment building wanted fiber service, the installer said the line had to come from an outside box and through holes drilled in the hallway, and the landlord refused the drilling. The poster asked whether the fiber could follow the building's existing coaxial path instead. The honest answer depends on the building. Sometimes an existing conduit or an unused chase can carry the new line without new holes; sometimes the only clean option is a planned riser that the owner approves once, for every provider, instead of a fresh hole each time a tenant changes service.

Big Floors and Long Runs: Cabling a Unit in Bathgate Industrial Park

A unit in Bathgate Industrial Park looks nothing like an apartment or a storefront. Ceilings are high, floors are open, walls are often concrete block, and the people using the network are moving around with handheld devices instead of sitting at desks. The park's mix of tenants listed on Wikipedia, a drug manufacturer, food distributors and training centers, means three very different kinds of network can sit in buildings a few hundred feet apart. These are the conditions that decide how we cable them.

Distance is the first constraint

Copper network cable such as Cat6 and Cat6A is designed for a channel of up to 100 meters, about 328 feet, from the switch to the device, including the patch cords at each end. In a long industrial building that limit arrives quickly once the cable has climbed the wall, crossed the ceiling and dropped back down. When a dock door, a far corner of the floor or a mezzanine office sits beyond that distance from the main rack, we add a second closet with its own switch and link it back over fiber, rather than stretching copper past what it can reliably carry.

Height changes how the cable is supported

Under a high roof there is no drop ceiling to hide anything, so the cable is visible and has to be supported properly along its whole length. We hang it on J-hooks or in cable tray attached to the building structure, never draped across sprinkler pipes, lights or ductwork, and we keep it clear of the space the sprinkler heads need. Drops to the floor come down columns or walls inside conduit or surface raceway, where a pallet or a cart cannot snag them.

Block walls are cored, not hammered

Many park buildings divide space with concrete block. When a cable has to pass from a warehouse floor into an office or a network room, we core a clean hole of the right size at a planned spot, sleeve it, and seal it. If the wall is fire-rated, the penetration is firestopped with a listed product so the wall keeps the rating it had before we arrived, and we photograph the result for the landlord.

Wi-Fi coverage starts with cable

Scanners and tablets only work as well as the access points they connect to, and access points only work as well as the cable feeding them. Racking, stacked product and metal doors all absorb and reflect signal, so an open warehouse usually needs more access points than its square footage suggests, placed over the aisles and the docks rather than on the office wall. Each access point gets its own cable back to a switch, which also powers it, so no outlet is needed at the ceiling. On our price list a Cat6A access point drop to the ceiling is $300 per access point.

Turn-of-the-century buildings: find a vertical path that already exists

In a walk-up or house from around 1900, the floors are stacked but the walls rarely line up cleanly, and the cavities are full of old plumbing, gas lines and earlier wiring. Our first move is to look for a vertical path that already exists: a stack of closets one above the other, an old pipe chase that is now empty, or the space beside a dumbwaiter or a disused flue that can be sleeved without opening the flue itself. Where nothing like that exists, we plan a small number of sleeved holes through the floors in places that are hidden, and we firestop each one. In a building of this age we also scan before drilling every time, because what is inside the wall has usually been changed several times over a hundred years.

Third Avenue store buildings: the cellar is the hallway

In the store buildings that line Third Avenue, the cellar is where the provider's line usually arrives and where cable can travel the length of the building without touching the shop. The usual plan is to bring the service to a cabinet in the back room, then drop to the counter, the office and any cameras from below or above, depending on what the shop ceiling is made of and whether the owner wants to keep it untouched. When the floors above are apartments or offices with different occupants, their cable stays in its own path and the store's cabinet stays locked.

Industrial park buildings: shared bones, separate networks

Each park building is shared by tenants who do not share networks. The landlord's room is the starting point for everyone, and from there each tenant's cable needs its own identified route to its own space. We label everything at the shared end with the tenant's name and suite, use separate conduit or a clearly marked tray section where several tenants run in parallel, and leave the shared room tidier than we found it. Inside the tenant's space the work follows the unit's use: a dense cluster of drops in the office, ceiling cable for access points over the floor, and protected drops at docks and work stations.

New buildings: get the telecom room on the drawings

For the buildings filed at 1899 and 1904 Bathgate Avenue and the planned eight-unit building at 2088, the decisions that matter most for cabling are made long before anyone pulls a cable. A telecom room large enough for several providers' equipment, with power and ventilation; a riser with spare sleeves on every floor; a closet or panel location in each apartment and each commercial space; and conduit to the roof for future antennas or rooftop equipment. Once those are on the drawings, the cabling itself is routine. When they are left out, they are expensive to add later, and the first tenant who wants a provider the building was not prepared for will feel it.

Distribution tenants: design around the people who move

In a food distribution unit, the important devices are in workers' hands and on forklifts, not on desks. The cabling plan therefore starts with a wireless survey of the floor as it is actually used: where the racks stand, where pallets are staged, where the docks open and where metal doors and cold rooms block signal. Access points are placed to serve those spaces, and each gets a cable back to a switch that powers it. Fixed devices such as label printers, dock-door controllers and receiving computers get hard-wired drops on columns or walls, protected where traffic passes. The office part of the unit is cabled like any office, but it shares the same rack, so the whole operation has one place to troubleshoot.

Manufacturing tenants: keep production and office apart

A manufacturer's production equipment and its office computers should not share one flat network, and the separation is easiest when it starts with the cable. We run production drops to their own patch panel, use a different cable or jack color for them, and label them by machine or room. That makes it simple for the tenant's IT provider to put them on a separate network segment and hard for anyone to plug a laptop into the wrong port. In rooms that are cleaned with liquids or chemicals, we choose sealed faceplates, place jacks above the splash zone and route cable so it does not sit in a corner where water collects.

Schools and training centers: density and timing

Classrooms and training rooms need more outlets per square foot than almost any other space: a station per seat in a computer lab, an instructor position, a ceiling drop for a projector or display, and access points sized for a full room of devices at once. The closets serving those rooms fill up fast, so we size patch panels and racks with room to grow. Timing matters as much as layout. Work in occupied classrooms is scheduled for evenings, weekends or breaks, and we confirm the calendar with the school or program before committing to dates.

Garages and yards on the truck-route streets

The garages, yards and small industrial buildings between Webster and Third Avenues often need a connection somewhere other than the office: a booth at the entrance ramp, a gate controller, a camera over the yard, or a trailer used as a second office. Cable to those places has to cross open floors, driveways or yards. Indoors we run it high or in conduit along walls; outdoors we use cable rated for the conditions, in conduit, entering buildings through sealed fittings. When the distance is too long for copper or the run crosses between two separate buildings, a fiber link with a small switch at each end is the reliable choice.

Rules that apply to business cabling in Bathgate

  • The low-voltage permit exemption. Local Law 128 of 2024 added a provision to the city's Administrative Code stating that no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data.   • Fire alarm wiring is a separate trade. Life-safety wiring is outside that exemption, and we keep it out of data jobs entirely.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Ask for the Ceiling Before the Floor Plan

We do not publish stories about specific customers' buildings unless we can document them, so this is a routine, not an anecdote. It is what we ask a Bathgate business for before the first visit, and why.

When a new tenant calls about a unit in a building that has been in use since the industrial park opened in 1982, the floor plan is the least useful thing they can send. What we want first is a photo of the ceiling. A space that has had several occupants over four decades usually has cable from every one of them above it: old phone wire, earlier generations of network cable, coax from a camera system long gone, and runs that end in a coil because someone cut them when they moved out. None of it is labeled. Some of it may still be live, feeding the landlord's equipment or a neighboring suite. Pulling new cable into that tangle without understanding it is how a new network inherits an old fault.

So the first part of a Bathgate walk-through is often a tracing session. We put a tone on every run that ends at the old rack or closet and follow it to its other end. Live runs that serve someone else are marked and left alone. Runs that are dead are tagged for removal, because the electrical code the city adopted, based on the 2020 National Electrical Code, expects accessible abandoned communications cable to be taken out rather than left to build up. Runs that are live and useful are tested to see whether they can be kept. Only then do we write the drop schedule, and the schedule shows the removal as its own line so the tenant can see what it costs and why.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Bathgate: Questions Answered

These questions come from contractor FAQ pages on cable ratings, a Verizon customer forum thread about fiber in an older apartment building, and the kinds of buildings and tenants the public record places in Bathgate. Each answer is written for the building it describes.

Our food distribution unit in the industrial park has a walk-in cooler, and the scanners lose their connection inside it. Can an access point be cabled into a refrigerated room?

Usually yes, and it is often the only real fix, because an insulated cooler box with metal skins blocks most of the signal from an access point outside it. The access point inside needs to be a model rated for the room's temperature and moisture, and the cable has to enter through the cooler wall in a way that does not let warm, humid air follow it in. We bring the cable through a sealed fitting or a short sleeve that is packed and sealed on both faces, slope it so condensation runs away from the hole, and keep the run inside the cooler short. Before drilling any cooler panel we confirm with the tenant and the landlord who owns the box and whether the panel can be penetrated at all, since some coolers are leased equipment with their own rules.

Our production room gets wiped down with cleaning chemicals every shift. What kind of jacks and cable hold up there?

The weak points in a washed-down room are the open jack and the cable entry, not the cable inside the wall. We use faceplates with covers or gaskets that close when nothing is plugged in, place outlets higher than the splash zone where the equipment allows it, and bring cable down inside conduit or a sealed raceway so liquid cannot run along it into the wall. Where a device has to connect at floor level, we use a sealed industrial connector rather than an ordinary office jack. We also ask the tenant for the cleaning products in use, because some solvents attack ordinary cable jackets over time, and we choose the jacket and the patch cords with that in mind.

Our training center moves its tables around for every class. How do you cable a room whose layout keeps changing?

We stop trying to cable the furniture and cable the room instead. That means outlets spread along the walls at regular intervals rather than clustered where today's tables happen to sit, a ceiling drop above the likely teaching wall for the projector or display, and one or two access points sized for every seat being occupied with a laptop and a phone. For rooms that switch between lecture and lab layouts, floor boxes or ceiling-fed power and data poles at fixed points give flexibility without cords crossing walkways. The patch panel is labeled by wall and position so a staff member can see which outlet is which after the room has been rearranged ten times.

Our office on Third Avenue has a suspended ceiling. Does every cable above it have to be plenum-rated?

Not automatically. Plenum-rated cable is required where the space above the ceiling is used to move air for the heating and cooling system, which is common in commercial buildings but not universal. If supply or return air travels through that ceiling space, the cable there needs the plenum rating, which uses a jacket that makes less smoke and burns less readily. If the ceiling is only a cover and the air moves through ducts, a riser-rated or general-purpose cable can be acceptable. We look above the tiles during the walk-through, check how the air system works, and list the jacket rating for each run on the drop schedule so the choice is visible rather than assumed.

On a new Bathgate Avenue building, how will the inspector know the cable inside the walls has the right fire rating?

Network cable carries its rating printed on the jacket at regular intervals, along with the manufacturer and the cable category, so an inspector at rough-in can read it directly off the exposed lengths. Common markings are CMP for plenum, CMR for riser and CM for general use. We keep the cable for each space in its correct rating, keep the spools and boxes on site until inspection so the labels can be checked, and leave enough of each run exposed at the closet and the outlet boxes to be read. Firestopped penetrations are photographed as they are completed, before anything covers them, so the general contractor has a record if the question comes up after the walls close.

The Fios installer says our old walk-up on Bathgate Avenue needs holes drilled through the hallway, and the owner said no. Is there another way in?

Often there is, but it has to be found by looking at the building rather than at the provider's standard method. Options include an unused conduit or chase from the cellar, a route up through stacked closets inside the apartments, the path an earlier provider already uses if there is space beside it, or a single riser the owner agrees to once so that every provider can share it instead of drilling a new hole for each tenant. A provider's installer works to a time limit and a standard method, so they tend to propose the most direct path. An owner who asks us to plan a shared route can usually offer providers something better than the hallway, and the building ends up with fewer holes over time.

Our new building will have stores on the ground floor and apartments above. Should the stores share the building's telecom room?

They can share the room, but not the network or the locks. The usual design is one telecom room where providers bring their lines into the building, with separate, labeled pathways out of it: a riser to the apartment floors and a separate route to each commercial space. Each store then has its own small cabinet inside its own premises, where its provider's equipment and its own network live under its own key. That keeps the residential and commercial sides from depending on each other, lets a store change providers without entering the apartment risers, and gives the building owner one place to manage every provider's entry. The filing at 1899 Bathgate Avenue, with apartments over about 4,700 square feet of commercial space, is the kind of building where this split should be on the drawings early.

Our building backs onto the Cross Bronx Expressway. Do traffic vibration and road grime shorten the life of the cable?

Cable inside walls and ceilings is not harmed by the kind of vibration a building feels from traffic, as long as it is supported properly and not stretched tight between fixed points. The parts that can suffer are connections and anything exposed. We leave a small service loop at each end so the cable never pulls on a jack, use cable supports that hold without crushing, and make sure rack equipment is mounted firmly rather than resting on a shelf. Dust and grime are a bigger practical issue for equipment than for cable, so a closed cabinet with filtered ventilation is a better choice than an open rack in a room with windows facing the highway. Exterior runs on that side of a building get conduit and sealed entries.

Our affordable building may get free broadband through a city program that puts equipment on the roof. What does that leave for the owner to cable?

In May 2026, HPD and Rep. Ritchie Torres announced a Neighborhood Internet initiative for more than 2,000 units in dozens of South Bronx buildings, describing microtrenching, rooftop network equipment, dedicated internet access infrastructure and in-unit connectivity. We cannot speak for what any city program will install in a particular building, and an owner should get that scope in writing from the program. What an owner can do in the meantime is make the building easy to serve: a clear path from the roof to each floor, a riser or sleeves with room for new cable, power where equipment will need it, and a closet or panel location in each apartment. Anything the program does not cover inside the building can then be added cleanly rather than improvised.

Our store on Third Avenue still has a pressed-metal ceiling we want to keep. How does cable reach the counter without cutting it?

Usually from below. In a store building with a cellar, the cable can travel along the cellar ceiling and come up through the floor directly under the counter or inside a wall, so nothing touches the ceiling at all. Where the counter sits in a spot that cannot be reached from below, a slim surface raceway painted to match the wall can bring cable down from the back room, and the ceiling stays untouched. If a ceiling-mounted device such as a camera or access point is truly needed, we look for an existing opening, a light fixture or a seam that can be used, and we explain exactly where the hole would be before making it.

We bought one of the 1901 houses on Bathgate Avenue, and it has no network cable at all. Where does a whole-house plan start?

It starts where the provider's line enters, usually a box on the front wall or a spot in the cellar, and with a decision about where the small network enclosure should live. In a house of that age, a closet near the middle of the house on the first floor or a dry, clean corner of the cellar is typical. From there we look for the vertical paths the house offers, often stacked closets or a former pipe chase, and plan drops to the rooms that matter most: a home office, the main television, and one or two access points placed high and central on each floor. The plan lists each hole, how it is sealed, and what it costs, so the work can be done all at once or in stages as rooms are renovated.

The only space for equipment in our walk-up is a corner of the cellar. Should it be a wall cabinet or a floor rack?

For most walk-ups, a wall-mounted cabinet. It keeps the equipment off a floor that may see water from a leak or a backed-up drain, closes and locks so the super and the tenants' providers cannot disturb each other's gear, and needs very little floor space. A floor-standing rack makes sense when the building has many apartments feeding one point, when providers need room for their own equipment beside the building's, or when a store or office in the building shares the same space. On our price list a wall-mount cabinet is $250 and a floor-standing rack is $450, and either one is mounted high enough to stay dry and away from the boiler, the gas meter and the main electrical panel.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Bathgate

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for Bathgate from our build environment, so we are not quoting one. These are claims that come up often in quick online answers about cabling and broadband, tested against the public record for Bathgate and against how the work is actually done.

"Bathgate is a residential neighborhood, so cabling there means home Wi-Fi."

Much of Bathgate is residential, but its center is not. Wikipedia describes Bathgate Industrial Park as seven city blocks and about 454,000 square feet in eight buildings, used for light industry, distribution, offices and schools.

Those tenants, a drug manufacturer, food distributors and training centers among them, need commercial cabling: racks, patch panels, certified runs, access points across open floors and backbones between closets. A home-network answer does not describe their buildings at all.

The residential side is varied too, from 1899 and 1901 houses to a 379-unit building finished in 2018. A useful answer about cabling in Bathgate has to start by asking which of those buildings you are in.

"Fiber is available across the Bronx, so every building already has it."

FCC-based data summarized by BestNeighborhood for Tremont shows Verizon fiber at about 93 percent availability and Optimum fiber at about 71 percent. That measures whether a provider serves the area, not whether a fiber line runs into your particular building and up to your floor.

Inside an older building, the provider still needs a path from the street to each unit, and as a Verizon forum thread shows, that path can stall when the installer wants to drill a hallway and the owner refuses. In a multi-tenant industrial building, the line may end in a shared room far from the tenant who needs it.

The gap is real at the household level as well. At the May 2026 Neighborhood Internet announcement, HPD Commissioner Dina Levy said forty percent of Bronx households do not have home internet access. Availability on a map is the beginning of the job, not the end.

"Any network cabling in New York City needs an electrical permit."

Local Law 128 of 2024 added a provision to the city's Administrative Code stating that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, and a Bronx low-voltage resource gives December 21, 2025 as the effective date.

That does not make the work unregulated. Fire alarm wiring is outside the exemption, new power circuits still need a licensed electrician and a permit, and penetrations of rated walls and floors still have to be firestopped to keep the building's fire separations intact.

New buildings add another layer: the general contractor's schedule, inspections at rough-in, and the cable ratings an inspector will read off the jacket. Skipping the permit is not the same as skipping the rules.

"Wi-Fi mesh has made cable unnecessary, even in a warehouse."

Mesh systems pass traffic from one wireless unit to the next over the air, which works in an apartment but struggles across a distribution floor full of racking, stacked product, metal doors and cold rooms. Every wireless hop shares the same air with the scanners it is supposed to serve.

In a working warehouse, the access points that serve the aisles and docks need their own cable back to a switch, both for a stable connection and for power. That is why a warehouse Wi-Fi project is mostly a cabling project.

Fixed devices such as label printers, dock controllers and receiving computers belong on wired drops anyway. Wireless is for the things that move; cable is for everything that stays put and for the access points themselves.

"New buildings come fully wired, so there is nothing to plan."

The new buildings on Bathgate Avenue, such as the 213-apartment project filed at 1904 and the mixed-use building filed at 1899, will be wired, but the quality of that wiring depends on decisions made early: how large the telecom room is, how many sleeves the riser has, and whether each home and store has a proper panel location.

If those decisions are left to the last trade on site, the result is often a cramped closet, a single provider's equipment in the only available space, and no room for the next provider a tenant asks for. Fixing that after the walls close costs far more than drawing it correctly.

Commercial tenants in the ground-floor space of a new building also usually receive a shell with no internal cabling at all. Their fit-out is a separate job that should still connect cleanly to the building's telecom room.

"Old cable above the ceiling can just stay there."

Industrial park units that have been leased since the 1980s often have decades of cable from former tenants above their ceilings, and quick answers rarely mention it. Leaving it in place adds fuel and smoke to a fire, crowds the space new cable needs, and makes every future fault harder to trace.

The city's 2025 Electrical Code is based on the 2020 National Electrical Code, which expects the accessible portion of abandoned communications cable to be removed. We trace what is live before anything comes out, so nobody's working connection is cut by mistake.

Removal appears on our drop schedule as its own line, so the tenant and the landlord can see what is being taken out and why.

"Cat6A is required for every new installation."

Cat6A is a strong choice for access points, high-speed links and new-building backbones, and we recommend it in those places. But a cashier's register, a label printer or a desk phone gets nothing extra from it, and its thicker jacket takes more space in conduit and trays.

Most Bathgate jobs end up with a mix: Cat6 at $175 a drop for ordinary devices and Cat6A at $250 a drop, or $300 to a ceiling access point, where the speed or the power load justifies it.

The right answer comes from the device list and the distances, which is why our drop schedule names the cable for each outlet rather than applying one category to the whole building.

FAQ

Bathgate Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Why does our quote show an extra charge on each drop for plenum cable?
+

Plenum cable uses a jacket made from materials that produce less smoke and spread flame less readily, and those materials cost more than the jacket on riser or general-purpose cable. It is required only where the cable passes through a ceiling or floor space that carries air for the heating and cooling system. On our Bronx price list the upgrade is $40 per drop, and the drop schedule shows it only on the runs that actually cross an air-handling space, so an office whose ceiling is not a return plenum does not pay it on every outlet.

Is riser-rated cable acceptable inside a two-family house on Claremont Parkway?
+

Yes. Riser-rated cable, marked CMR, is a higher fire rating than the general-purpose cable commonly used in houses, so it can go anywhere general-purpose cable is allowed, including the walls, floors and basement of a two-family house. Where a run passes vertically between floors through a shaft, riser cable is the right choice anyway. The only place in a house where it would not be enough is a space that moves air for the heating system, such as a return duct, and network cable should not be run inside ductwork in any case.

We are powering access points and cameras over their cables. Can a big bundle of powered cables get too warm in the ceiling?
+

It can if the bundle is large, tightly packed and enclosed, because each powered cable gives off a little heat and a tight bundle holds it in. We manage that by keeping bundles to moderate sizes, using J-hooks and open tray rather than stuffing cable into a small conduit, choosing Cat6A for the higher-power devices because its heavier conductors run cooler, and avoiding hot spaces such as the area just under an uninsulated roof deck. The drop schedule notes which runs carry power so the routing can be planned with that in mind.

Our business uses two separate buildings across a yard. How do we connect their networks?
+

The dependable way is a fiber link between a small rack or cabinet in each building. Fiber does not carry electrical current, so it avoids the grounding differences and surge risks that come with running copper between separate structures, and it handles distances well beyond copper's limits. Outdoors it runs in conduit, either underground or attached to a structure, and enters each building through a sealed fitting. Each end lands on a small panel and a switch, and the link is tested and labeled like any other run. Where the landlord controls the yard, the route is agreed before any digging or mounting.

Our office is on a dusty mezzanine above the warehouse floor. Where should the network equipment go?
+

Inside a closed cabinet rather than on an open rack. Dust from a working warehouse settles into switch fans and vents, and over time it causes overheating and failures. A closed, lockable cabinet with filtered ventilation, mounted on a wall away from the stair and the edge of the mezzanine, keeps the equipment cleaner and out of the way of anyone carrying boxes. We plan the cable from the floor below to come up in conduit at the cabinet, and we leave space and spare ports for the access points and devices the floor will add later.

The last tenant left Cat5e cable in our unit. Is any of it worth keeping?
+

Sometimes. We trace each run to both ends and test it before deciding. Cat5e that passes its tests, ends in sensible places and is not damaged can still serve devices such as printers, phones and card readers that do not need high speeds. Runs that fail, end in the wrong rooms, cross the space in a way that blocks new work or cannot be identified are better removed. The drop schedule shows which existing runs we propose to keep, which to remove and which to replace, so you can see what reusing the old cable saves and what it gives up.

We only need about 20 drops today. Does a 48-port patch panel still make sense?
+

Often it does. Businesses in Bathgate's industrial buildings tend to add cameras, access points, card readers and stations as they grow, and each one takes a port. Our network closet has no air conditioning and the switch runs hot in summer. What can be done?
+

First we look at what is creating the heat and how air moves in the closet. A switch powering many access points and cameras produces much more heat than one that only carries data. Common fixes are a vented door or a louvered panel, a small thermostat-controlled exhaust fan that moves air out of the closet, reorganizing the rack so equipment is not stacked with no gaps, and moving the battery backup and other heat sources away from the switch. If the closet still cannot stay cool, moving the equipment to a better-ventilated spot is often cheaper than adding cooling.

What happens if a drop fails its test after the cable is already in the wall?
+

We fix it before we leave. Most failures come from the termination at one end, so the first step is to re-terminate the jack or the panel position and test again. If the run still fails, the tester usually shows roughly where along the cable the problem is, which tells us whether it was damaged by a staple, a sharp edge or a tight bend. A damaged run is replaced with a new continuous cable rather than spliced. The final test result for every run, including any that were repaired, goes into the records you receive.
