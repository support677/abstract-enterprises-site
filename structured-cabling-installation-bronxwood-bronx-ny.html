The Neighborhood

Why Bronxwood Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Bronxwood packs houses from at least five different decades, small pre-war apartment buildings, two big housing developments and a long run of storefronts into a few dozen blocks, and almost none of those buildings were designed for a home or business network. That is the practical reason cabling here takes planning. A 1910 house, a 1950 house and a 1965 house can stand on the same stretch of Bronxwood Avenue, and each one hides its cable paths in a different place.

Start with the name, because it decides what we mean by "Bronxwood" on this page. Wikipedia describes Bronxwood as the southern part of Allerton and counts the Pelham Parkway Houses and Boston Road Plaza inside it. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws a box bounded by Pelham Parkway on the south, Bronx Park East on the west, Waring Avenue on the north and Williamsbridge Road on the east. Forgotten NY uses the name for Bronxwood Park, a tiny enclave of two curving roads, North and South Oak Drives, between Wallace and Cruger Avenues just north of Burke Avenue. Listings and buildings along Bronxwood Avenue itself, which runs north past Gun Hill Road toward Williamsbridge, are tagged Bronxwood, Allerton, Williamsbridge or Laconia depending on who wrote them. We serve all of it, and we would rather name the three definitions than pretend there is one official map. The Bronx Times reported in 2014 that the Department of City Planning itself said there are no official neighborhood boundaries in New York City.

The building records explain why one cabling method never fits the whole area. StreetEasy's records for five houses on Bronxwood Avenue list two-story, two-family houses built in 1910, 1925, 1950, 1950 and 1965. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes the internal grid around Bronxwood, Paulding and Barnes Avenues as mostly low-rise brick homes, two-family houses and small apartment buildings, and its profile of neighboring Laconia, across Williamsbridge Road, describes detached brick houses, two- and three-family houses and low-rise apartments built mostly between the 1920s and the 1950s. Wikipedia notes that tenements are scattered through the area, mostly within five blocks of the White Plains Road el. The Pelham Parkway Houses are 23 six-story brick buildings from 1950 with about 1,266 apartments, and Boston Road Plaza at 2440 Boston Road is a 20-story senior tower from 1972. In the space of a ten-minute walk, a cabling crew meets wood framing with plaster on lath, brick bearing walls, poured foundations, finished cellars, and institutional masonry, each with its own rules about where a cable may go.

The households inside those buildings are not all the same kind of customer either. Census Reporter's 2024 five-year profiles show how sharply the area changes across Bronxwood Avenue, which Wikipedia gives as the line between ZIP 10467 to the west and 10469 to the east: in 10467 only about 8 percent of homes are single-unit structures and about 14 percent are owner-occupied, while in 10469 about 46 percent are single-unit and about 51 percent are owner-occupied. West of the avenue, more of the people asking us for cable are tenants and building owners; east of it, more are homeowners who live in one half of a two-family and rent the other. That changes who approves the work, who pays for it, and how the network has to be split so two households can share a building without sharing a connection.

Finally, the commercial side has its own pressure. News 12 reported in December 2025 that the Allerton International Merchants Association counts more than 200 businesses along 15 blocks of Allerton Avenue from Laconia Avenue to Bronx Park East. White Plains Road runs under the el with stations at Pelham Parkway, Allerton Avenue, Burke Avenue and Gun Hill Road, and Gun Hill Road and Boston Road carry their own strips of shops, offices and clinics. A card reader that drops off Wi-Fi during the lunch rush, a pharmacy label printer that cannot find its computer, or a practice that loses its online records when a router overheats behind the counter: those are cabling problems, and on these corridors they are common because so many of the spaces were fitted out one tenant at a time.

Bronxwood facts that shape a cabling plan

  • Five decades on one avenue: two-family houses on Bronxwood Avenue recorded as built in 1910, 1925, 1950 and 1965, each era hiding its cable paths differently.
  • Two housing markets split by one street: about 8 percent single-unit and 14 percent owner-occupied in ZIP 10467 against about 46 and 51 percent in 10469.
  • Big developments with their own rules: 23 six-story NYCHA buildings at the Pelham Parkway Houses and a 20-story senior tower at 2440 Boston Road, now under private PACT management.
  • A dense small-business strip: more than 200 businesses across 15 blocks of Allerton Avenue, plus shops under the White Plains Road el and along Gun Hill and Boston Roads.
  • New construction arriving: permits filed in March 2025 for an eight-story, 55-apartment mixed-use building with 4,180 square feet of commercial space at 3006 Williamsbridge Road.
  • Fiber on most blocks, but not in every building: BestNeighborhood lists Verizon fiber at about 99.6 percent and Optimum fiber at about 81 percent availability in Williamsbridge, which says nothing about whether a given cellar has been connected.

Reading the House

1910, 1925, 1950, 1965: How the Build Year of a Bronxwood House Decides Where Cable Can Travel

The single most useful thing a Bronxwood homeowner can tell us before a walk-through is the year the house went up. It is printed on most listings and on the city's property records, and it predicts, better than anything else we can learn over the phone, where the hollow spaces are, what the walls are made of, and how hard it will be to get a cable from the cellar to a second-floor bedroom. The records for Bronxwood Avenue alone run from 1910 to 1965. Below is how we read those eras. These are tendencies drawn from houses of each age, not promises about yours; the walk-through is where we confirm them.

The 1910 house: generous cavities, delicate plaster

A house from the first decade of the last century is, oddly, often the easiest place to hide a cable. Many were framed so that the stud bays in the outside walls run without interruption from the cellar to the roof space, which means a cable dropped from the attic or pushed up from the cellar can reach the second floor without opening a single wall. The difficulty is everything that has happened since. A hundred years of owners add up: insulation blown into some bays, a bathroom moved, a blocking board nailed across a cavity during a repair, an electrician's new circuit running exactly where we wanted to go. Some houses of this age still have sections of old knob-and-tube wiring in the walls, which we treat as live until proven otherwise and keep well away from. And plaster on wood lath cracks if it is cut carelessly, so every new box goes in through a small, scored opening, and we check the bay with an inspection camera before we commit to it.

The 1925 house: closets, stairs and the porch problem

By the 1920s, Bronxwood houses tend to have more regular framing and a firebreak at each floor, so cavities no longer run straight from top to bottom. The routes we rely on shift to the places a house naturally stacks: a closet on the first floor directly below a closet on the second, the wall that encloses the staircase, or a chase beside a chimney that is no longer in use. Many houses of this period also gained enclosed porches or front rooms built outside the original exterior wall. A home office in one of those rooms is a common request, and it is a different job, because the cable has to pass through what used to be an outside wall, usually brick or a thick framed wall, and the porch room itself often sits over a crawlspace rather than the cellar. We plan that crossing first, because it decides whether the rest of the run is simple.

The 1950 house: masonry outside, finished cellar below

Two of the five Bronxwood Avenue records we found are from 1950, the same year the Pelham Parkway Houses were finished a few blocks south. Houses of this period are often brick or block on the outside, and that solid outer wall simply cannot be fished. Cable has to travel in the interior partitions, the closets, and any soffit or dropped section of ceiling. The other common feature is a cellar that a later owner finished, with a plaster or drywall ceiling covering the joists we would normally use as a highway from the front of the house to the back. A finished ceiling does not stop the job; it changes the route. We look for a utility room left unfinished, the space above a stair, a run along the top of a foundation wall behind a soffit, or, where nothing else works, a short, neat surface raceway in a place nobody looks.

Where Service Enters

Copper, Coax or Fiber: What Actually Arrives at a Bronxwood Cellar Wall

A structured cabling plan begins at the demarcation point, the place where the provider's equipment ends and the building's own wiring begins. In a Bronxwood two-family that is usually a gray box or a coax splitter on the cellar wall near the front of the house. In a walk-up it is a panel in the cellar or under the stairs, shared by every apartment. In a store it is wherever the provider's installer found a convenient spot, which is often behind the counter or in a back closet beside the electric meters. Before we plan a single jack, we find that point, look at what arrives there, and test it.

Provider maps make Bronxwood look well served. BestNeighborhood's availability figures for Williamsbridge, the area just north of Gun Hill Road, list Verizon fiber at about 99.6 percent of addresses, Optimum's fiber service at about 81 percent, Optimum's cable service at about 99.7 percent, and fixed wireless options from T-Mobile, Verizon and Starry at varying levels. BroadbandNow puts fiber availability across the whole Bronx at about 84.7 percent. Those figures are useful, but they describe whether a provider says it can serve an address, not whether a line has actually been brought into a particular cellar. In multi-family buildings, a provider usually needs the owner's permission to run its equipment inside, and a building that has never invited one in can sit on a fully mapped block with nothing but old coax.

NYCHA and senior-building apartments

The Pelham Parkway Houses and Boston Road Plaza are a different case. NYCHA's house rules limit what residents may alter, and Boston Road Plaza has been under private PACT management since September 2024, so its rules come from that manager. NYCHA's Big Apple Connect program provides residents of participating developments with free internet, including a modem and router, basic cable and common-area Wi-Fi hotspots; we could not confirm which Bronxwood buildings take part, so ask your management office. Inside a public-housing apartment we only do work the house rules or management allow, which usually means surface-mounted cable in raceway from the provider's equipment to a desk or screen, with nothing cut into walls, doors or frames.

The cable itself is the same from one Bronxwood building to the next. What changes is who controls the building, where the provider's line arrives, and which hollow spaces exist between the cellar and the rooms that need a connection. These are the building types the public record describes in and around Bronxwood, and what each one means for a cabling plan.

Two-story houses where the owner lives on one floor and rents the other, with one cellar, one front door and, increasingly, two internet accounts that need to stay apart.

Taller houses and small mixed buildings with a store at street level, where a business network and a household network share one cellar.

Apartment buildings on Bronx Park East and along Bronxwood Avenue, where the board or managing agent approves any work in common areas and shareholders pay for their own apartments.

Owner-and-tenant two-families: two households, one cellar

East of Bronxwood Avenue, where about half of homes are owner-occupied, the most common job is a house where the owner lives downstairs and rents the upstairs, or the other way round. Both households want reliable internet, and very often they now pay different providers. The plan has to respect that. Each apartment gets its own runs, landing on its own labeled panel or its own clearly separated section of one enclosure, with no shared switch that would let one household's equipment see the other's. If the owner wants to provide internet as part of the rent, we can build that too, but we keep it on its own network and say plainly who is responsible for it. The physical route is usually the stack of closets or the wall beside the stairs, because those are the only places both floors share.

Frame houses on the Oak Drives: siding outside, framing inside

The houses in Bronxwood Park, which Forgotten NY describes as many original multiroom buildings that typically housed two families and that have long since been aluminum-sided, are framed houses under their siding. That is good news for cabling, because the inside walls and many outside walls are hollow. What the siding changes is anything that has to go outside: a cable to a camera, to a detached workshop, or in from a provider's line. Aluminum siding dents and can carry a sharp edge where it is cut, so we plan as few outside penetrations as possible, put each one where water runs away from it, and use a proper sealed fitting rather than a hole and a blob of caulk. WFUV's piece on the enclave mentions its gated driveways and large porches, and the porch is often where the provider's line comes in, which we take into account when choosing where the network starts.

Walk-ups near the el: one stairwell, many tenants

In a small pre-war building near White Plains Road, every apartment's service eventually comes up from the same cellar. Over the years, each provider has run its own cable up the outside of the building, up the stairwell, or through whatever hole the last installer found. The result is a stairwell wall covered in staples and a cellar where nobody knows which wire feeds which apartment. A riser fixes that: one protected, supported pathway from the cellar to each floor, with room for more cable later, and a labeled panel at the bottom. Tenants still choose their own providers; the providers simply use the building's pathway instead of making a new one each time. For an owner, that also makes the building easier to rent, because a new tenant can get service without another hole in the hallway.

New mid-rise buildings: plan the telecom room before the drywall

New York YIMBY reported in March 2025 that permits were filed for an eight-story, 73-foot building at 3006 Williamsbridge Road, between Barnes and Wallace Avenues, with 55 residences averaging about 703 square feet, 4,180 square feet of commercial space and two enclosed parking spaces. YIMBY has reported other recent filings nearby on Holland Avenue, Adee Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. Buildings like that are where structured cabling pays back the most, because the pathways cost little while the walls are open and a great deal once they are closed. The questions we work through with the architect and general contractor are concrete: where the telecom room goes and how big it is, how fiber enters from the street, how each apartment's wiring panel is placed, what the storefront needs on day one, and what the lobby entry system, cameras and elevator will need from the same pathways.

Allerton Avenue storefronts: the counter is the network

In a typical Allerton Avenue shop, the network is a router on a shelf behind the register, a card reader or two on Wi-Fi, a receipt printer, an office computer in the back and a camera recorder somewhere it fits. Everything depends on that one router, and that router usually sits where the provider's installer first came in, often close to the front of the store. The fix is not complicated. The provider's line is carried to a small locked cabinet at the back of the store, every fixed device at the counter gets its own cable, and Wi-Fi is left for the things that move. A receipt printer on a cable stops disappearing. A card reader on a cable stops dropping off when the store fills up with phones. And the equipment that runs the store is no longer the first thing anyone sees through the window.

Rules that apply to business cabling in Bronxwood

New York City's Local Law 128 of 2024 added Section 28-105.4.7 to the Administrative Code: an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring that operates at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. The same section excludes life-safety systems and any connection to control circuits that switch light, heat or power, so fire alarm wiring and anything that turns equipment on and off stay with licensed specialists. New York State separately requires a license from the Department of State to install or service security and fire alarm systems; ours is NYS license #12000287431. Inside commercial ceilings that serve as air plenums, cable must carry a plenum rating, and in vertical shafts between floors it must be at least riser-rated. Where a new outlet is needed for a cabinet, a licensed electrician installs it. None of this is legal advice, and a building's own lease or board rules can be stricter than the code.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Laptop on the Cellar Floor

This is a routine, not a customer story. We do not publish stories about specific Bronxwood houses unless we can document them, but this is the first thing we do in every one.

Before we look at a single wall, the technician goes down to the cellar, finds the provider's equipment and plugs a laptop straight into it with a short cable. No router, no switch, no Wi-Fi in between. We run a speed and stability test there, then walk upstairs and run the same test on the household's own devices in the rooms where people complain. If the cellar result is poor, the problem starts with the provider, and we say so before we quote anything. In a two-family, we do this for each household's service separately, because two modems on the same wall can behave completely differently. In a shop, we do it at the counter and in the back office. The numbers go into the plan, so that after the job is finished the owner can compare the same test in the same rooms and see what changed. It takes about fifteen minutes, it can stop a homeowner from paying for cable that would never have fixed a provider fault, and it gives every Bronxwood plan a measured starting point instead of a guess.

Our two-family on Bronxwood Avenue dates from 1910 and still has some knob-and-tube wiring in the walls. Is it safe to fish network cable through the same cavities?

We avoid it. Old knob-and-tube circuits rely on air around the conductors and on porcelain insulators that crack with age, and nobody can tell from the outside of a wall whether a run is still live. Pushing a new cable past it risks disturbing insulation that is already fragile. So on the walk-through we look into each candidate bay with an inspection camera, test for voltage, and mark every bay that still holds old wiring as off limits. In a house of that age there are usually other bays, closets or a stair wall that can carry the cable instead. If the old wiring turns up exactly where the plan needs to go, we tell you, and the sensible order is for a licensed electrician to retire that circuit before we use the space.

The cellar ceiling in our 1950 brick two-family is finished plaster rather than open joists. How does a cable cross from the front of the house to the back?

A finished cellar ceiling closes the usual highway, but houses of that age almost always leave a gap somewhere. We look first for the boiler or utility room, which is often left unfinished, then for a soffit along a beam or duct, the space above the cellar stair, and the line where the foundation wall meets the ceiling, which sometimes hides a run behind trim. If none of those reach, the next choices are a short crossing through the first-floor framing from above, usually inside a closet, or a neat painted raceway along a cellar wall where it will not be noticed. Opening the plaster ceiling is a last resort, and if it is needed we say exactly where and how large before you agree to it.

I work from home and my employer requires the work laptop to be plugged into a wired connection. What should that one drop include?

A run from the network enclosure to a wall jack at the desk, certified end to end, with a second cable in the same path for a dock, a desk phone or a printer, since pulling two costs very little more than pulling one. The run should land on its own labeled port on the switch, so if the employer's IT team asks you to test something you can say which cable it is. We also check that the router and switch the drop connects to are not the weak link, because a gigabit jack plugged into an old, slow router will not satisfy an IT department. We do not configure the employer's laptop or its security software; that stays with your employer's support desk.

We are switching from Optimum's coaxial service to its fiber service. Does anything inside our house have to change?

The provider changes the outside line and the box on the wall. A fiber service usually ends at a small optical terminal that needs a power outlet, and from there everything is Ethernet. If your rooms are currently fed by coax from a splitter, those coax runs stop being useful for internet, so the switch is a natural moment to run Ethernet from the terminal to an enclosure and from there to the rooms. If your house already has Ethernet runs, the job may be as small as connecting the new terminal to the existing panel and testing every run. BestNeighborhood lists Optimum's fiber at about 81 percent of Williamsbridge addresses, so it is worth checking whether your own address qualifies before planning around it.

Bronxwood Park and the side streets

Our house in Bronxwood Park sits on one of the curving Oak Drives under big old trees. Does the tree canopy matter for the line that feeds the house?

It can. Forgotten NY describes the Oak Drives as curving roads lined mainly with chestnut and oak trees, and where the provider's line reaches a house through the air, branches rubbing against it over the years can wear the jacket and cause intermittent faults. That line belongs to the provider, so the fix for a damaged drop is a call to them, and tree work near utility lines belongs to the tree service and the utility. What we can do is test the service where it enters the house, so you know whether drops in the evening are the line outside or the wiring inside. If the provider offers a different entry, such as a line along the side of the house instead of across the yard, we plan the inside network around wherever the new entry lands.

We run a small daycare from our two-family off Gun Hill Road. Where can the network equipment and cables go so children cannot reach them?

Everything that carries power or blinks goes up high and behind a door. The enclosure mounts in a closet or cellar room children do not use, with the door locked if possible. In the rooms children use, jacks go above counter height or behind furniture that is fixed in place, and cable never runs loose along the floor. Access points mount on the ceiling, out of reach. Any camera you use to watch the rooms gets a wired run so it does not depend on Wi-Fi. If you are licensed as a child care provider, follow your licensing agency's rules on equipment and safety in program spaces; we design the cabling to fit those rules once you tell us what they are, and we schedule work for hours when no children are present.

We are about to look at two-family houses for sale on Paulding Avenue. What should we notice about the wiring during a showing?

Look in the cellar near the front wall. Count the boxes: one provider box per apartment is normal, a cluster of dead boxes and old splitters is a sign of a cleanup job. Look at the cellar ceiling: open joists make cabling easy, a finished ceiling makes it slower. Check whether any rooms have data jacks, and whether they say Cat5e, Cat6 or nothing on the cable behind them. Note whether the stairs and closets line up between floors, because that is where cable will climb. And ask the sellers whether either apartment already has fiber. None of this replaces a walk-through after you buy, but it gives you a realistic sense of whether the house will need a weekend of cabling or a single visit.

Businesses on Allerton Avenue, White Plains Road, Gun Hill Road and Boston Road

The front glass of our Allerton Avenue store is boarded up after damage. How do we keep the card readers and the camera recorder working while the storefront is repaired?

News 12 reported that the October 2026 crash at Allerton and Holland Avenues blew out nearby storefront windows, and a damaged front is exactly when a store learns how close its network sits to the street. First, check whether the router and provider box survived and are dry. If they did, the quickest fix is temporary: move the equipment to a safe spot at the back on a short cable and put the card readers on wired connections at the counter. During the repair, the right permanent change is to carry the provider's line to a small locked cabinet at the rear of the store, so the next problem at the front of the shop does not take the network with it. We coordinate with the glazier or contractor so our cable is in before any new wall or ceiling is closed.

How many cables does each workstation at a pharmacy counter on White Plains Road actually need?

Count the fixed devices at each station and add one. A typical counter station has a computer, a label printer and a card terminal, and often a scanner that connects through the computer and a desk phone. That usually means three or four cables per station, terminated on a two- or four-port faceplate under the counter, plus a spare. The back office, the drop-off window and any consultation area each get their own drops, and the camera recorder and security panel get theirs too. Wiring everything rather than relying on Wi-Fi matters most for printers and card terminals, which are the devices most likely to fail at the busiest moment. Your pharmacy software vendor will tell you if they need anything specific, and we build to that.

Our practice on East Gun Hill Road keeps its records online. How do we keep seeing patients if the internet line goes down?

By having a second way out. The cabling side is simple: a separate cable from the enclosure to wherever a backup connection works best, which for a fixed-wireless or cellular receiver is often an upper window or an outside wall, and a firewall or router that can switch to the backup automatically. The second connection should come from a different provider using a different technology, so the same fault does not take out both. Wired workstations keep working through the switch-over, and a battery backup in the enclosure keeps the firewall and switch running through a short power cut. Test the failover after installation by unplugging the main line, and repeat that test every few months. Your records vendor can confirm what speed the backup needs to carry.

We just leased the storefront next to ours on Boston Road and opened the wall between them. How do the two old networks become one?

We start by tracing and testing both sides, because each store probably has its own provider line, its own router and its own collection of unlabeled cable. Then you choose which provider line stays as the main service, and whether the other is cancelled or kept as a backup. One enclosure, usually in the back room of whichever space has the better location, becomes the center of the combined store. Cables that pass testing are re-terminated on its patch panel; cables that do not are replaced. The counter, the office and any kitchen are re-mapped so every device has one labeled cable to one switch. If the landlord still treats the two units as separate leases, we ask the landlord before running cable through the party wall.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Bronxwood

Quick AI answers about cabling are often right in general and wrong for Bronxwood's century-old two-families, pre-war walk-ups and new construction. Here are the common claims and what we find in practice.

"Structured cabling costs about $150 to $300 per drop."

Published 2026 guides do quote ranges like that, and a contributor on Reddit's r/networking said their company typically pays around $300 per Cat6 drop, terminated and certified. Those figures come from commercial jobs with open ceilings, dozens of drops in one visit and easy access to every endpoint.

A Bronxwood house is the opposite case. A job is often four to ten runs, which means the visit, the setup and the testing are spread over very few drops, and the same guides admit that small jobs carry minimums. Fishing a cable through plaster on lath in a 1910 house, or around a finished cellar ceiling in a 1950 one, takes longer than dropping it through a ceiling tile.

That is why we do not print a per-drop number for Bronxwood. Two runs that look identical on paper can differ by an hour of labor each. The honest price comes from a walk-through and a written plan that prices each run on its own route.

"Any cabling job in New York City needs an electrical permit."

Not since Local Law 128 of 2024. Its new Administrative Code Section 28-105.4.7 says no electrical permit is required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, when the installer is a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person.

The exemption has limits that summaries often leave out. It does not cover life-safety systems, and it does not cover connections to control circuits that switch light, heat or power. A new outlet for a network cabinet is ordinary electrical work and still needs a licensed electrician.

Separately, New York State requires a Department of State license to install or service security and fire alarm systems, and building rules can be stricter than the code: a co-op board, a commercial lease or NYCHA's house rules can all forbid work the code would allow.

"If fiber is available on your block, your building already has it."

Availability maps say whether a provider claims it can serve an address. BestNeighborhood lists Verizon fiber at about 99.6 percent of Williamsbridge addresses, but that does not mean a line has been brought into every cellar.

In multi-family buildings, the owner usually has to let the provider install equipment inside, and in a two-family the line still has to be run from the street to the house. Plenty of houses on fully mapped blocks are still on coax because nobody ordered the switch.

Inside the house, fiber service still ends at a terminal on the wall, and everything after that is copper. Getting fiber does not rewire the rooms; it is often the moment people discover the rooms need rewiring.

"Mesh Wi-Fi has made cabling unnecessary in homes."

Mesh works well in open, lightly built spaces. A Bronxwood two-family is rarely that: brick party and exterior walls, plaster on lath, a masonry chimney stack and a cellar below a concrete or heavily framed floor all absorb the wireless link between mesh units.

One commenter on r/AskNYC, answering a question about sharing internet between apartments, put the trade-off plainly: you cannot beat the reliability of wired. Mesh units connected by cable keep all of mesh's convenience and drop most of its weakness.

So the practical answer in most Bronxwood houses is not mesh or cable, but cable to two or three access points or mesh units, placed where each floor needs them.

"Coax already in the walls can carry your network instead of new cable."

Adapters that send data over coax exist and sometimes help. But the coax in many Bronxwood houses is several generations of splitters, staples and outside runs put in by different providers over decades, and a network carried over it inherits every weak connector along the way.

Old coax is also shared with whatever service still uses it, and in a two-family it may physically connect both apartments. That is exactly what you do not want when two households need private networks.

Old coax is more useful as a pull line. Where it runs through a hidden path, it can sometimes be used to pull new Ethernet through the same route, which saves opening a wall.

"Any outdoor-rated cable can go along the outside of the house."

Outdoor-rated cable resists sunlight and moisture, but it is not the whole answer. Each place it enters the house is a potential leak, and on aluminum siding like that on the Bronxwood Park houses, a careless hole can let water behind the siding for years before anyone notices.

Cable running along an outside wall is also exposed to ladders, landscapers and the provider's next technician, and a long outdoor run that enters the house without surge protection can carry a lightning-induced surge to the equipment inside.

We keep outside runs short, enter from below so water drains away, use sealed fittings and grounded protection where the run warrants it, and prefer an inside route whenever one exists.

"Testing a cable means checking that the light comes on."

A link light shows that two devices can see each other. It does not show whether the cable meets its category's performance, and a cable with a bad termination or a tight bend can light up and still drop speed under load.

One 2026 cost guide warns that some contractors quote tested but not certified. Certification means measuring each run against the standard for its category and saving the result, which is what a manufacturer's warranty and any later dispute will rely on.

Every run we install in Bronxwood is certified, and the results go into the handover file with the port map, so you have proof of what was installed rather than a promise.

Bronxwood Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Can we pay part of the price when we book and the rest when the cabling is finished?
+

Yes. Our service agreement asks for a 50 percent deposit when the job is booked and the balance when the work is complete and you have seen the certification results and the port map. The written plan shows every line item before you pay anything, and nothing is added on the day without your written approval. For a larger building job or new construction, the plan can be split into stages, such as rough-in and finish, with each stage priced separately. The full terms, including the warranty and the late-fee schedule, are set out in the service agreement you sign before work starts.

Our streaming box buffers every evening even though a speed test on the phone looks fine. Will a wired connection fix it?
+

Often, yes. A phone test near the router measures the best spot in the house, while a streaming box behind a television two rooms and a brick wall away gets a much weaker signal, and evenings are when every neighbor's network is busy on the same channels. A cable to the television removes Wi-Fi from that path entirely. Before we recommend it, we test at the provider's box and at the television itself, because if the buffering also happens on a cable plugged straight into the modem, the problem is the service, not the house, and you should call the provider first.

Is there a minimum job size, or will you come out to run a single cable to one room?
+

We will run a single cable. A one-run job still includes the walk-through, a certified test and a label at both ends, so it costs more per run than a job with ten, because the visit and setup are shared by fewer cables. That is why we usually suggest adding a second cable in the same path or a run for a future access point while we are there, since the extra cable adds little to the labor. The written plan shows the single run on its own and any extras as separate optional lines, so you decide.

How long can one network cable be before the signal needs help?
+

The standard limit for a copper Ethernet channel is 100 meters, about 328 feet, including the short cords at each end. No run inside a Bronxwood two-family comes close to that. It matters in larger buildings, long storefronts that connect to a rear building, and new mid-rise construction, where a run from a cellar telecom room to an upper floor can approach the limit. In those cases we use fiber for the long backbone and a switch closer to the rooms, and the plan shows the measured length of each run.

The car charger in our gated driveway wants a network connection. Can it have a cable instead of Wi-Fi?
+

Usually, yes, if the charger has a network port. The run leaves the house through a sealed entry, travels in outdoor-rated cable or conduit along the house or the driveway wall, and lands in the charger's connection compartment. The charger's own power circuit is electrical work and belongs to a licensed electrician; we only run the data cable, and we keep it separated from the power wiring. A wired connection is helpful where the driveway sits behind a brick house, far from the router, and it lets the charger report and update reliably.

What is the difference between a network cord from the store and the cable you put inside the wall?
+

Store cords are usually stranded cable with molded plugs, made to bend and be plugged in and out. The cable inside a wall is solid-conductor, made to be installed once and terminated on a jack and a patch panel, and it is rated for where it runs, such as inside walls, in risers or in air-handling ceilings. Stranded cords are fine for the short distance from a wall jack to a device. They should not be pushed through walls or stapled along a cellar ceiling, because they are not rated for it and they lose performance over long distances.

If we also want the television mounted over the new jack, can that happen on the same visit, and what does it cost?
+

Yes. Mounting on drywall over wood studs for a screen up to 55 inches is $209.99 at the Bronx rate, bracket sold separately, with $45 added for plaster walls, $65 for brick or stone and $130 above a fireplace. The in-wall power and cable kit that hides the cords behind the screen is $169, and a paintable cord cover is $59 where the wall should not be opened. Taking down an old television is $85. These are added to the cabling plan as separate lines, so the run to the television and the mounting are done in one visit.

Why does every cable go back to one central spot instead of being chained from one room to the next?
+

Because each room then has its own independent path. When cables are chained through a small switch in each room, one failed switch or one loose cord takes down every room after it, and nobody can tell where the fault is. With every run going back to one panel, each jack can be tested, labeled and repaired on its own, a single bad cable affects a single room, and new equipment can be added at the center without touching the rest. It is the core idea of structured cabling, and it is why the handover file lists every run separately.

Does a cable to a camera or ceiling access point need a wall jack, or can it plug straight into the device?
+

The far end of a run to a camera or access point usually gets a field-terminated plug or a surface jack right beside the device, rather than a wall plate in the room. That keeps the run testable and lets the device be replaced without disturbing the cable. The cellar end still lands on the patch panel with a label like any other run. What we avoid is a cable that dangles out of the ceiling with a plug crimped on in a hurry, because it is hard to test, easy to damage, and usually the first thing to fail.

The provider is coming next week to switch us to fiber. Should the cabling happen before or after?
+

Ideally we visit before, so we can tell you where the new terminal should go and ask the installer to mount it there, near the planned enclosure and an outlet. Then the cabling can happen either side of the switch. If the provider comes first, that is fine too: we start the house network from wherever they put the terminal and, if that spot is poor, run a short protected cable to a better one. What to avoid is having the provider install a router in a front room and then building the network around it by accident.

Do we still need the provider's router once wired access points are installed?
+

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. Some providers' equipment combines the line terminal and the router in one box, and it must stay connected. In that case we turn off its Wi-Fi or leave it as a backup and let the access points carry the wireless signal. Where the provider gives you a separate router, you can often return it and use your own router or firewall in the enclosure instead, which can also save a monthly rental fee. We check the provider's equipment on the walk-through and tell you which case you have before you return anything.
