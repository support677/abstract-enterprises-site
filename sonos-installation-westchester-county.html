The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Homes Need a Sonos Plan Before the Walls Close

Most Sonos jobs in Westchester County begin with a date that somebody else set. The contractor is closing the kitchen ceiling on the twelfth. The co-op’s managing agent meets the board on the first Tuesday of the month. The spray-foam crew is booked for the attic next Thursday. The movers arrive at the new house in Rye on the thirtieth. Sonos itself is designed to be set up by its owner in an afternoon, and a single speaker on a shelf still is. But the systems people in this county actually ask us for — ceiling speakers in a renovated kitchen, an Arc Ultra over a stone fireplace in a 1920s Tudor, music on the terrace of a townhouse whose association owns the walls — depend on decisions taken by other people on other schedules. Miss the window and the same wire that cost little to drop through an open stud bay costs a great deal more to fish through finished plaster.

The county’s housing explains why that matters so much here. Westchester is not one market but two living side by side. The Census Bureau’s QuickFacts table counts about 399,600 housing units for 2025 and an owner-occupied rate of 61.8 percent for 2020–2024 — far below Long Island’s suburbs — because a large share of households in Yonkers, New Rochelle, White Plains and Mount Vernon live in apartments: post-war elevator buildings converted to co-ops and condos in the 1980s, and a new generation of downtown rental towers. The other Westchester is the one people picture: railroad suburbs of Tudors and colonials from the 1910s to the 1930s in Scarsdale, Bronxville, Larchmont, Pelham and Rye, and larger houses on larger lots further north. Stacker’s September 2025 analysis of Census data put 28 percent of the county’s homes — 109,608 of them — at 1939 or earlier. Each half of the county has its own gatekeepers. In the apartments, it is the board, the agent or the landlord. In the houses, it is the renovation schedule, the village building department and, increasingly, the homeowners’ association.

The licensing landscape adds a layer most buyers don’t expect. Westchester law requires home improvement contractors to hold a county licence from the Department of Consumer Protection, which says there are almost 9,000 licensed contractors in the county and asks residents to check the licence before paying anything. Electricians are licensed by the county too. Individual towns and villages run their own building departments: the Town of Greenburgh, for one, publishes an “Electrical or Low Voltage Permit Application” that asks low-voltage contractors for liability, workers’ compensation and disability insurance naming the town as certificate holder. None of that is a reason to avoid Sonos. It is a reason to have the paperwork, the insurance certificates and the scope of work ready before a cut is made — which is the part of the job a speaker’s box doesn’t mention.

Then there is the network, which in Westchester fails in its own particular ways. In pre-war houses, plaster on wire lath and masonry chimney stacks block Wi-Fi far more than drywall does; Sonos’s own interference guidance rates plaster and concrete as high obstructions and metal as very high. In new downtown towers, the walls are concrete and steel, and dozens of neighbours’ routers share the same channels. In the larger houses up-county, the router sits in a basement corner and the terrace is two floors and sixty feet away. A Sonos speaker that can’t hold a steady connection drops out, vanishes from the app or refuses to group — and no amount of re-pairing fixes a signal that was never strong enough. We design the network as part of the job, not as an afterthought.

This page describes how we do Sonos installation in that setting. We are a licensed low-voltage contractor; speaker wire, network cabling, access points, in-ceiling speakers, TV and soundbar mounting are the work we already do across the county. We time the wiring to your renovation, prepare the paperwork your building or association asks for, run cable where cable is the only reliable answer and use wireless products where the walls argue against wire. What we don’t do is electrical work: a new outlet for an Amp closet or a circuit for an equipment rack is for a county-licensed electrician, and we tell you before the visit if your plan needs one.

Older Houses

Finding a Wire Path Through a Westchester Tudor or Colonial

In a finished pre-war house, the question is never whether a speaker can go in the ceiling. It is whether a cable can reach that ceiling from the Amp without opening a wall that took a plasterer a week to build. These are the routes our crews look for first in the Tudors and colonials of Scarsdale, Bronxville, Larchmont, Pelham, Rye and the river towns, roughly in the order they tend to work.

Stacked closets

Center-hall colonials were often built with a coat closet under a linen closet under an attic hatch. That vertical stack is the best friend a cable has: a single hole in each closet floor or ceiling, hidden behind shelving, carries wire from the basement to the attic with nothing visible in a finished room. We check every closet before suggesting anything else.

The attic over the second floor

Second-floor ceilings in many Westchester colonials can be reached from above, which makes in-ceiling speakers in bedrooms and the primary suite a manageable job. Tudors are harder: steep roofs, dormers and finished third-floor rooms leave kneewall spaces and short cavities rather than an open attic. We measure the depth above each proposed position, because Sonos’s in-ceiling speakers need roughly five inches of clear cavity, and a dormer can leave less than that.

The basement under the first floor

First-floor walls can often be fed from the basement through the bottom plate, which is how wire reaches a TV wall or an in-wall speaker in the living room. First-floor ceilings are the hard ones, because the floor above is finished. Unless there is a soffit, a bulkhead or a renovation under way, a first-floor family room is usually better served by a soundbar and wireless rear speakers than by ceiling speakers.

Old chases and retired chimneys

Older houses sometimes have an abandoned laundry chute, a retired gravity-heat duct or a disused chimney flue that runs between floors. Some of these make excellent cable routes; some are still connected to something, or carry fire-safety obligations. We only use a chase after we’ve confirmed what it is and that nothing still depends on it, and we never run cable through an active flue.

When no path exists

Sometimes the honest answer is that the cable can’t reach a particular ceiling without opening finished plaster. In that case we say so, and offer the alternatives: an Era speaker pair placed on shelves or brackets, a Five on a sideboard, or waiting for the next renovation and leaving a pull string when the walls do open. A good wireless room is better than a scarred ceiling, and Sonos’s wireless speakers are designed for exactly that choice.

Exterior walls deserve one more note. Many Westchester Tudors are stucco over masonry or over wire lath, and colonials may have brick veneer. Running a cable through an exterior wall for a terrace speaker means sealing the penetration properly against water and, in an association, getting approval first. We plan exterior penetrations as carefully as interior ones.

Compliance callouts for commercial Sonos work in Westchester

  • Insurance on file first. Landlords, managing agents and some town building departments ask for liability, workers’ compensation and disability certificates naming them before any work begins. Greenburgh’s low-voltage permit form specifies that the compensation and disability certificates cannot be on an Acord form. We send ours at no charge.
  • Permits where the town requires them. Whether a low-voltage job needs a permit varies by municipality and by the size of the job; Greenburgh’s form, for example, sets a fee of $15 per $1,000 of the cost of work. We check with the building department before the start date, not after.
  • Electrical work stays with electricians. In Westchester, electricians must be licensed by the county, and electrical work is inspected by an approved agency. New outlets for an Amp rack or a ceiling-mounted access point are theirs; the low-voltage cabling is ours.
  • Ceiling cable. Sonos’s in-ceiling instructions tell installers to consult local building codes before running speaker wire through ceilings. In a drop ceiling used as an air return, that means cable rated for the space — we specify it before buying wire.
  • Music licensing is the owner’s call. Playing music to customers can carry public-performance licensing obligations that have nothing to do with the hardware. We install the system; the business decides its music service.

Offices and practices with their own IT. Many Westchester offices run a managed network with separate VLANs, enterprise Wi-Fi log-ins and a firewall someone else administers. Sonos lists WPA and WPA2 Enterprise security as unsupported and needs its products and the controlling devices on the same subnet, so we agree with the tenant’s IT provider before the visit where the Sonos products will live on the network and which devices may control them. An Era 100 Pro on a PoE+ switch port, or an Amp on a dedicated port, keeps the music separate from the systems that run the practice.

Approvals

Who Signs Off Before a Westchester Sonos Job

A Sonos installation in Westchester can need a yes from as many as four parties before a single hole is cut. Knowing who they are, and what each one wants to see, is the difference between a job that starts on the booked day and one that waits a month for the next board meeting.

The county: licences you can check

Westchester’s Department of Consumer Protection licenses home improvement contractors and keeps a public list of licensed firms, along with a list of contractors to avoid. Its spring advisories ask homeowners to see a contractor’s licence and to verify it before handing over any payment, and note that licensed contractors display a green sticker on the rear bumper of their vehicles showing when the licence expires. The department’s number is (914) 995-2155. Separate county licensing laws cover electricians and plumbers. Whatever kind of contractor you hire for a Sonos job — us included — ask for the relevant licence, check it, and keep a copy with the contract. Our own NYS low-voltage licence number, #12000287431, is printed on every page and invoice.

The town or village: building departments

Westchester’s cities, towns and villages each run their own building department, and their rules for low-voltage work are not uniform. Some publish forms that name low-voltage work explicitly — the Town of Greenburgh’s “Electrical or Low Voltage Permit Application” asks low-voltage contractors for insurance certificates naming the town, a NYS alarm installer licence where applicable, and an application through the state’s inspection system. Others treat speaker wiring inside a larger renovation as part of the general contractor’s building permit. Croton-on-Hudson’s engineering page reminds residents that electrical work associated with a building permit needs a certificate of electrical inspection before a certificate of occupancy is issued. During a renovation, the simplest path is to make sure your contractor’s permit and inspection schedule already includes the low-voltage scope; for a stand-alone job we check with the building department first.

The building: boards, agents and landlords

For co-op and condo owners in the southern cities, the building’s alteration rules usually decide more than any code does. Managing agents commonly ask for a written scope, certificates of insurance naming the corporation and the agent, and work inside set weekday hours. Cutting a ceiling for an in-ceiling speaker is a different request from placing a wireless speaker on a shelf, and many boards treat it that way. In rental towers the lease and the management office set the limits: a soundbar on a stand needs nobody’s permission; a bracket in a concrete wall may need the landlord’s written consent.

The association: exteriors and common walls

In townhouse and condominium communities, the association typically controls the exterior: siding, decks, patios and anything mounted on them. Outdoor speakers under an eave or a cable through a rim joist can need the same architectural approval as a new storm door. We give you a one-page description of what will be mounted, where the cable enters the house and what the finished install looks like, so the request goes to the committee complete the first time.

What we send before the start date: a written scope, a certificate of insurance naming whoever needs to be named, our licence details, the product list and, for exterior or ceiling work, a marked-up photo of where each speaker goes. If the building or association needs something else, we ask early.

Renovation Timing

When Sonos Wiring Goes Into a Westchester Renovation

The Census Bureau counts 996 new housing units authorised by building permits in Westchester in 2025 — and that figure doesn’t include the kitchens, basements, attics and additions in existing houses that are opened up every year. For Sonos, those projects are the cheapest chance a house will ever offer to put wire where it belongs. The trick is knowing which stage of the project to join.

Before demolition: the plan

Decide which rooms get ceiling speakers, where the TV and soundbar go, where the Amps will live and where the network equipment sits. Sonos says one Amp can drive up to three pairs of its Architectural speakers wired in parallel, and that a single pair suits a room up to about 150 square feet. Those two facts set how many Amps you need and therefore how much space the equipment closet takes. We mark every speaker position on the architect’s drawings or on a phone photo of the bare room.

Framing and rough-in: the wire

This is our visit. With studs and joists exposed, we run speaker wire from each speaker spot back to the Amp location, Ethernet to the TV wall and to any speaker that should be wired, and a spare pull string or conduit to places you may want music later. Every run is labelled at both ends, tested and photographed with a tape measure in the frame. Speaker positions in the ceiling are checked against lights, sprinkler heads, HVAC registers and the joists, because Sonos’s in-ceiling speakers need a clear cavity: about 4.73 inches of mounting depth for the 6-inch model and 5.32 inches for the 8-inch.

Inspection, insulation and drywall: the hand-off

The wire has to be in before the inspector’s rough-in visit if the building department’s permit covers it, and before insulation in any case. Spray foam is the hard deadline: once it cures in an attic or around a rim joist, new wire through that space means cutting foam. We leave the coiled ends where the drywall crew can see them and protect them through taping and painting; mud-ring brackets or marked positions show the drywall crew where not to cover.

Finish: speakers, Amps and setup

When paint is dry, we come back to cut the ceiling openings — 8.25 inches for Sonos’s 6-inch in-ceiling speaker, 10.125 inches for the 8-inch — fit the speakers and grilles, terminate the Amps, set up the system, run Trueplay where supported and hand it over. If your contractor finishes faster than expected, the wire waits safely in the ceiling; the speakers can follow when you’re ready.

If the renovation has already closed up, all is not lost: wireless Era speakers and soundbars need only power, and wire can often still be fished from an attic or basement. It costs more and takes longer than doing it while the studs are open, which is why we ask for the contractor’s schedule at the first call.

Apartment Owners

A Sonos Checklist for Westchester Co-op and Condo Owners

Southern Westchester’s apartment buildings have their own rhythm, and a Sonos job that respects it goes through the board and the front desk without friction. This is the list we work through with co-op and condo owners in Yonkers, White Plains, New Rochelle, Mount Vernon, Hartsdale and Bronxville before the first visit.

  • Read the alteration agreement or house rules first. Many buildings distinguish between placing equipment and altering the apartment. A wireless system — soundbar, Sub and Era speakers — is usually the first kind; cutting a ceiling or chasing a wall is the second and goes to the board or agent.
  • Ask the agent what the certificate of insurance must say. The exact names of the corporation and the managing agent, the limits required and where to send it. We issue it at no charge, but the wording has to match before the doorman will let us up.
  • Book inside the building’s contractor hours. Weekday daytime windows are common in co-ops; some buildings also need the service elevator reserved. We schedule the first two-hour window that fits the building’s rules, not just ours.
  • Know what’s above and below you. Concrete slabs rule out most in-ceiling speakers unless a dropped or furred ceiling already exists. If it does, we confirm with the agent that speakers can go into it before any template is traced.
  • Check the building’s internet arrangement. Some newer buildings supply Wi-Fi to every unit. Sonos doesn’t support guest or portal log-in networks, so if the building’s service works that way, the apartment needs its own router or a network the building’s provider can configure for it.
  • Plan for the move-out. Condo owners may sell; co-op shareholders may be asked to restore alterations. Wireless speakers, a soundbar on a bracket and raceway that can be removed leave the fewest repairs behind.

None of this slows a well-prepared job. It simply moves the paperwork to before the visit instead of the morning of it, which is when a building’s front desk turns contractors away. When that happens because a certificate wasn’t filed, our $150 return-trip fee applies, so we’d rather send everything a week early.

Licences, permits and paperwork

County licences and what to ask any installer for

Westchester law requires home improvement contractors to hold a county licence, and the Department of Consumer Protection asks residents to see that licence and verify it on the county’s public list before paying anything. Separate county licensing laws cover electricians. Which licences apply to a particular Sonos job depends on its scope — a speaker set on a shelf is different from a renovation that opens ceilings — so the practical rule is simple: ask any contractor which licences they hold for the work they are quoting, check them on the county list or with the department at (914) 995-2155, and keep copies with the contract.

For our part, we are a New York State licensed low-voltage contractor, licence #12000287431, insured, and we put the licence number on the quote and the invoice. Where a job needs an electrician — a new outlet for an Amp closet, a circuit for a rack — we say so in writing and leave that work to a county-licensed electrician.

Village and town permits for low-voltage wiring

It depends on the municipality and the job. Westchester’s cities, towns and villages each run their own building departments. The Town of Greenburgh, which covers Hartsdale, Edgemont and the other unincorporated hamlets, publishes an “Electrical or Low Voltage Permit Application” that asks low-voltage contractors for insurance certificates naming the town and sets a fee of $15 per $1,000 of the cost of work. Other departments fold low-voltage wiring into the general contractor’s renovation permit or don’t require a separate filing for small jobs.

Before a stand-alone job we check with your building department and file if it asks us to. During a renovation, the cleanest route is for your contractor’s permit and inspection schedule to include our scope, so the inspector sees the wire at rough-in rather than discovering it later.

What goes in the scope letter for a Yonkers co-op’s managing agent

Agents and boards want to know three things: what will be touched, who is doing it, and how the building is protected. Our scope letter lists every product and where it goes, states plainly whether any ceiling, wall or slab will be cut and to what size, names the cable route, gives the expected hours and number of visits, and attaches our certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the agent as the building requires. If speakers are going into an existing dropped or furred ceiling, the letter says so, with photos.

Many co-op owners find the approval comes faster when the plan avoids cutting at all: wireless Era speakers, a soundbar on the TV wall, and a speaker cable hidden in a paintable raceway the board has approved before. We will draft both versions if you want to give the board a choice.

Patio speakers when the townhouse association owns the walls

Outdoor speakers are usually possible with the association’s written approval. Associations usually control siding, decks and anything attached to the exterior, so a speaker under the eave or a cable through the rim joist is their decision, not ours. We prepare a one-page request: a photo marked with each speaker position, the product and its finish, where the cable enters the house and how the hole is sealed, and confirmation that the Amp stays indoors.

If the committee says no, there are honest alternatives that need nobody’s permission: a Move 2 carried out to the patio on its charging base indoors, or speakers placed on furniture rather than fixed to the building. Sonos rates Move 2 at IP56, enough for a terrace in passing rain, though its charging base is for indoor use only.

Timing Sonos with a renovation

Kitchen gut renovations: the rough-in window

At rough-in, after framing and before insulation, drywall and the inspector’s rough-in visit if your permit covers low-voltage work. That is the only time the wire can go exactly where it should with no patching. We need about a week’s notice of the date the walls will be ready, and a short visit once the electrician has set the boxes, so ceiling speaker positions don’t collide with lights, ducts or joists.

Bring us in even earlier, before demolition, if you’re still deciding the layout. Where the Amps live and how many you need depend on the number of speaker pairs, and that decision affects the cabinetry or closet plan. A ten-minute conversation at that stage saves a lot of rework at the end.

The attic is about to be spray-foamed — what needs to be run before the insulation crew arrives?

Anything that will ever need to pass through that space: speaker wire to every second-floor ceiling speaker position you might want, Ethernet to an access point location on the top floor, and at least one empty conduit or sleeve from the attic down to the basement or equipment closet for future runs. Once spray foam cures around joists and rafters, adding a cable means cutting the foam and re-sealing it.

We label and photograph every run, coil the ends where the drywall or plaster crew will find them, and tag the conduit so the next installer knows it’s there. It’s one of the cheapest insurance policies in a Westchester renovation.

Our general contractor says his crew can run the speaker wire — what should we check first?

That they use the right cable and leave it where a speaker can actually go. Ask for speaker cable of the gauge the Amp manufacturer recommends for the run length, rated for in-wall use (and for plenum use if it passes through an air-return space), with no splices inside the walls. Ask that each run end at a marked position at least a joist bay clear of lights and ducts, be labelled at both ends, and be photographed before the walls close.

We’re happy to work either way: run the wire ourselves at rough-in, or give your contractor a marked plan and come back at finish to fit the speakers and set up the system. If someone else’s wire is already in the walls, we test every run before connecting an Amp.

Pre-war houses: fireplaces and ceilings

Soundbars under TVs on stone and brick chimney breasts

Usually on the mantel or on a bracket just below the TV, whichever keeps the bar closest to the screen. Sonos asks for at least 4 inches between an Arc Ultra and the TV so its upward-firing drivers aren’t blocked, and notes that Arc Ultra detects when it is wall-mounted and adjusts its sound for the wall. A deep mantel can be the better spot because it keeps the bar off the masonry entirely; a shallow one usually means mounting.

The HDMI and power routes matter more than the bar. On a masonry chimney breast we avoid drilling the flue or the chimney body, and route cable beside the breast inside the adjoining stud wall or behind a mantel return. If the chimney is drilled for the bracket, the brick or stone surcharge applies; the cable path is quoted at the walkthrough.

How should ceiling speakers be laid out in a beamed or coffered ceiling so the room still looks planned?

They don’t have to. Sonos’s in-ceiling speakers made with Sonance use paintable, bezel-less grilles, so they can be painted to match the ceiling and sit almost flush. In a coffered ceiling, we centre each speaker in a coffer and keep left and right speakers symmetrical; in a beamed ceiling, we set them between beams on the same line as the recessed lights so the ceiling reads as planned rather than patched.

What we won’t do is cut into a decorative beam or a plaster medallion. If the room has no clean cavity, a pair of Era speakers on brackets or furniture often sounds better than a compromise ceiling location.

Big houses, two households and houses that come with Sonos

The 32-product limit in large houses

Sonos’s system requirements list a maximum of 32 Sonos products in one system. A large house with ceiling speaker pairs on several Amps, a home theater with a Sub and surrounds, and speakers in bedrooms, baths and a gym can approach that. Surrounds and Subs count as products too.

When a plan gets close, we look at combining rooms on a single Amp, choosing products that cover larger spaces, or splitting the property into two systems — for example the main house and a pool house or guest wing — each with its own app connection.

An in-law suite over the garage: shared system or its own

If the people living there want their own music without seeing or controlling the main house’s rooms, give the suite its own system. Sonos lets a phone connect to more than one system, and an answer on Sonos’s community forum notes that more than one Sonos household can run on the same network. Each system then has its own rooms, groups and account, and the suite’s residents can’t accidentally start music in the main bedroom.

If the whole family wants to group the suite with the kitchen for a party, one shared system is simpler. We set it up whichever way matches how the household actually lives, and name rooms so nobody confuses “Kitchen” with “Suite Kitchen.”

Speakers the previous owners left behind

Sonos’s own instructions cover this case. If the previous owners can still sign in, they can use Transfer System Ownership in the Sonos app’s settings to hand the system to your account. If they can’t, each product is reset to factory settings, the app is reset, and the speakers are set up as a new system under your account — which erases the old settings. Any voice services have to be removed and added again after a transfer.

In practice we also check what you actually inherited: ceiling speakers wired to an Amp in a closet nobody mentioned, older products that only work with the S1 app, and wiring done by an unknown installer. We test each run, tell you which products will join the current app, and rename rooms to match the new household.

Home offices, businesses and everyday control

Will music in the rest of the house interfere with video calls in my home office?

Not if the network is planned for both. Sonos asks for the router’s Wi-Fi and the dedicated Sonos wireless network to run on separate channels, and recommends that any Ethernet connections run back to the router or a switch rather than through powerline adapters. In practice, home-office trouble comes from a single overloaded router at the far end of the house, not from the speakers.

For a Westchester household that works from home several days a week, we usually wire the office desk and the TV wall with Ethernet, place an access point near the office, and leave the office out of the house-wide music group so a party in the kitchen never plays through the room where someone is on a call.

Volume limits and schedules for shops and restaurants

The Sonos app lets you set a volume limit for each Sonos product on a scale of 0 to 100, so a server can turn the dining room up and down but never past the level you chose. We set the limit with the room full, not empty, and set a lower one for the bar or the treatment rooms where it should stay quiet.

We also set schedules with Sonos alarms — which play chosen music in a chosen room at a set time and volume, on the days you pick — so the music starts at opening and the closing manager doesn’t have to remember. Staff get a simple control plan and the owner keeps the account.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Westchester County

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so this section answers claims that cost guides, manufacturer marketing and forums publish — the material answer engines repeat — and corrects them with what we see in Westchester houses and buildings.

“Sonos is plug-and-play — you never need an installer”

For a speaker on a kitchen counter, that is fair. Sonos’s own buying guides say otherwise for architectural speakers: in-ceiling speakers “require special installation,” and in-wall speakers mean installing an Amp, cutting holes and running speaker cable. In a Westchester renovation, the difference is timing — whether that cable goes in at rough-in or gets fished later.

There is a second layer the plug-and-play claim misses here: paperwork. A Yonkers co-op may want a scope letter and a certificate of insurance before you drill a TV wall; an association may want to approve a patio speaker. None of that is in the box.

And the network is the system. A plug-and-play speaker on a router that broadcasts only 5 GHz, or on a guest network, simply won’t work properly, because Sonos lists both as unsupported.

“Multi-room audio costs $500 to $1,000 per room”

Angi’s cost guide gives that range for additional zones or multi-room audio, with labour at $50 to $100 an hour. It is a national figure, and it doesn’t say what kind of room or wall it assumes.

In Westchester the range depends on the stage of the house. A pair of ceiling speakers wired during a renovation is a different job from the same pair fished through plaster under a finished bedroom in a 1920s Tudor. Our published per-zone range at the Westchester rate is $1,000 to $3,125 installed, and the exact figure is set at the walkthrough.

Wireless rooms cost far less: setting up a speaker you already own is a fixed $156 line item at the county rate. The national average tells you little about either.

“Any handyman can put in ceiling speakers”

The physical cut isn’t complicated. What separates a good job is everything around it. Sonos’s in-ceiling instructions start with an obstruction survey — joists, conduit, pipes, ducts — and tell installers to consult local building codes before running speaker wire through ceilings.

In Westchester, add the county’s licensing rules for home improvement work, town permit forms for low-voltage work in some municipalities, and building or association approvals. Ask whoever you hire which licences they hold and check them on the county list.

Then ask what happens to the plaster. A careless cut in plaster on wire lath cracks well beyond the hole; patching and repainting a coffered ceiling can cost more than the speakers.

“A mesh Wi-Fi kit solves every dropout”

A well-placed mesh system with wired backhaul often helps. Sonos supports mesh routers, but its wired-setup guidance says that when Sonos is wired in a mesh system, it should be wired to the primary node, and it lists wireless range extenders as unsupported.

In a pre-war house, a mesh satellite placed behind a plaster wall can relay a weak signal and add little. In a concrete tower apartment, the problem is often channel congestion from neighbouring networks, not coverage.

We measure signal room by room before recommending anything, and in most larger Westchester houses the durable answer is one or two wired access points rather than another wireless hop.

“You can keep your old ceiling speakers and just add a Sonos Amp”

Sometimes you can. The Amp drives passive speakers rated at least 125 watts at 8 ohms or 200 watts at 4 ohms, according to Sonos’s Amp guide, and it should not be connected to loads below 4 ohms.

Houses changing hands in Westchester often come with ceiling speakers from an earlier system and wire of unknown age and route. We test each run for continuity and shorts, identify the speaker ratings where labels are readable, and only then decide how many pairs one Amp can safely drive.

Where the old speakers are past their best, the wire is often still worth keeping — and replacing a speaker in an existing hole is far quicker than cutting a new one, as long as the new speaker’s cut-out fits.

“Trueplay tunes every speaker from any phone”

Sonos says most of its products require an iOS device to run Trueplay. Arc Ultra, Beam Ultra, Era 100, Era 100 Pro and Era 300 offer quick tuning from iOS or Android; advanced tuning remains iOS-only. Sonos’s in-ceiling speakers on an Amp are tuned with a supported iOS device.

In an Android-only household, we bring an iPhone to run tuning during the visit. Sonos also advises re-tuning after moving a speaker, which matters in houses where furniture is rearranged after a renovation.

Tuning in an empty, echoing room after the renovation dust settles also gives a different result from tuning with rugs and sofas in place, so we prefer to tune on the final visit.

“Once it’s set up, Sonos runs itself”

Sonos systems need occasional attention. When Sonos released its redesigned app on May 7, 2024, owners lost features and reported problems, and the company’s chief executive publicly apologised; he stepped down in January 2025 while fixes continued.

Locally, the most common trigger is the internet provider replacing the router, which changes the network name or settings and takes every speaker offline. Renovation crews unplugging an Amp closet is a close second.

We leave a written record of how the system is set up — network name, Amp locations, wire labels — so a router swap is a quick fix rather than a mystery.

Access points, switches and Ethernet installed and tested, router settings checked against Sonos’s requirements, before any speaker is added.

5. Speakers, Amps and soundbar

Ceiling openings cut from template, speakers and grilles fitted, Amps terminated, soundbar mounted and connected by HDMI, every product added to one Sonos system.

Each product given a plain room name, house-wide and floor-by-floor groups created, Trueplay completed with an iPhone where needed, and maximum volumes capped for children’s rooms, party walls or a shop floor.

7. Handover and written record

We show every member of the household the controls, power-cycle the network to confirm every room returns, and leave a record of wire labels, Amp locations and network settings.

The network work is about channel congestion and router placement rather than distance. New Rochelle alone added 2,456 apartments in 14 buildings between 2018 and May 2023, according to Lohud, so a growing share of these jobs are in buildings only a few years old.

The Sound shore: Pelham, Larchmont, Mamaroneck, Rye and Port Chester

Tudors, colonials and Victorians on modest lots, many with plaster on lath and stone or brick fireplaces. These are the houses where timing matters most: a kitchen or primary-suite renovation is the moment to wire ceiling speakers, and outside renovation season we often recommend wireless speakers rather than fishing wire through finished plaster. Terraces and porches are common requests, and in condo communities along the shore, so are association approvals.

Central Westchester: Scarsdale, Eastchester, Greenburgh and Harrison

A mix of 1920s Tudors in Fox Meadow and Edgemont, post-war colonials and ranches, and garden-apartment co-ops in Hartsdale. Families working from home want the office left out of the house-wide group and wired for calls. Greenburgh’s low-voltage permit form is a reminder to check the building department’s requirements before a stand-alone job starts.

The river towns: Hastings-on-Hudson to Croton-on-Hudson and Peekskill

Older village houses with porches and Hudson views, walk-up apartments over storefronts and hillside lots where the terrace sits a floor below the kitchen. Steep sites make cable routes to a lower terrace the main design question; the walk-up line item applies to third-floor apartments without elevators.

Northern Westchester: Armonk, Chappaqua, Bedford, Pound Ridge, Somers and North Salem

Larger houses, finished lower levels, pool houses and long distances from the router. These are the systems that approach Sonos’s limit of 32 products in one system, that need wired access points rather than another mesh satellite, and that sometimes run better as two systems. Equipment closets, Amp ventilation and a written record of every wire matter more here than anywhere else in the county.

Sonos’s Amp product guide asks for at least 3 inches of clearance around the unit, and two Amps fit in two rack units of space. In a house with four or five Amps, that adds up to a real piece of furniture or a small rack, with its own outlet and somewhere for the heat to go. We size the closet or cabinet with the homeowner and the electrician during the renovation, rather than discovering at the finish that the Amps have nowhere to live. Outbuildings far from the house — a pool house or a detached garage with a studio above — are planned as their own network problem, with the cable or wireless link chosen for the distance.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Westchester Renovations

How our crews handle the hand-off with a renovation contractor — general practice, not the story of any one customer

The first thing our technicians ask on a Westchester renovation isn’t where the speakers go. It’s when the insulation is coming and who is doing the drywall. Those two dates decide whether the job is a morning of running wire through open studs or a week of fishing it through finished plaster later. With the dates in hand, the crew walks the space with the homeowner and the contractor together, marks each speaker position on the joists in paint, and checks each one against the electrician’s boxes and the HVAC runs before a single cable is pulled.

The second habit is photographs. Before the walls close, every run gets photographed with a tape measure in the frame and a label visible, and the pictures go to the homeowner with a simple drawing. Months later, when the speakers go in or someone wants a picture hung on that wall, that set of photos is the most useful thing we left behind.

Why do Westchester prices run higher than the Bronx or Brooklyn?
+

Our Sonos labour prices are published at a Brooklyn base and Westchester carries a 1.25 area multiplier, which covers travel time across the county and the longer visits older houses and building paperwork tend to need. Hardware such as a Sanus bracket stays at its $84.99 street price.

What deposit do you take, and when is the balance due?
+

The service agreement sets it out: a 50% deposit when you book, with the balance due on completion. Full terms — including 50% deposit, one-year parts-only warranty on AESS-supplied products, and the late-fee schedule — are set out in the service agreement.

Can we pay for the pre-wire now and buy the Sonos speakers later?
+

Yes. A renovation pre-wire is quoted and paid as its own job: wire run, labelled, tested and left coiled behind the drywall. Speakers, Amps and setup can follow months later at the Westchester line-item prices, and the photos we leave show exactly where each run ends.

Can a projector in a basement media room play its sound through Sonos?
+

Usually, but the connection decides how. Sonos soundbars and the Amp take TV sound over HDMI ARC or eARC, so the projector or its source needs that output; if it doesn’t, a receiver with a Sonos Port is the usual route, at the $161 Westchester receiver-connection line. We check the projector model before you buy anything.

Who supplies the speaker wire, Ethernet and mounting hardware?
+

We do, as part of the quoted labour for each run, sized for the distance and rated for the space it passes through. Sonos products, and any bracket you prefer to choose, can come from you; a Sanus soundbar bracket is $84.99 if you want ours.

Can you help us choose which Sonos products to buy before we order?
+

Yes. We look at the rooms, the walls and the network and recommend what to buy and where, so you don’t order an Era 300 for a wall it can’t be mounted on or more products than one system supports. You buy from the retailer you prefer.

Is Sonos the right choice over a dedicated whole-house audio system?
+

For most Westchester homes that want music in several rooms and good TV sound, controlled from phones, Sonos is a strong fit. Very large houses that want one control system for lighting, shades and audio may be better served by a dedicated control platform, and we’ll say so at the walkthrough.

Can music start by itself in the morning or when the shop opens?
+

Yes. Sonos alarms play the music you choose in the room you choose at a set time and volume, on the days you pick. We set them up during the handover for wake-up music at home or opening-time music in a business.
