The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Alarm Installation

East Williamsburg is not one kind of place, and an alarm here has to suit whichever part of it you are in. The western blocks, around Graham Avenue, Grand Street and Montrose Avenue, are mostly apartments: walk-ups, frame houses with a rental floor, shops with flats above, and big housing complexes such as the Williamsburg Houses and the Lindsay Park co-op. The eastern half, beyond Bushwick Avenue and Morgan Avenue toward Newtown Creek, is the East Williamsburg Industrial Park, one of the city’s eight in-place industrial parks, where warehouses, truck yards and workshops sit beside former factories that now hold studios, offices, rehearsal rooms and a growing number of homes. The Department of City Planning’s study of the North Brooklyn industrial area counts about 19,500 jobs across Greenpoint, East Williamsburg and Bushwick, and describes the historic loft buildings as generally three to five stories tall.

What that mix means for security is simple to say and easy to miss. Many buildings here are busy in the day and empty at night, or empty in the day and busy at night, and some are both in different parts. A metal shop shuts at four, a gallery opens at noon on Saturday, a rehearsal complex runs until two in the morning, and a music venue on Frost Street fills with up to 1,800 people a few nights a week. In between, long stretches of the industrial blocks have nobody on the street at all. An alarm earns its place in exactly those hours, when a door can be worked on for ten minutes without anyone walking past.

The thefts that get reported here also have a pattern worth knowing before you choose equipment. A good number of them did not involve a forced lock. In 2016 a burglar lowered himself through an air vent into a real estate office on Randolph Street at 2:17 in the morning and left with about $2,600 of computers. The same year, $3,010 of cymbals, pedals, microphones and recording gear vanished from a rehearsal room on Ten Eyck Street, and police noted no visible damage to the door. In 2014 a Thames Street apartment near Morgan Avenue was burglarized with no sign of forced entry. In May 2026, two food trucks parked outside 599 Johnson Avenue were hit about ten minutes apart early in the morning, and one of the generators went out through an open window. An alarm that only listens for a broken door would have missed most of these. That is why our survey in East Williamsburg starts with a list of every opening and every key holder, not with the front door.

The numbers do not support panic, and we will not sell you one. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 90th Precinct, which covers Williamsburg and most of East Williamsburg, shows 196 burglaries in 2026 through mid-September, against 181 at the same point in 2025, an increase of 8.3 percent. The same report shows burglary down 79.5 percent over 33 years and down 38.8 percent over 16 years. Robbery is down 19.6 percent this year. What has grown over the long run is grand larceny, the category for theft of property without a break-in: 487 so far this year, slightly fewer than last year, but up 48 percent over 16 years. For a studio tenant or a small business, that is the number that rings true. Things disappear from spaces that many people can get into.

Business break-ins nearby also tend to come in clusters. In March 2026, News 12 reported that one group had committed 15 burglaries between February 22 and March 9, nine of them in Williamsburg and nine of them within a six-hour stretch on March 4; at a Greenpoint bakery they used a crowbar on the storefront at about 1:45 in the morning and took $1,800 in cash and an $800 check. “I think we are in a little bit of a bubble in Williamsburg,” one resident told the station. A crew moving that fast does not linger. What protects a shop in a night like that is how quickly the owner and the monitoring station hear about the first opening.

An Alarm Left Behind by the Last Tenant, a Panel That Says “Not Ready” or a Store Siren That Calls No One: Alarm Repair in East Williamsburg

A lot of the repair calls we get from this neighborhood start the same way: somebody moved into a loft, a studio or a storefront and found an alarm already on the wall. The keypad still lights up, but nobody knows the master code, the monitoring account belongs to a company that went away with the old tenant, and the panel may still be trying to report over a phone line that was disconnected years ago. Other calls are about a panel that will not arm because a contact on a steel door has shifted, a motion detector that trips every night because a heater fan or a hanging sign moves in front of it, a roll-up door sensor knocked off by a forklift, or a siren that sounds for one second and then stops because its battery is long dead.

Most of these are fixed in one visit, often in one to two hours. A panel with an unknown code can usually be reset to factory settings and reprogrammed, and a hardwired system whose wiring tests sound can normally keep that wiring with a new panel and a cellular communicator. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for East Williamsburg’s Architecture

An alarm plan here depends less on the age of a building than on how many people share it and what each of them controls. A five-story factory with forty households, a three-story studio building with sixty leaseholders, and a one-story garage with one owner can all sit on the same block east of Bushwick Avenue. These six arrangements cover nearly every alarm request we get from the neighborhood.

The legalizing loft

A former manufacturing building where people live under the Loft Law, sometimes still registered as an interim multiple dwelling while the owner works toward a residential certificate. Heavy floors, big windows, freight halls and a landlord with obligations to the Loft Board.

The building of rented studios

Three to five stories of artist, design, photo and small-business studios behind one street door, often with galleries or a café on the ground floor. Dozens of key holders, a front desk or none, and every tenant responsible for their own room.

The rehearsal complex

A floor or a whole building divided into soundproofed rooms rented by the hour, the month or the year. Gear worth thousands in each room, many bands using the same corridor, and an operator who needs to know which room opened and when.

The truck-block warehouse

One- and two-story masonry and steel buildings on blocks such as Johnson Avenue, Scott Avenue and the streets toward Newtown Creek, with roll-up doors, a man door, skylights, a yard and often trucks or food trucks parked outside overnight.

The avenue building with flats upstairs

Three- and four-story buildings on Graham Avenue, Grand Street and Montrose Avenue with a shop at street level behind a gate, a cellar below, a yard behind, and apartments above reached by a separate street door.

The large housing complex

The Williamsburg Houses, the Bushwick and Hylan houses and the Lindsay Park co-op, whose seven buildings hold 2,702 apartments. Doors, halls and windows belong to the landlord or the co-op; a resident’s alarm stays inside the apartment.

The legalizing loft: protecting a home inside a building that is still changing

A loft apartment in East Williamsburg can be enormous by Brooklyn standards and still have very few doors. That sounds easy to alarm, and in one sense it is, but the openings that matter are rarely where a homeowner first looks. The unit door to the public hall gets a contact, of course. Then there is often a second door, onto a freight hall, a rear stair or a fire stair, that the household rarely uses and sometimes forgets is unlocked. There may be a roof hatch or a skylight in a top-floor unit. There are large windows, sometimes a whole wall of them, and some open onto a fire escape or a flat roof next door. And in buildings that were built for machinery, there can be old openings in walls and ceilings for ducts, belts or pipes that were boarded over decades ago and never looked at since.

We plan a loft alarm in two layers. The first layer is the perimeter that can be reached from outside the unit: both doors, the hatch, and any window a person could get to from a fire escape, a roof or an adjoining building. These get contacts, and the glass that a person could break without opening the frame gets a glass-break detector covering it. The second layer is inside: one or two motion detectors placed so that anyone who comes in, by whatever route, has to cross their view before reaching the bedroom area or the place where the valuables live. In a long open floor, the second layer matters more than usual, because a perimeter with one missed opening is still a perimeter with a hole in it.

Two practical points come up in almost every loft building. First, the landlord’s side. In a Loft Law building the owner is working through obligations that include fire protection and egress, and nothing we install may interfere with a fire door, a stair or a building fire alarm. A tenant’s alarm stays inside the unit, uses wireless sensors on doors and frames the tenant controls, and is mounted so it comes off without damage. Second, the panel location. Thick masonry and concrete floors can weaken a cellular signal, so before we settle where the panel goes we measure the signal there. A panel that reports reliably from a closet by the windows beats one tucked in a dead corner by the elevator.

The building of rented studios: your room, your code, your record

The most common alarm request we hear from the studio buildings around Bogart, Ingraham, Grattan and Thames streets is not about the street door. That door belongs to the landlord, it is opened by dozens of tenants and their visitors, and on an open-studios weekend it may stand open all day. The request is about the tenant’s own room: “I want to know if anyone goes into my studio when I am not there.” That is a smaller, cheaper and more useful job than people expect. A wireless panel and keypad inside the studio, a contact on the studio door, a motion detector covering the work area, and a code for each person who shares the space make up most of it. The alarm belongs to the tenant, reports to the tenant’s phone, and leaves with the tenant at the end of the lease.

The opening history is often worth as much as the siren. When a studio has three or four people sharing it, plus a landlord with a master key, the question after a theft is usually who was in the room and when, and a keypad that records each user code answers that without anyone having to accuse anyone. Bogart Salon’s owner described the thefts in his building in 2012 plainly, calling laptops and phones “easy pickings.” An alarm does not stop someone picking up a laptop from an open desk during an event, but it does tell you the moment your locked door opens after hours, and whose code, if any, opened it.

The rehearsal complex: many bands, one hallway

Rehearsal buildings are their own category. Each room may be rented by one band on a monthly lease or by a different band every few hours, every room has expensive gear in it, and the corridors are full of people carrying cases at one in the morning. The 2016 theft on Ten Eyck Street, where $3,010 of gear disappeared from a room whose door showed no damage, is the kind of loss operators and tenants describe to us. When the door is not forced, the useful questions are whether the room was locked, who had a key or a code, and when it was last opened.

For an operator, we set the panel up in areas, one per room or per group of rooms, with a code for each tenant that only works on their room, and if the operator wants it, codes that only work during the hours that tenant has booked. For a band renting a monthly room with the operator’s permission, a small self-contained system inside the room does the same job on a smaller scale. Sound is the other consideration. Glass-break detectors listen for the particular frequencies of breaking glass and are set up and tested so a cymbal or a snare does not count, and the motion detectors are chosen for rooms full of amplifiers and lights. We test both with a band playing before we leave.

The truck-block warehouse: big doors, skylights and a yard

The one-story buildings of the industrial park are the most straightforward commercial alarms in the neighborhood and the ones where a missed opening costs the most. Roll-up doors get heavy-duty contacts designed for their travel and vibration; the man door gets a standard contact; and skylights and roof hatches, which are common on these roofs, get contacts or motion coverage below them, because a roof that nobody can see from the street is a working route in. The Randolph Street burglar who came through an air vent in 2016 is a reminder that the roof and the ductwork belong on the list. Inside, the office and the tool or stock room usually become their own zone, so the owner can leave the shop floor open while the office stays armed.

Many of these buildings also have vehicles outside. The May 2026 thefts outside 599 Johnson Avenue took a generator from each of two food trucks within about ten minutes, around seven in the morning. A building alarm cannot sense a truck on the street, and we will not pretend otherwise, but a camera on the building aimed at the curb or the yard, on the same app as the alarm, with an alert drawn around the trucks and nothing else, does give an owner a chance to see it happening rather than find it afterward. Where trucks are parked inside a fenced yard, an outdoor beam or a gate contact can be part of the alarm itself.

The avenue building and the large complex: inside your own door

Above the shops on Graham Avenue and Grand Street, and in the walk-ups and frame houses on the side streets, the typical alarm protects one apartment: the entry door, any window on a fire escape or reachable from a yard or a lower roof, and a motion detector in the hall. In the large complexes, including the Williamsburg Houses and the Lindsay Park co-op, the same rule applies more strictly. The building’s doors, halls, windows and systems belong to the landlord or the co-op, so a resident’s alarm is wireless, mounted on the inside of the apartment, and connected to nothing that belongs to the building. Where a shop owner also owns the building, we usually suggest one panel with separate areas, one for the shop and one for each apartment, so the store can be armed at closing without touching the homes upstairs.

Openings and Key Holders

The Break-Ins That Leave No Mark: Planning an East Williamsburg Alarm Around Vents, Open Windows and Shared Keys

Most alarm advice is written for a house with a front door, a back door and some windows, and assumes a burglar will force one of them. East Williamsburg buildings break that assumption in two ways. They have more kinds of opening, because they were built for industry, and they have more key holders, because they are shared by tenants, staff, bands, crews and visitors. The reported thefts here reflect both. A man dropped through an air vent into a Randolph Street office. A generator left a food truck through an open window. Gear left a rehearsal room on Ten Eyck Street through a door that showed no damage. An apartment on Thames Street was burglarized with no sign of forced entry. None of these was a door kicked in.

So the first part of our survey here is an inventory of openings, and it is longer than people expect. We list every door, including the ones nobody uses; every window that can be reached from a fire escape, a roof, a yard, a loading dock or a parked truck; every roof hatch and skylight; every air-conditioner sleeve and window unit; and every vent, duct, louver or old opening large enough for a person, which in a former factory can include openings that were cut for machinery or chutes and later covered with plywood. Each one gets a decision: a contact if it opens, a glass-break detector if its glass can be broken without opening the frame, a motion detector behind it if it cannot practically be sensed itself, or a note that it is too small or too well secured to matter. The decision is written into the quote, so you can see why each sensor is there.

The second part is an inventory of people. We ask who has a key or a code today, including former tenants, cleaners, contractors, a landlord or super with a master key, and anyone who borrowed a key once and may have copied it. Then we set up the alarm so that each of those people has their own code, or none. Codes can be limited to certain areas, such as one studio or the shop but not the office, and on most panels to certain days and hours. When someone leaves, their code is deleted from the app in a minute, without changing a lock. This is the part that answers the no-forced-entry theft. If a door opens at three in the morning with a valid code, the history shows whose code it was; if it opens with a key and no code, the alarm goes off.

Finally, we look at how the building is used in the hours an alarm matters. A metal shop that closes at four and a rehearsal complex that closes at two need different schedules, and a building that holds both needs them side by side. We set exit and entry delays to the time it actually takes to lower a gate or walk a long loft, so the alarm does not become a nuisance that the household or staff learns to leave off. An alarm that is armed every night with a few sensors in the right places protects more than an elaborate one that is switched off because it kept going off.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in East Williamsburg

City Planning’s study of the North Brooklyn industrial area found that between 2010 and 2016 the number of non-industrial businesses grew by 219, a rise of 61 percent, and that more than a third of new jobs were in offices, retail, arts and entertainment, while manufacturing, wholesale distribution, construction supply, film and production, and food businesses stayed. The result is a commercial map unlike most of Brooklyn’s: storefront streets on the western side, and a large district to the east where a welding shop, a coffee roaster, a gallery and a concert hall can share a block. Each of these six settings asks something different of a business alarm.

Graham Avenue storefronts

The neighborhood’s main shopping street, with groceries, bakeries, salons, phone stores, bars and restaurants in mostly three- and four-story buildings. Gates at night, glass behind them, a cellar under the sidewalk and a yard at the back.

Grand Street toward the creek

Shops, cafés and bars give way to auto shops, small manufacturers and warehouses as Grand Street runs east past Bushwick Avenue toward the Grand Street bridge over Newtown Creek. Mixed frontages, side doors and yards.

Montrose Avenue near the L

Corner stores, vintage shops and restaurants around the Montrose Avenue station and Most Holy Trinity Church, many in older walk-ups with apartments above and storefronts that have been rebuilt several times.

The Bogart and Morgan studio blocks

Galleries, design firms, photo studios, cafés and small offices in former industrial buildings around the Morgan Avenue station, where one street door may serve dozens of separate businesses on different schedules.

Frost Street and the venue blocks

Music rooms, event spaces and bars on the industrial blocks near the expressway, led by Brooklyn Steel at 319 Frost Street. Big doors, backline and bar stock, late load-outs and early deliveries.

Johnson Avenue and the industrial park yards

Warehouses, distributors, commissaries, contractors’ shops and parked food trucks on the blocks between Bushwick Avenue and Newtown Creek, where the street is busy at six in the morning and empty by evening.

How a fast burglary crew changes the plan for a shop

The March 2026 pattern News 12 described, nine burglaries in about six hours in one night, tells a shop owner something useful: the people doing it are not planning to stay. In the Greenpoint bakery break-in at the start of that series, they pried the front with a crowbar and took cash. In July 2025 a crew on Kent Avenue, on the Greenpoint–Williamsburg border, used a crowbar on a store’s front door and took more than $300,000 in clothing. In both cases the store was shut and the street was quiet. An alarm cannot make a door uncrowbarable, but it can make the first ten seconds count, and that depends on three choices we make with every store owner.

The first is where the alarm starts listening. For a shop behind a roll-down gate, we usually put a contact on the gate itself, so the alarm reacts while someone is still lifting it, not after the glass behind it is broken. The glass then gets a glass-break detector, the front door a contact, and the back door, the cellar hatch and any skylight their own sensors. The second is what happens next. A store alarm should report to a monitoring station and to the owner’s phone within seconds over cellular, without relying on the shop’s internet router, which is often the first thing knocked out. The third is the call list. A station that reaches a voicemail box at two in the morning has not helped anyone, so we make sure the first two names on the list are people who answer and live or work nearby.

Groceries, bakeries and corner stores on the avenues

Food stores on Graham Avenue and Grand Street keep cash, cigarettes, lottery and often an ATM, and they open early and close late. Inside, we protect the places a thief heads for: the counter and the register area with a motion detector placed so the coolers and the door are not in its view, a shock sensor on the wall or cabinet behind the ATM or safe, and a contact on the office or stockroom door. The cellar is often the weakest part of the building, reached by a sidewalk hatch from outside and by a stair from inside, and sometimes shared with the apartments upstairs for meters and storage. We sort out who uses it before deciding how to protect it, because a sensor on a door that residents open every day will be switched off within a week.

Venues, galleries and event spaces

A venue or event space has two lives. During a show it is full, the doors are staffed, and the alarm is off except for the rooms that should never be open to the public: the office, the bar stock cage, the backline storage and the cash room. After load-out it is empty, often for twelve hours or more, with expensive equipment inside. We set these buildings up with areas, so the office and stock rooms can stay armed through an event while the hall is open, and so the whole building can be armed by the last manager out with one code. The side and stage doors crews use for load-in get contacts that report even during an event if they are opened outside the agreed hours.

Galleries are different again. Their risk tends to be daytime, during openings and open-studios weekends, when the doors are open and the room is full of strangers, and nighttime, when the work hanging on the wall is worth more than anything in a typical store. The night side is handled with contacts and motion detection placed so that moving walls between shows do not block the view of the entry path. The day side is mostly about staff: a desk that is never left unattended, a panic button or a fob for whoever is on duty, and a camera that records the door.

Warehouses, commissaries and contractors’ shops

The industrial park businesses we see most are distributors, contractors and food businesses, including commissary kitchens that serve the food trucks parked on the surrounding blocks. Their buildings are usually simple, one or two big rooms with a roll-up door, a man door and a roof, and their alarms are simple too, as long as nothing is left out. We fit heavy-duty contacts to roll-up doors, protect skylights and roof hatches, put the office and the tool room or walk-in on separate zones, and add opening and closing reports so the owner sees when the first employee arrived and when the last one left. In a building where trucks and generators are worth more than the contents, a camera on the curb or yard, on the same app, does what the building alarm cannot.

Compliance notes for East Williamsburg businesses and loft buildings

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes of going off. We set the cutoff well inside that limit on every system.
  • Chronic alarms are a noise matter. NYC 311 directs people to call 911 for an alarm that is sounding now, and treats chronic alarms as a Department of Environmental Protection noise complaint; DEP says an inspector must observe the condition in person before a summons is issued. A well-zoned alarm with a short cutoff keeps your building off that list.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, with 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431, and you can check any installer with the Department of State.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a unit or a shop, but it does not replace a building fire alarm system that a loft building, venue or factory may be required to have, and it must never be tied into one without the building owner’s fire alarm contractor.
  • Loft Law buildings have their own obligations. The Loft Board oversees interim multiple dwellings that must meet minimum standards of safety and fire protection while they are legalized. Nothing a tenant installs may lock, slow or obstruct an exit, a stair or a fire door, which is why we use contacts and never locking hardware on those doors.
  • Insurers may ask for paperwork. Brick Underground reported that most insurers credit a monitored burglar, fire or low-temperature alarm and usually want a certificate of installation from the installer. We provide one on request after the system is live.

Alarm Installation in East Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Loft Law and converted factory buildings

Our building is still an interim multiple dwelling while the owner works through Loft Board legalization. Can a tenant put in an alarm now, or should we wait until the work is done?

You do not need to wait. A tenant’s alarm sits inside your own unit, uses wireless sensors on the door and windows you control, and does not touch the building’s fire alarm, stairs, fire doors or wiring, so it does not get in the way of the owner’s legalization work. If construction later replaces your windows or door, we move or replace the affected sensors on a service visit. Tell your landlord or managing agent before we come, and we will send them a description of the work and a certificate of insurance if they ask.

The back door of our loft opens onto a freight hall the building uses as a second way out. How is a door like that alarmed without making it harder to get out in a fire?

It gets a contact, which only reports whether the door is open or shut, and nothing that locks or slows it. The door keeps whatever hardware the building requires, and the alarm simply tells you, and the monitoring station if you have one, when it opens while the system is armed. If the household uses that door from time to time, we can set it to chime when it opens while disarmed, so you know it was used and can check it is shut again.

Our loft is one long room with windows at both ends. Where does a motion detector go so it covers the floor without looking straight at the windows?

Usually high on a side wall or in a corner, aimed along the length of the room toward the doors rather than at the glass, so sunlight and headlights through the windows do not reach it directly. In a very long floor we use two detectors, each covering half, or a detector rated for a longer range. The windows themselves are then covered by contacts or by a glass-break detector, so the motion detector is the inside layer rather than the only one.

Studios, galleries and rehearsal rooms

A ventilation duct and an old boarded-over louver in our studio wall are both big enough to crawl through. Can openings like that be put on the alarm?

Yes, and in a former factory they often should be. A burglar lowered himself through an air vent into an office on Randolph Street in 2016, so it is not a theoretical route. If the opening has a grille or a panel that would have to be removed, a contact or a small shock sensor goes on it; if it cannot be sensed directly, a motion detector covers the floor below it, so anyone who comes through is detected as soon as they land. We list every such opening on the quote with what we propose for it.

Our building’s street door stands open for the whole open-studios weekend. How should we use our studio alarm while hundreds of visitors walk the halls?

Keep the studio alarm off while you are in the room, arm it every time you step out even for a few minutes, and arm it fully at night, when the building is still full of strangers’ footprints but nobody should be in your space. The 2012 open-studios weekend on Bogart Street saw a laptop and phone taken from an unattended front desk and a ring from a jewelry studio, which is the kind of loss that happens in the minutes a room is left open. A key fob makes arming on the way out one press, and a camera at the studio door gives you a record of who came in during the day.

We run a rehearsal floor of twelve rooms. Can one panel tell us which room was opened, instead of one alarm for the whole floor?

Yes. The panel is divided into areas, one for each room or group of rooms, each with its own contact on the door and its own codes, plus a common area for the corridor, the lounge and the office. The history then shows which room opened, at what time and with which code, and a room whose tenant forgot to arm it can be armed from the app. Larger buildings may need more than one panel, which we work out on the survey.

Our gallery hangs a new show every month and the temporary walls move with it. Do the sensors have to be moved each time?

The door and window sensors never move, because the perimeter stays where it is. Motion detectors are mounted high on fixed walls and aimed along the routes from the doors into the room, not across the hanging walls, so most re-hangs do not affect them. After a major change of layout, we recommend walking the room with the system in test mode to confirm a moved wall has not blocked a detector’s view, and we can do that check on a service visit if you prefer.

Shops, bars and venues

A crew hit nine businesses in Williamsburg in about six hours on one night in March 2026. If our Graham Avenue shop were one of them, who would hear about it first, and how quickly?

With central station monitoring, the station receives the signal over cellular within seconds of the first sensor tripping, calls the first people on your list, and asks for police dispatch if it cannot rule out a false alarm. With phone alerts only, you get the notification at the same moment and decide what to do yourself. Either way, the speed depends on where the first sensor is, which is why we put one on the gate rather than waiting for the glass.

The apartments above our Graham Avenue shop share our cellar for their meters and storage. How do we alarm a cellar that tenants are allowed into?

We protect the routes from the cellar into the shop, not the cellar itself. The stair or door between the cellar and the store gets a contact and the shop side gets motion detection, while the cellar stays open to the residents for their meters and storage. If the cellar also has a sidewalk hatch, that hatch can be on the shop’s alarm, as long as the building owner agrees and the residents do not use it.

Our venue keeps the bar stock and the bands’ backline in one locked cage room. Can that room stay armed through a show while the rest of the building is open?

Yes. The cage room becomes its own area with its own code, given only to the managers who need it, and it stays armed when the hall is disarmed for the show. If it is opened during the night without that code, the alarm sounds and reports like any other break-in, and the history shows which manager opened it and when.

Warehouses and the industrial park

The food trucks on Johnson Avenue were hit around seven in the morning, not in the middle of the night. Should our warehouse alarm still be armed at that hour?

Yes, until the first employee arrives and disarms it. In the industrial park the street is often busiest with deliveries early in the morning, but that does not mean anyone is watching your building, and the May 2026 thefts outside 599 Johnson Avenue happened in daylight. We can set the panel to remind you if it has not been armed by a set time at night and to report the first disarm of the morning, so the owner sees when the building opened.

Our one-story warehouse has no residential neighbors nearby. Is a loud outdoor siren worth having when nobody will hear it?

An outdoor siren on an empty industrial block mostly tells the burglar the alarm has gone off, which can shorten the visit but rarely brings help. The part that brings help is the report to the monitoring station and your phone, so we put the budget there first. An indoor siren, loud inside the building and limited by the city’s fifteen-minute cutoff, is usually enough, and a strobe outside helps responders find the right door on a block of similar buildings.

Renters and co-op apartments

I live in the Lindsay Park co-op. Does the board have to approve an alarm inside my apartment?

Check your proprietary lease and house rules, because co-ops differ, but a wireless alarm installed entirely inside your apartment, with nothing mounted in the hallway and nothing connected to the building’s systems, is the kind of work most boards treat as an ordinary alteration or do not regulate at all. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in East Williamsburg

If you ask a search engine’s AI summary or a chatbot about alarms here, you tend to get advice written for a suburban house, plus price ranges averaged from markets that look nothing like North Brooklyn. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, phrased the way they usually appear, and what we see on actual East Williamsburg jobs.

“Most burglars force a door, so start with strong locks and door sensors.”

Forced entry is common across the country; ConsumerAffairs, citing the FBI, puts it at more than half of burglaries. But that leaves a large share that are not forced, and in a neighborhood full of shared buildings that share shows. The reported cases here include a burglar dropping through an air vent on Randolph Street, gear taken from a Ten Eyck Street rehearsal room whose door was undamaged, a Thames Street apartment burglarized without forced entry, and a generator lifted through a food truck’s open window on Johnson Avenue.

Good locks and door contacts still matter, and every system we install has them. The correction is about what comes next. An East Williamsburg alarm also needs coverage of the openings that are not doors, and it needs a code for every person who can open the door legitimately, so that an opening with a key but no code is treated as an intrusion and an opening with a code is recorded under a name.

In practice, that means the survey takes longer than people expect and the sensor list includes items such as a roof hatch, a skylight or a duct grille. It rarely means the system costs much more. The extra items are usually one or two sensors, and a motion detector placed on the right route covers several openings at once.

“Burglaries are going up, so you need the biggest package available.”

The 90th Precinct’s burglary count is higher this year than last, 196 against 181 through mid-September 2026. A summary that stops there sounds alarming. The same CompStat report shows burglary down 79.5 percent over 33 years and down 15.5 percent over two years, and robbery down 19.6 percent this year. Numbers of this size move by a dozen incidents either way, and they say nothing about your particular door.

What decides the size of an alarm is the building: how many openings can be reached, how many people share it, and whether it stands empty overnight. A studio with one door and no windows to the outside needs a panel, a contact and a motion detector. A warehouse with three roll-up doors, skylights and a yard needs far more. Neither is decided by a precinct trend line.

We would rather install a small system that is armed every night than a large one that is ignored. If an owner asks for more than the building needs, we say so on the quote, and we explain what each sensor protects so you can take items off.

“A Wi-Fi alarm is fine; everyone has internet now.”

Plenty of self-install systems report over the home’s internet connection. In a loft or studio building, that connection is often shared, managed by the landlord or a tenant who left, or switched off when the power goes. An alarms sales manager told Brick Underground the point plainly: “you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.”

The panels we install report over cellular, with a battery that keeps them running through a power cut, and can use Wi-Fi as a second path where it is reliable. In factory buildings with thick masonry, we measure the cellular signal at the planned panel location and move the panel if it is weak, rather than discovering the problem after a real alarm.

For businesses on the avenues, the same logic applies to the router. It usually sits in the shop, where a burglar can unplug it, so an alarm that depends on it can be silenced in seconds. Cellular reporting removes that weak point.

“Renters can’t install alarms, because they don’t own the building.”

This one is simply wrong for most East Williamsburg renters. A tenant’s alarm, in a walk-up, a loft, a studio or a large complex, can be entirely wireless and entirely inside the tenant’s own space, with sensors attached to the inside of the door and windows, a panel in a closet and nothing wired into the building.

What renters cannot do is change the building: drill into common walls, mount devices in shared halls, connect to the building’s fire alarm or put locking hardware on exits. A properly installed tenant alarm does none of those things, and comes off without a mark when the lease ends.

For studio tenants, it is often the only realistic option, since the landlord’s building systems do not cover individual studios at all. The tenant owns the equipment, the codes and the history, and takes them along to the next space.

“A loud outside siren is the best deterrent.”

On a residential block, an outdoor siren may bring neighbors to the window. On an industrial block of East Williamsburg at three in the morning, there may be nobody within earshot, and in a loft building with dozens of households, a siren that wakes everyone without bringing help just makes enemies. The city also requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut itself off within fifteen minutes.

What brings help is the report: a signal to a monitoring station and to the owner’s phone within seconds of the first opening, with a call list of people who answer. We put the budget there first, and add a siren sized to the building, usually indoors, with a cutoff well inside the city limit.

A visible strobe or a well-placed outdoor sounder still has a place on some warehouses and shops, mostly to help responders find the right door on a block of similar buildings. We decide on that building by building.

“Sound sensors don’t work in noisy places like studios or bars.”

Glass-break detectors listen for the particular sound pattern of breaking glass, not for loudness, and a good one, correctly placed and tested, ignores music, drums and conversation. The ones that cause trouble are usually placed too close to a speaker, set to the most sensitive setting, or never tested once installed.

In rehearsal rooms, bars and venues we mount the detector where it can hear the glass it is protecting and is not facing the sound system, and we test it with the room in use. Most of the time the alarm is armed when the room is quiet anyway, which is when glass-break detection matters.

Where glass-break detection really does not fit, such as a room with no windows, we do not install it. Motion detection and door contacts cover rooms like that, and the quote will say so.

“Professional installation means a long monitoring contract.”

Many national dealers do bundle equipment into a multi-year monitoring agreement, and Reddit threads on home security are full of people working out what they really paid over three or five years. That is a business model, not a requirement of professional installation.

We sell the installation at the price on our Brooklyn list and offer central station monitoring through our partners as an option, month to month, from $30 a month. Plenty of studio tenants and renters start with alerts to their own phones and add monitoring later, and some businesses need monitoring from day one for insurance.

If you already have a system under a contract with another company, read the cancellation terms before you replace it. We can tell you what in your existing equipment can be reused, so you are not paying twice for the same sensors.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Look Up Before You Look at the Door

The habit our technicians bring to East Williamsburg surveys is to look up as soon as they walk into a space. In an apartment in a Brooklyn rowhouse, the ceiling is just a ceiling. In a former factory or a one-story warehouse here, it often has a skylight, a roof hatch, an exhaust fan, a duct running to the roof, or a patch of plywood over an opening nobody remembers. The 2016 Randolph Street burglary, where a man came down through an air vent into an office, is the reason we never skip that look. Then we look at the far walls for doors that nobody uses, then at the windows for fire escapes, flat roofs and neighboring buildings, and only then at the front door.

The second habit is to ask how many keys exist before writing anything down. In a studio shared by four people, a rehearsal room on a booking system, or a shop where the night manager changes every few months, the answer is usually more than the owner thinks. Each of those keys becomes a code on the alarm, or a reason to change the lock. A survey here that does not include that conversation is only half a survey.

How many sensors does a typical East Williamsburg studio or loft need?
+

A studio with one door and no reachable windows can be protected with a door contact and one motion detector. A loft usually needs contacts on two doors, contacts or glass-break coverage on the windows that can be reached from a fire escape or a roof, a sensor on any hatch or skylight, and one or two motion detectors. What should we do in the first two weeks after the alarm goes in?
+

Arm it every time the space is empty, even for short trips, and notice anything that feels awkward, such as an exit delay that is too short for lowering a gate or a motion detector that catches a heater. Make sure everyone who holds a key has their code and the app if they need it. Put the building operator or front desk first if they are on site at night, then the band member who lives closest, then the others. Why would a quote for a big loft come in lower than one for a much smaller studio?
+

Because the price follows the openings and the people, not the floor area. A large loft with one door and street-facing windows out of reach may need only a few sensors, while a small studio with a roof hatch, a fire-escape window, a door to a freight hall and five key holders needs more devices and more programming. The quote shows each item, so the reason for the difference is visible.

Will the sensors leave marks on brick walls, timber beams or steel doors when we move out of a rented loft?
+

Wireless sensors in rented spaces are fixed with adhesive or a small screw into the door or window frame you control, not into masonry or structural timber, and they come off with little or nothing to patch. On steel doors we use mounts designed for metal. If a panel needs to be screwed to a wall, we choose a spot inside a closet or behind furniture and leave the anchors in place or fill the holes when we remove it.

Besides break-ins, what else can an alarm in a loft, shop or workshop watch for?
+

The same panel can carry monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, heat sensors for rooms with kilns or equipment, low-temperature sensors where pipes could freeze, and water sensors under sinks, water heaters and roof drains. Each one reports through the same app and, if you have monitoring, the same station. These are separate from any building fire alarm, which belongs to the building owner and its own contractor.

Why do you ask about every key holder before quoting an alarm?
+

Because in shared buildings many losses happen without a forced door, and the alarm can only tell a legitimate opening from an intrusion if each legitimate person has a code. Knowing how many people hold keys tells us how many codes to set up, whether the space needs separate areas, and whether a lock should be changed so that old keys no longer work. It also helps us plan the call list, so the station reaches someone who can actually respond.
