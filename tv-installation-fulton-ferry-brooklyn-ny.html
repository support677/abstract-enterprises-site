The Neighborhood

Why Fulton Ferry Properties Need TV Installation

Fulton Ferry is one of the smallest neighborhoods we cover and one of the most particular. It is a handful of blocks between the river, Furman Street, Old Fulton Street, Front Street and Water Street, sitting under the Brooklyn Bridge between Brooklyn Heights and DUMBO, inside Community Board 2 and ZIP code 11201. Boats crossed from here to Manhattan from the 1640s until the ferry stopped in 1924, and the buildings that survive were put up to serve that traffic: warehouses, insurance and railroad offices, stores with lodgings above, a bank vault, a hotel. Very little of it was built as housing. That single fact decides more about a TV installation here than the size of the screen or the brand of the bracket, because a wall that was built to hold up a warehouse floor behaves nothing like the drywall partition a generic mounting guide assumes.

The homes came later, one conversion at a time. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report names the conversion of the old Brooklyn City Railroad Company building into apartments as the first real sign of new life in the district. That building, at 8 Old Fulton Street, went up in 1860 and 1861 with a cast-iron ground floor and brick above, and Brownstoner's history says the architect David Morton turned it into loft apartments in the 1970s, about ten at first, two to a floor; it is a co-op of eleven apartments today. The Eagle Warehouse at 28 Old Fulton Street followed. Built in 1893 and 1894 to store furniture and silverware, with vaults in the cellar, it became a co-op of roughly eighty-five apartments around 1980, and CityRealty lists it with nine floors, an elevator, a live-in superintendent and a doorman from 8 a.m. to midnight. A former stable at 7 Everit Street became a seven-apartment co-op in the early 1980s, still with its old hoist doorway on the third floor. Each of those buildings kept its masonry, its beams and its odd floor plans, and each is now somebody's living room.

Then there are the buildings that are new. Inside the district, Landmarks approved a four-story building with three apartments and a restaurant space at 9 Old Fulton Street, and a one-story retail building on the long-empty lot at 20 Old Fulton Street. On the park side of Furman Street, Pierhouse at 90 Furman Street opened around 2015 and 2016: a ten-story condominium of about 106 apartments by Marvel Architects, with a 24-hour doorman and residents' use of the 1 Hotel Brooklyn Bridge rooftop pool next door. Those new homes bring the walls most installers expect, but also long walls of glass facing the river and the bridge, management offices with rules, and in some cases concrete. Between a cast-iron storefront from the 1860s and a condo from the 2010s, a TV technician in Fulton Ferry can meet five different wall materials in one afternoon.

The neighborhood around those walls is busy in a way few residential blocks are. Old Fulton Street is one of only four ways into Brooklyn Bridge Park, which the park reported was visited by nearly five million people in its 2023 season and which stays open from 6 a.m. to 1 a.m. The pizza lines on Old Fulton and Front Streets are famous, the NYC Ferry has docked at Fulton Ferry Landing since a $4.7 million rebuild reopened it in June 2021, and on the Fourth of July the city now runs a ticketed fireworks viewing in the park with the Old Fulton Street entrance controlled. For a TV installation that means a van that cannot always park, a delivery that has to be timed, and a doorman or super who already has a lot of strangers to deal with. We plan the visit around all of it, and we ask about it on the first call rather than on the morning of the job.

And there is the water. Hurricane Sandy reached these streets in October 2012. The Brooklyn Daily Eagle reported a building owner on lower Old Fulton Street pumping out a basement "flooded from floor to ceiling with murky seawater," a restaurateur at 7 Old Fulton Street who said "I've been pumping the basement all night," and water five feet deep in a lobby on Main Street. The River Café at 1 Water Street was so badly damaged that it did not reopen until February 2014. Buildings rebuilt since then have moved their electrical equipment upward: Untapped Cities reported that new park buildings were designed "with all mechanicals and electricity housed above the new flood plain line," and The Real Deal reported that Empire Stores keeps its transformer vaults on the seventh floor. A TV installer cannot change a building's flood risk, but we can make sure the parts of a home theater that are easiest to ruin, the cable box, the router, the receiver and the power strip, do not live on the floor of a ground-level room or in a cellar closet.

Six Fulton Ferry facts that shape a TV job before we choose a bracket

  • Almost everything is old masonry. The National Register district counts fifteen contributing buildings from 1830 to 1895, brick buildings of two to four stories plus the eight-story Eagle Warehouse. Brick or stone is a +$65 add-on on our list, and it is the wall we expect first here.
  • The homes were made out of other buildings. Warehouse, railroad-office and stable conversions from the 1970s and 1980s mean beams, columns, thick bearing walls and partitions added decades after the shell. Two walls in one room can need two different fixings.
  • Cast iron sits at street level. The railroad building and the Long Island Safe Deposit Company building have cast-iron fronts, and Landmarks guidance says equipment should not be attached through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Inside, we treat cast iron as a surface we never drill.

The Commission says an interior job needs its permit only when the work needs a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark. A TV on an inside wall usually triggers none of those; a dish on the roof is a different matter.
  • The ground floors have flooded. Sandy filled cellars on lower Old Fulton Street with seawater, and park buildings have since moved their electrics above the flood line. We keep TV electronics off the floor in ground-level rooms and cellars.
  • The street has a calendar. Nearly five million park visits a year, ferry arrivals at the landing, weekend pizza lines and a controlled fireworks entrance on July 4 all affect parking, deliveries and timing. We book around them.

Same-Day Repair in Fulton Ferry

Need Repair in Fulton Ferry? A Screen Pulling Out of Old Brick, a Bracket Bolted Into Mortar, or a Media Cabinet That Sat in Wet Air

The urgent TV calls from buildings like Fulton Ferry's tend to come in three kinds. The first is a screen that has begun to sag or tip on one side because the bracket was fixed into the mortar joints instead of the brick. Nineteenth-century mortar is softer than the brick around it, often much softer, and an anchor set into a joint can feel tight on the day and slowly crumble its way loose under the weight of a screen, especially one on an arm that gets pulled out every evening. The second is a bracket that a previous owner hung on a partition added during a conversion, using fixings meant for a different wall, so the plate has started to lift away. The third is quieter: a set of electronics that has been sitting on the floor of a ground-level room or a cellar media space, with cables that corroded at the connectors and a power strip that has seen damp air for years.

If a screen is moving, do not push it back against the wall. If two people can lift it safely, take the weight off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly anchored mount in the same visit, the new mount is priced by the wall it goes on, with the take-down added. If the problem is in the wall itself, a cracked face on a brick, a loose course of masonry or a partition that is coming away from the old wall behind it, we say so plainly and stop, because that is work for a mason or the building, and a board or landlord will want to know about it before anything is fixed back to it. For electronics that have lived in damp air, we test each device and each cable before reconnecting anything, and we move the lot up onto a shelf or into a cabinet as part of the job.

The storage-warehouse co-op: masonry built for weight, and a building with a front desk

A warehouse built in the 1890s to hold furniture and silverware was designed to carry heavy loads, and its outer and bearing walls are correspondingly massive. When the Eagle Warehouse and buildings like it were converted to apartments, those walls stayed, and in many apartments the longest wall in the living room is one of them. For a TV, a wall like that is excellent at holding weight and hard work to drill. It takes a hammer drill and masonry anchors, and on our list it carries the brick or stone add-on of $65. The thickness that makes it strong also means no cable can be fished inside it, so before any drilling we agree where the box, the console and the power will be, and whether the cables will drop straight down into furniture or run in a paintable cord cover.

The other walls in these apartments are usually partitions built during the conversion, and they can be plaster, gypsum block or later drywall. We scan them and confirm with a pilot hole before choosing a fixing. The building itself shapes the visit as much as the wall. A co-op with an elevator, a doorman from morning to midnight and a live-in super will usually want to know who is coming, when, and with what insurance. We send the certificate of insurance before the visit, confirm whether the elevator needs pads or a reservation, and if your TV is being delivered separately we suggest timing it for desk hours so the box is not left in a lobby that visitors to the park walk past all day.

The railroad-office loft: high ceilings, exposed joists and a cast-iron front you never drill

The Brooklyn City Railroad Company building is the model for this type: brick upper floors over a cast-iron ground floor, an office building that became a co-op of large lofts. A Brownstoner listing for one ground-level unit described high ceilings, exposed joists, a mezzanine and a lower-level recreation room, sitting a couple of feet above the sidewalk. That layout raises questions an ordinary apartment does not. A screen on a high wall in a double-height room has to be placed so the people on the main floor can watch without tipping their heads back, which usually means mounting it lower than the scale of the room suggests and choosing a tilting bracket. Exposed joists are beautiful and often tempting as a place to hang things, but a TV should go on a wall rated for it, not on a ceiling joist of unknown condition, unless a structural professional has looked at it.

The cast iron is a firm rule for us. Cast iron is strong in compression and brittle under a point load, and a hole drilled into an old cast-iron pier or column can crack it. Landmarks guidance for exterior equipment says not to attach through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and we apply the same caution on the inside face of the same iron. If the only wall facing the couch is the inside of a cast-iron front, we look for a brick pier beside it, a partition that can be built out, or a floor-standing solution. In the brick upper floors, the work is ordinary masonry mounting at the $65 brick rate, with the usual care to set anchors in sound brick rather than old joints.

The 1830s commercial row: stores below, homes above, and walls older than anything around them

Commercial Row on Cadman Plaza West was built between 1836 and 1839 as stores with lodgings above, and the Historic Districts Council notes that the Long Island Insurance Company building of 1834 at Front Street may be the oldest surviving office building in the city. Buildings of this age and type typically have brick bearing walls, plaster finishes inside, and floors that have settled over nearly two centuries. On our list, plaster on framing is a $45 add-on and brick is $65; which one applies depends on whether the TV wall is an outer or party wall, usually brick under the plaster, or an interior partition, usually plaster on wood lath. A pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate tells us which before anything else happens.

Two other details matter in these buildings. Floors that are no longer level fool people into mounting a screen parallel to the floor or the ceiling line, so we level the bracket to true and check it by eye from the couch before tightening. And with a restaurant or shop on the ground floor, the timing of noisy work matters to the people downstairs as well as to you. Drilling into brick is loud for a few minutes at a time, and in a building where a dining room opens at lunch, we try to finish the drilling before it does.

The small converted co-op: a few apartments, no front desk and stairs that decide the size of the box

Fulton Ferry has a number of small buildings that became co-ops of a handful of apartments, like the former stable at 7 Everit Street with its seven apartments. In buildings like this there is often no doorman, the super may be a shareholder or a part-time contractor, and the board is a few neighbors who know each other well. That makes the human side easy and the logistics harder. Someone has to let the technician in, someone has to know which neighbor is home, and the TV has to come up the stairs. A 65-inch or larger screen in its box is long and awkward on a narrow stair, and the safest way to get it up is in its original packaging, carried on edge where the turns allow. We ask about the stair width and the turns before the visit, and if the TV has already been unboxed we bring blankets to wrap it for the carry.

Boards in small co-ops vary a great deal in what they expect. Some have a written alteration agreement, others a single page of house rules, and some none at all. Brick Underground notes that alteration agreements typically protect structures such as load-bearing walls and may set the days and hours contractors can work, and that where no agreement exists it is worth asking the board for its rules in writing. Hanging a TV is usually minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall kit can be treated differently. We are glad to send the board a short description of the job in advance, so the answer comes before the visit rather than on the stairs.

The Water Street warehouse office: a tenant suite inside a giant brick building

The largest buildings in the neighborhood are the old warehouses along Water Street. Empire Stores is now roughly half a million square feet of offices and shops in a nineteenth-century warehouse, with a glass-and-steel addition on the roof; West Elm has its headquarters there. The Tobacco Warehouse next door holds St. Ann's Warehouse. A screen in a building like this goes into a tenant's suite, and the suite sits inside a building with its own landlord, its own contractor rules and, in the case of the park's buildings, its own relationship with Brooklyn Bridge Park. Before any business job here we confirm in writing who has approved the work, which hours we can work in, and who should be named on the certificate of insurance.

The walls are a mix of the original brick, steel and timber of the warehouse and the partitions of the fit-out, often metal-framed drywall or glass. Meeting-room displays usually go on the fit-out partitions, where an in-wall kit can work if the cavity allows it, while lobby or common-area screens may go on original brick at the brick rate. Cable paths in these buildings frequently run above ceilings or in conduit along walls that cannot be opened, so we look at how the suite is already wired before suggesting where a screen should go.

The new building on an old lot: modern walls, a wall of glass and a management office

The newest homes around Fulton Ferry include the condominiums at Pierhouse on Furman Street, opened around 2015 and 2016, and the new four-story building with apartments above a restaurant space at 9 Old Fulton Street. In buildings of this generation the interior walls are usually drywall, on wood or light-gauge steel framing, and some walls may be concrete. Drywall on wood studs is the base case on our list at $179.99 for screens up to 55 inches. Steel framing and concrete need different fixings, and we price them from your photos before you book rather than guess.

What changes most in a new building by the park is the light. Living rooms with a long wall of glass toward the river and the bridge leave fewer solid walls, and the one that faces the couch may be shared with a corridor or a neighbor. The afternoon and evening light off the water moves across a room, and a screen opposite the windows can be hard to watch at certain hours. We mark the screen position with painter's tape and look at it at the time of day you usually watch, and where glare is unavoidable we suggest a tilting bracket, a slightly different wall or a position where the TV turns away from the window. A building with a 24-hour doorman and a management office will usually want insurance paperwork and set working hours, and we handle both before the day.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Converted Fulton Ferry Wall Before the Drill Comes Out

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like the ones in Fulton Ferry. They describe our practice, not a story about any particular address or customer.

Find out what the building used to be. A converted warehouse, a railroad office and a stable each leave different walls behind. The first question on the phone is often the simplest: what was the building before it was apartments? If you do not know, the building name or the address usually tells us, because most of the district's history is published.

Tell the shell from the conversion. In a converted building, the outer walls, the party walls and some bearing walls are original masonry, and most of the rest was built inside the shell later. A knock along the wall at screen height separates them quickly: a dull, hard sound for masonry, a lighter one for a partition. The scanner then shows framing or metal in a partition. On original brick it often shows nothing useful at all.

One pilot hole, hidden. The technician drills a single small hole where the bracket plate will later cover it and reads the dust and the feel. Red grit means brick, grey grit block or concrete, white powder and then a void a hollow partition, and white powder followed by a hard, chalky resistance often means plaster over gypsum block. That hole decides the anchor. If it changes the add-on from what was quoted, you hear the new total before any more drilling.

Brick, not mortar. Old mortar is soft. Anchors go into sound brick, away from the edges, and on soft or worn brick we spread the load over more fixings. If the brick face crumbles at the pilot hole, we stop and talk about another wall.

Look down before looking up. Before mounting anything in a ground-level room, the technician looks at where the electronics will sit. If the plan has a router, a box or a power strip on the floor, we suggest a shelf or a cabinet instead, and we leave enough slack in the cables to make that possible.

Label everything. Every cable behind the screen and in the console gets a label at both ends. In a building where the board, the super or the next owner may be the next person to open the cabinet, that is the difference between a five-minute fix and an afternoon of guessing.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Cast Iron, Gypsum Block or Glass: What Holds a TV in a Fulton Ferry Wall and What It Costs

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV of up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, with the bracket sold separately. In Fulton Ferry, that wall is the exception rather than the rule, so we explain the wall add-ons before anything else. Each exists because the wall needs different tools, different fixings or more time, not because an old building is an excuse to charge more.

Solid brick (+$65). The outer walls, party walls and bearing walls of the district's nineteenth-century buildings are brick, sometimes exposed and sometimes under plaster. Brick holds a TV very well when the anchor goes into the brick itself. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and holes kept back from the edges of each brick. Old Brooklyn brick varies in hardness, and softer or more worn brick gets more fixings at lower load each. The brick and stone add-on is the same $65 whether the brick is exposed or plastered over.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). In the older commercial rows, interior partitions are often plaster on lath over wood framing. Plaster holds well in compression and badly in pull, so a plug in the plaster alone can feel firm and fail over months. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and lath into the framing behind, drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward. Plaster laid directly onto brick is priced at the brick rate, because the brick is what holds the anchor.

Gypsum block and other pre-war partitions (priced after the pilot hole). A long-running StreetEasy Talk thread about mounting a TV in a pre-war building describes the wall many owners meet: plaster over gypsum block, hard and cement-like to a nail, and messy to cut into. These walls are neither brick nor hollow framing. They need anchors designed for that kind of block, set slowly so the block face does not break away, and sometimes a backing board to spread the load. Because a scanner reads them poorly and a pilot hole reads them well, we quote them once we know, and we tell you before going further.

Concrete (priced from photos). Some newer buildings and many commercial fit-outs have concrete walls or slabs. SlashGear's guide to concrete mounting lists the tools, a hammer drill, masonry bits and concrete anchors set deep enough to hold, and warns that "you may run into rebar," a steel bar inside the concrete. When a hole meets steel, we stop and move the bracket slightly rather than cut through it, because the bar is part of the structure.

Drywall on wood or steel framing (base price for wood). Most of the new apartments around Pier 1 and on Old Fulton Street have drywall partitions. Wood studs are the base case. Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The request after "mount it" is almost always "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out near the floor. National Electrical Code section 400.12, as EC&M's code column summarizes it, says flexible cords "are not permitted to be run through holes in walls" and "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord inside a wall can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and never be inspected.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list including the kit. It puts a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and network cables. It needs a hollow cavity between the two openings, so it suits drywall and plaster-on-lath partitions and does not suit brick, gypsum block or concrete. On those walls, and in Fulton Ferry that is most TV walls, the honest choices are a paintable cord cover at $59, which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match the wall or the brick's mortar color, or a position directly above a console so the cables drop straight down out of sight. The StreetEasy thread's advice from people who had tried it matches ours: a painted cover is "not ideal but it isn't bad," and channeling a power cord into a pre-war wall is the thing to avoid.

Exposed brick deserves a word of its own. Many owners do not want anything on the face of a brick wall, cover or not. In that case the cleanest answer is usually to put the TV on the brick with the console directly below, keep the cable drop short and straight behind the screen, and bring power from a nearby outlet along the baseboard where furniture hides it. We show you the options with tape on the wall before choosing.

Boards, Landlords and Landmarks

Co-op Boards, Leases and the Historic District: Mounting a TV When Others Have a Say

In a neighborhood made of co-ops, rentals and a landmark district, permission comes up on a large share of TV calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and what we have learned from doing this work, so you can ask the right person the right question before the visit.

The landmark district. Every building in the Fulton Ferry Historic District is regulated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which worries people about anything they do. For TV work inside an apartment the worry is usually misplaced. The Commission's own guidance says "Most exterior changes to the front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require review by LPC," but that interior work needs an LPC permit only "when the work requires a permit from the Buildings Department," "when work on the interior affects the exterior," or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. The Buildings Department, for its part, says the code defines minor alterations and ordinary repairs that need no permit, and it lists painting and plastering among them. Hanging a TV on an inside wall and running cables in a cover is the kind of interior work that normally falls outside both. Things that touch the outside of the building are different: a satellite dish or antenna on a landmarked roof, a cable run on the facade, or anything fixed to the street front. The Commission's guidebook says rooftop equipment should not be visible, with staff able to approve a minimally visible installation only when invisibility is impossible. If your plan includes anything outside, we stop at the building line and point you to the Commission, at 347-934-8335, before any work.

The co-op board. Most Fulton Ferry homes are co-op apartments, and a co-op's alteration agreement or house rules are what actually govern TV work. Brick Underground's explainer says these agreements typically protect building-wide structures such as load-bearing walls, may set the days and hours contractors can work, and in the example it gives require contractor liability insurance of at least $1 million. In a converted warehouse, the TV wall may well be one of those bearing walls. Drilling a few anchor holes in a masonry bearing wall is a very different thing from opening it, and we never chase or open a bearing wall, but the board decides what its rules mean. Send us the agreement or rules and the insurance requirements, and we send the certificate of insurance and a one-paragraph description of the job for the board or managing agent before the visit.

The lease. If you rent, Brick Underground's Realty Bites column put the question to a New York real estate attorney, who said the first place to look is the lease's alteration provision, and that a ban that is not in the lease "wouldn't be enforceable." A property manager in the same column said a nail-size hole is "fine" but a larger one "is considered damage," and that a tenant who removes a mounted TV must repair significant damage before leaving. A TV bracket on brick leaves four to six anchor holes; a cord cover leaves a few screw holes. Both can be filled, and on brick they can be pointed to match. Patch before you move out, photograph the repaired wall and ask for a move-out inspection; the column also notes that a landlord must itemize deductions and return the rest of the deposit within two weeks.

Lead paint. Many Fulton Ferry interiors were painted long before 1978. NYC HPD's safe work practices apply to housing built before January 1, 1978, and are triggered by work that will "disturb 2 square feet or more of paint in a single room." A few anchor holes are far below that, but cutting openings for an in-wall kit in old painted plaster comes closer, and households with young children should know where they stand. We drill with dust collection at the bit, clean up as we go, and if you have a lead test report for the apartment we plan around it.

Not drilling at all. If your lease is strict, the board is slow, or you expect to move, a TV on its own stand on a good console is a legitimate choice. On our list a TV placed on its stand is $119.99 and a soundbar placed on the console is $79. The CPSC's 2023 warning is the reason we anchor a standing TV to its furniture where children live: it counted about 19,400 emergency-room injuries a year from furniture, TV and appliance tip-overs and said 70 percent of child deaths in those accidents involved a falling television. As its chair, Alex Hoehn-Saric, put it, "Furniture and TV tip-overs remain a major safety hazard in American households."

Walls a warehouse left behind

The walls in our converted storage-warehouse apartment are far thicker than a normal brick wall. Can the cable box sit in the next room, with the HDMI going straight through?

Sometimes, and it is worth asking the board first. A hole through a thick masonry wall is a bigger job than a bracket: it takes a core bit, it makes dust and noise, and in a co-op the wall may be a bearing wall the alteration agreement protects. Where the board agrees and the wall is a suitable interior one, a small, clean pass-through with a grommet on each side lets the box, the receiver and the router live in the next room while only the screen is in the living room. Where it does not agree, the alternatives are a short drop to a console under the screen, a cable in a paintable cover along the baseboard to a nearby room, or a wireless HDMI link for some setups. We price a pass-through after seeing the wall, never on the spot.

The ground floor of our building has an 1860s cast-iron front, and the only free wall facing the sofa is the inside of it. Can a bracket go there?

Not into the iron. Cast iron of that age is strong under load from above but brittle under a point load, and a drilled hole can start a crack in a pier or column that is part of the building's landmarked front. Landmarks guidance for outside work says equipment should not be attached through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and we apply the same caution on the inside. What usually works instead is one of three things: a brick pier or the masonry wall beside the iron, if it faces the seating well enough; a freestanding solution such as the TV on its stand on a solid console, $119.99 on our list; or, with the board's agreement, a framed panel built in front of the iron and fixed only to the floor and the masonry around it. We look at all three with you before deciding.

The interior walls in our conversion feel like cement and a nail just bends. Could that be gypsum block, and does it change the plan?

It could well be. Pre-war partitions were often built of gypsum or clay block with plaster over it, and people in an old StreetEasy Talk thread about mounting a TV in a pre-war building described exactly that: a cement-like wall that will not take a nail and is messy to cut. It changes two things. The fixing has to be an anchor made for that kind of block, drilled slowly so the face does not break out, and sometimes a backing board spreads the load over more points. And it rules out an in-wall power kit in most cases, because there is no hollow cavity to run cable through, so the wires go in a paintable cover or straight down to a console. Our pilot hole tells us which wall it is before the bracket goes up.

Lofts, mezzanines and big rooms

Our ground-level loft has a mezzanine and exposed joists above the living area. Can the TV be placed so it is watchable from the mezzanine as well as the sofa?

Usually one screen can serve both, but it has to favor the main seating. Our loft ceilings are very high and the living area is open to the kitchen. How do we pick a screen size that will not look lost on the wall?

Size depends on the distance from the seat, not the height of the ceiling. Measure from where you sit to the wall, and choose the screen for that distance; a tall wall can make a well-sized TV look small, but making it bigger than the seat distance suits only makes it harder to watch. In high rooms we often suggest placing the TV on a console or low on the wall with a wall-mounted soundbar below it, $129 on our list, so the group reads as one piece rather than a small rectangle floating on a large brick surface. 1. Send photos and the building

The wall, the TV's model sticker, the spot for the box or console, and the building name or cross streets. You get the wall we expect, the add-ons and the total on the Brooklyn list.

2. Settle building access

Doorman or super hours, elevator use, the board's or landlord's paperwork and the certificate of insurance, checked against the park and street calendar.

3. Read the wall

Knock, scan and one pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate to confirm brick, plaster, gypsum block, concrete or drywall. Cast iron is never drilled.

4. Agree position and cable path

Painter's tape marks the screen; you sit where you watch, at the hour you watch; power and signal routes are agreed before drilling.

5. Fix the bracket

Anchors matched to the material, set in the brick rather than the mortar, with the bracket leveled to true rather than to an old floor.

6. Hang the TV and dress the cables

The screen goes up and is leveled; cables go into an in-wall kit, a paintable cover or the console, each labeled at both ends.

7. Test, set up and hand over

Every input, the soundbar, streaming and the network connection tested with you, electronics placed off the floor, the area cleaned and the paperwork left.

FAQ

Fulton Ferry TV Installation FAQ

Is exposed brick charged the same as brick hidden under plaster?
+

Yes. The brick or stone add-on is $65 on our Brooklyn list whether the brick is bare or plastered over, because it is the brick that holds the anchors in both cases. Plaster over framing is the $45 add-on instead.

Can the anchor holes in a brick wall be made to disappear when we sell or move?
+

Mostly. Holes in brick can be filled with a mortar or masonry filler tinted to the brick, and on exposed brick the result is close but rarely invisible up close. Holes under plaster are filled and painted like any other wall. Planning the bracket so its holes land in a few bricks, rather than across many, helps.

Does the fireplace add-on still apply if the fireplace has not worked in decades?
+

Yes. The $130 above-fireplace add-on covers drilling a chimney breast and setting a screen at that height, which is the same work whether or not the fireplace is used. A working fireplace also means checking that heat will not reach the TV.

What exactly does the setup-only visit cover if our TV is already mounted?
+

It covers connecting and setting up the TV and its devices: the smart-TV menus, streaming, the cable box, a soundbar and the network connection, plus tidying and labeling the cables. It is $195 for the first hour on our list, then 30-minute blocks.

Do you need anything from our doorman or super before the day?
+

Usually just a heads-up with our name and arrival window, whether the elevator needs pads or a reservation, and where we may unload. If the building wants a certificate of insurance, we send it ahead so the desk has it when we arrive.

Will you bolt the TV stand or console to the wall if we have small children?
+

We recommend it for any TV on a stand in a home with children, and we fit an anti-tip strap or bracket where the furniture and the wall allow it. On brick, that strap needs a masonry anchor, which we set at the same visit.

How long does a brick-wall mount usually take from arrival to finished?
+

A single TV on brick with a cord cover is normally finished in one visit, including reading the wall, drilling, hanging, dressing cables and testing. Fireplaces, in-wall kits and long carries up stairs add time, and we tell you before booking.

Can you paint the cord cover to match old brick?
+

The $59 cover is paintable, and many owners paint it to match the mortar color or the plaster around the brick, which usually blends better than trying to imitate brick. We can install it primed and ready for you or your painter to finish.

Is it better to wait for the board's answer or put the TV on its stand now?
+

If you want to watch now, the stand setup at $119.99 is a real job, not a stopgap, and nothing it does gets in the way of mounting later. When the board approves, the wall mount is priced on its own and the setup work is already done.

Do you mount TVs outside on a Fulton Ferry roof deck or terrace?
+

We look at it case by case. Anything visible from the street or the park on a landmarked building is exterior work that the Landmarks Commission may review, and wind and salt air off the river are hard on outdoor hardware, so we price outdoor jobs from photos after those questions are answered.

Can you put a second TV in the bedroom on the same visit?
+

Yes. Each TV is priced on its own from the same list by its size, wall and extras, and doing both on one visit saves a second trip through the building and a second round of paperwork for the desk or the board.

What should we have ready when the technician arrives?
+

The TV and its stand or bracket, the remote, your streaming and provider passwords, and clear space of about six feet in front of the wall. If the building needs anything signed or shown at the desk, have that sorted the day before.
