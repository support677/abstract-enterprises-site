The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need Access Control

Kensington sits just south of Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, and its housing comes in layers: brick rowhouses and detached Victorians on the side streets, the duplex-porch two-families that Forgotten NY called almost the signature style of the neighborhood, and the brick apartment houses that line Ocean Parkway and Coney Island Avenue. The six-story elevator buildings went up from the late 1920s to 1941, and a second wave of towers followed on the parkway in the early 1960s. That mix decides what an access control job looks like here. A single front door in Kensington can serve two families, or it can serve ninety-four apartments, one elevator and a live-in super.

Those big buildings are where most of our Kensington door questions come from, and the reason is simple. One lobby door carries the traffic of a small village: residents, home attendants, couriers, grocery drivers, the porter, the oil delivery and every visitor buzzed in from upstairs. A listing for 300 Ocean Parkway, built in 1941 with six floors and ninety-four apartments, names a voice intercom and a live-in super as its service level, with no doorman and no fobs listed. That is a common starting point on the parkway: a building that works because one person knows everyone, and a front door that relies on brass keys that have been copied for decades.

The doors themselves are the second reason. HPD's own records show what goes wrong in Kensington halls. At 415 Beverley Road, the city's violation history lists door and lock items including a compactor room door on a public hall and the entrance to a basement apartment, both written up because they did not close by themselves. Citywide, HPD said in November 2025 that it issued 72,714 self-closing door violations in fiscal year 2025. An electric strike or a reader cannot fix a door that hangs open; the closer, the hinges and the latch come first, and every plan on this page starts there.

The third reason is who lives behind those doors. When the single elevator at 514 Ocean Parkway was out for about three weeks this spring, News 12 reported that a tenant who uses a wheelchair stayed home the whole time. The same report described water coming through the lobby ceiling on and off for nearly two months, leaving mail and packages wet. A lobby that floods from above and an entrance some residents cannot open on their own are both access control problems, even though neither one is about strangers. A good Kensington door system has to keep working through a leak and has to be usable by the residents who most need the door to open for them.

Then there are the doors that open to the public on a timetable. Kensington is one of Brooklyn's most mixed neighborhoods: Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Orthodox Jewish, Uzbek, Latin American, Polish and Ukrainian communities live side by side, and McDonald and Church Avenues were co-named Little Bangladesh in October 2022. Mosques, synagogues and churches sit on the same blocks as the apartment houses. The street in front of the mosque on Chester Avenue, near Louisa Street and Church Avenue, is closed to cars from noon to 3 p.m. on Fridays so the crowd can pray outside. A door that has to be wide open at one hour and firmly locked at another needs a schedule, not a key under the mat.

The last reason is what happens when nobody is watching. Around 1 a.m. on January 25, 2025, a man walked in through the front entrance of Congregation Agudath Sholom of Flatbush on 18th Avenue and took about $15,000 from the donation boxes, according to News 12. Nobody was inside. In 2025, Boro Park 24 reported three overnight break-ins at one building on Church Avenue, through a side window, a forced side door and over a fence. Access control does not stop a rock through a window, and we say so on this page. What it does is keep the doors that are supposed to be locked actually locked, tell someone the moment one opens when it should not, and leave a record that is far more useful than "somebody must have had a key."

Building Specialists

Access Control for Kensington's Architecture

Kensington's buildings were put up over more than a century, and each generation hung its doors differently. The parkway's pre-war houses have heavy wood or steel-and-glass street doors set into brick or limestone; the 1960s towers have aluminum storefront-style lobby doors; the two-families have wood doors in wood frames behind a porch; the lanes have plain doors opening almost straight onto a narrow walk. The card below each type is what we usually find, and the table after it is how we usually handle it.

Six-story pre-war elevator houses

Built from the late 1920s to 1941 along Ocean Parkway, Coney Island Avenue and the side streets near them. Usually a street door and an inner lobby door, a long row of intercom buttons, mailboxes in the vestibule or lobby, a cellar with the boiler and meters, and one elevator.

Post-war towers on the parkway

The buildings of the early 1960s, many of them later converted to co-ops. Wider lobbies, aluminum and glass doors, a garage or service entrance on the side street, compactor rooms on each floor and more doors to manage than the pre-war houses.

Newer mid-size buildings

Smaller elevator buildings of the last decade, such as the eight-story, fourteen-apartment building at 233 Ocean Parkway, sold with a virtual doorman and a package room with cold storage. The door system came with the building; the question is who controls it now.

Duplex-porch two-families

Two households stacked one over the other, each with its own porch, often sharing one street door or a front gate. Wood doors in wood frames, the cable route usually down into the cellar.

Foursquares and Victorians

The detached houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, with wide front porches, side doors to the driveway and cellar doors at the back. Often family-owned for decades, sometimes with a rental apartment.

The lanes and the avenues

Attached houses and small brick buildings on lanes like Kermit Place and Friel Place, and the mixed-use buildings on Church, McDonald and Coney Island Avenues with a shop below, apartments above and a cellar shared by both.

What the materials mean for the install

The pre-war doors on the parkway are often original or close to it, and they are heavy. A heavy door needs a closer strong enough to pull it shut against the draft from the lobby, and a strike rated for the load the latch puts on it. When an old door sags on its hinges, the latch rides on the edge of the strike instead of dropping into it, and no electronic lock will hold that door. We shim or replace hinges, re-set the strike, and only then cut the jamb for an electric strike.

The aluminum doors of the 1960s towers are a different problem. Narrow aluminum stiles leave no room for a standard strike, so the lock usually moves into the door: an electrified panic bar, a concealed electrified lock in the stile, or an electrified hinge or power transfer hinge to carry the current into the door leaf. On the two-families and Foursquares, the wood frames make strikes easy, and the hard part is getting cable from the porch into the cellar without opening finished walls; we usually go down through the floor under the threshold or along the porch framing.

Ninety-Four Apartments and a Voice Intercom: Changing Over a Parkway Lobby

The listing for 300 Ocean Parkway sums up a whole class of Kensington building in one line: 1941, six floors, ninety-four apartments, a voice intercom, an elevator and a live-in super. Buildings like that have run for generations on brass keys and a super who recognizes faces. The trouble is that a key handed out in 1985 still opens the door, and nobody can say how many copies exist. A voice intercom lets a resident hear a visitor but not see one, so the easiest way into the building is often to press buttons until someone buzzes. Moving that kind of lobby to fobs and video is less about the reader and more about the changeover: ninety-odd households, some of whom have lived there for decades, need to keep getting in every day while the system changes under them.

We plan a parkway changeover in three parts. First, the door itself: closer, hinges, strike, and the inner lobby door if there is one, so both pull shut and latch. Second, the hardware: a video station on the street door that reads fobs and lets visitors call an apartment by number, a reader on the inner door if the board wants a second barrier, a controller and battery in the cellar, and the door release wired so the fire alarm and the exit side still work. Third, the people: a list of every lawful occupant from the managing agent or the board, a set number of fobs per apartment agreed in advance, and a few days where the old key and the new fob both work, so nobody is locked out if they miss the handout.

The old voice intercom does not always have to go on day one. In some buildings the apartment handsets can stay for a while and the new street station rings them through an interface; in others the old riser cable is too far gone and the building moves to phone calls or app calls instead of handsets. We test the riser before we promise either. Whatever the board picks, the key path stays: a building's street door still needs a mechanical way in for the fire department and for the day the power and the battery are both gone.

Latch Before Lock: Self-Closing Doors, Compactor Rooms and HPD's 14 Days

HPD put it plainly in its November 2025 release on self-closing doors: city law requires apartment doors and common-area doors, including hallways, stairwells and building entrances, to close and latch automatically, and owners must keep them working. In fiscal year 2025 the agency issued 72,714 self-closing door violations and spent more than $6.7 million fixing doors that owners did not repair. Once a Notice of Violation is issued, the owner has 14 days to fix the door and certify the correction. Acting HPD Commissioner Ahmed Tigani said "the last thing you should have to worry about is whether your door will close behind you."

Kensington buildings show up in that record like everywhere else. At 415 Beverley Road, Lucid Rents' copy of the HPD record lists four door and lock violations; the two it shows in full, both since dismissed, were orders to make the doors self-closing at a compactor room off a public hall in November 2021 and at the entrance to a basement apartment in October 2020. Those are not exotic failures. A compactor room door gets propped with a bag, the closer arm loosens, and within a month the door rests open. A basement apartment door swells in the damp and stops reaching the latch.

This matters for access control because an electrified lock only locks a door that is shut. If a closer cannot pull the door all the way into the frame, the latch never meets the strike, and the reader is decoration. So every Kensington quote we write has a line for the door itself: closer adjustment or replacement, hinge work, strike alignment, sweeps or weatherstrip where they stop the door from closing. When an owner calls us inside HPD's 14-day window, we fix the closing first, so the violation can be certified, and schedule the reader for a second visit if the electronics will take longer than the deadline allows.

Which doors get readers and which just get closers

Not every self-closing door needs a reader. Compactor room doors on residential floors are part of the building's fire separation; residents need to reach them at any hour, and a lock there mostly creates a reason to prop them. We leave those as plain self-closing doors and, where the board wants it, add a door contact that reports when one has been held open for too long. Cellar doors, boiler rooms, meter rooms and roof doors are different: those belong on the system, with a reader or keypad for staff, a contact, and in the roof door's case, hardware that still opens freely from the inside, as the Multiple Dwelling Law requires.

When the Elevator Stops: Entry Hardware for Residents Who Can't Take the Stairs

The News 12 report from 514 Ocean Parkway in April 2026 was about a leak and a broken elevator, not a door. But it showed something every Kensington board should think about when it plans an entry system: in a six-story building with one elevator, the residents who depend on that elevator are often the same residents who struggle most with a heavy street door. A tenant who uses a wheelchair stayed home for about three weeks while the elevator was down. When the elevator runs again, the street door is the next thing between that resident and the sidewalk.

The US Access Board's guide to the 2010 ADA Standards sets the basic numbers for controls people operate: unobstructed reach from 15 inches to 48 inches above the floor, use with one hand, no tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist, and no more than 5 pounds of force. A reader mounted at the height of a standing adult's chest, on the latch side of a door that swings toward the person, can be out of reach from a wheelchair. A keypad with small buttons can be hard for a resident with arthritis. We plan reader height, button size and placement for the residents the board tells us about, and we place exit buttons where someone seated can press them without reaching across the door's swing.

Longer unlock times for the people who need them

Most access systems let us set how long a door stays unlocked after a fob is read, and many let that time differ from one credential to another. A resident who walks quickly needs a few seconds; a resident who has to back a wheelchair through a heavy door needs much longer. Instead of leaving the lobby door unlocked for everyone for half a minute, we can give longer unlock times to the specific fobs that need them, so the door stays secure for everyone else.

Power door operators

Where a building has a power door operator, or adds one, the access system can trigger it: the resident presents a fob, the door unlocks and the operator swings it open, then closes it under its own control. That pairing has to be wired carefully so the operator never pushes against a door that is still locked, and so the exit side opens with one motion. The operator itself is door hardware installed by a door company; we coordinate the wiring, the timing and the sequence with them. We do not do electrical work, so where the operator needs a new circuit, the building's electrician runs it.

Friday Noon to Three: Door Schedules for Kensington's Mosques, Shuls and Churches

Houses of worship in Kensington live by a calendar that no office building keeps. On Chester Avenue, where it meets Louisa Street and Church Avenue, the city closes the street to cars from noon to 3 p.m. every Friday so the crowd at the mosque can pray outside; Council Member Shahana Hanif said of the gatherings, "This Muslim community, we've really embraced taking to the streets for our faith and to keep our neighbors safe." The daily prayers move with the sun through the year. Synagogues on 18th Avenue and the surrounding blocks have their own early and late hours, and churches have weekday services, meetings in the hall and holiday seasons that turn the schedule upside down.

A fixed "open at nine, lock at five" schedule fails all of them. What works is a schedule built from the congregation's own timetable: the front door unlocked for the windows when people arrive in numbers, locked but readable by members' or officers' fobs the rest of the time, and an exception calendar for holidays. Most systems we install hold several schedules and a holiday list, and an officer can change them from a computer or a phone without calling us. For a mosque, that can mean a midday window every Friday and shorter windows around the daily prayers that an officer shifts by a few minutes as the seasons change.

The hour nobody is inside

The theft at Congregation Agudath Sholom of Flatbush on 18th Avenue happened around 1 a.m. on January 25, 2025. News 12 reported that surveillance video showed a man coming in through the front entrance, and that nobody was in the building. That is the hour a door system can do the most for a house of worship: it knows the building is supposed to be empty, so any opening of the front door, whether by a credential, a key or force, can send an immediate alert to two or three officers' phones. A door contact reports a door that was opened without a valid credential. A reader log shows whether a member's fob was used. Together with a camera, that turns "we found the boxes empty in the morning" into a time, a door and a picture.

Who carries a credential

A congregation is not a household. Dozens of people may have a legitimate reason to open the door at odd hours: the imam or rabbi, the gabbai, the sexton, the volunteer who sets up for a funeral, the caterer for a wedding. We set those people up in groups with their own schedules, so a caterer's code works only on the day of the event and a volunteer's fob works only during setup hours. When someone steps down from the board, their credential is switched off in the software that same day, which no locksmith can do for a key that has been copied five times.

Exits and crowds

A full prayer hall or sanctuary is an assembly space, and its exits must open freely from the inside the whole time people are there. Electrified locks on those doors have to release on loss of power and on the fire alarm, and the exit side needs hardware that opens with one motion. We plan the locked-at-night, open-for-prayers schedule around those rules, not the other way round.

Newer Buildings on the Parkway: The Door System That Came With the Keys

Kensington is not only pre-war. The eight-story, fourteen-apartment building at 233 Ocean Parkway, listed as built in 2015 and described as completed in 2016, is sold with a virtual doorman, an elevator and a package room with cold storage. Buildings like that arrive with their door system already installed, chosen by the developer and often tied to a monthly service. The owners or the board inherit hardware they did not pick and an administrator login they may not hold.

When we are called to a newer Kensington building, the first job is an inventory: what panel is on the street door, what controller runs it, which company holds the cloud account, what the monthly contract covers, and whether the readers will accept credentials from more than one supplier. Then the board can decide what to keep. Often the hardware is fine and only the account needs to move into the board's name. Sometimes the virtual doorman service is worth keeping for the hours the building wants a person watching; sometimes a fourteen-apartment building does better with video calls to each unit and no monthly fee at all.

The Rules Behind a Kensington Door

A short list of laws and codes shapes almost every Kensington access job. None of them is complicated on its own; the trouble comes when an installer ignores one of them and the board finds out from a tenant, an inspector or a fire officer. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Entrance doors and intercoms in apartment buildings

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors on class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, and an intercom with voice communication and a remote door release at the main entrance of buildings with eight or more apartments. Older buildings, which includes most of the pre-war houses on the parkway, must install the same equipment when a majority of tenants ask in writing. Roof doors must be self-closing but not self-locking, and must open from the inside without a key.

Keyless entry and tenants' data

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law covers buildings with three or more apartments that use fobs, cards, apps or biometrics. The owner must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, not track tenants outside the building, and not use it to harass or evict. A tenant cannot be required to use smart access to enter their own apartment. We set retention and reporting up to match before the system goes live.

Rent-stabilized apartments

State rulings described by Brick Underground in 2018 have required owners who replace keys with fobs in rent-stabilized buildings to give a fob to every lawful occupant without charge, to keep replacement charges for lost fobs modest, and not to demand new identifying information as a condition. In 2020 the state Attorney General reached a settlement with a security company over a fob rollout in a rent-stabilized Queens building that recorded drivers' license numbers and capped fobs per apartment. We follow those limits in every regulated Kensington building.

Locked doors that are also exits

The NYC Building Code allows electrically locked egress doors only with specific safeguards: a sensor on the egress side that unlocks the door, release on loss of power, a manual "push to exit" button mounted 40 to 48 inches high and within 5 feet of the door that cuts power to the lock and keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, release on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and listed equipment. Magnetic locks have their own rules, including release by hardware with a built-in switch that unlocks immediately. We design every lobby, shop and house-of-worship exit to those rules.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Kensington

Kensington's businesses are mostly small and family-run: restaurants, sweet shops and grocers around McDonald and Church Avenues, clothing and jewelry stores on Coney Island Avenue, auto shops and contractors on the side streets, schools and day programs in converted houses. The first person in and the last person out

In a family restaurant the opening cook may arrive at dawn and the closing manager may leave after midnight. A door system lets each of them carry their own code, so the record shows who opened and who closed, and it lets the owner set rules: for example, the cooks' codes work from early morning, the delivery code works only in the morning delivery window, and nobody's code but the manager's works after closing. When a seasonal worker leaves, their code is switched off without changing anyone else's.

Rules that apply to Kensington business doors

  • Exits open from inside. A shop or restaurant exit can be locked against entry from outside but must open from inside with one motion, without a key or special knowledge.
  • Power loss. Locks on egress doors must release when power is lost; locks on doors that are not exits can be fail-secure, so the door stays locked in a blackout.
  • Apartments above. In a mixed-use building, residents' entrance rules and the tenant data rules apply to the residential door even when the store owner owns the building.
  • Your lease. If you rent the store, your lease usually decides whether you may change the doors; we ask to see the landlord's written approval before cutting a frame.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Kensington: Questions Answered

Ocean Parkway's elevator buildings

HPD wrote up the compactor room door on one of our floors for not closing by itself. Should the compactor rooms get readers too, or stay plain doors?

In nearly every Kensington building we would leave compactor room doors as plain self-closing doors with no lock. Residents need them at any hour, the door is part of the floor's fire separation, and a lock on it mostly gives people a reason to wedge it open with a bag. What those doors need is a closer strong enough for the draft in the hall, a latch that catches every time and hinges that do not sag, which is also exactly what HPD checks.

If the board wants to know when a compactor door is being held open, we can add a simple door contact that reports to the same system as the lobby reader. It sends an alert after the door has been open longer than a set time, without recording who opened it, and the super goes up and frees it. The doors that do belong on readers are the ones residents should not open: the cellar, the boiler and meter rooms and the roof door, the last of which must still open from inside without a key.

Water came through our lobby ceiling onto the mailboxes on and off for two months. Where in a lobby like that should the reader, the call panel and their cable go?

Outdoor readers and door stations are built to take rain, but a ceiling leak is a different kind of water: it runs down the inside of the wall, along conduit and into the back of anything mounted flush. We keep the controller, power supply and battery out of the lobby entirely, in the cellar or a ground-floor closet on a wall that does not share a plumbing chase with the apartments above. The cable from the lobby comes down to it in a sealed path, with a drip loop, so water following the cable stops before it reaches the electronics.

At the door, the call panel goes on the street side where it was built to be, on a surface mount or a sealed back box. Inside, a reader on the inner lobby door goes on the latch-side frame at a height away from the mailbox wall, so water spreading across the ceiling toward the mailboxes does not run down onto it. When we walk the lobby, we look for stains on the ceiling and ask the super where the last leaks came through; those spots get nothing mounted below them.

HPD gave our building 14 days to fix a self-closing street door. Should we fix the closer first and add the reader later, or do both in one job?

Fix the closing first, inside the 14 days, and certify it to HPD. The closer, hinge and strike work is usually one visit, and it is the part HPD is looking at. Ordering a video panel, a controller and the right electric strike for an old Kensington door can take longer than two weeks, and a reader installed in a rush on a door that still does not latch helps nobody.

We write both into one plan so the money spent on the closing is not wasted later: the new strike plate is chosen to match the electric strike that will replace it, the closer is sized for the door with a strike in it, and any cable that can go in while the frame is open goes in then. The second visit adds the electronics to a door that already closes on its own, which is the order it should happen in anyway.

Houses of worship

Our mosque fills from noon to three on Fridays, and the daily prayer times move with the sun the rest of the year. Can the door schedule follow the prayer timetable instead of fixed hours?

Yes. Most controllers we install hold a weekly schedule with several unlock windows per day plus a holiday or exception calendar. We set the Friday midday window as its own fixed block, and set the daily windows around each prayer with enough margin on either side for people arriving early or leaving late. As the times shift through the year, an officer moves those windows by a few minutes from the mosque office computer or a phone, the way they would update a printed timetable on the notice board.

Outside the windows, the door stays locked and opens for the fobs of the imam, the board and the volunteers who need it. During Ramadan, when the evening and night prayers draw the biggest crowds, the exception calendar carries a separate schedule for the month and the system returns to normal by itself afterward. We never lock a full hall's exits; those open from inside at all times.

A man walked through the front entrance of a shul on 18th Avenue at about 1 a.m. and emptied the donation boxes while nobody was inside. Who should be told when our front door opens at that hour, and how fast?

Two or three people who live close and will actually answer their phones at night, told within seconds. We set the front door's contact and reader to report any opening outside the building's scheduled hours as an alert: a forced or unexplained opening as an urgent one, a valid fob used after hours as a notice with the credential's name. Those alerts go as push notifications and texts to the people on the list, and where the building has an alarm with central-station monitoring, the door can also be a zone on that alarm.

Speed matters less for catching someone than for what happens next: an officer who sees the alert at 1:02 a.m. can check the camera from bed, call the police with a time and a door, and stop the next door from being tried. The donation boxes themselves are better kept behind a second locked door or in a room with its own reader, so getting through the front door does not mean reaching the money.

Little Bangladesh, Little Pakistan and the avenue shops

Our store on Coney Island Avenue shares a cellar with the shop next door through an opening in the old dividing wall. Can our side of the cellar be locked off from theirs?

Usually, yes, but the first question is not about hardware. Shared cellars on the avenues often hold meters, a boiler, a sprinkler valve or a gas shutoff that both stores, the landlord and the utility need to reach. Before we put a door with a reader into that opening, the landlord and both tenants have to agree who may cross it and when, and the fire department and utilities must still be able to get to what they need. We ask for that agreement in writing.

With that settled, we fit a steel door and frame into the opening if one is not there, put a fail-secure strike and a reader or keypad on your side, and an exit device on the other side if the door is a required way out of the neighbor's cellar. Your staff get codes that work during your hours, the landlord's super gets a credential for the meter side, and the log shows every opening. Where the opening cannot take a door, a locked steel gate with the same reader can sometimes do the job.

Two-families, Foursquares and the lanes

Our duplex-porch two-family on Turner Place has a street door for both households and a door from the upstairs apartment onto its own porch. Does that upper porch door belong on the same system?

Usually not with a reader. The upper porch door opens from the upstairs apartment onto a porch that is reached only from inside that apartment, so nobody comes in through it from the street. It needs a good lock, and if the family wants to know it was left open, a contact that reports to their phone, but it does not need a credential. The shared street door is the one that matters, because both households, their visitors and the mail carrier all depend on it.

On that street door we give each household its own codes or fobs and a call button for each floor, and we let each household see only its own entries. Where the downstairs porch has its own door into the ground-floor apartment, that door can be on the system too, with only the downstairs family's credentials. The cable for both runs down into the cellar under the porch floor, which avoids opening the finished walls of either apartment.

Our 1941 building has ninety-odd apartments, a live-in super and no doorman. What should the super be able to do on the new system, and what should stay with the managing agent?

The super should be able to do what keeps people getting in at night: look up a fob by apartment, switch off a lost one, issue a replacement from a small stock, and let a vendor in from the phone. The managing agent's office should hold the full resident list, the entry history, the schedules and the ability to add or remove administrators. We set up two administrator roles to match, so the super's phone can fix a lockout at 2 a.m. without carrying the building's whole history in his pocket.

This split also protects the building when people change. If the super retires or the building changes managing agents, the office account stays with the owner or board, and the super's access is switched off and handed to his successor in minutes. Under the Tenant Data Privacy Law, fewer people with access to the entry history is better for everyone, so we keep that list short and write it into the building's policy.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Kensington

Search for access control and you will get confident general answers from AI summaries and from cost sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Many are true somewhere. Below are seven claims we hear repeated by Kensington owners and board members, often word for word from what they read, and what we find when we get to the door.

“Put a fob reader on the front door and the building is secure.”

A reader decides who may unlock the door. It does nothing about a door that never closes. HPD issued 72,714 self-closing door violations citywide in fiscal year 2025, and Kensington buildings appear in that record, including compactor room and basement apartment doors at 415 Beverley Road.

If the closer cannot pull the door into the frame, the latch never meets the strike, and a building with a brand-new reader stands open to anyone who pushes.

So our first visit to any Kensington building is about the door: closer, hinges, strike, latch and weatherstrip. The reader is the second step, not the first, and the quote says so in writing.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars per door.”

Cost guides often quote prices for a lock or a basic keypad, or for a security door, and they are not wrong about those items. They are not describing a controlled door with a strike, a reader, a controller, a battery, an exit device and cable through a brick wall.

On our Brooklyn list, a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, wiring and 10 fobs is $1,500 to $2,500, and a video panel with fob access replacing a buzzer is $1,500 to $3,000. A multi-door system for a parkway building runs $4,000 to $15,000, and a full building $15,000 to $40,000.

What moves a Kensington price is mostly the door and the route: a sagging pre-war door that needs hinge work, a steel frame grouted solid, an aluminum stile that needs an electrified lock instead of a strike, a long run to a dry cellar. We price those after seeing them, once, in writing.

“Wireless smart locks are the easy answer for old buildings.”

Battery smart locks are easy on a single apartment door. On a lobby door used by ninety households, the battery drains in weeks, the lock's small motor works against a heavy door all day, and the lock often cannot tell the system whether the door actually latched.

Old buildings are also hard on radio. Brick and plaster walls, steel door frames and a cellar far below the lobby all weaken a wireless signal, and a lock that drops off the network cannot be updated when a fob is lost.

For a Kensington lobby, a wired strike or electrified lock with a controller on site is more reliable and costs less to keep running.

“A doorman or a virtual doorman means you don't need access control.”

A doorman watches the door; access control decides who can open it and keeps a record. Even a building with staff around the clock has cellar doors, service doors and compactor rooms that no doorman sees.

A virtual doorman service, like the one listed for 233 Ocean Parkway, is a remote person answering a call at the door. It is usually built on top of an access system, not a replacement for one.

We help boards sort out which service they are paying for, which hardware they own, and whether a staffed or remote doorman is worth its cost once residents can see and release visitors themselves.

“Fail-safe locks are always the safest choice.”

Fail-safe means the door unlocks when power is lost; fail-secure means it stays locked. The words sound like one is safe and one is not. The truth depends on the door.

An exit door in a mosque hall, a shul sanctuary or a restaurant must let people out when the power fails, and the Building Code requires electrically locked egress doors to release on power loss and on the fire alarm. A Kensington street door that is not a required exit lock is different: if it unlocks whenever Con Edison drops the power, the building is open during every outage.

On street doors we usually fit a fail-secure strike with free exit from inside through the mechanical latch, plus a key override. On required exits we fit fail-safe or exit-device hardware. The choice is made door by door.

“An app can replace keys for everyone in the building.”

Phone keys are convenient, and many residents like them. But in a Kensington apartment building, some residents do not carry smartphones, some share one phone in a family, and some home attendants change week to week.

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law also says a tenant cannot be required to use smart access to enter their own apartment, and it limits what data a building may collect and how long it may keep it. A system that works only through an app is the wrong tool for a building with many long-time residents.

We set up fobs as the default, phone keys for those who want them, a code where a fob does not suit someone, and a mechanical key path on every street door. The board decides the mix, and the policy is written before the first fob goes out.

“Access control is the answer to break-ins on the avenues.”

Access control is the answer at the door. The 2025 break-ins reported at one Church Avenue building came through a smashed side window, a forced side door and over a fence. A reader would have mattered only for the second one, and even there, a forced door is a hardware and alarm problem as much as an access problem.

What a door system does well for an avenue shop is keep the staff and cellar doors locked when they should be, show who opened them and when, and send an alert the moment a door opens outside business hours.

For windows, fences, roofs and walls, the tools are cameras, alarms and physical hardening, and we say so plainly when we quote a shop.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Let the Door Go Three Times

The first thing our technicians do at a Kensington lobby is not open a toolbag. They open the street door all the way and let go, then halfway, then a hand's width, and watch. On the parkway's heavy pre-war doors, the hand's-width test is the one that fails most often: the closer has enough force to swing the door from wide open, but not enough to push the latch past the strike from the last inch. That is the door that rests open at night, the one HPD writes up, and the one that would make a new reader useless. We write down what we see on every door, including the compactor rooms and the cellar, before talking about fobs.

The second thing is to ask who cannot use the door today. In a building with one elevator, the answer is often a short list of residents, and the super knows every name on it. Their needs set the reader height, the button size, the unlock time and whether a power operator belongs in the plan. A system designed around the residents who find the door hardest tends to work for everyone else, and it is far cheaper to plan that way from the start than to move a reader after it is installed.

FAQ

Kensington Access Control FAQ

Can one resident's fob keep our Kensington lobby door unlocked longer than everyone else's?
+

On most systems we install, yes. The unlock time can be set per credential, so a resident who uses a wheelchair or a walker gets enough time to get through a heavy door while everyone else's fob relocks it after a few seconds. If the building has a power door operator, that same fob can also trigger it.

How can we check whether our building's front door closes and latches properly before we pay for a reader?
+

Open the door fully and let go, then open it halfway and let go, then open it about a hand's width and let go. A ready door closes and latches from all three positions without a push. If it stops short, bounces off the frame or needs a shove at the end, the closer, hinges or strike need work first, and we put that work in the quote before any electronics.

Do you install the Akuvox and AXIS door stations shown on this page, or other brands too?
+

The photos on this page are from our job library and show Akuvox and AXIS stations, which we install. We also install other door station, reader and controller brands, and we quote our own non-branded system by default unless you ask for a specific brand. If your building already has a brand you want to keep, tell us the model numbers and we will tell you what can stay.

Can our Kensington board get the door log as a spreadsheet for its own records?
+

Most systems we install can export entry history as a file, and the administrator can choose the date range and the door. In a building with three or more apartments, the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what the building may collect and requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized, so exports should be pulled only for a real reason and stored under the building's written policy.

Can a mosque or shul change its door schedule from the office computer instead of a phone app?
+

Yes. The systems we install for houses of worship can be managed from a web page on an ordinary office computer as well as from a phone. An officer can shift prayer or service windows, add a holiday, or switch off a volunteer's fob from the office, and we set up at least two officers as administrators so the schedule never depends on one person.

Can door alerts from our Kensington building go into the WhatsApp group the board already uses?
+

Not directly on most systems. Access control platforms send alerts through their own app, by text message or by email. We set those alerts to reach the same people who are in the board's group, and many boards forward a screenshot into the group when something needs discussion. Keeping the alerts in the system's own app also keeps a record that a chat message does not.

Can a resident carry both a fob and a phone key and use whichever is handy?
+

Yes, on most readers we install a resident can have a fob, a phone key and in some cases a personal code, all tied to the same person and apartment. Switching that person off disables all of them at once.

Do we need a new front door to get access control, or can our building's original door stay?
+

Most original Kensington doors can stay. Heavy wood and steel pre-war doors take an electric strike in the frame once the hinges and closer are sound, and older aluminum lobby doors can take an electrified lock or panic bar inside the stile. A door needs replacing only when the frame is rotted, the door no longer fits it, or the glass is unsafe, and we tell you that on the first visit.

What happens to a cloud-managed system if the company behind the software goes out of business?
+

On the systems we install, the controller at the building keeps the credential list and opens the door on its own, so fobs keep working even if the cloud service disappears. What you would lose is remote management until the controller is moved to another platform or a local manager. We tell boards before they sign which parts depend on a subscription and which do not.

Do Kensington residents need to download an app to use a new fob?
+

No. A fob works on its own; the resident holds it to the reader and the door opens. An app is needed only for phone keys, for answering visitors on a phone, or for administrators. Residents who do not want an app, or do not have a smartphone, can use a fob and answer visitors on an indoor screen or by phone call where the building sets that up.

Who in our Kensington building should try the new fobs first, before everyone gets one?
+

We suggest a small test group for the first few days: the super, one or two board members or the owner, a resident who uses a wheelchair or walker if there is one, and a resident who comes and goes at unusual hours. They find problems with unlock times, reader height and schedules before ninety households do, and the old key keeps working for everyone else while the test runs.
