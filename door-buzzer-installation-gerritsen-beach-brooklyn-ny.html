The Neighborhood

Why Gerritsen Beach Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Gerritsen Beach is the small peninsula at the end of Gerritsen Avenue, between Sheepshead Bay to the west and Marine Park to the east, with Plumb Beach Channel and Shell Bank Creek on its water sides. Wikipedia counts 4,797 residents in the 2020 census. It began in the 1920s as a summer colony of one-story bungalows, most of which were later winterized and many raised to two stories, and the Department of City Planning's 2017 report found about 1,900 buildings holding more than 2,680 homes, nearly all of them one- and two-family houses. That is the starting point for every buzzer job here: very few lobbies, a great many front doors, and a lot of houses with a second household upstairs or down.

The second thing that shapes the work is the way the houses meet the street. The old section south of the Gotham Avenue canal is laid out in narrow courts, many with names that run through the alphabet, where the city's report measured streets from 30 feet down to as little as 15 feet including the sidewalks. Houses were built close to the lot line and close to each other. A visitor or a courier often has to walk in from Gerritsen Avenue or a cross street, find the right house among very similar ones, and then find the right door, which after Sandy may be a full story up an outdoor stair. A buzzer that rings but cannot be reached, found or answered from the floor where people actually live fixes nothing.

The third is what Sandy left behind. The storm put water up to seven feet above grade in parts of the neighborhood, and more than 200 households here were in the city's Build It Back program by spring 2017. Many houses were lifted onto new foundations, others were demolished and replaced, and some of the replacements came back as two-family houses. New York YIMBY reported one such filing on Noel Avenue in 2017: a two-story house to be replaced by "another two-story and two-family home." Every raised or rebuilt house we see here has a front door that moved, a bell wire that was cut, abandoned or never run, and a ground level that the Buildings Department's flood rules limit to parking, storage, building access or crawlspace.

The fourth is the mix of people inside. Many houses hold two generations of one family, or an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other. Openigloo's directory lists 57 rental buildings in Gerritsen Beach, and most of the addresses on its first page are on courts such as Beacon, Ebony, Eaton, Frank, Bevy, Abbey, Fane, Hazel, Canton and Keen, which tells you how small the rentals here are. When the person who receives visitors lives at the top of a stair, or is an older parent on the floor below, the ability to see the visitor and release the door from inside is not a convenience. It is how they avoid going up and down an outdoor stair in the rain to sign for a package.

The last is the water itself. Every house here is within a few blocks of salt water, and many on the canals have docks. Salt air, wind-driven rain and the occasional flood tide shorten the life of cheap door hardware, and the outdoor parts of a buzzer, the station, the strike and the gate release, are exactly the parts exposed to all three. Choosing those parts for the setting, and keeping the electronics that do not need to be outside well above the flood level, is most of what separates a buzzer that lasts here from one that is replaced every couple of seasons.

Six facts that shape a Gerritsen Beach buzzer installation

  • It is a neighborhood of small houses. City Planning's 2017 report counted about 1,900 buildings and more than 2,680 homes, mostly one- and two-family houses of two stories, many built as summer bungalows.
  • The streets are narrow. The same report measured streets from 30 feet down to 15 feet including sidewalks, and a broker quoted by Brick Underground in 2022 said some old-section streets are "so narrow you think you're on a driveway."
  • Many front doors moved after Sandy. More than 200 households were in Build It Back by spring 2017, and the city's flood design elevation here runs roughly 3 to 5 feet above grade inland and 5 to 10 feet on the waterfront.
  • The ground level under a raised house is restricted. The Buildings Department's Appendix G rules say enclosed space below the design flood elevation in a residential building is usable solely for parking, building access, storage or crawlspace.
  • The rentals are small. Openigloo lists 57 rental buildings here, most of those it shows on courts, so most of them fall below the eight-apartment line at which state law requires an intercom.
  • There is one way in. Gerritsen Avenue is the only road onto the peninsula and its only commercial corridor, which is where the neighborhood's store doors and most of its deliveries arrive.

Install or Repair in Gerritsen Beach

Need Repair in Gerritsen Beach? When the Old Bell Is Worth Saving and When It Is Not

A lot of our Gerritsen Beach calls start with a doorbell that has not worked since the house was lifted or rebuilt, or a buzzer that the previous owner put in and nobody has touched since. Sometimes the fix is small. A transformer that has failed, a button whose contacts have corroded in the salt air, or a strike whose screws have pulled out of a softened frame are repairs, and we price them as repairs.

It stops being a repair when the wire itself is gone or untrustworthy. On a house that was jacked up and set on a new foundation, the old bell wire usually ran through a floor or a crawlspace that no longer exists in the same place, and what is left is a stub at the old door and another at the old transformer. On a house that sat in floodwater, wire that still carries a signal may be corroded inside its jacket and fail a season later. In both cases we meter what is there, tell you plainly whether any of it can be used, and price a new run where it cannot. If a repair is all your house needs, our Brooklyn door buzzer team prices it that way.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Gerritsen Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of property cover nearly every door we are asked about here. What separates them for a buzzer job is how high the front door sits, how many households share it, where the old wiring went, what the walls are made of, and how close the salt water is.

Winterized bungalows on the courts

The original summer houses of the 1920s, closed in, insulated and often enlarged, with a front door opening almost straight onto a narrow court and frame walls under later siding.

Houses raised after Sandy

Existing houses lifted onto new foundations, with the living floor now a story or more above grade, an outdoor stair to the front door and a ground level kept for parking, storage and access.

Two-family rebuilds

Houses demolished and replaced, some as two-family homes, built to current flood rules with new framing, new doors and a chance to run cable before the walls close.

Canal-side lots

Long, narrow lots on the canals with the house near the street, a yard and often a dock behind, where some households use the water side as much as the front.

Brick houses north of the canal

The newer blocks between the canal and Avenue U, with brick and attached houses, wider sidewalks and more conventional stoops and vestibules.

One-story stores on Gerritsen Avenue

The attached commercial row along the avenue, single-story buildings sharing side walls, with a front door on the avenue and often a delivery door at the back.

Winterized bungalows: easy walls, a door right on the court

The bungalows were built as light wood-frame summer houses, so the walls are hollow and new cable usually travels quickly from the door to wherever the household wants its indoor station. Many have had aluminum or vinyl siding added over the years, and the station is fastened to the door casing or a solid backing block, never to siding alone, so it does not work loose as the siding moves with the weather.

The harder part is the doorway. Urban Omnibus's walk through the neighborhood described homes built right to the property line, and on many courts the front door opens a step or two from the walkway with no porch roof and no vestibule. A station there takes wind and rain head on, so it needs a hood or a recessed mount, a sealed entry for its cable and a gasket that is checked at handover. We also look at what is directly across the court, because on a walkway this narrow a station's camera and light are pointed at a neighbor's front window from a few yards away.

Original doors on these houses are often lighter than a modern entry door, with less wood around the latch. An electric strike needs enough solid frame to hold its screws and a door that stays aligned so the latch drops into it. Where the frame is too thin or has been chewed up by years of replaced locks, a reinforcing plate or a new frame section comes first, and it is named and priced on the quote, not discovered on installation day.

Houses raised after Sandy: the door moved up and the wire did not

When a house is lifted, the front door ends up at the top of a new outdoor stair, usually steel or wood, with a small landing. The old bell button may still be on the wall beside that door, but the wire behind it ran down into a crawlspace or a floor structure that was cut, rebuilt or filled during the lift. We expect to run new cable on nearly every raised house we see, and we price it that way from the start rather than hoping the old pair survived.

The landing is the station's new home, and it is not always a good one. It is exposed on several sides, it is often steel, and on DNAinfo's 2016 visit to a Build It Back house near Gerritsen Avenue the family's steel stairs had collected rainwater, and "Rust has started to form around the drainage holes." We mount stations to the house wall or the door casing rather than the stair, use stainless or coated fasteners, and route the cable into the house through the wall beside the door, not along the stair, so a rusting stair never takes the buzzer with it.

The ground level below the house is the other half of the plan. Under the Buildings Department's Appendix G rules, enclosed space below the design flood elevation in a residential building may be used only for parking, building access, storage or crawlspace, and new equipment the rules do not permit there counts against the building. For a buzzer that means the power supply, the splices and anything that would be ruined by water go upstairs on the living floor. If a gate or a door at ground level gets a release, only the release and its cable live down there, and the cable is run so it can get wet and dry out without damage.

Two-family rebuilds: the cheapest moment is before the drywall

A house that is being rebuilt from new framing is the easiest buzzer job in the neighborhood if we are in before the walls close. A cable from the front door to each household's station, a second one to wherever the gate will be, and a pull string or conduit through the ground level for later all cost little when the studs are open. The same runs after the drywall is up mean fishing, cutting and patching, and AESS does not patch or paint walls, so that work falls to the builder.

Two-family houses also need the households planned as two households. Each gets its own button on the door station, its own station inside, and a release it can work from its own floor. If the owner wants to see the tenant's visitors, or not, that is decided before ordering, because some systems tie both households to one app account and some keep them apart.

Canal-side lots: two fronts to the house

The city's 2017 report described the canal lots as about 24 by 66 feet, and Urban Omnibus noted that most waterfront houses have docks and boats. For many households the dock side is a real entrance: family arriving by boat, neighbors walking along the bulkheads, the household itself coming home from the water. A back gate with its own small station and release, ringing the same indoor stations as the front, lets them answer both sides from one place.

The dock side is also the most exposed spot on the lot. A station there faces open water and spray, so it is a fully weather-rated unit in a sealed box, on a post or the gate pier set back from the bulkhead, with its cable run underground or along the fence in a protected path rather than across the yard where it will be stepped on.

Brick houses north of the canal: closer to a conventional Brooklyn job

Wikipedia describes the blocks north of the Gotham Avenue canal as the newer section, with brick houses and wider sidewalks. These look more like the rest of southern Brooklyn: a stoop or a few steps, a solid masonry wall beside the door, sometimes a vestibule and an inner door. Cable routes through brick take more planning than through a frame wall, and an old riser or bell pair that tests sound is worth reusing. Many of these houses have their front door at a normal height, so the stair and gate questions that dominate the old section do not arise.

One-story stores on Gerritsen Avenue: shared walls and a flat roof

City Planning's report describes the commercial buildings on Gerritsen Avenue as one-story attached structures, and notes that a 2013 zoning change exempted floodproofing from floor-area limits for commercial buildings here. A store in that row has no apartments overhead and no cellar route shared with residents, so a buzz-in door is usually a single-door job: a station outside, a release in the frame and a button at the counter. The cable runs inside the store's own space, along the ceiling or above a drop ceiling, never through the shared side walls into the next store.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking the Visitor's Route Before Choosing a Station in Gerritsen Beach

On a Gerritsen Beach walkthrough our technician does one thing before looking at any equipment: they walk in the way a stranger would. That means starting where a delivery van can actually stop, often on Gerritsen Avenue or the nearest cross street, and walking down the court to the house.

On that walk they note how a visitor finds the house, whether the number is visible from the court, where the first gate or fence is, and whether the front door can be seen from it. On a raised house they stop at the foot of the stair and ask the obvious question: will a courier climb this, or leave the box at the bottom? The answer decides whether the house needs one station at the door, one at the gate, or a station at the door and a release at the gate.

Then they go inside and up to where the household lives, and ask where people actually are when the bell rings: the kitchen, a back bedroom, a tenant's floor, the dock. A station in the front hall of a house whose family spends its evenings at the back, or on the water side, is a station nobody hears.

Only after that do they find the old wiring, meter it, and look at the door frame and the stair. Those checks decide the price; the walk decides whether the system works for the people who live there.

The Raised Front Door

A Gerritsen Beach Front Door at the Top of a Stair Needs a Plan for Three Places, Not One

On a house at normal height, a buzzer is one decision: where to put the station beside the door. On a raised house it becomes three, because the visitor's journey has three stops. They arrive at the gate or the foot of the stair, they climb to the landing, and they stand at the door. A system that only works at the last stop leaves couriers, older visitors and anyone carrying something heavy at the first.

The foot of the stair

This is where most deliveries end. If the household wants packages taken in rather than left on the walk, a small station or a call button here, wired to the same indoor stations, lets the courier ring without climbing. If there is a gate at the front of the lot or the foot of the stair, a release in its latch lets the household open it from upstairs. Anything mounted here is below the design flood elevation, so we keep it to the parts that have to be here, a weather-rated station and a release, and nothing that cannot take a soaking.

The stair itself

The stair is a route, not a mounting surface. Cable that runs down a steel stringer or along a handrail is exposed to every rain, every boot and every repair to the stair, and the steel itself may be rusting. Where the gate station's cable has to reach the house, it goes through the house wall at the ground level and up inside, or in a protected conduit fixed to the foundation, so the stair can be painted, repaired or replaced without anyone touching the buzzer.

The landing and the door

The main station belongs beside the door, on the house wall or the casing, at a height a visitor standing on the landing can reach. Its camera is aimed for the landing, which is narrow, so the lens sees a face at arm's length rather than the top of a head. The release goes in the door frame, and because the landing is often the most exposed spot on the house, the strike, the station and the cable entry are all sealed. Where the household also wants to see the foot of the stair from upstairs, a small camera under the landing does that better than trying to aim a door station down a full story of steps.

Put together, a raised two-family typically ends up with one two-button station at the door, a release in the door frame, a call button or small station at the gate with its own release, and a station on each household's floor. That combination is the most common raised-house layout we price here, and it usually lands in the video range of our Brooklyn list.

Salt Air and Weather

What a Door Station Has to Survive Near Plumb Beach Channel and Shell Bank Creek

Every outdoor part of a buzzer in Gerritsen Beach lives within a few blocks of salt water. The station, the release in the frame, the gate latch and the screws that hold them are what fail first, and they fail from the inside: water gets behind a plate mounted flat on uneven siding, salt film builds on the contacts, and a button that worked in August sticks by February. Choosing those parts by their ratings rather than their looks is the single biggest thing an owner can do for a buzzer here.

The ratings that matter, in plain words

  • Water and dust (IP rating). 2N's guide to outdoor intercoms explains that IP65 means "completely dust-tight and resistant to water sprayed from a nozzle," and IP66 means "dust-tight and protected against strong jets of water." For a station on an exposed landing or a dock gate, wind-driven rain behaves more like the stronger case, so we choose accordingly.
  • Impact (IK rating). The same guide puts IK10 at 20 joules, the "maximum rating," roughly a 5-kilogram object dropped from 400 millimeters. That matters for a station at a gate where bikes, bins and boat gear go past.
  • Housing material. Painted steel rusts at the edges near salt water. Viking's listing for its 316 stainless steel enclosure says the material "resists rust, salt air, and chemical exposure," which is why stainless or anodized aluminum housings are our default on the water side.
  • Shelter. Even a rated enclosure is usually designed for "sheltered or covered locations," as Viking's own listing puts it. Where a landing has no roof, a hood or a recessed mount is part of the job, not an extra.
  • Fasteners. A stainless station held on with plain steel screws stains and loosens. Every outdoor fastener we use here is stainless or coated, including the ones holding the strike.

None of this makes a station immune to a flood tide. Equipment at the foot of a stair or on a dock gate can be submerged in a storm surge, and that is outside any warranty. What the ratings buy is years of ordinary weather without the slow corrosion that kills cheap stations in a couple of seasons, and the dry-side plan described above keeps the expensive parts out of reach of the water.

Rules and Responsibilities

Which Gerritsen Beach Houses Must Have a Buzzer, and What an Owner Takes On by Installing One

  • The intercom law starts at eight apartments. Habitat magazine summarized the state's Multiple Dwelling Law in 2024: buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom linking the front door to each apartment. ButterflyMX's guide adds that buildings with fewer than eight units "are not generally treated the same way under this rule." Almost every house in Gerritsen Beach is far below that line.
  • Older buildings follow a different trigger. For buildings put up before 1968, the same law ties the duty to a written request or consent from tenants occupying a majority of the apartments. Few buildings here are large enough for that to matter, but if yours is one of them, ask HPD or your lawyer which applies.
  • A system that exists has to be kept working. The Housing Maintenance Code also requires a functioning intercom where one is in place, and Habitat reported that courts have treated intercoms as "an essential service." In a co-op case it discussed, attorney Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group put it simply: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." An owner renting out the other floor of a two-family should treat a buzzer the same way once it is in.
  • Doors must always open from inside. Whatever the system, a person inside must be able to leave without a key or a button. We use electric strikes on house doors and gates for that reason, as explained in the equipment section.
  • Flood rules govern the ground level. On a raised or rebuilt house, the certificate of occupancy for wet-floodproofed space must state that "Levels subject to flooding shall not be used for any other use except as stated on this certificate." A buzzer does not change that use, and we keep its power and electronics off that level.
  • New outlets are an electrician's job. AESS does low-voltage work and does not do electrical work. If a house has no outlet where the power supply should go, your electrician adds one, and we tell you exactly where before the installation date.

None of this is legal advice for a particular property. If you are unsure which rules apply to your building, HPD, the Buildings Department or your own lawyer can tell you. What we do is install a system that respects those rules and leave a written record of what was installed and tested.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Gerritsen Beach: Questions Answered

Raised houses and their stairs

Our Build It Back house has a steel outdoor staircase, and the rails already show rust around the drainage holes. Should the door station be bolted to the stair, or does it belong somewhere else?

Somewhere else. A steel stair that is already rusting will need scraping, painting and probably repairs over the years, and anything bolted to it has to come off each time. Worse, a station fastened to rusting steel works loose as the metal around the bolts flakes, and water then gets behind it. The station goes on the house wall beside the door or on the door casing, where the backing is solid, dry and will not move.

The same goes for the cable. It enters the house through the wall right behind the station, so nothing runs along the stair at all. If you later want a call button at the bottom, its cable reaches the house through the foundation wall at ground level and climbs inside, again without touching the stair. When the stair is eventually repaired or replaced, the buzzer is not part of that job.

Couriers leave our boxes at the foot of the stairs because they will not climb to the door. Can a release on the gate down there let them put packages inside the space under the house?

Yes, and on a raised house it is often the most useful thing a buzzer can do. A call button or small station at the foot of the stair rings the household upstairs; they see or speak to the courier, release the gate or the ground-level door, and the courier sets the box inside and pulls the door shut behind them. Storage and building access are exactly what the city's flood rules allow that level to be used for, so a parcel drop fits within them.

Two details matter. The ground-level door must lock again by itself after the courier leaves, so it needs a closer and a latch that drops into the strike every time. And the release down there should open only that space, not the stair door into the house. New York Times reporting in 2019 found that more than 90,000 packages "disappear without explanation" in the city each day; a locked drop under the house takes yours out of that count without anyone going up and down the stair.

From the top of our tall front stair, will the door station's camera even show us who is standing at the gate on the walk below?

Usually not well. A door station's camera is designed to show a face at arm's length on the landing. Aimed a full story down a stair to a gate, it sees mostly steps and the top of a head, and at night its light does not reach that far. Trying to angle it down also makes it worse for the person who does climb up.

The better answer is to give the gate its own eyes. A call station at the gate with its own camera, or a small camera under the landing aimed at the gate, shows the household who is at the bottom on the same indoor screen or phone. The door station then does its own job well, at the door, and the household sees both stops.

Where would video at the top of our raised two-family's stair plus a release on the gate at the bottom land on your Brooklyn list?

In the video range, $700 to $1,800. That covers a two-button video station at the front door, a release in the door frame, a station or phone answering for each household, and a call button and release at the gate. A one-family raised house with audio only at the door and a gate release sits lower, near the top of the $400 to $900 audio range.

What moves a raised house within its range is the gate and the route. A gate with a latch that can take a release and a short path into the house sits near the bottom; a gate that needs new latch hardware, a long run to reach the house without touching the stair, or a second camera at the gate sits near the top. The written quote shows the gate as its own line so you can decide whether to do it now or later.

Our replacement house is still open framing and the walls close next month. Does putting the cable in now change which price range we land in?

It usually keeps you lower in whichever range fits the house. With open studs, cable from the front door to each household's station, and from the house to the gate, goes in quickly and needs no cutting or fishing. The equipment is the same price either way; it is the labor of reaching through finished walls that you save.

If you are not ready to choose stations yet, we can run the cable now, label both ends and leave it coiled in the wall boxes, then come back to mount and connect everything once the house is finished. Tell the builder before the insulation goes in so the boxes are left where we marked them.

The water side of the house

Our house is a few doors from the canal. What should the specification sheet of a door station say before we accept it for this location?

Look for three things. First, a water and dust rating of at least IP65, which 2N describes as "completely dust-tight and resistant to water sprayed from a nozzle," and IP66 if the door has no roof over it. Second, an impact rating such as IK08 or IK10 if the station is at a gate or anywhere it can be knocked. Third, the housing: stainless steel or anodized aluminum rather than painted steel, because paint chips and the steel under it rusts quickly in salt air.

Then check the parts the sheet does not mention: the screws, the gasket and the cable entry. A rated station held on with plain screws, or with its cable coming in from the top, will still let water in. On our installations here every outdoor fastener is stainless or coated, the cable enters from below or through the wall behind, and the gasket is checked at handover.

Friends often arrive by boat and tie up at our dock. Can the buzzer let them in through the back gate without someone walking out to meet them?

Yes. A weather-rated station at the dock gate, wired to the same indoor stations as the front door, rings the house when someone arrives from the water. The household sees or hears them, presses release, and the gate opens. Many systems can give the dock station its own ring so you know which side the visitor is on.

The station sits on the gate pier or a post set back from the bulkhead, in a sealed housing, and the cable to the house runs underground or in a protected path along the fence. The gate needs a latch that closes by itself after each visitor, since a dock gate left open undoes the whole point. If the gate is a light fence gate, we check whether its frame can hold a release before quoting.

Close neighbors, stores and family

The house across our court is only a few yards from our front door. Will the station's light and the visitor's voice disturb them at night?

They do not have to. The light on most door stations can be set to light only the button, or to come on only when someone approaches, rather than glowing all night into a neighbor's window. The speaker volume is adjustable, and on a court this narrow we set it as low as still lets the household hear the visitor clearly.

Placement helps too. A station on the side of the door casing, facing along the court rather than straight across it, sends its light and sound toward the visitor and away from the house opposite. We look at the neighbor's windows during the walkthrough and settle the position and settings with you before mounting.

Our store on Gerritsen Avenue shares side walls with the shops on both sides and has a flat roof. How does the cable get from the front door to the counter?

Inside your own store. In a one-story building the usual route is up from the door frame, across the ceiling or above the drop ceiling, and down to the counter, with the power supply in a back room or above the ceiling where staff can reach it. Nothing goes through a shared side wall, and nothing goes on the roof, which is often shared or maintained by someone else.

Where the store has a finished ceiling with no space above, the cable runs in a slim raceway along the top of the wall, painted to match if you want. Either way the work stays within your space, and if you rent, the landlord's written approval is the only thing we need from outside it.

My mother lives on the ground floor of our two-family and has fallen before. If she cannot get to the door, how would an ambulance crew get in?

With a system that can be answered by phone, a family member upstairs or at work can see the crew at the door and release it from wherever they are. That is the most useful feature for a household in your situation, and we set it up on at least two family members' phones so one is always reachable.

The release has to reach the right door. If your mother's apartment has its own entrance, it gets its own release, not only the shared front door. We also recommend a key held by a trusted neighbor on the court, because no electronic system replaces a person nearby, and we can show you how to check that the remote release works on a quiet day rather than finding out in an emergency.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Gerritsen Beach

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary about door buzzers and you get confident numbers lifted from national cost guides and from companies that sell cloud entry systems. Some are fine somewhere. Here are seven we see repeated, each traced to its source and set against what we find at the bungalows, raised houses and two-family rebuilds of Gerritsen Beach. Where a claim below is described as something an AI says, it is a claim from these cost guides that AI summaries commonly repeat, not a quote from any one AI tool.

"Installing an intercom costs an average of $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's cost page gives that figure, with a spread from $1,500 to $15,000. Its average mixes whole-house room intercoms in suburban homes with apartment entry systems, which are different products bought by different people.

For Gerritsen Beach the average lands in the wrong place for nearly everyone. A one-family bungalow pays well under it for a first video station. A raised two-family with a gate station usually pays under it too, in our $700 to $1,800 video range. On our list, about $2,500 is where a system for a multi-apartment building starts, and there are very few of those here.

The figure that helps is the range for your kind of house, which is why we quote from photos and a count of households rather than a national average.

"A wired intercom takes about ten hours to install"

HomeServe's cost guide says "Installing a wired system typically takes around ten hours," with professional labor between $1,193 and $1,446. That estimate is for running new wire between rooms of a whole house.

A door buzzer for a bungalow on a court is a smaller job. The station, the release and one or two indoor stations, with cable fished through hollow frame walls, usually fit in a single visit. Brick Underground's 2019 intercom guide quoted an installer estimating that a whole five-apartment building could be done in a day.

Where time does grow here is on the stair and the gate: a new route into a raised house that avoids the stair, a gate that needs latch work, or a dock station with an underground run. Those are named on the quote, so the length of the visit is known before it starts.

"Wired systems require a licensed electrician"

The same HomeServe guide says wired systems "require professional installation by a licensed electrician." For the low-voltage parts of a door buzzer, the station, the indoor stations, the strike and the cable between them, that is not how the work is usually divided.

AESS installs those low-voltage parts under New York State license #12000287431. What we do not do is electrical work. If the house has no outlet where the buzzer's power supply belongs, that outlet is an electrician's job, and we tell you exactly where it is needed before the installation date.

On a raised house this split matters more than usual, because the outlet should be on the living floor, not in the flood-level space below. Telling your electrician that up front avoids an outlet in the wrong place.

"Wireless is the best option if you want to save money"

HomeAdvisor says "Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY," with wireless systems from $150 to $1,500. For a renter or a single room, that is fair.

At a Gerritsen Beach door it runs into two problems. The release in the frame is wired whatever the stations are, so a wireless kit still needs a wired strike and power at the door. And a gate or dock station at the far end of a lot, below a raised house, is exactly where a home Wi-Fi signal from the floor above is weakest.

In a frame house, where new cable climbs easily, wired stations are often no more expensive than wireless and far more dependable. We use wireless only where a run truly cannot be made, and say so on the quote.

"Modern video entry costs $1,000 to $5,000 per door"

Swiftlane's cost guide gives "$1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door for modern IP or video systems." Swiftlane sells cloud entry systems for apartment buildings, and its figures are national.

Per door is the wrong unit for a Gerritsen Beach house. A raised two-family may have a front door, a gate and a ground-level door, but only the front door needs a full station. The gate needs a call button and a release, and the ground-level door needs a closer and a strike. Multiplying a per-door figure by three would wildly overstate the job.

On our list, the whole of that layout usually fits inside the $700 to $1,800 video range, because what drives cost here is the household count, the route and the door hardware, not a count of doors.

"Small buildings don't need an intercom, so a buzzer is optional"

ButterflyMX's guide to the city's intercom law says the rule is "generally described as applying to Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more units," and that smaller buildings "are not generally treated the same way." That is accurate for nearly every house here.

It leaves out two things. Once a buzzer is in a rented two-family, the owner should expect to keep it working, since the Housing Maintenance Code also requires a functioning intercom where there is one. And "not required" does not mean "not needed" on a house whose front door is a story above the walk and whose courier stops at the gate.

So the decision for a Gerritsen Beach house is rarely about the law. It is about whether the household can see who is there, let them in from where they are, and keep packages off the walk.

"Video entry comes with a monthly fee per unit"

Swiftlane's guide says software subscriptions "commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month." Habitat's 2017 article on smartphone buzzers cited a charge of $2.50 per unit per month at one building. Cloud panels do usually carry a subscription.

Wired door stations with indoor stations carry none. For a two-family house that wants to see and release visitors from inside, there is nothing to pay each month once the system is installed.

FAQ

Gerritsen Beach Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Everyone on our court knows each other. Why would we need to see who is ringing?
+

Because most of the people who ring are not neighbors. Couriers, contractors, meter readers and strangers all come to the door, and on a raised house the person answering may have to come down a full story of stairs to find out who it is. Seeing and speaking to the visitor from inside lets the household decide before opening, and lets an older parent or a tenant on another floor deal with a delivery without going outside at all.

Is a keypad at the door a good idea on a court where people walk past a few feet away?
+

It can be, with a little care. A keypad is mounted so the person typing stands between it and the walkway, codes are long enough not to be guessed, and each person gets their own code so one can be changed without changing everyone's. If you would rather nobody types a code in view of the court, a station that rings the household and a key for the family is just as good, and we set it up whichever way you prefer.

Can each of our teenagers get a personal code so we know who came home and when?
+

Yes, on systems with code entry and an app. Each person gets their own code, and the app can show which code opened the door and at what time. Codes can be suspended or changed in a minute if one is shared too widely. Simpler audio and video stations without code entry do not keep this kind of record, so if it matters to you, mention it before we quote and we will choose equipment that does.

Our rebuilt house has a fiberglass front door. Can it take an electric strike?
+

Usually yes, because an electric strike goes into the frame, not the door. What matters is the frame around the latch: it needs enough solid material to hold the strike and its screws, and a door that stays aligned so the latch drops into it. Many newer doors come in a pre-hung frame that is fine for a strike. If yours is not, we say so at the walkthrough and price a reinforcing plate or a different release before you approve anything.

Our house is new and the builder's warranty is still running. Will drilling for the buzzer affect it?
+

That depends on your builder's terms, so check them before the installation. What we can do is keep the work small and documented: one sealed hole beside the door for the station's cable, a strike in the frame, cable run through existing cavities, and photos of each penetration before and after. Many owners send the builder our plan first, and we are happy to adjust the route if the builder asks for a particular wall or entry point.

Our router is upstairs. Will a gate station at the bottom of the lot reach our Wi-Fi?
+

Often not reliably, which is why we wire gate and dock stations rather than relying on Wi-Fi. A station at the foot of a raised house or at the back of a long canal lot is far from an upstairs router, with floors, walls and sometimes a steel stair in between. A cable to the gate keeps the station working whatever the Wi-Fi is doing. Phone answering still needs the house's internet, but the station and the indoor stations do not.

Can the door station take its power from the porch light that is already by our front door?
+

No. A porch light runs on household current and is switched on and off, while a door station and strike need steady low-voltage power from their own supply, with battery backup so the door works in an outage. The power supply plugs into an outlet on the living floor, and the low-voltage cable runs from there to the door. If there is no outlet in the right spot, your electrician adds one; AESS does not do electrical work.

Can we put in the door station now and add the station for the upstairs apartment after it is finished?
+

Yes, if we plan for it now. We install a station with a button for each household, run and label the cable to the upstairs apartment while it is easy to reach, and leave it ready to connect. When the apartment is finished, adding its station is a short visit rather than a new installation. The written quote can show the second station as a separate line so you know its price before you decide when to add it.

Our front gate is a light aluminum fence gate. Can it hold a release?
+

Sometimes. A release needs a solid post to mount on, a gate that closes against it squarely and a latch that drops into it every time.
