The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Bushwick is not a neighborhood of single, tidy camera systems. It is a neighborhood of buildings that have changed hands, changed use and changed tenants many times, and the cameras on them show it. A six-family walk-up on Wilson Avenue may have a recorder the landlord bought ten years ago, a doorbell camera a tenant added, and two cameras from a store downstairs, none of them talking to each other. A converted factory near Morgan Avenue may have a building system in the lobby and a dozen private cameras put up by the people who live and work in its units. A frame house on a side street may run its cameras over Wi-Fi from a router in the second-floor apartment. When something stops working, the first job is usually working out what is there, who owns it and where it is wired back to.

The second thing that shapes repairs here is power. On the evening of August 7, 2025, a manhole fire in front of 18 Irving Avenue, at Irving and Jefferson Street, cut power to about 240 customers at its height, and News 12 Brooklyn reported that 18 Irving Avenue was still without power the next evening. "The lights started flickering then there was a very loud pop outside and everything just went dark," a witness, Harris Smith, told the station. In February 2026 it happened again on a larger scale: BK Reader reported that hundreds of Con Edison customers lost power on a Saturday night in Bushwick, after the utility found "an electric problem in some parts of Bushwick in Brooklyn" and said, "We had to shut off power temporarily to prevent more extended outages." In the July 2026 heat wave, News 12 counted more than 1,500 Brooklyn customers without power on a Sunday morning, and Con Edison said it had reduced voltage in parts of the city to protect the system. Each of those events is hard on a camera system. A recorder that loses power while writing to its drive can lose its recordings or its settings, a network switch can come back with dead ports, and a cheap power box can fail when the current returns.

The third thing is what the footage is for. In Bushwick, camera video regularly turns into the only account of what happened. In July 2026 News 12 reported that a woman took five rugs worth about $4,000 from the balcony of Chyelle, a vintage furniture store and cafe on Cook Street, and that "surveillance video shows a woman arriving at Chyelle" around 2 a.m.; a detective told the owner she came back three times and used a hand truck. In June 2026, after a fire at the South Bushwick Reformed Church at 855 Bushwick Avenue, Brownstoner reported that "security video from a property neighboring the church shows a person making repeat trips" along the Stanhope Street side, and that the longtime resident who owned that camera had shared it with city investigators. In May 2025, News 12's account of a stabbing inside a discount store at 937 Broadway was built almost entirely from the store's own video, beginning with a man buying a knife at about 10:30 a.m. And in February 2026, when armed men walked into two apartments in a building on Myrtle Avenue near Stockholm Street just after 2:15 a.m., News 12's report made no mention of any video at all. Footage helps only when the camera was recording, the clock was right and the picture was good enough to use. Repairs here are mostly about making sure all three are true on the night it matters.

The buildings themselves are the fourth factor. The community board's history of Bushwick describes a neighborhood of two- to six-family houses, large apartment houses built in the 1920s, and mansions along Bushwick Avenue built between 1880 and 1913 for brewers and doctors. Many of the early houses are wood frame, so a camera on one of them is fixed to wood or siding rather than brick. North of Flushing Avenue and around Morgan Avenue, factories have been turning into lofts and studios since the 1990s, and the McKibbin Lofts at 248 and 255 McKibbin Street alone housed about 400 people when Untapped Cities wrote about them in 2012. The J and Z run over Broadway and the M over Myrtle Avenue, and in 2023 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Linden Street Historic District, one block on Linden Street and Bushwick Avenue with 32 late-19th-century brownstone houses.

Finally, Bushwick is a neighborhood of renters. Most people who live here do not own the cameras on their building, and many do not know who does. Brick Underground, answering a Brooklyn renter whose packages kept disappearing, noted that New York City does not require buildings to have security cameras and that a landlord is not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process, while attorney Catherine Grad said of asking a landlord to review footage during ongoing thefts, "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord." In practice, a Bushwick repair often starts with a tenant who noticed the camera was dead and a landlord who agrees to pay for it. We work with both, but the person who controls the recorder approves the work.

The six-family walk-up: the recorder in the cellar and the cameras on three floors

The six-family brick walk-up is the standard Bushwick apartment building, and its camera system is usually the landlord's. The cameras sit over the outer door, inside the vestibule, in the stair hall and sometimes at the back yard or roof door, and their cables run down the stair hall to a recorder in the cellar, next to the meters, or in a locked hall closet. That location is the root of many faults. Cellars are damp, closets get hot, and both are places where the recorder shares a power strip with a boiler control, a router or whatever a tenant plugged in. When the power on the block drops, the recorder goes down with no warning, and if it was writing to the drive at that moment, the file system can be damaged.

The repair in a walk-up usually begins at that box. We check whether the recorder is actually recording, whether its clock is right, whether the drive reports any errors, and what the power strip and battery backup look like. Then we go up the stairs camera by camera. We label each cable as we go, so the next call about the same building starts with a map instead of a guess, and we tell the landlord in writing whether the recorder should move somewhere drier or cooler.

The 1920s apartment house: cameras added by different hands over the years

The bigger apartment houses that went up in Bushwick in the 1920s have lobbies, long corridors and more doors, and they have collected cameras the same way they collected wiring: one improvement at a time. A lobby camera from one installer, a mailbox camera from another, a set of hall cameras from a third, sometimes on two recorders that nobody has looked at together. When a tenant says "the cameras are down," it is common to find that one recorder is fine and the other has been dead for months. On these buildings we inventory both recorders, trace which camera goes to which, and fix what failed. If it makes sense to bring everything onto one recorder, that is a separate written price that the owner can approve on its own.

The two- and three-family house: one system, several households

On the side streets between the shopping strips, two- and three-family houses are the most common building, and their cameras usually belong to the owner who lives in one of the units. The recorder sits wherever that owner put it, often in the cellar or their own closet, and the cameras watch the stoop, the alley down the side and the yard. The fragile link is often the internet. Many of these systems reach the owner's phone through a router in someone else's apartment, so when a tenant moves out and cancels their service, the cameras keep recording but vanish from the owner's phone. We check that first, because it is cheaper than any camera, and we can move the recorder onto the owner's own internet connection so a tenant's move no longer takes the app with it.

The frame house with siding: water, wood and holes in the wall

Many Bushwick houses are wood frame; the New York Times described it in the mid-1960s as "a neatly maintained community of wood houses," and many of them still stand. On these houses the cameras are fastened through siding into wood, and the cable usually enters the wall through a hole drilled beside the camera. Over a few winters that hole opens up, the sealant cracks, and water runs along the cable into the wall and sometimes into the camera's own connection. The symptoms are a camera that drops out after heavy rain, a picture that flickers in wet weather, or a stain on the wall inside. The fix is not just a new camera. The camera goes back on a proper mounting block that sits flat on the siding, the screws go into solid framing, the cable enters through a sealed fitting with a drip loop below it, and old holes are filled so water cannot follow them in.

The Bushwick Avenue mansion: big rooms, deep cellars and cable through several apartments

The community board's history describes the mansions along Bushwick Avenue as built between 1880 and 1913 for brewers and doctors, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission has called the group at Bushwick Avenue and Linden Street one of the finest of its kind in the city. Many of these houses are now divided into apartments. That makes camera repair a puzzle of access: the original wiring may run up through a closet in one unit, across a ceiling in another and out to a camera on the porch roof, and the recorder may still be in the owner's former apartment. Before the visit we ask which units we will need to enter, so the owner can give notice to tenants, and we plan the trace so we open as few doors as possible. Where the house is inside the Linden Street Historic District, we keep the replacement camera in the same position and on the same mounting points wherever they are sound, so the repair stays an ordinary maintenance job.

The converted factory loft: concrete, steel and cameras from many owners

Bushwick's loft buildings are the hardest camera systems in the neighborhood to repair, and not because of the equipment. Untapped Cities described the area around Morgan Avenue in 2012 as a place where factories had been turning into lofts since the 1990s, and Brownstoner reported office conversions under way or planned on Moore Street, Cook Street, Johnson Avenue, Jefferson Street and Evergreen Avenue in 2016. These buildings have thick concrete floors and steel frames that stop Wi-Fi cold, long corridors, freight elevators and loading docks. The landlord usually has a system covering the lobby, the elevator and the dock. Then the residents and studio tenants add their own cameras on their doors and inside their units, some wired to the building's network and some on their own Wi-Fi, and over time the line between the building's system and everyone else's gets lost.

On a loft repair we start by drawing that line: which cameras belong to the building, which recorder they go to, and which are tenants' devices. Then we fix the building's system and tell tenants honestly when their own Wi-Fi camera in a concrete corridor is never going to hold a connection.

The mixed-use building on a shopping strip: shop and building sharing walls

On Knickerbocker Avenue, Wyckoff Avenue, Myrtle Avenue and Broadway, most buildings have a store at street level and apartments above. The store's cameras and the building's often share a cellar, a riser or even a recorder a previous tenant left behind, so before we change anything we confirm which camera and recorder belong to whom. Field Notes

Field Notes: Checking a Bushwick Camera System the Morning After an Outage

These notes describe how our technicians work through a camera system in a building that has just been through a power cut, the most common reason for a Bushwick call. They set out standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Read the screen before touching the box. The technician photographs the recorder screen first: which channels show live video, which are black, what the drive status says, and what time the recorder thinks it is. If the recorder is sitting on a prompt to initialize its drive, nobody presses anything until we know whether there is footage on it you need.

Go back to the outlet. Next comes the power the system actually gets: the outlet, the power strip, the battery backup and its battery, the central power box for analog cameras, and the switch that powers network cameras. After an outage, a dead battery backup or a switch with a few failed ports explains more dark cameras than any other single part.

Test the dark cameras from the recorder end out. Each dark camera gets a power reading at the port or the power box, then a cable test, then a check at the camera itself. Only when power and cable are good is the camera blamed.

Fix the clock and the warnings. Before leaving, the recorder's time is set to update itself, failure alerts are switched on and tested, and the owner sees a playback from after the repair. Every cable we traced is labeled and noted on the job record.

Fault by Fault

Outages, Access, Weather or Wear: How Bushwick Camera Systems Break and What Each Repair Involves

Four causes cover most of what we find in Bushwick buildings and stores. Each leaves its own signs, and recognizing them is what keeps a repair to the part that actually failed instead of a new system you did not need.

Outages: what a block-wide power cut does to a camera system

When the power drops on a block, as it did around Irving Avenue and Jefferson Street in August 2025 and across parts of Bushwick in February 2026, every piece of the camera system goes down at once and comes back at once, and they do not all come back in the same condition. The recorder is the most vulnerable. If it was writing to its drive when the power went, the drive's file system can be damaged, and the recorder may come back saying the drive is unformatted or asking to initialize it, or it may run normally but quietly record nothing. The network switch is next. Switches that power cameras through their data cables sometimes come back with individual ports that no longer supply power, so three or four cameras stay dark while the rest return. Central power boxes for older analog cameras can fail outright when the current returns, taking every camera on them with them. And a battery backup that was meant to protect all of it may have had a worn-out battery for years.

The repair follows that order: protect any footage, test the drive, test the switch ports and power box, and only then look at cameras. A drive swap runs $200 to $450 on our Brooklyn list depending on capacity, a PoE port swap $150 to $350, and power supply repair $150 to $400. If the recorder itself is damaged, a DVR repair averages $300 to $700 and an NVR $400 to $900. In most outage calls the cameras on the wall are fine.

Access: the recorder nobody can get into

Bushwick buildings change hands, managers and tenants often, and the camera system's passwords rarely change with them. We are regularly called to recorders that are working perfectly but cannot be used, because the admin password belonged to a previous owner, a former super, a store's last tenant or a roommate who moved out. The symptoms are a recorder that rejects every login, an app account tied to someone else's email, or a former owner who still gets the alerts. The fix depends on the brand and model. Many recorders have a manufacturer reset process that requires proof of ownership, some can be reset at the box, and a few cannot be reset without losing settings. We tell you which kind you have before we start, protect any footage first, and then set the system up with new accounts for the people who should have them, and nobody else.

Weather: frame houses, roofs and the cold vestibule

Weather does its damage slowly in Bushwick. On frame houses, the seal around the hole where the cable enters the wall cracks after a few winters, and rain follows the cable in. On the flat roofs that run along many rows of buildings, cameras on bulkhead doors and parapets take full sun, wind and ice, and their plugs work loose or corrode. In vestibules, cameras sit in the cold air that blows in every time the outer door opens, and condensation inside the housing fogs the picture on winter mornings. Reolink's own guidance on cameras that go offline lists exactly these causes, from "corroded RJ45 connectors" to outdoor damage from moisture, heat and freezing temperatures.

Most of these repairs are at the connection, not the camera. The plug is cut back to clean wire and terminated again with a sealed fitting, the cable entry is resealed with a drip loop, and loose mounts are refixed into solid material. Where water has already reached the camera's board, the camera is replaced: an outdoor camera with the weatherproofing redone runs $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list.

Wear: drives, clocks, apps and old recorders

Some failures are simply age. Recorder drives wear out, especially in hot closets and back rooms, and they usually fail by recording less and less, or recording nothing, long before they stop completely. Recorder clocks drift if they are not set to update themselves, so that months later the time on a clip is minutes or hours off. Phone apps change, and older recorders stop working with them after an update or a change in the manufacturer's service. Reolink also points to firmware mismatches between the camera and the recorder, and to two devices sharing one network address, as common reasons cameras drop off a system, and both happen when a building adds equipment piecemeal. These are repairs of a drive, a setting or a firmware version far more often than of a camera. Where an older analog system needs to keep its cameras but gain network cameras, a hybrid recorder swap runs $700 to $1,200 installed on our Brooklyn list.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Bushwick: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bushwick landlords, tenants, loft residents, homeowners and shopkeepers describe when they call us about their cameras. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Planned shutoffs and block outages

Con Edison cut power to part of Bushwick on purpose in February to stop a longer outage. If that happens again, should we shut the camera system down ourselves first?

If you get warning and have a few minutes, yes, shutting the recorder down properly is better than letting it lose power while it writes. Use the recorder's own shutdown option from its menu, wait for it to finish, then switch off the power strip. When power returns, switch on the switch or power box first and the recorder last, and check playback within the hour. If you get no warning, a battery backup sized for the recorder lets it ride through short cuts and shut down cleanly in long ones. Either way, the first sign of trouble is usually the drive, so if the recorder comes back asking to initialize anything, stop and call us before you answer it.

Finding and using footage

Our camera ran all night and we need to find a person who came back several times between 2 and 4 a.m. Is there a faster way than watching all of it?

Usually, yes. Most current recorders can search a time range for motion events only, show the night as a strip of thumbnails, or play back at high speed with the motion marked on a timeline. Many also let you draw a box over one area, such as a balcony, a gate or a doorway, and list only the moments something moved inside it. Older recorders may not have these tools, and then high-speed playback is the only option. We can find the moments for you on the visit and save each one with its timestamp.

At night, anyone walking past our camera turns into a smeared ghost, though people standing still look fine. Why?

That is the camera slowing its shutter to gather more light. In the dark, many cameras keep each frame open longer so the picture looks bright, and anything moving across the frame during that time blurs. A person standing still looks sharp, a person walking looks like a smear, which is the opposite of what you need. The fix is usually in the settings: a faster shutter limit, a little more gain, and infrared or white light that reaches the spot where people actually walk. If the camera's settings cannot do it, a replacement camera that handles low light better is the answer, and night vision repair on our Brooklyn list runs $250 to $650.

Loft buildings and freight elevators

Several loft tenants put up their own cameras in our shared corridor. Can the building's system and theirs live side by side, or do theirs have to come down?

That is the landlord's decision and the lease's, not ours, but the technical answer is that they can coexist if someone decides who owns what. The building's cameras should go to the building's recorder on the building's network, under the landlord's control. A tenant's camera on their own door is their own device on their own Wi-Fi, and it should not look into another unit. Problems start when tenant cameras are plugged into the building's network or power, or point down the whole corridor. We can map which is which, move the building's cameras onto their own network so tenant devices cannot interfere, and give the landlord a list of what we found.

The camera in our loft building's freight elevator only works when the car is near the ground floor. What causes that?

That pattern almost always points to the cable that travels with the car, not the camera. As the car rises, the traveling cable flexes, and a damaged conductor or a loose connection opens and closes with the movement. A camera powered or connected over a weak run can also fail as the run gets longer. We test the camera and its connection on the car with the elevator stopped at different floors, then work back along the run. Any work in the hoistway or on top of the car is done with your elevator maintenance company, who controls safety there, so we arrange that visit through the building once we know where the fault is.

Frame houses and divided mansions

There is a water stain on the inside wall behind the camera on our frame house. Did the camera cause it, and can it be fixed without opening the wall?

It very likely came in where the camera's cable goes through the wall. When that hole was drilled, it was sealed, and after a few winters the sealant cracks and rain follows the cable inside, especially if the cable enters level or angled down into the house. Fixing it does not usually mean opening the wall. We take the camera down, reseal or replace the cable entry with a proper fitting, make the cable drop below the entry before it rises into it so water drips off, and put the camera back on a mounting block that sheds water away from the hole. The stain itself is a job for whoever repairs your drywall or paint, but the leak stops at our end.

Our Bushwick Avenue house was divided into apartments, and the old camera wiring runs through three different units. How do you reach it without going into every apartment?

We plan the trace before the visit so we enter as few units as possible. From the recorder end we can test each cable, use a tone generator to find which one leads where, and often reach a fault from the cellar, the hall or the outside of the house. Where we do need a unit, the owner gives the tenant notice, and we tell you in advance which unit, why and roughly how long. If a run through a tenant's apartment has failed, it is often simpler to replace it with a new path up the outside or through a common hall than to repair it inside the unit, and we price both options so you can choose.

Doors, buzzers and the street outside

Someone buzzed a stranger into our building at 2:30 in the morning. The lobby camera shows the door opening, but not who came in. Can the camera be changed so that does not happen again?

Often it can. Many lobby cameras are mounted high and aimed at the whole lobby, so a person walking in is a small figure with their head down. A camera aimed back at the inner door from about head height, or a second camera inside the vestibule pointed at the person standing at the intercom panel, gives you a face. We look at where the existing camera sits, whether it can be re-aimed or moved on the same cable, and whether a second camera is worth it. The camera cannot show which apartment pressed the button, but it can show clearly who was let in and when.

A building next door put up a sidewalk shed, and our cameras now see nothing but plywood. Can they be moved for the months the shed is up?

Yes. The usual answer is to move or re-aim the cameras that face the shed so they look along the sidewalk under it, or to add a temporary camera at the point where people come out from under the shed toward your door. Cable can often be extended along your own facade without touching the shed, which belongs to the neighbor's contractor. When the shed comes down, we put the cameras back in their original positions and check the aim. Each move is priced on its own, and if a camera can simply be re-aimed, that is usually covered within the service call.

Store systems under the els

Our discount store's recorder has sixteen cameras, but the screen behind the counter only shows four big pictures. Are the other twelve recording?

Possibly, possibly not. The screen layout and the recording are separate settings. Someone may have switched the monitor to a four-camera view, or set it to cycle, while all sixteen cameras keep recording in the background. Or the other twelve may have stopped and nobody noticed because they were never on the screen. The quick check is playback: pick a time from last night and open each of the twelve. We check every channel's recording schedule on the visit, set the counter screen to the view you want, and turn on alerts so a camera that stops recording tells someone.

Our door camera on Broadway sits in the shade of the el all day, and anyone in the doorway is a dark shape against the bright street. Can it be fixed?

In most cases, yes, without replacing the camera. The camera is exposing for the bright street behind the person, so the doorway goes dark. Turning on the camera's backlight compensation or wide dynamic range mode, setting its exposure for the doorway rather than the whole frame, and lowering or narrowing the view so the bright street takes up less of the picture usually brings faces back. If the camera has no such settings, a replacement camera made for strong backlight is the fix, priced as an outdoor camera replacement at $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list.

Elevator and corridor cameras

Cameras in freight and passenger elevators and on long loft corridors, fed by traveling cable or long runs that test fine at one end and fail at the other.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes for analog cameras, plug-in adapters and battery backups, tested under load and replaced when they no longer carry the system through a short outage.

Counter screens, lobby monitors and app access for owners, managers, roommates and family, set up again after an internet change or a change of owner.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Bushwick

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers and in national cost and how-to guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is paraphrased below, then checked against what repairs actually look like in Bushwick's walk-ups, lofts and stores.

"After a power outage, just unplug everything and plug it back in"

Troubleshooting guides almost always start with a power cycle. Reolink's guide, for one, suggests unplugging a camera for about ten seconds and the router and switches for about thirty, then waiting a few minutes for everything to boot.

For a single Wi-Fi camera that is sensible. For a recorder that has just been through a block outage like the ones around Irving Avenue in 2025 and across parts of Bushwick in 2026, it can be the wrong first move. If the drive was damaged when the power dropped, every extra restart is another chance for the recorder to overwrite or lose what was on it, and a restart can bring up a prompt that, if accepted, wipes the drive.

If you need nothing from the recording, a careful restart is fine. If you might need last night's video, leave the recorder running, photograph the screen and call first. We save the footage before anything is restarted.

"If the app says a camera is offline, the camera has failed"

Generic answers often treat an "offline" message on the phone as proof that a camera is dead and needs replacing.

In Bushwick the most common reason a camera shows offline in the app is that the building's internet changed. A tenant moved out and cancelled the service the recorder was using, a landlord switched providers, or a new router replaced the old one. The cameras keep recording on the recorder, but the recorder can no longer reach the app. Reolink's list of offline causes includes the router, Wi-Fi distance, network address conflicts and cloud outages long before it gets to a broken camera.

Check the recorder's own screen first. If the cameras are live there, the fault is the network or the app account, and it is usually fixed in settings within the $150 service call, with no new camera at all.

"If the recorder asks to initialize the drive, say yes to clear the error"

Forum answers and quick guides often treat the initialize or format prompt as a routine fix for a drive error.

Initializing a recorder's drive erases it. Every recording on it is gone, including the night you might need. After an outage that prompt is common, because the recorder has found something wrong with the drive, and the drive may still hold readable footage that a technician can try to recover before it is wiped.

If you see that prompt, cancel it, leave the recorder on and call. If there is nothing on the drive you need, initializing it may be the right step, but it should be a decision, not a reflex, and a drive that has failed once is worth testing before you trust it again.

"Wireless cameras are the easy fix for any building"

AI summaries often recommend replacing a troublesome wired camera with a Wi-Fi model, on the grounds that it avoids cable work.

Bushwick's buildings are hard on Wi-Fi. Converted factories have concrete floors and steel frames, walk-ups have brick walls and long stair halls, and in a loft building dozens of tenants' networks crowd the same air. Reolink's own guidance names thick walls and distance from the router as leading reasons wireless cameras go offline. A Wi-Fi camera in a concrete loft corridor or a cellar may never hold a steady connection, however good the camera is.

Wi-Fi cameras make sense on a frame house close to its router. In most Bushwick walk-ups and lofts, repairing or replacing the wired camera on its existing cable is the cheaper and more reliable route, and the existing cable is usually fine.

"The landlord has to keep the cameras working and show tenants the footage"

Some answers suggest that New York landlords must provide working cameras and must share video with tenants who ask.

Brick Underground, putting the question to two New York attorneys, reported that city buildings are not required to have security cameras, and that a landlord does not have to show tenants footage outside a legal process such as a police request or a court order. Attorney Jennifer Rozen suggested that "a tenant's association [can] advocate for the landlord to install a better lock/intercom system or a package locker."

So in a Bushwick rental, a dead hallway camera is fixed when the person who controls the recorder decides to fix it. If you are a tenant, the useful move is a short written request to the landlord naming the camera and the problem. If you are the landlord, we can give you a written cause and price you can share with the building.

"Camera repair in Brooklyn averages about $1,400"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page for surveillance camera installation or repair gives an average of $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and AI answers sometimes repeat that figure as the cost of a repair.

That average combines new installations with repairs. Most Bushwick repairs are a single fault after an outage or a change of tenant: a drive, a switch port, a power box, a password, one camera. On our Brooklyn list those single faults land between $150 and $700, and only a new recorder, a partial rewire or several cameras at once reach $700 to $1,500.

The realistic budget for one problem is the $150 service call plus the one failed part, and you see that price in writing before the work. A big average can scare an owner into replacing a whole system that needed one part.

"Anyone handy can fix a camera system"

Many answers present camera repair as a quick do-it-yourself job with a ladder and a screwdriver.

On your own house, you are free to try. But the New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and the rules cover closed-circuit television used to detect intrusion or theft. A landlord, a managing agent or an insurer will usually ask whether the person who worked on the building's cameras was licensed and insured, and work on a walk-up roof or a loft facade is not a stepladder job.

If you hire someone, ask for the license number and a certificate of insurance. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and a certificate for a landlord or managing agent can be arranged before the visit.

Bushwick Security Camera Repair FAQ

Can the camera app be set up in Spanish for a parent, a tenant or an employee?
+

Usually, yes. Many recorder and camera apps either follow the phone's own language setting or have a language choice in their settings. On the visit we check the app your system uses, set it up on each person's phone in the language they prefer where the app allows it, and show them how to open live view and playback.

Our landlord lives outside New York. Can the repair be approved and paid for remotely?
+

Yes. We send the written cause and price by email or text, the owner approves it in writing and pays through our payment link, and the super, a tenant or a neighbor lets the technician in. We confirm who has authority over the recorder before we change any passwords or accounts.

Can a camera be mounted on our fire escape to watch the street or the back yard?
+

We avoid it. A fire escape is an exit, and anything fastened to it can get in the way of people climbing out or catch on clothing in a hurry. We mount the camera on the wall beside the fire escape window instead, aimed past it, with the cable kept clear of the landing and the ladder.

Can our cameras show color at night under the streetlights instead of black and white?
+

Sometimes, depending on the camera. Many cameras switch to black and white with infrared when the light drops, and some can be set to stay in color when there is enough streetlight. Cameras built for color night viewing do this much better. We can test the setting on your existing cameras at the visit and price a replacement where it cannot be done.

What are the signs that a recorder's hard drive is close to failing?
+

A worn drive usually shows itself before it dies: the recorder keeps fewer days than it used to, playback has gaps, the recorder beeps or shows a drive warning, or it restarts on its own. If you notice any of those, send us a photo of the drive status page, and we read the drive's health report on every visit.

Can a replacement camera go higher so nobody can reach it from the sidewalk?
+

Yes, within reason. A camera mounted out of arm's reach and angled down is much harder to turn, cover or pull off. Going too high loses faces, so we aim for a height that still shows a person's face at the door, use a vandal-resistant housing where it is needed, and extend the existing cable to the new position.

Our camera cable runs across the roof onto the building next door. Do we need the neighbor's permission to repair it?
+

For any work on their roof or wall, yes. On rows of buildings with connected roofs, cables sometimes cross a neighbor's property without anyone remembering. We repair what is on your property, and where a run crosses next door we either get the neighbor's agreement first or reroute the cable so it stays on your own building.

We stopped paying for our camera's cloud plan. Will the cameras still work?
+
