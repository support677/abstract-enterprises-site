The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need Intercom Installation

The Brooklyn Navy Yard is bounded by Navy Street, Flushing Avenue, Kent Avenue and the East River, and after the Navy left in 1966 the City turned it into an industrial park run by a not-for-profit, the Brooklyn Navy Yard Development Corporation. That history explains most of what an intercom installer finds inside. The buildings were made for ships, supplies and munitions, not for receptionists. Building 77, the 16-story concrete storehouse that reopened for manufacturing in 2017, was built in about five months in 1941, and its first eleven floors had no windows at all until the renovation cut them in. Floors in that building run 60,000 to 67,500 square feet. The newer buildings, like the Dock 72 office tower and the light-industrial floors at Building 212, were designed for companies rather than ships, but they share the same basic arrangement: a building lobby with security, elevators, long corridors, and a tenant's own door at the end of it.

What makes the intercom matter here is the distance between that door and the people behind it. In a typical Brooklyn office, the person who hears a knock is twenty feet away. In a Navy Yard production suite, the person responsible for receiving might be on a forklift at the far end of the floor, in a gowning room, at a sewing table, or at lunch in the food hall downstairs. A video intercom at the door, with calls routed to the right phones and indoor stations, turns "someone go see who that is" into a two-second decision made from wherever that person is standing. And because the Tenant Manual makes couriers the tenant's responsibility and forbids leaving packages in hallways, a delivery that goes unanswered does not wait politely outside. It leaves, or it ends up somewhere it should not be.

The Yard is also busy and growing. eWEEK's case study on its visitor system described thousands of visitors a day and upwards of 10,000 people coming and going through seven gates, and BNYDC now counts more than 550 businesses on the campus. Marc Cecere, Director of Applications at BNYDC, explained why the Yard replaced its old visitor portal: "Our old system was slow and not very user-friendly. So most of our tenants weren't pre-registering their visitors." The fix the Yard chose, pre-registration with a QR code at the gate, handles the campus. It cannot tell you whether the person now standing at your suite is the buyer you invited, a driver with the wrong building number, or someone a neighbor let onto the floor. That last step happens at your door, and an intercom is how you take it without opening first.

Finally, the Yard's tenants are not the usual customers for a door station. BNYDC's own industry lists run from food and beverage to fashion and visual design, manufacturing, robotics, medtech, life science and clean energy. A robotics company may want its door station to ring the engineering bay and the office at the same time. A food producer needs a station that survives washdown. A lab needs the door answered from the clean side of a gowning room. A design studio may care most that the panel looks right beside its door. The same model of door station will not suit all of them, and the sections below explain how we choose.

Seven things about the Navy Yard that change an intercom plan

  • The landlord does not hold your keys. The 2026 Tenant Manual says tenants and licensees install their own door locks and that BNYDC does not maintain or duplicate keys to tenant spaces. Whatever releases your door, and whoever can operate it remotely, is your company's decision and your company's record to keep.
  • Couriers are your job, not the mailroom's. The Yard mailroom is in Building 6 near the Clinton Gate and runs 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays. The manual says deliveries by FedEx, UPS, Amazon and other couriers are managed directly by the tenant, and that packages are not allowed to be left in hallways or other shared spaces.
  • Floors are huge. Building 77's flyer lists floor plates of 60,000 to 67,500 square feet with columns 20 feet on center, and Building 212's light-industrial floors are 33,000 square feet split into units of 3,000 to 10,000. A desk station by the door does not reach someone at the far end.
  • Building lobbies run on their own systems. Building 77 lists a 24-hour lobby security desk and keycard access, and the Tenant Manual requires a swipe card for that lobby between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. Your intercom picks up where the building's control ends, at the suite.
  • The telecom closet belongs to the building. Four providers serve the Yard, and the manual tells tenants to arrange closet access with their property manager. An IP intercom's network path has to be planned with that in mind.
  • Freight elevators keep hours. The manual shuts freight cars from 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. on weekdays and all weekend, with after-hours service billed by BNYDC. Install days are planned around that schedule.
  • Alterations and signage go through BNYDC. The manual says alterations generally need plans submitted to and approved by BNYDC, that electrical work needs plans and a licensed electrician, and that signage needs approval. A door station with a company name on it touches both.

Repair Calls in the Navy Yard

Intercom Down at a Navy Yard Suite? Video That Went Black, One-Way Audio, or an App That Stopped Ringing

Repair calls from workplace intercoms sound different from apartment calls, and in the Yard they tend to fall into four groups. The first is the station that still lights up but no longer reaches anyone. Visitors press the button, the panel plays its tone, and nothing rings upstairs or on anyone's phone. In a networked system that is usually not the door station at all. It is an expired cloud subscription, a phone app that was logged out when someone changed handsets, a call group that still points at an employee who left, or a router or firewall change that blocked the traffic. The second group is one-way audio, where staff can hear the visitor but the visitor cannot hear them, or the reverse. That is often a settings or network problem as well, though a speaker damaged by water or impact gives the same symptom. The third is video that went black or froze, which can be a failing camera module, a power supply at the edge of its rating, or a network switch port that has stopped delivering power over the cable. The fourth is the door that will not release when staff press the button, which points to the strike, the lock power, or the wiring between them.

Each of those has a different first check, and we do them in that order because the cheapest fix is often in software. Before a technician comes out, we ask what changed recently: a new internet provider or router, a phone upgrade, a person who left, a fit-out next door, a power event. If the problem is a forgotten administrator password or a subscription that lapsed, we tell you that up front. If the panel itself is damaged or the model is no longer supported, we tell you whether a repair still makes sense or whether the money is better spent on a replacement that fits the way your team works now. A repair visit is quoted before work starts.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Every Navy Yard intercom job starts with three questions: which doors visitors actually arrive at, who should answer them at different times of day, and where the equipment can live given that the building controls the shared closets. The services below are the combinations we plan for most often inside the Yard's wall.

Suite entry video stations

A camera door station beside the suite door off the corridor, so staff see the visitor's face, talk to them and decide before anyone opens up.

Receiving and dock stations

A rugged station at the receiving door or loading side, with a call that reaches the shipping lead wherever they are instead of a bell nobody hears.

Call groups and phone answering

Calls that ring a group of phones at once, or one after another, with day and night schedules, so a visitor is never left talking to an empty desk.

Desk phone and SIP integration

Door stations registered as an extension on a company's internet phone system, so the door rings the same phones staff already carry.

Internal talk-back

Stations between the office, the production floor, a mezzanine, a lab and the receiving area, so staff talk across a big suite without walking it.

Overhead paging tie-ins

A door call that also sounds over speakers or a horn on a loud floor, where a phone in a pocket and a desk station both go unheard.

Fit-out intercom design

A written plan for white-box suites: door station locations, cable rough-in, rack space, network ports and power, drawn before the ceilings close.

Washdown and lab-grade stations

Sealed, cleanable door and indoor stations for food production rooms and labs, mounted where gloves, gowns and cleaning routines allow.

Takeovers and moves

Documenting and reprogramming a system a previous tenant or contractor left, and moving the administrator account into your company's name.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for the Brooklyn Navy Yard's Architecture

The Yard holds buildings from the 1850s through the 2020s, and an intercom is planned differently in each kind. What changes is the wall the station mounts on, the ceiling the cable runs through, the distance to the nearest network closet, and how many companies share the floor. The cards name the six kinds of space we see most; the notes below them explain the method for each.

Wartime concrete storehouse floors

The upper and lower floors of Building 77: very large concrete plates, columns on a 20-foot grid, ceilings around ten to eleven feet, and walls on the lower floors originally built with no windows.

Mid-rise light-industrial floors

Buildings like Building 212, with 33,000-square-foot floors divided into units of a few thousand square feet, 15-foot slab heights, freight cars and box-truck docks.

Office floors in a new tower

Dock 72's sixteen stories of office space, with a large coworking anchor, finished corridors and suites that look like any Manhattan office until you look out the window.

Nineteenth-century machine shops

The older shop buildings, such as the three that form the Green Manufacturing Center, which date from 1857 to the 1940s and were converted for multiple tenants.

White-box startup suites

Subdivided suites delivered as raw space, like the eight planned for the ninth floor of Building 303, where the tenant fits out everything from the door in.

Labs and R&D suites

Wet labs, clean rooms and automated labs, such as the materials lab announced for Building 20, where the person answering the door may be gowned on the far side of an airlock.

Wartime concrete storehouse floors: plan the path before the price

On a Building 77 floor, the first thing we look at is not the door. It is the ceiling. The concrete deck and the column grid leave very little to hide cable inside, so the question is whether the suite has a drop ceiling, an open ceiling with existing cable trays, or bare concrete with conduit on its face. With a drop ceiling, cable from the door station can run above the tiles to the rack, and only the short drop to the station is visible. With an open ceiling, we follow the same pathways the company's network cable uses, supported properly and kept clear of sprinkler lines, lighting and ductwork. With bare concrete, the honest answer is surface conduit or raceway, run in straight lines along the column grid so it looks deliberate rather than patched.

The second thing is the wall at the suite door. In this building it may be the original concrete, a block partition added for a fit-out, or a stud wall with drywall. Each takes a different mounting box and a different anchor. A station flush-mounted in a stud wall looks clean; flush-mounting in concrete means cutting the concrete, which is rarely worth it, so concrete walls usually get a surface box that matches the station. And because the floors were built as storehouses with very thick walls, we never assume a wireless link from the door to the rack will hold. Cable is the default here.

The third thing is scale. A suite on a 60,000-square-foot floor can easily run several hundred feet from its door to the farthest workbench. That is where call routing to phones and a speaker or horn on the floor earn their place, and where an internal station at the far end saves someone the walk.

Mid-rise light-industrial floors: one door for people, one for freight

Light-industrial units of 3,000 to 10,000 square feet on 33,000-square-foot floors usually have two ways in: a person door off the corridor and a wider door toward the freight elevator or dock. People and deliveries tend to arrive at different ones, and both need a way to call in. A single video station at the person door and a simpler, tougher audio or video station at the freight side, both ringing the same group of phones with different labels, is the arrangement we suggest most often. The labels matter, because whoever answers needs to know which door to walk to.

Slab heights of about fifteen feet mean cable can travel high, out of the way of carts and racking, but they also mean drops to door stations are long and need support all the way down. The shared corridor belongs to the building, so we keep everything we install on the tenant side of the line the lease draws, and anything that would sit on the corridor wall is cleared with property management first.

Office floors in a new tower: fit in with what the building already runs

A Dock 72 suite looks like a conventional office. The building lobby, the elevators and the base-building systems are the landlord's and the developer's, and a large share of the space is run by a coworking operator. A tenant there usually wants something modest at its own suite door: a slim video station that matches the finishes, a release on the glass or wood door, and calls that ring the front desk first and then a group of phones. The work above the ceiling is mostly fishing cable through an occupied office without disturbing anyone, then terminating it in the tenant's own rack or network cabinet.

The design question that comes up most in office suites is what happens when the front desk is empty. A good plan says exactly who gets the call next, and after how many seconds, and what the visitor hears if nobody answers at all.

Nineteenth-century machine shops: old masonry, new tenants

The Green Manufacturing Center's three buildings date from 1857 to the 1940s and were machine shops making ship components during the Second World War, before a 2015 renovation turned them into a multi-tenant complex. Avison Young's March 2026 financing note says the complex is fully leased to four tenants. Older shop buildings like these mix heavy masonry walls with newer partitions, and the high, open interiors mean cable usually travels on the structure rather than inside walls. A door station on old brick or stone goes in a surface box, fixed into the mortar where possible so it can come out later without damaging the masonry.

Because these buildings were converted for several tenants at once, it is common to find one company's entry beside another's, or a shared vestibule. In that case the station has to make clear which company a visitor is calling, and calls have to reach only that company.

White-box startup suites: the best time to plan is before anything is built

BNYDC and the city's Economic Development Corporation sought a designer in 2025 for the ninth floor of Building 303, planning eight subdivided white-box suites for small and mid-sized companies, an office, a shared amenity area and a research and development suite. A white-box suite gives a tenant a rare chance to get the intercom right the first time. The architect is already drawing walls, the ceiling is still open, and the network cable is going in anyway. Adding the intercom to that plan costs far less than adding it after the ceiling tiles are in.

For these suites we provide a short written design that the architect or the general contractor can drop into their drawings: where the door station goes and at what height, what box it needs in the wall, where its cable runs, which network port it uses, where the indoor stations or answering phones are, and how the release is powered. If the fit-out contractor is pulling cable, we can specify the cable and test it before we terminate.

Labs and R&D suites: answer from the clean side

The Brooklyn Eagle reported that Radical AI plans an autonomous materials science lab in Building 20, running about 100 experiments a day, and the Building 303 redesign includes a suite that can be set up as a wet lab or a clean room. Labs change two things about an intercom. The stations inside have to tolerate the room's cleaning routine, which usually means sealed faces, no exposed grilles that trap residue, and buttons that can be pressed with a gloved hand. And the station that answers the door has to be on the side of the airlock or gowning room where people actually work, so nobody breaks gown to see who is at the door. Hands-free answering, or answering from a wall-mounted screen near the bench, is often the right choice.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk the Visitor's Route Before Picking the Door Station

The habit that saves the most trouble on a big commercial floor is simple: before choosing equipment, walk the route a first-time visitor walks. Start at the building lobby, take the elevator they would take, and follow the corridor signs to the suite. On large floors with long corridors and similar-looking doors, that walk tells you three things a floor plan does not. It shows where visitors actually stop and look for a way to call in, which is not always the door the company thinks of as its front. It shows whether the corridor is bright or dim at the door, which decides whether a camera station will show a usable face. And it shows how loud the corridor is when the freight elevator, a neighbor's machines or a cart goes by, which decides how much speaker the station needs.

The second half of the walk is the return trip: from the door to every place a person who answers might be standing. The desk by the door, the far end of the production floor, the mezzanine, the lab, the receiving area. If the plan only works when someone is sitting at the front desk, it will fail the first afternoon that desk is empty. We write the plan around the empty desk.

Call Routing

Answering the Door From Anywhere on a 300-Acre Campus: Call Routing for Navy Yard Teams

Most of the value in a workplace intercom is not the panel on the wall. It is what happens in the next fifteen seconds after a visitor presses the button. In an apartment, the answer is easy: the call rings the apartment. In a Navy Yard company, the right person to answer might be the receptionist at 9 a.m., the production lead at 3 p.m., the shipping manager when a truck is due, and nobody at all at 2 a.m. unless an alarm company or a manager has asked to be reached. A good design writes those rules down and builds them into the system.

The basic tools are the same on most modern door stations, though the names vary by brand. A call can go to one destination, to a group that rings all at once, or to a list that rings in order and moves on after a set number of seconds. Destinations can be indoor screens, phone apps, desk phones on an internet phone system, or a mix. Schedules switch between day and night rules. And there is a final step for the call nobody picks up: a recorded message, a second group, or simply a polite tone that tells the visitor to try again or call the office number. We pick from those tools based on how your company actually works, not on what the brand's brochure shows.

The Yard adds two complications. The first is that people move around. Many tenants have staff who spend part of the day in another building, at a meeting on another floor, at the food hall in Building 77, or out at a vendor. Phone-app answering covers them as long as the phone is charged, logged in and not silenced, which is why we always pair an app with at least one fixed destination that does not depend on a person's phone. The second complication is noise. On a production floor, a phone in a pocket vibrates unnoticed, and a ring from a desk station near the door is inaudible fifty feet away. For those floors, a door call that also triggers a horn, a strobe or an overhead speaker in the work area is the difference between an answered door and a missed one.

There is also the question of what the person who answers can do. Some companies want everyone in the call group to be able to release the door. Others want everyone to be able to see and speak to the visitor, but only a few people, such as a manager or the front desk, to be able to open it. Some want the receiving door releasable only while someone is watching the video. Those permissions are set per person and per door, and we record them in the handover so the company can review them when staff change.

One more point that applies especially here: because BNYDC does not hold keys to tenant spaces, nobody from the landlord will be able to open your door on your behalf in the middle of the night. If your plan includes remote release from a phone, decide in advance who is allowed to do it after hours, and make sure at least one of those people can be reached. If it does not, make sure the people who answer after hours know what to tell a visitor who cannot be let in.

Inside the Suite

Station-to-Station Talk Between the Office, the Production Floor, the Mezzanine and the Receiving Door

An intercom is not only for visitors. In a large suite, the same system that answers the front door can let the office call the shop floor, the shop floor call the receiving door, and the lab call the front desk. Older buildings used separate paging systems or a radio for this. A modern networked intercom can do it on the same cable and equipment as the door stations, with indoor stations that both answer visitors and call each other.

Where that helps most in the Yard is in suites that are long rather than wide, or that are split across a mezzanine and a main floor. Building 77's floors and the light-industrial units in buildings like Building 212 often place the office at one end, the production area in the middle and the receiving side at the other. A station in each zone lets a supervisor call the dock to say a truck is due, lets the receiving door call the office when paperwork is needed, and lets the person who answered a visitor at the front hand the call to whoever the visitor actually came to see.

We plan internal stations with three rules. They go where people work, not where there happens to be a free wall. They are labeled by place, so "Dock" or "Lab 2" shows on the caller's screen rather than a station number. And they are tested with the machines running, because a station that works in a quiet survey can be useless once the floor is in production. If a zone is too loud for any speaker, the station there gets a visual signal and the call goes to a phone or a horn as well.

Internal talk is optional, and for a small office suite it is often unnecessary. The point is to decide it at the design stage, because adding internal stations later usually means more cable and a second visit, while including them up front often costs little more than the cable itself.

Network and Power

Your Network, the Building's Closet: Where a Navy Yard IP Intercom Should Live

Most new workplace intercoms are networked. The door station connects with a single network cable that carries both data and power from a switch, and calls travel over the company's network to indoor stations, phones and apps. That raises a practical question that is specific to multi-tenant campuses like the Yard: where does the switch live, whose network does the intercom use, and who can get to the equipment when something stops working?

The Tenant Manual lists four telecom providers serving the Yard and says BNYDC works with them to route service into tenant spaces, while access to a building's telecom closets is arranged with the property manager. In practice, that means the building closet is where your internet service arrives, and your own equipment, the router, firewall and switches, usually lives inside your suite. We put the intercom's equipment there too: a switch port or a small dedicated switch, labeled patch positions, and a battery backup sized so the door station, the release and at least one answering point keep working through a short power interruption. If your IT team prefers, the intercom gets its own network segment, so a door station on a corridor wall is not a doorway into the company's computers.

Three things we confirm before installation, in writing. Who holds the administrator account for the intercom: it should be the company, not the installer and not one employee's personal email. What happens to the intercom if the internet goes down: on a good design, local calls between the door and indoor stations keep working even when phone-app calls cannot. And who is allowed to change the network: if your IT provider changes a firewall rule or replaces the router, the intercom should be on their checklist, because that is the most common reason a working system suddenly stops ringing phones.

The Yard's own history adds one more consideration for equipment location. Hurricane Sandy put up to six feet of water across the campus in 2012, and Dock 72 was later built with its mechanical systems 19 feet above the flood plain. For ground-floor tenants, we keep intercom switches and power supplies off the floor and off low shelves wherever the space allows, and we note on the design which components would need to be checked after a flood.

Approvals

Low Voltage, BNYDC Review and the Licensed Electrician: Who Signs Off on a Navy Yard Intercom

Intercoms sit in an unusual place in New York's rules. The published text of the City's Administrative Code, Title 27, Chapter 3, defines low voltage electrical work as wiring "designed to operate at less than fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm, and data transmission circuits," and its permit section says those provisions "shall not apply to low voltage electrical work." That is why a door station, its cable and a typical strike or release powered from a low-voltage supply are installed by a low-voltage contractor rather than under an electrical permit. The same definition excludes wiring that is part of life safety systems or that interfaces with light, heat or power circuits, so anything that ties into a fire alarm, or needs a new 120-volt circuit, falls outside it.

Inside the Yard, the city's rules are only half the picture, because your lease adds BNYDC's. The Tenant Manual says alterations generally require plans submitted to and approved by BNYDC under the lease, that some non-material alterations may not, and that tenants should confirm with property management. It separately says that before any work on a tenant's electrical equipment, plans must be submitted to BNYDC and the work must be done by a licensed electrician. And it says signage requests must be submitted for review. A door station with your company's name on it, mounted on a corridor wall, can touch all three.

Our approach is to make the review easy rather than to guess at it. The written design we send lists every device, where it mounts, what it is fixed to, how the cable gets there, how everything is powered, and whether any part of the work involves the building's electrical equipment. It shows whether anything sits outside the suite, and whether there is a nameplate or logo on the station. You send that to your property manager with your request, and if they need more detail, we provide it. If the design does need a new outlet or circuit, which is uncommon because most intercom power comes from a network switch or a plug-in supply, that piece goes to your licensed electrician under BNYDC's process. AESS does not do electrical work and does not pretend to.

One more rule worth knowing comes from the Building Code rather than the lease: a door that a release controls must still let people out freely in an emergency. We test that on every job, with the intercom and lock power both off, and the result goes in the handover notes.

A studio may have a front entry and a freight entry. The door stations should be labeled so staff know which door is calling, and the release permissions can differ between doors. The second pattern is the company that is growing. Many Yard tenants start in a small suite and move to a larger one within a few years. Compliance points for Navy Yard business intercoms

  • Accessible entry. The U.S. Access Board's guide to the ADA Standards says that where two-way communication systems are provided to gain entry, "they must include visual and audible signals," that lighted signals should be labeled, and that handset cords, where handsets are used, must be at least 29 inches long. We choose stations with both and mount them within reach range.
  • Free egress. A door with an electric release still has to open from inside without special knowledge or effort. Strikes are matched to the door and frame, and every job is tested with the power off.
  • Fire alarm interfaces. If a door's locking is tied to the building's fire alarm, that connection belongs to the fire alarm side and its approvals, not to the intercom. We identify any such door during the survey and coordinate rather than rewire it.
  • Rated doors. Many suite doors off corridors are fire-rated steel. Release hardware for those doors has to be listed for use on rated openings.
  • Lease and BNYDC review. Alterations, signage outside the premises and work on electrical equipment go through BNYDC as the Tenant Manual describes. We supply the drawings and device list for that review.
  • Privacy. A camera door station sees whoever stands in front of it. If yours records, decide who can view the footage and how long it is kept, and tell staff and regular visitors that the door station has a camera.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

These are the situations Navy Yard tenants, facilities managers and fit-out teams are most likely to face, grouped by topic. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Visitors and couriers at the suite door

Couriers aren't allowed to leave boxes in the corridor here. How should our intercom handle a driver who shows up while the suite is empty?

Route the receiving station's calls to someone who can answer from wherever they are, and decide in advance what they will say. With app answering, the person who picks up can see the driver, tell them whether to wait, try again later or take the package back, and in some cases let them into a defined receiving area while watching on video. What the intercom cannot do is make a hallway drop acceptable, because the Tenant Manual does not allow packages in shared spaces. If deliveries regularly arrive when nobody is in, the better fix is a schedule with the carrier or a staffed receiving window, with the intercom making sure the driver reaches a person rather than a closed door.

Our suite shares a corridor and pantry with other companies. Can the door station go on the corridor wall, or does it belong on our own door frame?

Whenever possible, we mount it on or right beside your own door, inside the area your lease gives you control of. The corridor is a shared space, and the Tenant Manual says notices outside a tenant's premises need consent and that hallways must stay clear. A station on the corridor wall a few feet from your door may be fine, but it is a question for property management, not a decision for the installer. Mounting at the door also has a practical benefit: visitors look for a call button at the door they are trying to open, and a station placed elsewhere gets missed.

Some of our guests reach our floor without being pre-registered, because the lobby desk waved them through. How does the door station help us decide whether to open?

A video station gives you the visitor's face and voice before the door moves, which is the information you need. Staff can ask who they are here to see, check with the person they named, and either open or direct them back to the lobby. If your company wants a record of who arrived, a station that saves a snapshot of each call helps. The point is that the decision stays with your staff, at your door, instead of with whoever happened to hold the corridor door open.

Reaching people who are not at a desk

Our team splits its day between the office, the production floor and a second space elsewhere in the Yard. Who should the door station ring first?

Ring the place most likely to have a free person first, then widen the circle. A common plan is the front-desk indoor station for ten or fifteen seconds, then a group of phone apps that rings together, and finally a fixed fallback such as a desk phone or a horn on the floor. For teams that move between buildings, phone apps carry most of the load, but they depend on each person's phone being logged in and audible, so we never let them be the only destination. The rules can change by time of day, and we write them out on the handover sheet so they can be reviewed when people's roles change.

Can the office and the production floor talk to each other on the same system that answers the front door?

Yes, on most networked systems. Indoor stations can call one another as well as answer the door, so a station in the office, one on the floor and one at the receiving side become a small internal intercom. Each station is labeled by location, and a visitor's call can be passed from whoever answered to the person the visitor came to see. It adds cable and stations, so we suggest it for long suites, split levels and companies whose staff currently walk the floor to deliver messages.

Can a call from the door also come over loudspeakers on a floor too big to hear a desk station?

Often it can. Many door stations have an output that can trigger a horn, a chime or a strobe, and some can send a call or announcement to network speakers. On a loud floor, a short tone or flash when the door is called tells people to check their phone or the nearest screen. We choose the method after hearing the floor in production, and we keep the overhead signal short and distinct so it does not become one more noise people learn to ignore.

Concrete, columns and fit-outs

Our space is open concrete with columns every twenty feet and no drop ceiling. Where does the intercom cable go so it isn't in the way of carts and pallet jacks?

High, and along the structure. On an open concrete floor we run cable at ceiling level, following existing cable trays or the column lines, then drop to each station in conduit or raceway that is fixed tight to a column or wall. Nothing crosses the floor and nothing hangs at cart height. Where the drop runs down a column near a busy aisle, the conduit can be steel rather than plastic. The route is shown on the written design so you and property management can see it before the work begins.

We just signed for a white-box suite that will be built out over the next few months. When should our architect bring in the intercom installer?

Before the electrical and data drawings are finished, ideally when the walls are being laid out. At that stage we can tell the architect where each door station and indoor station should go, what box goes in the wall, which network ports are needed and where the release is powered, and the fit-out contractor can pull the cable with everything else. Coming in after the ceiling is closed is still possible, but it usually means more labor, more visible raceway and sometimes reopening finished work.

Our lab is a wet lab with chemical-resistant finishes. What kind of station holds up to washdown and disinfectant?

A station with a sealed front, no open speaker grille that traps residue, and buttons that work through gloves. Some manufacturers publish water and dust ratings and list cleaning agents their housings tolerate, and we check those against your cleaning routine before choosing. Mounting matters as much as the model: the station goes where splashes are least likely, with its cable entering from behind or below so water cannot run into it. If the lab has a gowning room, the answering station goes on the working side so nobody has to leave the clean area to see who is at the door.

Schedules, events and the look of the door

We run two shifts. Can the door station answer differently at night than during the day?

Yes. Most systems switch call rules on a schedule. During the day the door might ring the front desk and an office group; on the night shift it might ring the shift lead's phone and a station on the floor. You can also change who is allowed to release the door at night, and set what the visitor hears if nobody answers. We set the schedule to match your shift times and show you how to adjust it for holidays.

Our space hosts open studio days and launches with outside guests. Can the intercom cope with a crowd arriving at once?

It can help, but for a crowd a person at the door does the job better. A sensible event plan is to switch the door station to an event schedule that rings the event lead, hold the door under staff supervision while guests arrive, and let the station's camera record who comes in. Afterward, the schedule switches back automatically. Because the Tenant Manual makes tenants responsible for security inside their space during events, the plan should be decided by your company before the day, not improvised at the door.

Our brand wants the door station to match our entry design. How much can the panel's look change?

More than most people expect. Door stations come in several finishes, sizes and mounting styles, from slim single-button units to larger panels with a screen, and many can be flush-mounted in a stud wall so only the face shows. Some allow a custom name or logo on the button. Anything with your name on it that faces a shared corridor may count as signage under the Tenant Manual, so we include a picture of the proposed station in the design you send to property management.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Quick answers and cost guides about intercoms are written mostly for houses and apartment buildings. Here are claims that come up often, checked against what the Yard's own documents and our survey routine show.

"New York intercom law decides what your building needs"

Search answers about New York intercoms usually describe the housing rules: which apartment buildings must have an intercom at the entrance and what counts as a working system.

Those rules are written for residential multiple dwellings, and the Brooklyn Navy Yard is an industrial park with no apartment buildings inside its wall. What decides your suite's intercom is your lease, BNYDC's review of alterations, signage and electrical work, the Building Code's requirements for doors and egress, and accessibility standards for entry systems.

So the right first question for a Yard tenant is not "what does the law require at the door" but "what does my lease allow, and what will property management need to see." The written design we provide is built to answer the second question.

"Any intercom job needs a licensed electrician and a city permit"

Generic advice often lumps intercoms in with electrical work and tells readers to file for a permit and hire an electrician.

The published text of the Administrative Code treats wiring under fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data as low voltage work and exempts it from the electrical permit provisions, while excluding life safety interfaces and connections to power circuits. Most door stations, indoor stations and releases fall on the low-voltage side.

In the Yard, BNYDC's process applies on top: the Tenant Manual requires plans and a licensed electrician for work on a tenant's electrical equipment, and approval for alterations. The accurate answer is that a typical intercom is low-voltage work done by a low-voltage contractor, reviewed under your lease, with an electrician only if a new circuit is needed.

"Wi-Fi intercoms work anywhere you have internet"

Many summaries recommend wireless door stations for commercial spaces because they avoid running cable.

Building 77 is a concrete storehouse whose lower floors were built with thick walls and no windows, and many Yard suites sit at the end of long concrete corridors. A Wi-Fi station at the suite door may be far from the access point and separated from it by concrete, and the corridor itself belongs to the building, not to your network.

A door station on a cable to your own switch avoids all of that and gets its power the same way. Wireless has a place for a temporary setup or a door where cable is truly impossible, but in the Yard it is the exception.

"National averages tell you what an intercom will cost"

HomeAdvisor's national cost guide says installing an intercom costs an average of $2,500, with a typical range of $1,200 to $8,500, and estimates one to two hours of labor per station.

Those figures blend houses, apartments and offices from across the country. A Navy Yard suite adds factors a national average cannot see: how far the door is from the rack on a 60,000-square-foot floor, whether the ceiling is open concrete, whether work must fit around freight elevator hours, and what documentation property management wants.

That is why we quote per space, after a survey, with each item on its own line. A national average is a reasonable sense of scale, not a budget for a specific suite.

"The phone app means you will never miss a visitor"

App-based intercoms are often described as the end of missed visitors, since everyone can answer from anywhere.

An app only rings if the phone is charged, logged in, connected and not silenced, and on a production floor a vibrating phone in a pocket is easy to miss. Staff change phones, leave the company and switch off notifications on weekends, and each of those quietly removes a destination from the call group.

The plans we build always include at least one destination that does not depend on someone's personal phone, such as an indoor screen, a desk phone, or a horn or strobe on the floor, and the call group is reviewed whenever staff change.

"An intercom is just for the front door"

Most product summaries describe an intercom as a door station and an answering device, and stop there.

In a large Yard suite, the same networked system can link the office, the floor, the mezzanine, the lab and the receiving door, and can trigger signals where people cannot hear a phone. Companies that already walk the floor to pass messages often get more daily use out of the internal stations than out of the door.

It is worth deciding at the design stage whether you want that, because adding internal stations is easiest when the cable for the door stations is going in anyway.

"Accessibility rules don't apply to a tenant's own door"

Accessibility is often treated as a building lobby issue rather than something a tenant's suite intercom needs to think about.

The U.S. Access Board's guide to the ADA Standards says that where two-way communication systems are provided to gain entry to a facility or to restricted spaces, they must include visual and audible signals. A visitor who cannot hear the station's voice needs a light or screen that tells them the call connected and the door is released.

We choose stations that give both kinds of signal and mount them where a seated visitor can reach the button. It costs little at the design stage and avoids replacing a station later.

FAQ

Brooklyn Navy Yard Intercom Installation FAQ

Can the door station at our suite read the BNYDC ID cards our staff already carry?
+

Sometimes, but it is not something to assume. The BNYDC ID belongs to the Yard's access system, and whether a reader at your suite can read the same card depends on the card technology and on what BNYDC permits. We check both during the survey and never copy or reprogram a Yard credential. If the Yard card cannot be used, staff get a separate fob, card or code for your door, managed by your company.

Can our suite intercom link to the Yard's own visitor registration so a guest invited through it is let straight in upstairs?
+

No, not directly. The visitor registration and gate system are run by BNYDC, and we do not connect tenant equipment to it. Some door stations can issue their own guest codes or QR passes for your door, which your staff send alongside the Yard invitation. That keeps your door under your control while the gate stays under the Yard's.

What should we send BNYDC property management before an intercom goes in?
+

Send the written design we prepare: a device list, where each station and release mounts, what it is fixed to, the cable route, how everything is powered, and a note on whether any device sits outside your premises or carries your name. Your property manager will tell you whether the work counts as an alteration under your lease and whether anything more is needed. We supply extra detail if they ask.

Does a nameplate or logo on our door station need BNYDC's sign-off like other signage?
+

It may. The Tenant Manual says all signage requests go to BNYDC for review and approval, and a station facing a shared corridor with your company's name on it can fall under that. The simplest approach is to include a picture of the proposed station, with its label, in the design you send to property management, so it is reviewed with everything else.

Will the freight elevator hours affect when your crew can work in our building?
+

They shape the schedule. The Tenant Manual shuts freight cars from 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. on weekdays and all weekend and on federal holidays, and after-hours service is booked two business days ahead and billed by BNYDC. We plan deliveries of ladders and materials inside those hours wherever possible, and tell you in advance if a job truly needs after-hours freight.

Can the intercom ring the internet desk phones our office already uses?
+

Often, yes. Many door stations can register as an extension on an internet phone system that supports the standard SIP protocol, so a visitor's call rings the same desk phones and softphones staff already answer. We confirm compatibility with your phone provider before choosing the station, and if video does not carry to your phones, we pair them with an app or screen for the picture.

Our suite is on one of Building 77's lower floors, which were built without windows. Will a video intercom still reach our phones there?
+

Yes, if it is cabled. A door station on a network cable to your own switch does not need any cell or Wi-Fi signal at the door. Calls to phone apps travel over your internet connection, so staff phones need Wi-Fi or data where they are standing. If parts of your floor have weak coverage, an indoor screen or desk phone in those areas covers the gap.

Our lab has a gowning room before the clean space. Where should the intercom go so staff don't have to break gown to answer?
+

Put the door station outside at the lab entrance and the answering station inside the clean space, near where people work. A hands-free station or a wall screen that answers with one press lets someone see and talk to the visitor without leaving. If the door can be released from inside, the release button goes on the clean side too.

Can you install without shutting down our production line for the day?
