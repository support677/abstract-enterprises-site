The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Buyers Want a Local Security Camera Store

Brooklyn people buy security cameras the way they buy most things: they want it to work in their building, on their block, with their landlord or tenants, and they want someone to call when it doesn't. A camera kit from a big-box website arrives with a four-channel recorder, a one-size cable and instructions written for a ranch house with an attic. A Brooklyn house has a stoop, an areaway, a shared vestibule, a cellar that floods some years, a backyard behind a party wall and maybe a rented basement unit. A Brooklyn bodega has a sidewalk, a counter, a back room and a delivery door that opens at five in the morning. That gap between the box and the building is why we sell security camera equipment as a licensed store instead of as a warehouse.

The borough is big enough that the mix of buildings matters. The 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey counted about 1,111,000 housing units in Brooklyn, and found that citywide 54 percent of all units sit in buildings built before 1947. In Brooklyn that old stock is the brick and brownstone row house, the frame two-family with aluminum siding, the four-story walk-up with a store on the ground floor. Every one of those has thick masonry that eats Wi-Fi, short wall runs that make wired cameras practical, and a basement or cellar where a recorder can live out of sight.

So most of what we sell here is wired: PoE network cameras on an NVR, or HD coax cameras on a DVR where good coax is already in the walls. We still sell WiFi security cameras where they make sense — a single view at the end of a long yard, a temporary camera on a renovation — but we tell buyers up front where brick, plaster and steel will make a wireless camera drop out.

The other reason people call a local store is support after the sale. A recorder that stops recording after a power cut, a phone app that logs out after an update, a hard drive that fills in four days instead of fourteen: those are the calls we take from people who bought online. Buying from a Brooklyn store means the person who sold you the NVR can also program it, size the drive and set up phone viewing.

Those are the incidents where a clear clip with a correct time stamp decides whether a police report goes anywhere and whether an insurance claim gets paid. That is why we push buyers toward fewer, better cameras with enough resolution and the right lens, not a box of eight blurry ones.

Packages, stoops and lobbies

Package theft is the single most common reason a Brooklyn homeowner or tenant calls us in November and December. News 12 Brooklyn reported in December 2024 that a Mill Basin shipping store was taking in about 100 packages a day for customers who no longer wanted deliveries left at their doors, and that the mayor's office had estimated earlier that year that 90,000 packages a day are stolen or lost in transit across the city. One customer in that story described thieves who "come up, look at the camera, and put their head down. They're getting smarter" (News 12 Brooklyn, Dec. 2, 2024).

That line is the buying lesson. A camera mounted high, looking down at the top of a hood, records a hood. A camera at the top of the stoop aimed at head height across the landing, plus a second one over the gate at the sidewalk, records a face on the way up or the way down. On the r/Brooklyn thread "Package theft from our building is out of control," the top advice was to get a security camera and file a police report every time, so there is a record. We agree, and we sell for it: wired cameras that can't be unplugged, a recorder hidden away from the door, and enough storage that the clip from last Tuesday is still there when the police ask for it.

The national picture is similar. News 12 Brooklyn, citing C+R Research, reported that 14 percent of Americans said they had a package stolen at least once in 2022 (News 12 Brooklyn, Nov. 29, 2023). In a borough where so many people live in buildings with a shared front door, the share of people who have lost a package is a question of when, not if.

Brooklyn's industrial pockets

Brooklyn is not only houses and storefronts. New York City's Economic Development Corporation lists six Industrial Business Zones in the borough — Brooklyn Navy Yard, East New York, Flatlands/Fairfield, Greenpoint/Williamsburg, North Brooklyn and Southwest Brooklyn. The Department of City Planning describes the North Brooklyn zone and its adjoining blocks as 1,066 acres across Greenpoint, East Williamsburg and Bushwick, along Newtown Creek. The Brooklyn Navy Yard alone reports over 550 businesses and more than 13,000 jobs on more than 300 waterfront acres (FY25 impact report, January 8, 2026).

Those zones are full of auto repair shops, body shops, tire shops, wholesalers, small manufacturers and storage yards. Their camera needs are different from a house: long fence lines, roll-up doors, unlit lots, cars left overnight and, given the vehicle theft numbers above, a camera that can actually read what happens around a parked car at night. That is where 4K cameras, long-range lenses, active-deterrence models and bigger recorders earn their keep.

Outdoor security cameras

Outdoor surveillance cameras for a Brooklyn property live under a cornice, over a gate, on a rear wall above a yard, on a light pole in a lot or under the awning of a store. What matters outdoors is the weather rating, the night picture and the lens. Our house-brand outdoor cameras carry IP66 or IP67 weatherproof ratings, depending on the model, and night vision rated from 60 to 120 feet. For the darkest spots — an alley between two row houses, the back of a lot off an avenue — we steer buyers toward full color night vision cameras with a built-in spotlight, which keep color after dark so you can tell a red jacket from a black one.

Winter security cameras are just outdoor cameras with the right rating and the right mount. IP66 and IP67 are both dust-tight ratings that cover rain and snow; the difference is that IP67 is also rated for temporary immersion. Near the water in Red Hook, Coney Island or Sheepshead Bay, salt air is harder on cheap metal mounts than on the cameras themselves, so we pair outdoor cameras with junction boxes that seal the connectors and keep the cable out of the weather.

Indoor security cameras

Indoor surveillance cameras cover lobbies, hallways, stairwells, store aisles, registers, stockrooms, basements and garages. Indoors the job is usually a wide view from a ceiling corner, so dome and turret cameras are the default: small, hard to knock out of position, and easy to mount on drop tile, plaster or a slatwall. Lobby security cameras and hallway security cameras for small buildings are mostly turrets, because they avoid the night-time glare that a dusty dome bubble can cause in a lit corridor.

For a store, cash register security cameras and point of sale surveillance cameras sit closer and higher than you might expect — above and slightly behind the counter, so the camera sees the drawer, the hands and the bills, not just the top of the cashier's head. Restaurant kitchen security cameras need a housing that tolerates grease and steam, so we put them away from the hood line. Elevator security cameras in a small building need a cable path the elevator company will approve, which is something to ask about before buying.

PoE, WiFi or wired analog: how each camera connects

PoE security cameras take one Cat6 cable each, carrying both power and video, back to a PoE NVR or a PoE switch. This is our default for new systems: one cable, no outlet needed at the camera, and a picture that doesn't drop when a neighbor's microwave runs. Wired analog cameras (HD coax, also called HD-TVI, HD-CVI or AHD depending on the maker) run on RG59 or Siamese coax to a DVR, which makes them the cheapest upgrade where a house or store already has coax from an older system. Wireless cameras connect over WiFi, but still need power, either from an outlet or a battery.

In Brooklyn's masonry buildings we sell WiFi security cameras mainly for single, isolated views. When a buyer wants six or eight cameras around a brick house, wired almost always wins on reliability. Buy PoE cameras or wired security cameras here, and we will size the switch or the recorder's PoE ports so every camera gets enough power, including the extra draw of infrared at night.

Smart detection and analytics

AI security cameras is a broad label. In practice, what most Brooklyn buyers want is person and vehicle detection, so phone alerts fire for a human at the gate at 3 a.m. and not for every cat, leaf or headlight. Several of our house-brand packages list smart AI human and vehicle detection on the spec sheet, and that is the feature we recommend first. Smart motion detection cameras and security cameras with person detection and vehicle detection cut false alerts; smart search security camera systems let you search a day of footage for "person" or "vehicle" instead of scrubbing the timeline.

License plate recognition cameras (LPR, also called ANPR cameras or license plate capture cameras), facial recognition security cameras and people counting cameras are separate products with their own lenses, settings and, in New York City, legal considerations. We only describe a product as having LPR, facial recognition, NDAA compliance, ONVIF, H.265, IK10 or edge AI when its manufacturer's spec sheet says so, and we will show you that sheet before you buy.

Digital video recorders (DVR)

A digital video recorder records coax cameras. Modern HD DVRs handle 5MP and 4K analog cameras, which makes them the cheapest way to upgrade an older Brooklyn system: keep the coax that is already fished through the walls, swap the old 1080p cameras and the old DVR, and the picture gets far sharper with no new cable. We sell 4-, 8- and 16-channel DVRs, and on the 4K kits the DVR is matched to the 8MP cameras. Before you buy a DVR to reuse old coax, we test the cable; a run that was crushed under a stoop step or chewed in a cellar will not carry a 4K signal however good the recorder is.

Surveillance hard drives

CCTV hard drives are built for continuous writing around the clock, which a desktop drive is not. We sell surveillance hard drives sized to the retention you want: a 4TB surveillance hard drive for a small kit, an 8TB surveillance hard drive for an eight-camera house or store, and 16TB surveillance storage (usually two drives) for larger recorders. Security cameras with two weeks recording and security cameras with one month recording are a storage decision, not a camera decision — the worked example further down this section shows how we estimate it.

PoE switches, injectors and power

When the recorder runs out of PoE ports, or the cameras are far from the recorder, a PoE switch for security cameras adds ports and power. We size it to the total power budget, not just the number of ports, because cameras with infrared, spotlights or sirens draw more at night. A security camera PoE injector powers a single camera where a switch would be overkill. For coax cameras, a CCTV power supply (a fused box feeding several cameras) or a single CCTV power adapter does the same job. A small battery backup on the recorder and switch keeps the system recording through the brief power dips Brooklyn buildings get in summer storms.

Mounts, brackets and junction boxes

Security camera mounts and security camera brackets matter more in Brooklyn than people expect. A brownstone cornice, a brick party wall, aluminum siding over wood, a steel roll-down box, a pole in a lot and a drop ceiling each need a different mount. A camera junction box (also sold as a CCTV junction box) sits behind the camera, hides the connector and the spare cable, and keeps water out of the joint — the most common failure point on outdoor cameras we are called to replace. Wall, pole, corner and pendant mounts are all available. Call or text for today's price.

Cable, connectors and baluns

Cat6 cable for security cameras is the standard for IP systems, and outdoor ethernet cable with a UV- and water-resistant jacket goes anywhere the run is exposed. For coax systems we sell RG59 and Siamese cable, BNC connectors and other CCTV connectors, and a CCTV video balun where an old building already has spare twisted-pair wiring that can carry an analog camera signal. A security camera extension cable can save a camera whose original lead is a few feet short. We also carry security camera replacement parts and surveillance camera accessories — sunshades, lens covers, wall plates, cable glands — for systems we sold and many we didn't.

Storage math: a worked example

This is the estimate method we use to size a drive. It is an estimate: the real number depends on the camera's bitrate setting, the compression, how busy the scene is and whether the system records continuously or only on motion. The recorder shows the actual per-channel bitrate once the cameras are running, and we check it after the first week.

StepCalculation (example only)Result
1. Bitrate per cameraAssume 4 Mbps per camera (a mid-range setting)4 Mbps
2. Per camera, per hour4 Mbps × 3,600 seconds ÷ 8 bits per byte1.8 GB per hour
3. Per camera, per day1.8 GB × 24 hours43.2 GB per day
4. Eight cameras, per day43.2 GB × 8345.6 GB per day
5. Days on a 4TB drive4,000 GB ÷ 345.6 GBabout 11.5 days
6. Days on an 8TB drive8,000 GB ÷ 345.6 GBabout 23 days

The formula is bitrate × hours × days, divided by eight to turn bits into bytes, multiplied by the number of cameras. If the same eight cameras ran at 8 Mbps, which is closer to what a 4K camera can use, every figure doubles and the days halve. Recording on motion only, or using H.265 compression on cameras that support it, stretches the same drive further. That is why our quotes state retention in days, and why a 2K option and a 4K option for the same Brooklyn house usually come with different drive sizes.

Security camera laws for Brooklyn businesses

Two sets of rules come up most when a Brooklyn business buys cameras. The first is New York City's biometric identifier law, Administrative Code §22-1201 and following. Since July 9, 2021, food and drink establishments, places of entertainment and retail stores that collect biometric identifier information from customers — face geometry, for example — must post a clear and conspicuous sign near every customer entrance, and may not sell or share that information for profit. Ordinary video recording is outside the disclosure duty when the footage is not analyzed by software that identifies people and is not shared except with law enforcement, as a Troutman Pepper summary of the law explains. In practice: a plain camera system does not trigger it; facial recognition security cameras can.

The second is audio. Under New York Penal Law §250.05, eavesdropping, which includes the mechanical overhearing of a conversation, is a class E felony, and §250.00 defines mechanical overhearing as recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party to it. Audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras are therefore features we set up deliberately: often off by default in a store, on only where there is a clear reason and a posted notice. This is general information, not legal advice; for a specific situation, ask an attorney.

CCTV regulations and security camera privacy laws for homes follow common sense more than statute: record your own property and shared areas, not the inside of anyone's home, a bathroom or a changing area, and do not aim cameras into a neighbor's windows. Are security cameras legal in a Brooklyn store or house? Video of your own property and public-facing areas generally is; the edges are audio, biometrics and private spaces.

What is the difference between a DVR and an NVR?

A DVR (digital video recorder) records analog HD cameras that connect over coax cable; the camera sends a video signal and the DVR turns it into a digital file. An NVR (network video recorder) records IP cameras that connect over network cable; each camera already encodes its own video and the NVR stores it. In a Brooklyn building the practical difference is the cable. If good coax is already in the walls from an older system, a DVR reuses it. If you are wiring fresh, an NVR with PoE gives you one Cat6 cable per camera for power and video, and more room to grow into smart features.

How do you choose an NVR for a Brooklyn property?

Start with the channel count: the cameras you need now, plus two to four for later, rounded up to 8, 16 or 32. Then check that it decodes the resolution of your cameras (a 4K NVR for 8MP cameras), that it has enough PoE ports and power budget for cameras with night lights or sirens, that it holds the drive size your retention needs, and that its phone app supports separate user accounts if tenants, staff or a property manager will log in. Finally, think about where it will live: a locked closet in the owner's apartment, a cabinet in the store office, never on a shelf by the front door.

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Buying Security Cameras in Brooklyn

Search summaries and "best security cameras" lists are written for a generic house. Here are seven claims Brooklyn callers repeat to us after reading them, and what we see when we look at their buildings.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for any home"

The claim assumes a router in the middle of an open-plan house. In a Brooklyn row house the router sits in an apartment two floors above the stoop, and the signal has to pass through brick, plaster on lath, and sometimes the steel of a fire escape or a roll-down gate box.

Battery models also lose charge faster in a Brooklyn January, and a plug-in WiFi camera still needs an outlet at the camera — which on a stoop or a rear wall usually means running a cable anyway.

Wireless has its place for a single view at the end of a yard. For four or more cameras around a masonry house, PoE wiring is the more reliable buy, and often not much more work.

"More cameras always means better coverage"

Box-store kits push camera counts. But coverage is about what each camera can identify. Eight wide-angle cameras that record shapes at 40 feet give less evidence than five well-aimed ones that record faces at the door.

The NYPD's numbers point to small thefts at doors and counters, not sweeping intrusions: petit larceny in Brooklyn stood at 22,530 complaints in 2026 through November 15. Those incidents happen at a few predictable spots.

Put the money into the right lens and resolution at the gate, the door, the register and the lot entrance. Add cameras where there is a real blind spot, not to fill channels.

"Any camera on the front of the building will stop package thieves"

A reply on r/AskNYC put it bluntly: most building lobbies have cameras, and thieves still steal from them, often in hoods and sunglasses. A News 12 Brooklyn customer described thieves who look at the camera and put their heads down.

Cameras deter some people, but the job of a camera is evidence. A camera mounted high, pointing down, records the top of a hood. One at head height across the landing records a face.

So we place one camera low and one high, record continuously, and keep enough storage that the clip from last week is still there. That combination turns a complaint into a report with a picture.

"You need a cloud subscription to see your cameras remotely"

Many consumer brands tie playback and alerts to a monthly plan, so summaries assume every system works that way.

NVR and DVR systems record locally and offer phone viewing through the recorder's own app: live view, playback and clip export, with no monthly fee. Security cameras with remote monitoring and smartphone viewing are standard on every system we sell.

Cloud storage has one advantage — footage survives if the recorder is stolen — which is why we hide the recorder and show you how to export key clips to your phone.

"4K is always better"

Resolution helps only when the subject is far away or the area is wide. On a stoop, a 5MP camera already records a clear face at a few feet.

4K typically needs around twice the storage of 2K at similar settings, and a recorder that can decode it, so a 4K camera on an underpowered recorder or a small drive can leave you with fewer days of footage.

The fair comparison is a 2K and a 4K option for the same views and the same retention. That is how we quote, so you can see the trade-off in your own building.

"Buying online is always cheaper than a local store"

An online kit can look cheaper until you count the extras the kit left out: a bigger drive, a PoE switch, junction boxes for outdoor connections, the right mounts for brick, longer cable, and the time to program the recorder.

Local buyers also get delivery from someone who can answer setup questions. We never compare our prices to a retail listing; we compare complete systems.

"Facial recognition makes a store safer, no strings attached"

Some summaries recommend facial recognition cameras for retail without mentioning New York City's biometric identifier law.

Under Administrative Code §22-1201 and following, a Brooklyn store that collects biometric identifier information from customers must post a sign at every customer entrance and may not sell or share that data for profit.

Most small stores do not need biometric features at all. Good placement, person detection for alerts and a monitor at the counter cover what owners actually want.

How long will a 4TB drive hold footage from eight cameras?
+

As an estimate, eight cameras at about 4 Mbps each fill roughly 345 GB a day, so a 4TB drive holds about 11 days of continuous recording. Higher bitrates shorten that; motion recording and H.265 on cameras that support it lengthen it. We confirm the real figure after the first week.

Can cameras be mounted on a landmarked Brooklyn brownstone facade?
+

Yes, within the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules.
