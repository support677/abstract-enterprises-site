The Neighborhood

Why New Lots Properties Need Alarm Installation

Start with the name, because it explains the map. In the 1650s Dutch settlers laid out farms east of Flatbush, and the “new lots” east of that town were treated for more than a century as an extension of it. Wikipedia’s East New York article records that New Lots was formally ceded from the Town of Flatbush in 1852, kept its own town hall at 109–111 Bradford Street until 1886, and was then annexed as the 26th Ward of the City of Brooklyn. In 1835 the Connecticut merchant John Pitkin bought the part of the town north of New Lots Avenue and sold it as “East New York”; over the next century that newer name spread across most of the old township. What is called New Lots today is the older core around New Lots Avenue itself, on the blocks between Linden Boulevard and Livonia Avenue where the 3 train ends.

Then the avenues. When the NYC Department of Small Business Services studied the commercial district with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation between July 2024 and June 2025, it assessed four corridors: New Lots Avenue, Livonia Avenue and Sutter Avenue, each between Linwood Street and Van Sinderen Avenue, and Van Sinderen Avenue between Sutter and New Lots. The count was 367 storefronts, of which 78 were vacant, a 21 percent vacancy rate against a citywide average of 12.1 percent. Van Sinderen Avenue, beside the tracks, was 81 percent empty. The businesses that remain are small and local: 48 salons and barbershops, 41 delis, bodegas and smoke shops, 33 houses of worship, nonprofits and social service offices, 22 medical offices and pharmacies, and long tails of restaurants, auto shops, laundromats, check cashers and phone stores. The oldest business has been open since 1949.

Then the hours, which is where an alarm comes in. Among the challenges the report lists, two belong together: “inconsistent business hours of stores on all four corridors,” and “storefront gates closed at odd hours,” which make an already high vacancy rate look higher. The report also found that “negative perceptions of crime and public safety contribute to shorter business hours and fewer shoppers,” and that the elevated train over Livonia Avenue leaves much of that corridor in shade by day and affects perceptions of safety at night. When 66 percent of merchants and 67 percent of shoppers name safety as the first change the district needs, the conversation about an alarm in New Lots is really about time: when the shop is empty, for how long, and who finds out first if someone tries the gate.

Then the homes behind the shops. City Planning’s East New York framework describes the low-density blocks as two- to three-story row houses, and Wikipedia sums up the wider neighborhood as mostly semi-detached houses, two- to four-family houses and apartment buildings, with eleven NYCHA developments nearby, among them the Louis Heaton Pink Houses, Linden Houses and Boulevard Houses. A Cushman & Wakefield sale notice for 690–702 New Lots Avenue describes six two-family buildings two blocks from the New Lots Avenue station, and a 2020 listing in the Courier describes a brick two-family on Berriman Street between New Lots and Hegeman Avenues. That is the typical New Lots house we are asked about: brick, attached or semi-detached, an owner on one floor, a tenant on another, and a basement under both.

Last, the ground under those basements. In 1976 the U.S. Geological Survey published a report titled Basement flooding and foundation damage from water-table rise in the East New York section of Brooklyn. It found that once public-supply wells stopped pumping, in Brooklyn in 1947 and next door in Woodhaven in 1974, the water table in the central area rose from about 12 feet below sea level in 1936 to roughly 8 to 10 feet above it by March 1976, flooding basements from below. Fifty years later we do not claim to know how high the water stands on any particular block, but the history explains why so many New Lots owners ask about water before they ask about burglars, and why our basement layouts here treat a floor sensor as seriously as a door contact.

Most New Lots shop owners pair the alarm with two or three cameras on the gate and the counter, and most two-family owners pair it with a video intercom at the shared front door; both are covered further down.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Storefront churches and offices

Former shops converted for worship, social services or medical use, with display glass still in front and valuable sound equipment, files or medication behind.

Larger apartment buildings

Walk-ups and NYCHA or PACT-managed towers at the edges of the neighborhood, where an alarm belongs inside one apartment and the building’s doors belong to management.

Materials and methods, by surface

  • Brick and block party walls. Wireless sensors talk easily along a floor but less well through two layers of brick; on two-families we test signal from the basement and the top floor before the panel location is fixed, and add a repeater rather than guess.
  • Steel and aluminum entrance doors. Magnetic contacts work on metal doors when the right type is chosen; heavy-duty or recessed contacts go on steel shop doors and on the iron security doors many houses carry in front of the wood door.
  • Roll-down gates. A contact fixed at the bottom slat and the sidewalk channel, or a shock sensor on the slats, depending on how the gate sits and how often it is raised during the day.
  • Basement masonry. Screws in mortar joints rather than adhesive, because basement walls here are often damp; water sensors sit on the floor, never on a shelf.
  • Drywall in newer rows. Cable can be fished between floors in an afternoon, so hardwired contacts are a realistic choice on doors that will never move.
Odd Hours

Gates Down at Odd Hours: Designing a Shop Alarm Around a Week That Never Looks the Same

Most commercial alarm advice assumes a store that opens at nine and closes at six, with a manager who arms the system at the same minute every night. The New Lots merchant survey describes something else. The median business has two full-time employees. Many are run by the owner and one helper, sometimes by the owner alone. Hours stretch on a busy Friday, shrink on a slow Tuesday, and the gate comes down in the middle of the afternoon when the only person working has to go to the bank, the wholesaler or a child’s school. Shoppers noticed it too: 42 percent of the 162 consumers surveyed said they shop at no set time, and one asked the stores to “have consistent hours.”

An alarm built for fixed hours fails that kind of week in predictable ways. A schedule that auto-arms at seven catches the owner still restocking. A closing report that expects a six o’clock arm fires every day the store stays open late, until the owner turns the reports off and stops looking. A system that only the owner can arm sits disarmed on the afternoon a cousin minds the shop. None of that is a technical failure; it is a design that does not match the business.

So we start a New Lots shop alarm with the week, not the floor plan. The owner tells us who opens, who closes, how often the gate comes down during the day and for how long, and which nights the store stays open late. From that we decide three things. First, how arming happens: a keypad by the door for staff who prefer buttons, the phone app for the owner who locks up and walks to the train, and a key fob for someone who would rather press one button on the way out. Second, which kinds of closing exist: a full night arm with every zone live, and a short “gate down, back soon” arm that watches the gate, the door, the glass and the back room but skips nothing important. Third, who hears about what: the owner on every alarm, a second keyholder on alarms only, and the monitoring station only if the owner wants one.

The gate itself deserves its own paragraph. The report found that gates closed at odd hours make the corridors look emptier than they are, and gates are where burglars start. In October 2022 ABC7 reported a crew that used bolt cutters to get through store gates in East New York and nearby neighborhoods, taking an ATM holding $7,000, a register, $3,000 in cash and $13,000 in merchandise from one store on September 13, and more than $43,000 in cash across at least five break-ins in the first half of that month. A contact on the door behind the gate tells you when someone is already inside. A sensor on the gate itself tells you while they are still on the sidewalk working at it, which is the alert that gives a phone call time to matter. Where a gate goes up and down several times a day we use a contact that the panel ignores while the store is open; where a gate rattles in the wind or under passing trucks we use a contact rather than a shock sensor, so that weather does not train the owner to ignore the alarm.

Finally, the glass behind the gate. Many New Lots storefronts still have full display windows behind solid or perforated gates. If a gate is defeated, the glass is the next layer, and an acoustic glass-break detector covering the whole front costs less than contacts on every pane. Inside, one or two motion detectors watch the route from the front to the register and the back room, and a separate zone covers the cellar door or hatch. The point of all that layering is simple: an alarm in a shop that keeps odd hours has to be easy enough to arm for twenty minutes, and informative enough that the owner knows within seconds which layer was touched.

Under the Tracks

Livonia Avenue in the Shade of the El, and the Last Stop at New Lots Avenue

The IRT New Lots Line opened to Pennsylvania Avenue on Christmas Eve 1920 and to New Lots Avenue in October 1922, according to Sergey Kadinsky’s 2016 walk along Livonia Avenue for Forgotten New York, and the street underneath has lived with it ever since. Kadinsky describes Livonia as “completely in the shade from beginning to end.” Max and Clara Fortunoff opened their first store under those tracks in 1922; the family moved the business to Westbury in 1964, and a fading ad for it still survives on Pennsylvania Avenue just north of Livonia. The 2025 SBS report makes the same observation in planners’ language: the elevated structure “leaves much of the corridor shaded during the day and affects perceptions of safety at night.”

For an alarm, the el changes two things. The first is light. A storefront under the tracks is darker at noon than a shop on New Lots Avenue is at dusk, and at night the columns and the deck break up the streetlight into patches. A camera chosen for a bright avenue can show nothing useful here after dark, so where an alarm is paired with video under the el we choose cameras with good low-light performance and aim them at the gate and the door from inside the glass where possible, out of reach and out of the weather. The second is sound and vibration. Trains pass overhead on a schedule that never quite stops. We test glass-break detectors with a simulator and then listen through a few trains before we leave, and we avoid shock sensors on windows directly under the structure.

The New Lots Avenue station itself, at Livonia Avenue and Ashford Street, is the southern terminal of the 3 for most of the day, with the 4 at night. Beyond it the tracks curve into Livonia Yard, which Kadinsky notes straddles Linden Boulevard with bumper blocks at Sutter Avenue. The city’s transportation department later turned the triangle where Livonia and New Lots meet into a pedestrian plaza, the New Lots Plaza that appears in the SBS report’s photographs. People wait, change trains, catch buses and pass through at every hour. That is good for the shops around it and neutral for an alarm: a motion detector inside a store must never be able to see through the glass to the plaza, and a camera covering the frontage will record a lot of lawful foot traffic. We set those devices up to report on the gate and the door, not on movement outside.

Below the Floor

Basements Where the Water Comes Up From Below

Julian Soren’s 1976 USGS report on East New York is short, technical and still worth knowing about if you own a basement here. Its finding is that the water table in the central part of East New York had risen about 20 feet in forty years, from roughly 12 feet below sea level in 1936 to 8 to 10 feet above it by March 1976, after the wells that once pumped groundwater for public supply were shut down: Brooklyn’s in 1947 and the neighboring Woodhaven field in Queens in 1974. From 1975 the result was basement flooding and foundation damage. The report projected a further rise of about two feet, possibly to an ultimate level near 15 feet above sea level, and noted that pumping to relieve it would create problems of its own.

We are not hydrologists and we do not know the water level under any particular house today. What we can say is what owners tell us and what we see on surveys: basements in this part of Brooklyn are often damp, some take water after heavy rain without any pipe failing, and a good number of the basements we walk into have a sump pit, a dehumidifier or a tide line on the wall. The New York Fed’s research on basement housing and flood risk puts the highest risk to basement homes in south and east Brooklyn. An alarm in a New Lots house is the cheapest way to hear about water early, because the panel, the cellular radio and the app are already there.

The design is different from a burglary zone. Water sensors sit directly on the floor at the lowest corner of the slab, next to the boiler and water heater, under the washer, and at the foot of any basement stair that opens to the yard. They report to phones, not to a siren, and not to the police. Where a basement is merely damp, we choose sensors that trigger on standing water rather than surface moisture, and we place them where water would collect first rather than where condensation forms. A temperature sensor in an unheated basement warns about freezing pipes in January. And because a basement that floods also tends to lose power, the panel and its battery never go in the basement: they go on the first floor, out of the water’s reach.

For two-families, the basement often belongs to both households, or to the owner while the tenant uses the laundry. We decide with the owner whose phones the basement alerts reach and whether the basement door is part of the owner’s alarm area, the tenant’s, or a shared area that arms only when both are set.

Compliance notes for New Lots businesses

  • Siren time limit. NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop sounding within fifteen minutes. Every panel we install is programmed to that cutoff; the alert to the owner’s phone continues after the siren stops.
  • Installer licensing. New York State requires anyone who installs, services or maintains a security alarm to hold a Department of State license. Ours is #12000287431; ask any company for theirs.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm with a smoke detector on it is not a code fire alarm system. Where a building needs one, it is designed and filed as its own system through fire alarm installation in New Lots.
  • Your lease. Seventy-two percent of surveyed merchants rent. Most commercial leases allow a tenant’s alarm; some require notice to the landlord or a certificate of insurance first. We can send ours before the visit.
  • No city permit invented here. We found no citywide New York City permit requirement for a burglar alarm and do not charge for one.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in New Lots: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from how New Lots shops and houses actually work: the hours, the gates, the el, the basements. General questions about cost, timing and paperwork are in the FAQ near the end of the page.

Shops that keep their own hours

I run my store alone most days, and two or three afternoons a week I pull the gate down for an hour to go to the bank or the wholesaler. Can the alarm protect the shop for that hour without the full closing routine?

Yes, and it should, because a store that is shut for an hour in daylight with the gate half down is easy to recognize as empty. We set up a short arming mode, usually called something plain like “Back Soon,” that you start from the keypad, a fob or the app as you leave. It arms the gate, the door, the glass-break detector and the back room, and sends every alarm straight to your phone. It does not start a long exit countdown, it does not page the monitoring station unless you want it to, and you disarm it from the app as you walk up, before you raise the gate.

The night arm is a separate mode with everything live and whatever monitoring you pay for. Keeping the two apart means the hour-long closure is quick enough that you will actually use it, and the night setting is not weakened to make the daytime one convenient.

Our deli is open around the clock most of the week, but some nights we have to close when nobody can cover the shift. Is an alarm worth having in a store that is almost never shut?

It is, for three reasons that have nothing to do with the front door. While you are open, the cellar, the stockroom and the roof hatch are unattended for long stretches, and those can stay armed as their own area while the counter is busy. While you are open, a hidden hold-up button gives whoever is on shift a silent way to call for help. And on the nights you do close, which are by definition the ones nobody planned, the store needs to be armed in one step by whoever is last out.

For a 24-hour store we design the alarm as two areas: back of house, armed nearly all the time and disarmed only for deliveries and stock runs, and front of house, armed only when the gate comes down. Whoever closes on an unplanned night presses one button. If the store has never been closed on purpose, we test the gate contact and the glass-break detector at installation so we know they work on the night they are finally needed.

Four barbers rent chairs in my shop and keep their own schedules. Can each of them open and lock up with a personal code without being able to change anything in the system?

Yes. Modern panels separate users by what they are allowed to do. Each chair renter gets a personal code, or a fob, that arms and disarms the shop and nothing else: they cannot add users, bypass zones, change who gets alerts or delete history. You keep the master code. If you want, a renter’s code can be limited to the days and hours that renter works, and the app records which code opened the shop and which one armed it at night.

When a renter leaves, you delete that one code from the app; nobody else has to learn a new number and no locks change. If renters keep their own tools in locked drawers at their stations, an alarm cannot protect each drawer separately, but a camera over the stations, paired with the alarm, gives a record of who was in the shop and when.

Our gate gets tagged every few weeks. Will a sensor on the gate set off the alarm every time someone sprays it?

Not if the sensor is the right kind. The SBS report noted graffiti on storefront gates across the district, especially on those that have been down for months. A spray can makes no contact and almost no vibration. A magnetic contact at the bottom of the gate only reports when the gate actually lifts, so a tagger standing at the gate does not trip it. Shock sensors, which react to blows and cutting, can occasionally react to someone leaning hard or kicking the slats, so on a gate that gets a lot of attention we prefer the contact and save the shock sensor for gates that are actually being forced in the area.

If you want to know about graffiti, the tool is a camera aimed at the gate with an alert only during closed hours, sent to your phone and never to a siren or the police. That gives you a picture to report and a time stamp, without turning the alarm into something you learn to ignore.

The 2022 crew that hit an East New York store took the ATM. We have one on the floor by the door. Is protecting the ATM our alarm’s job, or the ATM company’s?

Both, but they cover different things. The ATM’s owner or operator, whether that is you or a company that places machines in stores, is responsible for the machine itself and whatever cash-loading and insurance arrangements come with it; ask them what alarm, tether or anchoring their contract requires. Your alarm’s job is to make the moment someone goes for the ATM loud and immediate. That means the gate zone and the glass-break detector report before anyone is inside, a motion detector covers the floor area around the machine, and the alert reaches you or the station in seconds.

Practically, an ATM that is bolted to the floor in view of the motion detector and a camera is much harder to remove quietly than one standing loose by the door. If the machine has its own tamper output, some panels can take it as a zone, and we check that with the operator before wiring anything to their equipment.

Someone keeps dumping bags and old furniture by the side wall of our store at night. Can a camera on the alarm help us catch it without the siren going off?

Yes. Illegal dumping near commercial intersections was one of the problems the SBS report listed for the district, and an alarm is the wrong tool for it in one sense and the right one in another. The wrong part is the siren and the police: nobody wants an alarm dispatch for a mattress. The right part is the system you already have. A camera on the side wall, added to the alarm app, can send a picture to your phone when it sees a person or vehicle stop there during closed hours, while the burglar alarm itself stays quiet.

The useful output is a clip with a time stamp you can attach to a 311 report. We aim the camera so it covers your wall and the sidewalk in front of it, not the neighbor’s property or apartment windows, and we set the alert window to your closed hours so daytime deliveries do not ping you.

Our store is on Livonia Avenue under the el, and the block is in shadow even at noon. Will a camera tied to the alarm show anything useful at night?

It will if it is chosen for that block. A camera that looks fine on a bright avenue often turns the space under the tracks into grey noise at night, because the light is low and broken up by the columns. For storefronts under the el we choose cameras with strong low-light sensors and built-in infrared, mount them inside the glass where possible so the glass itself is not lit up by the camera’s infrared, and set them up before we leave by looking at a live picture with the gate down and the shop lights off.

Two other habits help. A small light left on inside the shop at night improves every camera picture through the glass, and it costs very little. And we aim the camera at the gate and the door, where an alarm will actually trip, rather than trying to cover the whole street.

Houses, basements and gardens behind the avenues

Our front entrance has an iron security door in front of the wooden door. Which one should get the alarm contact?

Usually the wooden door, with the iron door as an optional second zone. The wooden door is the one that is closed and latched whenever the house is armed, and its contact tells you reliably when the house itself has been opened. The iron door often stays unlocked during the day, swings in the wind and may not close to the same gap every time, which makes it a poor primary sensor.

If you want to know when someone opens the iron door, for example because packages are left between the two doors, we put a heavy-duty contact on it as its own zone set to chime or send a phone alert, not to sound the siren. That way you get early notice of someone in the vestibule without false alarms from a door that was never really shut.

Our basement gets damp from below for a day or two after heavy rain, even when no pipe is leaking. Will a water sensor keep alarming over a film of moisture, or only when there is real water?

That depends on the sensor and where it sits. Basic water sensors trigger when water bridges two metal contacts on their underside; in a damp basement, condensation can occasionally do that if the sensor sits in the wrong spot. We use sensors that need standing water to trigger, set them in the lowest place where water would actually collect rather than in the middle of a sweating floor, and raise them a fraction where a thin film is normal. Some panels also offer temperature and humidity sensors that report a damp basement as information rather than as an alarm.

The test is the first heavy rain after installation. If a sensor reports moisture you consider normal, we move it, not silence it. A sensor you learn to ignore is worse than none, because it will be ignored on the day the basement actually floods.

Our community garden’s tool shed has no electricity and no internet. Can it be alarmed at all?

Yes, within limits. East New York has a strong community-garden tradition, from East New York Farms’ half-acre youth farm on Schenck Avenue between New Lots and Livonia to the Success Garden shown in the SBS report. A shed without power can take battery-powered sensors that report to a battery-backed hub with its own cellular connection, or to a panel in a nearby building that the garden has permission to use. Battery life on the sensors runs to years; the hub’s battery is the part that needs a plan, usually a small solar charger or a scheduled battery swap.

Keep expectations realistic. A siren in an empty garden at 3 a.m. may do little; the useful part is an alert to the gardeners who hold keys, and possibly a battery camera covering the shed door. We will tell you honestly whether the cellular signal at the shed is good enough before you buy anything.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in New Lots

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, its question boxes or related searches from where this page was built, so nothing below is quoted from them. These are claims that commonly appear in AI-written summaries and cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, and what we find on New Lots jobs instead.

“A small business alarm is a set-and-forget system armed at closing time.”

Summaries describe store alarms as if every shop closes at the same time each night. The SBS report on New Lots found the opposite: inconsistent hours on all four corridors and gates closed at odd hours, in stores whose median staff is two people.

An alarm designed around a fixed closing time either nags an owner who stays open late or is left off on the afternoons a solo owner steps out. Both end with a system nobody uses.

What works is an arming design built from the real week: a short daytime mode, a full night mode, codes for everyone who opens or closes, and alerts sent to the person who will act on them.

“A security gate makes an alarm on the storefront unnecessary.”

Guides sometimes treat a steel gate as the security plan. In 2022, ABC7 reported a crew that cut through store gates with bolt cutters across East New York and nearby neighborhoods and carried off an ATM, a register and thousands of dollars in stock.

A gate slows a burglar down; it does not tell anyone that someone is working on it. Without a sensor, the first notice is the owner raising a cut gate the next morning.

We treat the gate as the outer layer of the alarm, with its own contact, and back it with glass-break behind the window and motion inside, so each layer reports on its own.

“Professional installation adds a few hundred dollars to equipment you buy online.”

Cost guides often quote labor as a small add-on. For a simple kit that can be true: our Brooklyn list charges $200 to $400 to install a kit you already own.

A shop with a gate, a cellar, a glass front and staff codes, or a two-family with two alarm areas, is a different job, and that is why our list runs from $600 for a basic residential system to $1,500 to $3,000 for a standard small business.

Every price on this page is the published Brooklyn figure, fixed in writing after one visit, with the deposit rules spelled out in the pricing section.

“Water sensors are an optional extra for houses near the coast.”

AI summaries tend to tie flood sensors to beachfront homes. New Lots is not on the water, but USGS documented basement flooding from a rising water table across central East New York in the 1970s.

A basement can take water from below without a storm surge or a burst pipe, and in a two-family the basement often holds the boiler, the laundry and stored belongings of two households.

We recommend floor-level water sensors in most New Lots basements, reporting to phones rather than to the siren, with the panel itself upstairs and out of the water’s way.

“ADT, Ring or SimpliSafe will cover any home or shop the same way.”

National brands are often presented as interchangeable answers. ADT typically sells monitored systems on a contract; Ring and SimpliSafe sell kits designed for owners to install themselves.

None of them knows that a barbershop with chair renters needs restricted user codes, that a deli open around the clock needs two areas, or that a gate under the el needs a contact rather than a shock sensor. A kit installs the parts in the box.

We install Ring and SimpliSafe kits when that is what an owner wants, and we tell them what the kit lacks. For systems we supply, monitoring is optional and month to month.

“Burglary is rising everywhere, so every alarm must call the police.”

Generic pages lean on fear. The 75th Precinct’s own CompStat report shows 145 burglaries this year through October 18, 2026, down from 192 a year earlier, and 247 for all of 2025 against 2,433 in 1990, for the whole of East New York and Cypress Hills.

Lower numbers do not mean no risk, and safety was still the top concern of merchants and shoppers in the SBS survey. They do mean an owner can choose how an alarm escalates rather than sending every event to dispatch.

Many New Lots owners use alerts to themselves first, monitoring for the night arm only, and cameras to check before anyone calls. The design is the owner’s choice.

“You need a city permit before installing an alarm.”

Some generic guides say a local alarm permit is required. We found no citywide New York City permit requirement for a burglar alarm in a home or shop.

What the city does regulate is the siren: Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop within fifteen minutes. The state requires the installer to be licensed by the Department of State.

So the questions to ask are whether the installer is licensed and whether the siren is programmed to the cutoff. Our license number is on every page, and every panel we program stops at fifteen minutes.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask About the Week Before You Count the Windows

These notes describe how we survey a New Lots property, not a particular job. On a shop, the first ten minutes are spent away from the walls. We ask the owner to walk us through last week: what time the gate went up each day, when it came down, who was there, which afternoon the store closed for an hour, and which night it stayed open late. We write it down as a simple grid, and the arming modes come straight out of it. Owners are often surprised that the question comes before the sensor count, but a system that matches the grid is a system that gets armed.

Then we go outside and look at the gate from the sidewalk: how it locks, where it meets the channel, whether it sticks, whether it rattles when a truck passes, and what is on either side. An empty storefront next door, a lot behind, an apartment door beside the gate: each changes where a sensor goes and who should get an alert. Inside, we stand at the register and look back at the glass to make sure the motion detector we are planning cannot see the plaza or the bus stop. Under the el, we wait for a train before choosing a glass-break position.

On a house, we go to the basement first. We look for tide lines, a sump pit, a dehumidifier, stored belongings on pallets, the boiler and the laundry, and the door to the yard. Water sensors go where the floor is lowest, not where it is convenient. Then we decide with the owner whose floor is whose, and the panel goes upstairs.

Our roll-down gate is motorized. Can the alarm app raise or lower it?
+

It is technically possible to connect a gate motor to an alarm panel through a relay, but we do not recommend raising or lowering a gate remotely when nobody can see it, because a moving gate can injure someone on the sidewalk. What we do recommend is a gate contact that tells you from anywhere whether the gate is up or down, and arming that refuses to complete or warns you if the gate is left up.

What usually causes false alarms in small shops, and what do you do at installation to prevent them?
+

The usual causes are motion detectors that can see moving things through the front glass, balloons, signs or hanging displays that move with the heating or air conditioning, gate contacts on gates that do not close to the same spot every time, and staff who do not know the code or the exit delay. At installation we aim motion detectors away from the glass, ask about anything that hangs or moves, test the gate contact by closing the gate several times, and walk every keyholder through arming and disarming before we leave.

We already own sensors from a store-bought kit of a different brand. Can they report to the panel you install?
+

Usually not. Wireless sensors speak the radio language of the brand that made them, and most panels only accept their own family of sensors or a few compatible ones. Send us the model numbers on the back of the sensors and the base station, and we will tell you whether they can be kept, whether the kit is worth installing as it is for $200 to $400, or whether replacing it makes more sense.

We are opening in a storefront that stood empty for a long time. What should we check about the space before choosing alarm equipment?
+

Look at every way into the space, not just the front: the cellar or sidewalk hatch, the back door and what it opens onto, any roof hatch or skylight, and holes left in walls by the last tenant. Check whether an old alarm keypad or siren is still on the wall and whether its wiring runs anywhere useful. Ask the landlord who owns the gate and who repairs it. We look at all of that on the survey, and an empty space is the easiest time to run wire.
