The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Fiske Terrace is a small place with an unusually clear split in how its front doors are wired. The neighborhood sits between Foster Avenue on the north, Ocean Avenue on the east, the Brighton line's B and Q tracks on the west and the Bay Ridge freight cut on the south. Most of the land inside those lines is the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated on March 18, 2008. City land-use records count 252 lots in that district holding 319 homes, so most of its houses are still one household behind one front door. Those houses went up between roughly 1898 and 1929, almost all of them freestanding wood-frame houses with porches, driveways and back yards. None was built with an intercom. A bell, a button and a transformer were added at some point, often more than once, and the circuit has been extended, patched and partly forgotten as owners came and went.

The other half of the picture sits on the edges. The ten tax blocks that contain the historic district also take in the apartment buildings that line Ocean Avenue, the walk-ups on East 16th and East 17th Streets near Foster Avenue and a few buildings on Glenwood Road. Those blocks hold about 1,485 homes in all, and roughly 1,114 of them, three out of every four, are in just 36 apartment and condominium buildings. The largest went up after the Second World War: one Ocean Avenue building finished in 1953 has 108 apartments, another from 1955 has 96, and a condominium completed in 2008 has 68. In buildings like those, one lobby panel, one power supply and a set of risers carry every visitor call in the building, and a single bad splice can silence a whole line of apartments.

That split shows up sharply in the city's records, which we pulled on November 11, 2026. HPD has written 31 intercom and buzzer violations on these ten blocks since 1994, and every one of them is at an apartment building. Not one has been written at any of the 252 historic district lots. The 311 system tells the same story: 30 bell, buzzer and intercom service requests from these blocks since January 2020, all from apartment buildings and none from a house. That does not mean house buzzers never fail. It means the people who live in the houses don't call the city about them; they call a technician, live with a silent bell, or put a battery doorbell over the old button and hope for the best.

So a call about a "broken buzzer" in Fiske Terrace can mean two quite different jobs. On a house on East 18th Street, East 19th Street or one of the courts such as Wellington Court, it is usually an hour or two of measuring and tracing in a cellar and along a porch. On Ocean Avenue it can mean an open HPD order with a correction deadline, a managing agent, a super with the cellar keys and a set of apartments that all need to be tested before the repair is done. We work both. In a house with a rented floor, the buzzer repair usually pairs with a look at the latch and closer, so the door locks again after every visitor.

The City Record

Thirty-One Orders and Not One at a House: Reading the HPD File for Fiske Terrace

We queried HPD's Housing Maintenance Code violations for the ten tax blocks that contain the historic district and searched every order that mentions an intercom or a buzzer. There were 31, the oldest from July 1994. Twenty-nine are class B, the tier HPD uses for hazardous conditions, and two are class A, the lowest tier. None is class C. They sit at eleven addresses, all of them apartment buildings: elevator buildings along Ocean Avenue, a building on Glenwood Road at the eastern end of the district, and walk-ups on East 16th and East 17th Streets near Foster Avenue. The 252 lots inside the historic district have none.

For years the file barely moved. There were a handful of orders in the 2010s, two in 2023 and one in November 2024. Then, between June 11 and October 29, 2026, inspectors wrote nine more, at four different buildings, and on November 11, 2026 all nine were still open. That is the largest burst in the file's thirty-two years. One apartment on Ocean Avenue was cited four times in 2026 alone, on June 11, June 16, September 6 and September 29. An apartment in another Ocean Avenue building was cited three times, on July 5, August 27 and September 2. A third order, on October 29, 2026, is written not against an apartment but against "the building entrance door at public hall, 1st story", in other words the lobby station itself.

What four orders at one apartment usually mean

When the same apartment collects order after order over a few months, it is rarely because four separate parts failed. More often an inspector has come back, found the condition still there or back again, and written it once more, sometimes with slightly different wording for the location of the station. In practical terms it tells a technician that whatever was done between visits, if anything, did not reach the real fault. A station swapped in an apartment whose riser pair is broken will stay dead. A riser repaired at the cellar end will not help if the break is in the wall behind the station. And an apartment that works on the day of the repair but fails again a week later usually has an intermittent fault: a corroded splice, a loose terminal on a block, or a conductor that makes contact only when the building is warm.

So when an Ocean Avenue owner or agent calls us with a repeat order, we test more than the named apartment. We test the apartments above and below it on the same riser, check the terminals where that line leaves the supply, and measure the station's pair at both ends, at rest and while calling. We note what we find in writing with photos, so that if HPD comes back, the owner can show what was found and fixed and where. Certifying the correction with HPD is the owner's step, not ours, but the record we leave is written so the paperwork matches the work.

The wording on the order is a map of the fault

Every recent order on these blocks cites section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, the owner's general duty to keep a building in good repair, and then describes the defect as a path. One 2026 order reads "from building entrance to return buzzer at west wall in the private hallway" at a fourth-floor apartment. Another reads "from north wall at vestibule to return buzzer in the private hallway" at the same apartment a few weeks earlier. A third, written in a different building in August, runs "from building's vestibule to east wall in the private hallway." The inspector is not naming a part. The inspector is saying the chain between the street door and that apartment's station does not work, and leaving the owner to find where it breaks.

Two details in that wording help us. A path that starts at the vestibule and ends at a station on a named wall tells us the inspector tested from inside the apartment, so the complaint is about calls or voice reaching that unit, not only about the door. And an order written at "the building entrance door at public hall" points to the lobby station, the panel's own wiring or its supply, which affects everyone rather than one household. Send us the order wording or a photo of the notice when you book, and we start the visit where the inspector's description points.

Why the 311 numbers say more about access than about wear

311 bell, buzzer and intercom requests from these blocks went from 6 in 2022 and 5 in 2023 to 3 in 2024 and 4 in 2025, then jumped to 12 so far in 2026. Seven of those twelve came from one Ocean Avenue building. Reading the closing notes is instructive: some 2026 requests ended with violations issued, some were closed because the inspector "was not able to gain access" or "was unable to complete the inspection", and a few were closed because the conditions the inspector saw "did not violate the housing laws". An intercom that works when the inspector presses the button and fails at eight in the evening produces exactly that last result.

The Houses

The Bell in a Freestanding House: Porch Buttons, Cellar Transformers and Long Runs to the Kitchen

A Fiske Terrace house is not a row house. It stands on its own lot with four exposed sides, a front porch, usually a side door off a concrete driveway, and often a freestanding garage at the back. The Wall Street Journal described the enclave in 2014 as large detached Victorian houses with driveways, garages, gardens and back yards. For a bell circuit, that shape matters. The button is at the porch or the side door, the transformer is in the cellar, and the chime or bell is often in the kitchen or the back hall, so the wire makes a long trip around or through the house. In a row house, a bell wire might run a few feet from the stoop to the cellar. Here it can run the depth of the house and up a floor before it reaches anything that rings.

The transformer nobody can find

The single most common question on a house call here is where the transformer is. In an older house it may be mounted on the side of the electrical panel, on a cellar joist near the old fuse box, or in a closet next to the chime. A Brooklyn homeowner posting on Brownstoner in December 2007 described a different version of the same problem: a cellar transformer that buzzed so loudly it "drives me insane". The reply on that thread gave the two usual causes, a transformer that is "mounted poorly and is vibrating" or one that "is failing", and noted that these transformers are often fixed to the side of a breaker panel that later got walled in. That is still what we find. If the transformer is behind a finished cellar ceiling or a new panel enclosure, we locate it by following the bell wire from the chime back, rather than by opening walls on a guess.

Once we find it, we read its label and meter its output at rest and while the button is held. A transformer that reads correctly at rest but sags badly under load is undersized for what has been hung on it since it was installed: a second chime, a lit button, a wired video doorbell or a strike for a tenant upstairs. Replacing it with one of the right voltage and capacity is a common fix, and it sits on our Brooklyn list as its own line. Where the transformer is wired into the electrical panel itself, the line-voltage side is a licensed electrician's work, and we name that separately in the quote rather than folding it into ours.

Houses with a tenant, and houses built in series

Only 37 of the 384 lots on the ten blocks are two-family houses, and a few houses have a rented attic or top floor. Where a house has a second household, the bell circuit usually gained a second button, a second chime and an electric strike or release at some point, often added by whoever was handy that year. The usual complaints are a tenant's bell that rings in the owner's kitchen, a release that works from one floor but not the other, and a strike that hums but holds. We test each household separately, follow each pair from the plate to its floor, and label them, so the next repair does not start from scratch.

The designation report notes that the Ackerson company built houses in series of ten to twenty and that the Corbin company's Midwood Park houses were largely made in a factory and assembled on site. For a repair, that can be useful. Houses from the same series often hide their cellar stairs, their electrical service and their original bell wiring in similar places, and once we have found a transformer in one house on a block, we know where to look first in the next. The builders also avoided putting identical houses side by side and owners have changed a great deal since, so we never assume; we check.

The Edge Buildings

Ocean Avenue, Glenwood Road and the Foster Avenue Walk-Ups: Where Lobby Intercoms Live

The apartment buildings on the edges of Fiske Terrace are a different kind of job. Ocean Avenue's larger buildings are post-war elevator buildings, the biggest on these blocks with about a hundred apartments. Their intercoms are usually audio systems with a lobby panel in the vestibule, a supply in the cellar, a riser for each line of apartments and a station by each apartment's door or in its kitchen. The walk-ups near Foster Avenue, put up around 1931, are smaller, with HPD orders written as high as the fifth floor, a few dozen apartments, a panel at the street door and an inner vestibule door with its own lock. The condominium finished in 2008 is newer again, with an entry panel that can be programmed and, in some buildings of that age, connected to a network.

In buildings like these, the pattern of failure tells us where to look. If every apartment has lost voice but the door still releases, the panel's speaker and microphone or the supply are suspect. If one vertical line of apartments has gone quiet, the riser for that line is. If one apartment never rings, its station or its own pair is. If the door releases from every apartment but visitors still can't get in, the strike, the latch or the closer is the problem, not the intercom. We call a sample of apartments from the panel before we open anything, and we ask the super which apartments have complained, because the HPD file only shows the ones that became orders.

Access is half the job

Many of the 2026 311 requests on these blocks closed without an inspection because the inspector could not get into the apartment. We meet the same problem from the other side. A riser repair can be finished in the cellar, but it cannot be confirmed without ringing the apartments on the line, and a station repair cannot be done at all without someone at home. When we book an Ocean Avenue visit, we ask the managing agent or super to arrange access to the named apartment and, where possible, to the apartments directly above and below it, and we tell them which ones we were and weren't able to test in the written record.

When the door, not the intercom, is the problem

A surprising number of "broken intercom" calls on Ocean Avenue end at the lobby door. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice. A closer that has lost its tension, a latch that no longer reaches the keeper, or a frame that has shifted can leave the door unlocked all day, or locked so firmly that the strike cannot release it. So every lobby visit includes the strike, the latch and the closer. If the buzzer releases the strike but the latch is binding, the fix is alignment, not a new strike, and it is usually quicker and cheaper.

The Landmark Line

Fixing a Bell on a Landmarked Porch Without Changing the Front of the House

Inside the historic district, the outside of every house is regulated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and the LPC's Permit Guidebook says permits are needed for work that affects a building's exterior even on parts not visible from the street, and for work in front, side and rear yards. That sounds as if a buzzer repair might need paperwork. In practice, most do not. The guidebook's chapter on health and safety equipment treats ordinary maintenance and repair as work that needs no permit, and a buzzer repair that replaces a failed transformer in the cellar, renews the wire behind an existing button, or swaps a worn button for one of the same size in the same hole changes nothing on the face of the house.

The line is crossed when the repair turns into a change. Moving a button to the other side of the door, adding a second plate for a tenant, putting a video station where a small button used to be, or running new exterior conduit to reach a side door are alterations, and those are the owner's Landmarks decision. The LPC's rules let staff approve small equipment such as intercom panels within or next to a door opening, with one panel per opening, attached at mortar joints or plain material, in a finish that matches, and with no visible conduit or at most about ten inches of it, painted to match. We explain where a planned change sits relative to those rules before the owner commits to it, and we photograph and measure the door and its trim before anything is opened, so the owner has a record either way.

How we keep the porch looking the way it did

On a wood porch, the button usually sits in a door casing, a porch post or a clapboard beside the door. We take the button off its existing screw holes, repair or replace the wire behind it, and refit it, or a same-size button, in the same position. If the wire has rotted where it passes through the porch framing, we bring a new length in from the cellar or the crawl space under the porch along the old path where we can, rather than surface-mounting a new route across the facade. Where the old path is gone and the only practical route is on the outside, we stop and price the options in writing, because that decision may need the LPC.

Diagnose Before Replacing

"Just Replace the Whole Thing": Why a Fiske Terrace Buzzer Gets Tested Part by Part First

Owners here hear the replacement pitch often, and sometimes it is right. But a buzzer is three separate paths sharing some wire: the path that makes the bell ring or the station call, the path that carries voice, and the path that releases the door. Each can fail without the others. A house where the bell rings but the release no longer opens the side door has a working transformer and button. An apartment building where everyone can hear the lobby but nobody can buzz in has a working panel and risers. Replacing the whole system in either case would fix the problem by brute force, at many times the cost of the failed part.

A Brownstoner thread about a three-unit Brooklyn house shows how this goes. The owner's buzzer worked "only sporadically" in all three units, and the vendor quoted $2,000 to replace it, saying that troubleshooting would cost about the same at "$150 per hour for the technicians, a minimum of 3 hours". The owner wrote that they had been "hoping for a repair bill for a few hundred bucks". A fault that hits every unit at once and comes and goes is usually a shared part, the transformer, the supply or a connection near it, and that is exactly the kind of fault that is cheaper to find than to replace around. Our Brooklyn list starts with a $250 service call that includes diagnosis, and the repair is priced before any part goes in.

Winter, heat and the wood-frame door

Fiske Terrace's houses are wood, and wood moves. ButterflyMX's 2026 guide to cold-weather intercom problems points out that metal parts contract in the cold and change the fit between latch, strike and frame, and that "a buzzer can sound without granting entry" when the signal reaches the door but the release hardware is binding. On a wood porch door or a side door off the driveway, seasonal swelling and shrinking do the same thing: a strike that releases freely in October can hold the latch in a January cold snap or a humid August. If the failure follows the weather, we check alignment and the keeper before we condemn the strike.

The same guide notes that an intercom which works by day and fails at night is pointing to a cold-sensitive connection or condensation. On the houses that usually means the button or its wire where it passes through the porch, and in the cellar, a corroded splice near an exterior wall. In the Ocean Avenue buildings it can mean a lobby panel that takes on moisture through a worn gasket or a vestibule that drops close to outdoor temperature at night. Telling us when the failure happens is one of the most useful things a customer can do before a visit.

These are the building types we meet most here.

The Foursquare

A tall, boxy house with a full-width porch, a center or side front door, a side door to the kitchen and a cellar under the whole footprint, where the bell transformer usually sits.

The Bungalow and the Temple-House

Lower houses behind deep porches, or houses with the gable facing the street, where the bell wire runs through porch framing and a crawl space before reaching the cellar.

The Stucco or Half-Timbered House

The district's Tudor Revival houses, where a button may be set into stucco rather than wood and any new opening needs care not to crack the finish.

The House With a Rented Floor

A one-family house with a rented attic or top floor, or one of the district's few two-family houses, with two buttons, two chimes and a release on the shared door.

The Ocean Avenue Elevator Building

A post-war or newer building with a lobby panel, a cellar supply, a riser per line of apartments and, in the 2008 condominium, a programmable entry panel.

How the building type changes the repair

The foursquare is the easiest house to work on and the one with the longest bell wire. The cellar runs under the whole house, so the transformer and most of the wire can be reached without opening anything. The trouble spots are the point where the wire leaves the cellar for the porch and the point where it comes up to the chime, which may be in a kitchen on the far side of the house. We meter the transformer, test the button at the porch, and if both are good, put a tone on the wire and follow it across the joists to the break.

The house with a rented floor is two small systems sharing one door. The owner's bell, the tenant's bell and the release often share a transformer that was sized for one chime decades ago. We check that the transformer can run everything on it, separate the two households' buttons and chimes if they have been crossed, and make sure the release opens the door from the tenant's floor without anyone downstairs having to come to the door.

Rules and practical limits at business and apartment doors

  • Multiple dwellings: the NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that buzzes the door open; older buildings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request it.
  • Self-closing doors: HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice, so every buzzer repair on a building door includes the closer and the latch.
  • The landmark district: inside the historic district, ordinary repair is maintenance, while a new or moved panel is placed within or beside the door opening, one per opening, with little or no visible conduit; the edge buildings on Ocean Avenue are outside the district.
  • Egress: on any door people leave through, a strike or magnetic lock must never stop someone inside from getting out; we explain fail-safe and fail-secure choices before replacing either.
  • Line voltage: a new circuit or work inside an electrical panel belongs to a licensed electrician under a Department of Buildings permit; we name that work separately and handle the low-voltage side.
  • Reach: a call button or keypad the public uses at a business entrance should sit within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range, with 48 inches as the high forward reach.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Tracing a Silent Bell Through a Wood House From the Porch to the Kitchen

We don't publish stories about particular Fiske Terrace addresses, because we only print what we can document. What we can describe is the order a technician follows in the building type that makes up most of the district, a freestanding wood house with a porch bell that has stopped ringing.

The visit starts outside. We press the porch button and listen at the front hall, then ask where the chime is and whether anyone remembers it ever ringing from the side door. Then the cellar: we find the electrical service, look along the joists near it and near the front wall for a small transformer, and meter it. If it reads correctly, we go back to the porch, take the button off its screws and test it. If the button is good too, the fault is in the wire, so we put a tone on it at the transformer and follow it across the cellar ceiling toward the front of the house, checking every splice we can reach. The break, when we find it, is most often where the wire passes through the sill or porch framing, or at an old splice above a newer plumbing or heating line. We repair it along the old path, refit the button in its own holes, ring the bell from both doors, write down where the transformer is and where the wire runs, and photograph both, so the next owner does not have to search the cellar again.

Apartment buildings on the edges

The lobby station itself has an open order, not any single apartment. Where does a repair like that start?

At the panel and its supply, because a lobby-station fault affects everyone. Seven complaints came from our building this year, but only a couple turned into orders. Should the board or owner wait for HPD, or test the system now?

Test it now. Repeated complaints that close without an order usually mean an intermittent fault that was not present during the inspection, and that kind of fault tends to return as an order eventually. A building-wide test, with every station, the panel, the supply and the door hardware checked and written up, is the fastest way to find it while it is still a repair rather than a deadline.

Our walk-up near Foster Avenue has both a street door and an inner vestibule door. Visitors get through the first but not the second. Is that the intercom?

Usually not. In these buildings the intercom's release often controls only one of the two doors, and the other is opened by a key or a separate strike. What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Fiske Terrace

AI summaries, national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and vendor guides are where many owners start. They are written for the whole country, and Fiske Terrace's mix of landmark houses and Ocean Avenue apartment buildings is rarely in the summary. Here are seven common claims and how they hold up on these streets.

"Plan on $100 to $300 for a diagnostic and $500 to $1,500 to replace a component."

ButterflyMX's 2026 guide to intercom repair in New York quotes one NYC integrator's estimates of $100 to $300 for diagnostics, $500 to $1,500 for component replacement and $1,000 to $3,000 for limited wiring repairs. They are reasonable planning figures for an apartment building, and say nothing about a house with one porch bell.

In a Fiske Terrace house, the failed component is often a bell transformer or a button, and both sit well below the low end of that component range on our Brooklyn list. In an Ocean Avenue building, a lobby panel or a riser can sit above it. A single headline range blends the two kinds of building into one number that fits neither.

Our approach is to start with the $250 service call, which includes diagnosis, and then price the specific repair in writing from the Brooklyn list.

"A dead intercom almost always comes down to one of four causes."

Swiftlane's 2026 troubleshooting guide says the issue "almost always comes down to one of four causes: a blown low-voltage transformer fuse, corroded in-wall wiring, a stuck call button, or a failed door strike relay."

It misses what we find most often on these blocks. In the houses, it is a transformer that was never meant to carry what is now on it, a button that has taken on water, or a wire broken where it passes through a wood sill. In the edge buildings, it is mismatched stations on a shared line, a latch or closer that defeats a working strike, or an intermittent splice that only fails at night.

The practical point is that a four-item checklist encourages guessing. We test ringing, voice and release separately and measure before we replace, because the cheapest repair is the one that fixes the actual fault the first time.

"Once repairs reach 30 to 40 percent of a new system, it is time to replace."

The same Swiftlane guide offers a rule of thumb: if repeated repairs are approaching 30 to 40 percent of what a modern replacement would cost, a planned upgrade typically works out cheaper over three years. Swiftlane sells cloud intercoms, so the rule leans toward replacement.

The trouble is the word "repeated". The Ocean Avenue apartment cited four times in 2026 does not prove the system is worn out; it more likely proves that the fault has not yet been found. Brick Underground's 2019 guide quoted a locksmith saying "it's all in the wire connections" and that a total overhaul is unlikely, and in buildings of the 1950s that is usually right.

Before applying any percentage rule, find out what actually failed. If the riser and the supply are sound, station or panel repairs are rarely a reason to replace a whole system. If the cable itself has failed in several places, a replacement estimate is a reasonable next step, and we will say so.

"In a landmark district, you can't change anything at the front door."

Owners inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District sometimes hear that a buzzer can't be touched without the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That overstates it. The LPC's Permit Guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair as work that needs no permit, and a repair behind an existing button changes nothing on the facade.

Changes are possible too. The LPC's rules allow staff to approve small equipment such as intercom panels within or next to a door opening, one per opening, attached at plain material or mortar joints, with no visible conduit or at most about ten inches of it, painted to match.

What the rules do require is that the owner treat a change as a change. We repair first, describe any alteration separately in the quote, and photograph the door before and after, so an owner can make the Landmarks decision with the facts in hand.

"If it fails every winter, the system is wearing out."

ButterflyMX's 2026 guide on cold weather is more careful than most: it says cold "can push a borderline component past the point where it works reliably" and that a buzzer can sound without granting entry when the release hardware binds. Many summaries skip that nuance and treat seasonal failures as a sign of an aging system.

On Fiske Terrace's wood porch and side doors, a seasonal failure is more often the door than the electronics. Wood frames move with temperature and humidity, and a strike that clears the latch in mild weather can hold it in a cold snap. In the apartment buildings, a cold vestibule or a worn panel gasket can make a connection fail only at night.

So a failure that follows the weather calls for a check of alignment, the keeper, the closer and any connection exposed to the outdoors, before anyone talks about replacing the system.

"An audio-only intercom no longer meets New York requirements."

Vendor content often implies that a building without video or app access is out of date in a legal sense. ButterflyMX's own 2026 NYC repair guide says otherwise: the Multiple Dwelling Law baseline for covered buildings is voice communication and apartment-controlled release of the lobby door, and an audio-only intercom "isn't automatically noncompliant merely because it lacks newer features."

That matters on Ocean Avenue, where most buildings run audio systems. HPD's orders on these blocks describe broken paths between the entrance and an apartment's station, not a missing camera.

An owner may still want video, and we can quote it. But a working, well-maintained audio intercom is not a compliance problem, and an open order for a broken one is fixed by repairing the path the order describes, not necessarily by buying a new system.

"For a house, skip the repair and use a plug-in wireless chime."

Forum advice and some AI answers suggest abandoning old bell wiring altogether in favor of a battery doorbell and plug-in chimes. For a one-family house with one door and a working Wi-Fi network, that can be a reasonable choice.

It is a poor fit for many Fiske Terrace houses. A house with a rented floor needs a release the tenant can use, and a wireless chime does not open a door. A long, freestanding house may have a porch, a side door and a back door that all need to reach the kitchen and an upper floor.

If the old wiring is sound, repairing the transformer or a single break is usually cheaper than replacing every chime and button with wireless parts. If it isn't, we say so in writing, and the owner can choose between a wired repair and a wireless setup with the costs side by side.
