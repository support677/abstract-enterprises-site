The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need TV Installation

Kensington sits south of Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, between Borough Park to the west and Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South to the east, with Windsor Terrace above it and Midwood below. Most maps draw it from Caton Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway down to Foster Avenue, with Coney Island Avenue on the eastern side and Ocean Parkway running through the middle. The neighborhood was laid out after the parkway opened in the 1870s, and it filled in over the next sixty years in the order that still decides what a TV installer finds behind the wall: frame houses and Victorians first, then attached houses and two-families on the numbered East streets, then the brick elevator buildings that line Ocean Parkway, Caton Avenue and the streets around them. A smaller wave of taller apartment buildings went up on the parkway in the early 1960s. Very little was built in the last forty years, which is why a Kensington wall is almost never the fresh drywall that a big-box installer expects.

For a TV, that age profile matters more than anything else on the block. Openigloo's directory lists 918 prewar buildings in Kensington, and the six-story elevator co-ops are the ones we are called to most often. StreetEasy's record for 260 Ocean Parkway describes a typical example: six stories, 77 apartments, built in 1930 in an Art Deco style with two elevators and a live-in super. Brownstoner's 2025 story on 40 Tehama Street describes another, a six-story, 75-unit red-brick building in the Streamline Moderne style from 1939, with arched doorways into the living room, an arched niche with built-in shelves in the foyer, and original moldings and picture rails. Those details are what people love about the apartments, and every one of them narrows the choice of TV wall. An arch takes away the corner you wanted. A picture rail sets the eye line for the whole room. A wall of windows at the end of the living room leaves the long side wall, and in these buildings that wall is often a solid partition of plaster over block.

Inside those partitions is the part generic advice gets wrong. In a StreetEasy discussion about mounting a TV in a prewar building, one experienced poster described the walls as plastered gypsum block two to four inches thick and said cutting channels and boxes for hidden wires is a major job, "the price you pay for solid walls." Architect Michael Fasulo, interviewed by Brick Underground about renovating prewar co-ops, called such walls "a delicate mix of plaster and concrete" and said they are "not as fast and forgiving as sheetrock." Apartment Therapy's piece on stud finders in old apartments gives three reasons the tool goes quiet or beeps everywhere: thick plaster over lath, plaster laid directly on brick on shared walls, and an old cast iron pipe behind the plaster. We meet all three in Kensington in a single week. None of them stops a TV from going on the wall. They change the anchor, the drill, the cable plan and the price line, and they are why we ask for a photo before quoting a number.

The side streets have their own patterns. Forgotten NY's tour of the "Lanes of Kensington" calls the duplex porch, two stacked front porches on a two-family house, "almost the signature style of the neighborhood," and those houses put two households under one roof with one set of walls between them. Untapped New York points to the Foursquare houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, built roughly from the 1890s to the 1930s, and to Parkville, the old village between 18th Avenue and Foster Avenue whose streets run on a diagonal grid. In a frame house, the living room wall is usually plaster on wood lath over studs that were not spaced to any modern standard. In an attached brick house, the longest wall is usually the party wall, and a Brownstoner forum thread on mounting TVs in old Brooklyn houses puts it bluntly: party walls typically have no studs at all, just plaster on brick. A technician who arrives expecting studs at sixteen-inch centers is going to have a long afternoon.

Then there are the people, which is the other reason Kensington TV jobs look different. The neighborhood is one of the most mixed in Brooklyn: Bangladeshi and Pakistani families around Church, McDonald and Coney Island Avenues, where the city co-named a stretch Little Bangladesh in October 2022; Orthodox Jewish and Hasidic households; Uzbek, Latin American, Polish and Ukrainian neighbors in between. For a TV that means a household often wants something a standard install never covers: a South Asian channel package or streaming app signed in on the new screen, cricket matches from the other side of the world that air live in the middle of the New York night, a grandparent who needs the remote simplified to two buttons, or a TV placed so a large family can all see it from a narrow living room. Willow, the cricket broadcaster in the United States, has carried matches on its own service and, since 2025, on a free channel and a $9.99-a-month add-on inside Prime Video. Getting an app like that signed in and working before the technician leaves is as much a part of the job here as the bracket.

Same-Day Repair in Kensington

Need Repair in Kensington? A Bracket Pulling Out of Plaster, a Screen Hung Crooked, or a Wall Full of Old Holes

Three kinds of urgent call come out of Kensington's older buildings again and again. The first is a TV that has started to lean forward or drop on one side. In a prewar apartment the cause is nearly always the anchor, not the bracket: plastic plugs or small drywall toggles that bit into the top layer of plaster and nothing behind it. Old plaster is hard on the face and soft underneath, so a plug that felt solid on the day slowly crushes its own hole, especially on a full-motion arm that gets pulled out every evening. The Brownstoner forum thread on TVs in old Brooklyn houses has a homeowner describing exactly this: short anchors that failed, and long lead shields set into the brick behind the plaster that had held for about forty years. The second call is a screen that went up level on the bracket but not level with the room, because the floor of a ninety-year-old building has settled and the installer trusted the floor. The third is the leftover mess when someone moves out of a co-op: an empty bracket, four or six holes, and sometimes a power cord pushed into the wall above the TV and pulled out again near the baseboard.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back against the wall, because that loads the failing anchors even harder. If two people can lift it safely, take it off the bracket, unplug it and lay it face up on something soft. Then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, and putting the same TV back up on a properly anchored mount is priced as a new mount on that wall type, with the plaster or brick line added as it applies. If the old holes sit exactly where the new fixings need to go, we move the bracket a little rather than reuse holes that have already crumbled. A power cord inside a wall is pulled out and replaced with an in-wall kit or a surface cover, because a TV's cord is not allowed inside a wall at all. And if what we find is a problem with the wall itself, a long crack through the plaster or a loose patch around a radiator riser, we tell you plainly so the super or the landlord can deal with it before anything else goes up.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Kensington's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every Kensington TV call. They differ in three ways that decide the plan long before anyone talks about TV brands: what the wall is made of, how far and how high the screen has to be carried, and who besides you has a say over the drill.

The prewar elevator co-op

Six-story brick buildings from the late 1920s to 1941 on Ocean Parkway, Caton Avenue and the side streets, like 260 Ocean Parkway (1930, 77 units). Elevators, a live-in super, a board and plaster over solid partitions.

The Streamline Moderne six-story

Late-1930s buildings such as 40 Tehama Street (1939, 75 units), with arched doorways, built-in niches, picture rails and rounded corners that leave fewer clear walls than the floor plan suggests.

The 1960s parkway tower

Taller apartment buildings from the early 1960s wave on Ocean Parkway, where block and poured concrete replace plaster, and balconies and window walls shape the living room.

The duplex-porch two-family

Two stacked households under one roof, each with its own front porch, sharing framing, stairs and sometimes a chimney, with the owner often living on one floor.

The detached frame house and Foursquare

Older houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue and in Parkville, with plaster on lath over irregular studs, deep window trim and sometimes a decorative fireplace.

The attached brick house on a lane

Rows like the ones Forgotten NY describes on Kermit Place and Friel Place, where the living room's long wall is the masonry party wall shared with the house next door.

The prewar elevator co-op: an easy carry, a hard wall and a board in the background

A six-story co-op on Ocean Parkway is the most common Kensington TV job we see, and it is the one where the building matters as much as the apartment. The elevator makes the carry simple, even for a 75-inch screen still in its box, though some of these elevators are small and a long box may have to stand on end. The live-in super is usually the first person we deal with: they know which walls carry pipes, which have been opened before, and whether the building wants contractors to sign in. Hauseit's summary of New York co-op renovation rules says co-ops usually allow work only from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays, often hold loud noise until 10 a.m., and generally ban weekend and holiday work; many also ask for contractor insurance naming the co-op, the managing agent and the shareholder. A TV is minor work in most buildings, sometimes covered by a short decorative rider rather than a full alteration agreement, but the hours still apply to the drill. We book inside them and send the certificate of insurance before the visit.

The walls are the harder part. In buildings of this age the outer walls and the walls around the elevator and stair shafts are masonry, and many of the partitions between rooms are gypsum or clay block with plaster over it. Block is the wall that fools people. It sounds solid, and it is solid enough for a TV, but its faces are thin with hollow cores behind them, so a heavy sleeve anchor driven too hard can crack the face and a plastic plug spins in the void. The right approach is a slow drill without hammer action until the bit is through the plaster, an anchor made for hollow masonry set into the solid part of the block, and more fixings at a lower load each. Because block has no cavity, an in-wall kit is usually not possible, and the StreetEasy poster's warning about channeling a solid wall applies: it can be done, but it means chiseling a groove, patching and repainting, which most co-op boards would treat as real alteration work. For most Ocean Parkway apartments, the clean answer is a console below the screen with the cables dropping straight behind it, or a $59 cord cover painted to match the wall.

The Streamline Moderne six-story: arches, niches and the one wall left over

Kensington has a run of late-1930s buildings with the rounded corners, brick banding and curved details of the Streamline Moderne style, and their apartments are full of features that people buy them for. Brownstoner's 2025 story on a one-bedroom at 40 Tehama Street lists wide arched doorways to the living room and kitchen, an arched niche with built-in shelves, original moldings, original doors and picture rails, and two windows at the far end of the living room. Put a floor plan like that in front of a TV installer and the choice of wall narrows quickly. The windows rule out the end wall unless you want to watch with the light behind the screen. The arches rule out the wall beside the doorway, because a screen next to an arch looks crammed and the arch itself is often formed in plaster on metal lath, which is weak for a load. That leaves the long side wall, and that wall is usually a partition of block or a party wall with the apartment next door.

Picture rails help more than people expect. A rail runs around the room about a foot below the ceiling, and it gives a natural line for where the eye expects things to sit. We use it as a reference rather than the floor, which in a building this age is rarely level across a whole room, and we make sure the top of the screen sits well below it so the rail stays visible. We never fix anything to the rail or the moldings themselves, and we keep drilling well clear of them, because original plaster moldings chip easily and are very hard to match. If the room has a built-in niche, it is tempting to put the TV inside it, but most niches in these buildings are too shallow for a modern screen with a bracket, and an arched top does not frame a rectangle well. A niche is usually a better home for the cable box, the router or a speaker, with the cables run neatly to the screen beside it.

The 1960s parkway tower: concrete, balconies and long sightlines

Wikipedia's article on Ocean Parkway notes that 26 apartment buildings went up on the parkway between 1960 and 1965, and that co-ops and rentals along it reach up to twenty stories. These are a different job from the prewar co-ops. Partitions are often concrete block, outer walls may be poured concrete, and some apartments have balconies that turn the living room's end wall into glass. Poured concrete is the hardest drilling in the neighborhood, and the hidden steel inside it is the one thing that stops a hole dead; when the bit hits rebar, we move the bracket slightly rather than fight it. Concrete and block also mean no cavity for an in-wall kit, so the cable plan is a console or a cord cover in almost every apartment.

The duplex-porch two-family: two households and one set of walls

Forgotten NY's description of the duplex porch as Kensington's signature style is accurate on block after block of the East streets: two-family houses with a porch for each floor, one stacked above the other. Inside, each floor's living room usually runs across the front of the house, and the wall everyone wants for the TV is either the interior wall with the stair behind it or the side wall shared with the next house. The stair wall is framed, with plaster on lath, so an in-wall kit is often possible there, but the stair is also where the house's old gas line, the electrical riser or the radiator piping may run. We scan before drilling and stop if anything reads as metal in a line. The side wall, where the houses are attached, is brick under plaster and takes a heavy screen easily at the +$65 brick line.

The real issue in a two-family is that the people below or above you hear everything. A hammer drill in brick is loud for a few seconds per hole, and in a frame house the sound travels down the studs. If you rent the upper floor and the owner lives below, the owner is your neighbor, your landlord and probably the person who decides whether the TV goes on the wall at all. A short message before the visit, saying which wall, how many holes and whether cables will go inside the wall or in a cover, prevents nearly every objection. If you own the house and are setting up the rental unit, it is worth mounting the tenant's TV yourself on the best wall with a proper in-wall kit, so that the next ten years of tenants are not each drilling their own set of holes.

The detached frame house and Foursquare: plaster on lath and studs nobody measured

The older detached houses in Kensington, including the Foursquares Untapped New York points to on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue and the frame houses in Parkville, are built of wood with plaster walls on wood lath. That is good news for hidden wiring, because a framed wall has a cavity and an in-wall power and cable kit can go in between the studs. It is less convenient for finding the studs, because builders of that era did not hold to modern spacing, and the lath itself confuses a scanner by reading as continuous wood. We find the studs one at a time with a scanner set to deep mode, a magnet that picks up the nails holding the lath, and a single small test hole where the bracket plate will cover it.

Plaster on lath is strong in compression and weak in pull, so the fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the stud behind, drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward around the hole. Where the bracket cannot reach a stud, we use a toggle rated for the load on hollow plaster, never a plastic plug. These houses also have the deepest window and door trim in the neighborhood and, in some cases, decorative fireplaces in the front parlor. A mantel is a tempting TV spot, but the Brownstoner forum thread on Brooklyn fireplaces records the common complaint that a screen over a mantel is "awfully high." If you want it there anyway, the above-fireplace line is +$130 on our list, the brick line usually applies to the chimney breast as well, and we set the screen with a downward tilt so the household is not looking up all evening.

The attached brick house on a lane: the party wall does the work

Forgotten NY's walk through Kensington's small lanes describes a row of attached homes on Kermit Place from around the 1910s, two-story boxy brick houses on Friel Place with one apartment per floor, and duplex porches on Turner Place. In attached houses like these, the long living room wall is nearly always the party wall, and party walls in Brooklyn are masonry with plaster applied straight onto the brick. That is the strongest wall in the house for a heavy screen, as long as the anchors go into the brick itself and not the softer mortar joints between bricks. A sleeve or wedge anchor in sound brick holds far more than a TV needs; the same anchor in a crumbling joint can pull out under a full-motion arm.

The two cautions on a party wall are depth and neighbors. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, which is a small fraction of the wall's thickness, and we ask what is on the other side if you know, because a chimney flue or a neighbor's kitchen cabinets can sit right behind the spot you have chosen. The plaster on brick is often thick and sometimes loose, so we tap around the area first; a hollow sound means the plaster has let go of the brick and needs to be fixed by a plasterer before anything heavy hangs from it. And because the wall is solid, there is no in-wall route for cables. The answer is a cord cover painted to the wall color or a console directly under the screen, which in a narrow rowhouse living room is usually where the cable box ends up anyway.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Kensington Living Room

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like Kensington's. They are our standing practice, not a story about any single apartment.

Meet the super, then the room. In a parkway co-op the technician checks in where the building asks, confirms the hours, and asks the super one question: has anyone opened this wall before? Supers often know where a previous owner ran a cable or patched a hole, and that saves a guess.

Look at what the walls are telling you. Outlets in surface-mounted metal boxes mean wiring was added later in conduit. A radiator in the corner means a riser in the wall above it. An arch or a niche means metal lath. A wall that is noticeably thicker at the doorway than a framed wall would be is probably block.

Knock, scan, then one test hole. The knock tells hollow from solid. The scanner finds framing or metal. Neither is final in a prewar wall, so the technician drills one small hole where the bracket plate will hide it and reads what comes out: white plaster dust followed by wood for lath, red dust for brick, grey grit for block, a sudden drop into nothing for a cavity. That hole decides the anchor and confirms the price line before any more drilling.

Tape it and sit down. Painter's tape outlines the screen on the wall, and the household sits where they actually watch, including the grandparent who has their own chair. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket leaves holes in a wall you love.

Drill in one burst, with dust caught. All the drilling is done together, inside the building's hours, with a vacuum at the bit and drop cloths below. Old paint in these buildings is treated as lead paint until proven otherwise, so dust is caught at the hole rather than swept up later.

Wall by Wall

Lath, Brick, Block or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Kensington Wall and What It Adds

The $179.99 starting price on our Brooklyn list covers a TV of up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. In Kensington that exact wall is the exception, found mostly in renovated rooms and finished basements. Here is how the walls we actually meet are handled and how each one is priced.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The wall of the frame houses, the two-families and many interior walls in the older side-street buildings: thin wooden strips nailed across studs and covered in coats of plaster that can add up to an inch. The fixing goes through plaster and lath into the stud, or uses a toggle rated for hollow plaster where the stud is not where the bracket needs it. Pilot holes are drilled slowly and without hammer action so the plaster does not spall around the hole. This wall has a cavity, so an in-wall power and cable kit is possible.

Plaster on brick (+$65). Party walls, outer walls and many chimney breasts. Apartment Therapy's note that shared walls in old townhouses are "almost always plaster over brick" holds in Kensington's attached houses and two-families. We price it at the brick line once the test hole shows red dust. The anchor goes into the brick, the holes stay out of the mortar, and older, softer brick gets more fixings at a lower load each. No cavity means no in-wall kit.

Plaster on gypsum or clay block. The partition wall of many 1930s elevator buildings. It looks and sounds like masonry but has hollow cores behind thin faces. We treat it as masonry for fixings and confirm the price line from the test hole and your photos before the job is booked, so you are never surprised on the day. Anchors made for hollow masonry go into the solid webs, never into a void, and the bracket uses every fixing point on its plate.

Plaster on metal lath. Common around arches, curved corners and some ceilings in Streamline Moderne and Art Deco buildings. Metal lath confuses every scanner by reading as metal everywhere. Once the test hole tells us what is behind it, we price by that material: framing at the plaster line, masonry at the brick line.

Drywall. Found in renovated apartments, finished basements and some 1960s buildings. On wood studs it is the base price. Drywall over light steel studs needs fasteners made for steel, and we confirm the total from your photos before booking.

Hiding cables when the wall is solid, and when it is not

"Hide the wires" is the request that follows "mount it" on nearly every call, and the honest answer in Kensington depends on the wall. Start with the rule that does not change: a TV's power cord is a flexible cord, and the electrical code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls. The StreetEasy discussion on prewar TV mounting put the practical version simply: low-voltage cables such as HDMI and speaker wire can be buried, power cords should not be. A cord inside a wall can be pinched by a fixing, heat up where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected.

On a framed wall, plaster on lath or drywall, the right tool is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV with a separate pass-through for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It connects to nothing in the building's wiring, which is one reason most co-op boards and landlords accept it.

On a solid wall, block, brick or concrete, there is no cavity to fish through. The options are a paintable cord cover ($59) that runs neatly on the surface and takes the wall color, a console placed directly under the screen so the cables drop behind it and out of sight, or, where the building and the owner agree, a channel chiseled into the plaster for low-voltage cables only and then patched, which is plaster work rather than TV work and is usually priced by a plasterer. For most Ocean Parkway co-op apartments, the console or the cord cover is the answer, and when it is painted to match a cream prewar wall it disappears from across the room.

Old paint, small holes and why we vacuum at the bit

NYC HPD's Safe Work Practices page says residential buildings built before January 1, 1978 are presumed to contain lead-based paint, and that work disturbing 2 square feet or more of paint in a single room, or 10 percent of a small component, must follow lead-safe work practices by an EPA-certified firm, with dust clearance testing afterward. Those rules fall on building owners and their contractors for that size of work. A TV bracket's handful of quarter-inch holes, and even the two openings of an in-wall kit, disturb far less paint than that, and the EPA's own guidance draws a line between demolition and a clean cut-out that removes a piece of wall intact.

Being under a threshold is not the same as being careless, though, and nearly every Kensington wall we drill is old paint. So the practice is the same on every job: drop cloths under the work, a vacuum held at the bit while drilling, kit openings cut out cleanly rather than broken through, and the area wiped and vacuumed before we leave. If you have a child under six at home, tell us when you book and we will plan the cable route to keep any opening to the minimum. This is not legal advice, and if your building is carrying out larger renovation work, the owner's lead rules apply to that work, not to us hanging a TV.

Co-ops, Owners and Renters

Boards, Supers, Landlords and Leases: Getting a Yes Before the Drill

Ocean Parkway and the streets around it are full of co-ops, the two-families are full of owner-landlords living one floor away, and the rest of Kensington is mostly rented. On a large share of our calls, someone other than the person watching the TV owns the wall. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the public guidance says and what we see in practice, so you can ask the right question before booking.

In a co-op, start with the managing agent. Hauseit's guide to co-op renovation rules says the rules are usually set out in the alteration agreement, available from the managing agent or the board, that minor work such as painting often needs no agreement at all, and that some buildings use a lighter "decorative rider" for small jobs. Hanging a TV is usually treated as minor. Cutting an in-wall kit into a partition may be treated differently, and chiseling a solid wall for cables almost certainly will. Architect Michael Fasulo told Brick Underground that the alteration agreement is "a great guide to the dos and don'ts of renovating in your building," and that is exactly how to use it for a TV: read the section on what counts as minor work and what the hours are.

Expect hours and insurance. The same guide says co-ops typically allow contractor work from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays, often limit loud noise before 10 a.m., and usually ban weekends and holidays, and that many require contractor liability coverage of at least $1,000,000 with the co-op, the managing agent and the shareholder named on the certificate. We send the certificate of insurance before the visit with the names the managing agent asks for, and we book the drilling inside the building's hours. If your building is stricter than that, tell us and we will plan around it.

Use the super. Many Kensington co-ops have a live-in super, as StreetEasy's record for 260 Ocean Parkway and Brownstoner's story on 40 Tehama Street both note. A super who knows the TV is coming, which wall, and roughly when, can tell us where the risers run, unlock the service entrance for a large box, and confirm that the job is minor work. A short message from you a day ahead is worth more than any paperwork.

In a rental, read the alteration clause. Most leases say something about alterations or fixtures. If yours requires written consent, ask in writing, say the installer is licensed and insured, and say whether cables will go in a cover or in the wall. A landlord who knows exactly how many holes there will be is usually easy to persuade. When you move out, patch and paint the holes or have them done, and photograph the repaired wall. Brick Underground's advice column once dealt with a landlord threatening to keep $250 of a deposit over anchor holes left by a departed roommate's TV and called that "a gross overestimate for the work even in this town." The cheapest dispute is the one avoided by patching before the inspection.

Or skip the drill. If the lease is strict or you expect to move within a year, a TV set up on its own stand on a solid console is a proper job on our list at $119.99, including connecting the devices, and a soundbar placed on the console is $79. You get the same tidy cables and the same setup, and nothing to patch.

Walls that will not tell you what they are

My stud finder goes silent along the whole living room wall of our 1930s Kensington co-op. Is there anything to screw into?

Almost certainly, just not studs. A scanner that finds nothing across a whole wall in a building of that age is usually looking at a solid partition of gypsum or clay block under plaster, which has no studs because it does not need them. That wall can hold a TV well with anchors made for hollow masonry, set slowly into the solid parts of the block. Our technician confirms it with one small test hole hidden behind the bracket plate, and you hear the price line before any more drilling. The trade-off is cables: block has no cavity, so plan on a console below or a $59 cord cover.

The plaster around the arch in our Tehama Street-style living room sounds hollow when I tap it. Can a TV go on the wall right beside the arch?

It can usually go nearby, but not on the arch itself. Plaster on curved shapes like arches and coves in buildings of the 1930s was often laid on metal lath, and a hollow sound near the curve is normal. Metal lath holds a picture, not a 50-pound screen on a bracket. We keep the bracket on the flat wall at least a hand's width clear of where the curve begins, where the plaster usually sits on block or framing, and we check with the test hole. If the flat stretch beside the arch is too narrow for the screen you bought, the long wall opposite is almost always the better choice.

Someone told us our prewar walls might contain asbestos. Should that stop us from mounting a TV?

Not on its own, but it is a fair question to ask the building first. Architect Michael Fasulo told Brick Underground that asbestos can turn up in plaster, window caulking, floor tiles and around heating pipes in buildings constructed before the 1970s. A co-op's managing agent will usually know whether the building has been surveyed. A TV bracket makes a few small holes, and we drill with a vacuum at the bit, but if the building tells you a wall has been identified as containing asbestos, we will not drill it, and we will help you choose another wall or a stand setup instead.

Getting the TV, the box and the people into the right place

Our co-op sits near the Prospect Expressway trench at the top of Ocean Parkway, and we feel the trucks. Will traffic vibration loosen a wall-mounted TV over time?

Not if it is anchored for the wall it is on. North of Church Avenue, Wikipedia notes, the parkway's center gave way in the 1950s to the sunken Prospect Expressway, and residents in a 2008 Brownstoner thread about 81 Ocean Parkway talked about the traffic and one owner's soundproof windows. Vibration from traffic is small compared with the load of a screen being pulled out on an arm every evening. What loosens brackets in these buildings is the wrong anchor, plastic in plaster or short screws in block. With anchors rated for the material, a properly torqued bracket stays put. We check every fixing at the end of the visit, and if you ever see the screen sit lower on one side, call us before it gets worse.

We watch cricket live in the middle of the night while the rest of the house sleeps. What setup keeps it watchable without waking everyone?

Three things. First, a wired network connection to the TV or streaming device if the router is within reach, because a live stream at 2 a.m. is the worst time to discover that Wi-Fi does not pass through a block wall. Second, a soundbar with a dialogue or night mode, mounted on the wall under the screen rather than sitting on a hollow console that passes bass into the floor; the wall-mounted soundbar is $129 on our list. Third, the app itself signed in and tested before we leave, whether it is Willow inside Prime Video or another service. A setup-only visit covers the app work at $195 for the first hour.

Half our household watches in Bengali and the other half in English. Can one TV serve both without someone hunting through inputs every night?

Yes, and in Kensington it is one of the most useful things a setup visit does. The usual tangle is a cable box on one input, a streaming stick with a South Asian app on another, and a game console on a third, with nobody quite sure which remote does what. We put everything on a single streaming device or smart TV home screen where possible, place the apps each person uses on the first row, set the TV to switch inputs automatically when a device turns on, and label the remote buttons that matter. Menu languages differ from one brand and model to the next, so we check what your exact model offers. A setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour on our list.

Owners, boards and the people next door

The owner downstairs in our two-family asked how loud the drilling will be. What should I tell him?

Tell him it is short. On plaster over lath, drilling for a bracket takes a few minutes in total. On a brick party wall, each hole takes a few seconds of hammer drilling, and a bracket needs four to six holes, so it is a couple of short bursts. Everything else, measuring, hanging the screen, running cables and setting up, is quiet. Give him the time window, say which wall and whether the cables go in a cover or in the wall, and mention that the installer is licensed and insured. That answers nearly every question an owner downstairs asks.

The previous shareholder left a bracket on the wall and four holes where another one used to be. Can we use the existing bracket?

Only after checking three things: whether it is rated for your TV's weight and matches its bolt pattern, what it is fixed into, and with what. In a prewar co-op the most common problem is a good bracket held by bad anchors, plastic plugs in plaster or short screws in block. If the bracket and fixings are sound, we hang your TV on it at the mount price for that wall. If not, it comes down, and the new bracket goes in a fresh position rather than into old holes that have already crumbled. The four empty holes can be filled and painted by the building's painter or by you.
