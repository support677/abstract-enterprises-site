The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need Floodlight Camera Installation

Putnam covers more than 240 square miles split between six towns and three villages, and the county's own hazard-mitigation profile puts about three quarters of it inside the New York City watershed, with forest covering roughly two thirds of the land in 2011. Of its 38,713 homes the Census Bureau counts 83.8 percent as owner-occupied. Put those numbers together and you get the typical Putnam floodlight call: an owner, not a landlord, with a house set back among trees, a driveway that bends out of sight of the road, a detached garage or shed, and no streetlight anywhere near any of it. When the porch light is off, the dark starts a step from the door.

That dark is what thieves work in. In August 2026 Mahopac News reported Carmel police warning residents after seven unlocked cars on four streets in the Lake Casse, Lake Drive and Ridge Road neighborhoods were gone through between about 3:37 and 5:05 in the morning. The department's advice included parking in well-lit areas and closing garage doors, and it asked anyone with a doorbell, driveway or dash camera to check the overnight footage. Six years earlier The Examiner reported State Police from the Brewster barracks investigating ten vehicle break-ins and a stolen car in Carmel, Patterson and Southeast over a single October weekend, and urging people to lock their cars even in their own driveways. A floodlight camera aimed at the place the car actually sits is the piece of that advice an owner can install once and stop thinking about.

The other side of Putnam's dark is that people moved here for it. Cold Spring spent about five years getting low “dark skies” bollard lamps installed on its riverfront dock, and when they were dedicated in 2012 The Highlands Current wrote that the new lights cut glare and light pollution so the stars, the river and the Highlands hills stayed visible after dark. Town codes say the same thing in numbers. Philipstown's zoning allows no more than half a foot-candle of glare across a property line, says floodlights must be shielded or baffled to light only what they are meant to, and restricts anything brighter than 2,000 lumens to full-cutoff fixtures. Patterson's lighting standard sets 0.2 foot-candle at a residential property line and applies to single-family houses. Carmel's outdoor lighting section sets 0.1 foot-candle at the line and forbids light trespass. A floodlight bought for a city driveway, aimed straight out across the lawn, breaks every one of those.

And then there is the wiring. Putnam is one of the counties that licenses electricians itself. Its Electrical Licensing Law requires a county license for master electricians and for low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians, and it defines the technician's work — including video surveillance cabling — as starting at an outlet or connection that a properly licensed electrician already installed. Plugging an ordinary device into an existing circuit, with no splicing, sits outside the chapter. In plain terms: a plug-in floodlight on an outdoor outlet is one kind of job, a camera cable from an existing outlet is another, and a new hardwired outdoor box is a third that belongs to a Putnam-licensed master electrician. Storms make the case for getting it right. On the morning after Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020, Daily Voice reported 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers without power. A floodlight that comes back on but a camera that never rejoins the network is a common sight after days like that. Most owners who light the drive next want the door covered, and a floodlight visit is a sensible time to plan a doorbell camera for the front step as well.

  • Owners, not tenants, make the call: 83.8 percent of Putnam's 38,713 homes are owner-occupied (Census QuickFacts), and 1,757 are held for seasonal or occasional use (ACS 2023), many of them around the lakes.

Floodlight Out After the Storm, or Lighting Up for Every Deer?

A drive light that stayed dark after the power returned, a camera stuck offline since the last NYSEG outage, a light that has flashed on and off all night for a family of deer — call (845) 640-3835. Four checks to make before you call

  1. Look at the router and modem first. After an outage the light often returns on its own while the internet equipment is still restarting or stuck. If the router's lights are wrong, the camera is not the problem.
  2. Find the switch and the breaker. Outdoor lights in older Putnam houses are often on a wall switch by the side door or in the garage. A tripped GFCI outlet in the garage can also kill a plug-in floodlight.
  3. Check whether it is on the generator. If a standby generator ran during the outage, note whether the floodlight's circuit was one of the circuits it carried.
  4. Open the app's light settings. A sensitivity set to maximum, a motion zone that reaches the tree line, or a timer set to ten minutes explains most lights that seem to never go off.

Mention what you saw on the phone. Nine times in ten it points straight at a setting, the router, the power feed or a mounting spot that needs to move.

Putnam's houses were built in waves — nineteenth-century villages along the river and the railroad, summer colonies around the lakes, and post-war and later houses on wooded lots off the Taconic and Route 6. Each wave leaves a different wall and a different distance to cover.

Wooded-Lot Colonials and Raised Ranches

Carmel, Kent, Putnam Valley, Southeast. Vinyl or wood siding, an attached or detached garage, a long drive and woods on three sides. The commonest Putnam floodlight job.

Lake-Colony Cottages and Converted Bungalows

Lake Carmel, Putnam Lake, Lake Peekskill. Summer cottages on small lots laid out from 1928, many since winterized and enlarged, with the next house a few yards away.

Lakefront Houses on Lake Mahopac

Houses facing a lake of roughly 587 acres, with a road side, a water side, docks and stairs, and neighbors who see your lights across the water.

Village Houses in Cold Spring and Nelsonville

Pre-Civil War and Victorian frame and brick houses on small lots, many inside the Cold Spring historic district, where street-facing changes need the review board.

Estates and Hillside Houses in Garrison and Philipstown

Large lots on ridges above the river, long gravel drives, stone walls and outbuildings, and a town code that is strict about lights larger than 2,000 lumens.

Wooded-lot houses: light the parking spot, not the forest

On a colonial or raised ranch set back in the trees, the temptation is to put a big floodlight at the garage corner and point it down the drive. It lights the first stretch of trees, makes a bright wall of leaves for the camera to stare at, throws light toward the road and the neighbors, and triggers on every branch that moves in the wind. The better plan is smaller: one light over the door people actually use, one over the garage doors or the parking pad, both tilted well below the horizon. Siding needs a proper mounting block so the plate sits flat and water stays out of the box; we do not screw a floodlight plate straight through vinyl. Where the garage is detached, the second light usually takes power from the garage itself, if it has any, rather than a new run from the house.

Lake-colony cottages: little lots, close neighbors, old wiring

The Smadbeck lake colonies at Lake Carmel, Lake Peekskill and Putnam Lake were sold as summer lots — at Putnam Lake, Historic Patterson records 11,000 lots mapped at twenty by a hundred feet. Many of those cottages are now year-round houses, but the lots did not grow. A floodlight on one of them is closer to the neighbor's bedroom than to the end of your own property. That calls for a small, warm, shielded light on a short timer, aimed at your own steps and car, and a camera with motion zones that stop at your lot. Older cottages also bring older wiring: a porch box mounted on thin siding, a cable that was added on a weekend decades ago. We look before anything is connected, and if the box or cable needs replacing, that is work for a Putnam-licensed master electrician.

Lakefront houses: two fronts and a mirror

A house on Lake Mahopac has a road side and a water side, and light behaves differently on each. On the road side the job is the drive and the door. On the water side a floodlight on a dock, a boathouse or the back deck reflects off the water and carries across it to the houses opposite, which is exactly what the dark-sky sections of the town codes are written to stop. We keep water-side lights low, shielded and on motion only, aimed at the stairs and the dock gate rather than out over the lake.

Village and estate houses: what the street sees, and what the ridge sees

In Cold Spring's historic district, exterior changes visible from the street need approval from the village's Historic District Review Board, while work not visible from the street is not reviewed, so the floodlight usually goes at the back or side, and the front gets a small, period-appropriate fixture. On a Garrison or Philipstown estate the problem is the opposite: the house is visible from far away, up on a ridge, and a bright white light at the top of a long drive shows for miles. There we keep each light under the 2,000-lumen threshold where we can, choose full-cutoff fixtures when we cannot, and put light only where cars stop and people walk.

Rules that apply to floodlight work in Putnam County

  • Line-voltage wiring belongs to Putnam-licensed electricians. The county's Electrical Licensing Law says no one may carry on the trade of master electrician, special electrician or low-voltage/limited-data technician in Putnam without the county license or registration, and makes unlicensed master-electrician work a Class A misdemeanor. A new outdoor box or a spliced connection to house wiring is master-electrician work.
  • Camera cabling is licensed too, and it starts at an outlet. The county defines the low-voltage/limited-data technician's work — video surveillance included — as systems at 90 volts or less and five amps or less, starting at an outlet or connection a properly licensed electrician installed. Ask any installer, including us, to show where they appear on the county's published lists.
  • Plug-in devices sit outside the chapter. Attaching or plugging in ordinary devices to existing circuits, with no joining or splicing of conductors, is listed as an activity the chapter does not apply to — which is why the plug-in floodlight is the simplest legal route where a good outdoor outlet already exists.
  • Town lighting codes set limits at the property line. Carmel 0.1 foot-candle; Patterson 0.2 at a residential line and 0.5 at a nonresidential one, with full shielding; Philipstown 0.5, floodlights shielded or baffled, PAR lamps and wall packs prohibited, and full cutoff above 2,000 lumens. In Cold Spring, nonresidential floodlighting near a residence district must not glare toward it.
  • Your camera, your land. Under § 52-a of the state Civil Rights Law, people whose backyard is filmed from next door without their written consent can take the camera owner to court; masks and aiming keep every view on your own lot.
  • A state credit for small shops. Firms with 50 or fewer employees can apply by October 31 for New York's Commercial Security Tax Credit, worth as much as $3,000 per retail location and including camera systems — your accountant can confirm whether you qualify.

Floodlight Camera Installation in Putnam County: Questions Answered

Questions Putnam owners and businesses actually ask, grouped by what drives them: county licensing, town lighting limits, wildlife, storms, lake living, cars, and the ground between house and light.

The County's Licensing Law and Who Does Which Part

Where can we check that the people wiring our floodlight hold a Putnam County license?

On the county's own website. Putnam's Consumer Affairs office runs the Electrical Board, which licenses master, special and reciprocal electricians, limited data communication technicians, journeymen and helpers, and it publishes two downloadable lists: licensed master electricians and licensed low-voltage technicians, each dated (the current versions are dated August 11, 2026). Look the name up before work starts, not after. The same office runs a separate home-improvement contractor registration, which checks that contractors working on Putnam homes are insured, bonded and covered for workers' compensation. On a floodlight job you may meet both: the electrician who connects the box, and the low-voltage technician who runs the camera cable and sets up the recorder. Ask each one which list they are on, and keep the answer with your paperwork.

Kent's building department says it issues no electrical permits — so who signs off a new outdoor box at our house?

Electrical work in Putnam is generally checked by an approved third-party electrical inspection agency rather than by a town building inspector. The Town of Kent's own FAQ says it does not issue electrical permits and sends residents to Putnam County Consumer Affairs, and the county's page lists inspection agencies with filing instructions — State Wide Inspection Services in Fishkill and New York Electrical Inspection Services in Tarrytown, for example, in the Town of Patterson instructions. In practice the licensed master electrician who installs the new box files with the inspection agency, the agency inspects the work, and you get a certificate at the end. Keep that certificate; insurers and buyers ask for it. Our part, the camera, network and app, follows once the box is live. Requirements differ slightly between towns, so we confirm the filing route for your address when we quote.

Is a plug-in floodlight on an existing outdoor outlet treated differently under Putnam's licensing law than a hardwired one?

Yes. The county's Electrical Licensing Law lists activities it does not apply to, and one of them is attaching or plugging in ordinary electrical devices to existing circuits where no joining or splicing of conductors is needed. A floodlight camera with a factory power cord, plugged into a sound outdoor outlet that is already there, falls on that side of the line. A hardwired floodlight connected by splicing into a box does not, and neither does adding a new outlet to plug into. Two practical cautions. The outlet must be an outdoor, weatherproof, ground-fault-protected outlet in good condition; many garage and porch outlets in older Putnam houses are not. And the cord has to be fixed tight along a protected route, because a loose cord on a garage wall meets snow shovels, ladders and chewing animals within a season.

Town Lighting Limits and Dark Hillsides

Patterson's zoning sets a light limit at the property line — does that apply to a floodlight on a single-family house?

According to the version of Patterson's zoning we read (Chapter 154, as amended in 2009), yes. Its lighting standards say fixtures on a parcel used for a single-family residence must comply with the standards, but are not subject to Planning Board review. The standards cap light at 0.2 foot-candle at a property line in or next to a residential zone, measured 30 inches above the ground, call for full shielding, and prohibit spot or flood fixtures aimed toward residential buildings on neighboring land or up at the sky. They apply to new fixtures and to replacements, which the code defines as a change of fixture type, mounting height or location — so swapping a porch light for a floodlight camera counts. Ask the town whether a later amendment has changed any of this. Either way, a small, downward-aimed light covering your own door and car will normally sit well inside the limit, while a wide floodlight aimed across the lawn often will not.

Our motion light in Patterson stays on for five minutes after it triggers — does the timer setting matter?

It can. The same Patterson standard exempts motion-detector security lights that are normally off and stay on for less than four minutes when motion turns them on. A light that runs five minutes after every trigger may fall outside that exemption, and then the ordinary limits apply to it in full. Most floodlight cameras let you set the light's on-time in the app, separate from the camera's recording length, so the fix is usually a setting: one or two minutes of light, with the camera still recording for as long as you like. We set it during installation and show you where it lives in the app.

Philipstown’s code bans PAR lamps and wall packs — what goes on the barn instead?

Something that lights the barn door and nothing else. Philipstown’s zoning prohibits parabolic aluminized reflector (PAR) lamps and wall packs, the two cheapest ways to flood a barn wall, and adds that floodlights must be shielded or baffled so they light only the object intended and that anything above 2,000 lumens must be a full-cutoff luminaire. On a barn that usually means a downward-facing, fully shielded fixture over each door, on a motion sensor or a timer, and a separate low-light camera mounted where it looks along the doors rather than into the light. If you would rather have one unit, choose a floodlight camera rated at or under 2,000 lumens, tilt both heads well below horizontal and turn the brightness down in the app. Whether an existing PAR flood or wall pack must come down now is a question for the town’s building department; what we install new will meet the rule as written.

Our land backs onto Fahnestock and we moved here for the stars — can a floodlight camera still do its job?

Yes, if the light is treated as something that switches on rarely and briefly rather than a lamp that runs all night. The camera does most of the watching in the dark by itself, using infrared or a low-light colour sensor; the floodlight only needs to come on when a person is close enough to matter, for long enough to get a clear clip and to let them know they have been seen. So we set person detection, keep motion zones close to the house, choose warm colour temperatures, shield the lamp heads so none of the light goes above the horizontal, and leave the light off on wind, branches and animals where the model allows it. Done that way, the light is on for a few minutes a week, and the sky over the back of the property stays as dark as it was before.

Deer, Bears and Other Night Visitors

A bear is on the deck and the floodlight just came on — how should we use the camera before anyone opens the door?

Watch first, from inside. Bears are regular night visitors in Putnam — in May 2024 the Putnam County News & Recorder reported a large bear in a Carmel backyard less than half a mile from the historic courthouse, and the state Department of Environmental Conservation urged residents to secure food sources. When the light comes on, open the live view on your phone, check the deck, the grill and the trash area, and look at the camera covering the door you would use. Most floodlight cameras let you turn the light on manually and talk through the speaker; a voice from the speaker often moves an animal along, though a wildlife expert quoted by Homes & Gardens notes that security lights themselves do not keep deer, raccoons or bears away. Keep the light’s motion zone on the deck and the path, so it tells you something is there, and save the clip: it shows what drew the bear and what to put away tomorrow.

Moths swarm the lamp heads on July nights — will they keep setting the floodlight off?

They can, especially once the light is already on. Swann’s support pages note that insects flying close to a camera can trigger its motion detection and leave you with false alerts and recordings, and a lit lamp beside the lens draws them right into view. Three things help. Use person detection for alerts and recording where the camera has it, so a moth in front of the lens is ignored. Keep the light’s on-time short, so the swarm has less to gather around. And many owners find a warmer colour setting attracts fewer insects than a cold white one. Spider webs across the lens, which follow the insects in late summer, are the other common cause; a wipe with a cloth on a pole clears them. If the false alerts persist, moving the camera a little away from the lamp heads usually ends them.

Power for Garages, Barns and Buried Lines

Our detached garage gets power through a cable buried decades ago — can the garage floodlight run off it?

Probably, if the old feed is sound. A floodlight camera draws very little, so it rarely strains an existing garage supply. The questions are condition and safety: what kind of cable it is, whether it was buried deep enough or in conduit, whether the garage circuit is protected properly, and whether it trips when the ground is wet. Testing that, and adding a box or outlet on the garage wall for the light, is splicing into line-voltage wiring, which under Putnam’s Electrical Licensing Law is work for a county-licensed master electrician. Where the feed is fine, the garage light is one of the cheaper jobs on a Putnam lot, because nothing new has to cross the lawn. Where it is not, the electrician’s report tells you whether to repair it or run a new line, and we can often send the camera’s network connection through the same trench.

Our well line and septic field run under the lawn we would have to cross — how is a cable route planned around them?

Carefully, and mostly before anything is dug. Utility locate services mark the utility-owned lines, but not the owner’s own: the water pipe from a private well, septic lines and leach field, the old feed to a garage, irrigation and invisible-fence wire. On many Putnam lots those cross exactly where a cable to a light post would go. We start with whatever paperwork you have — a septic drawing, a survey, the well installer’s notes — and walk the lawn looking for the tank lid, the well cap and the line between them. The cable then follows a route that avoids them: along the driveway edge, around the leach field rather than across it, at a shallow depth in conduit where the code allows. Where no safe route exists, a network-powered or plug-in light on the garage wall, or a battery camera on a post, avoids digging altogether.

Lake Communities and Shared Waterfronts

The lake association owns the beach and dock next to our lot — can our floodlight camera look over it?

Only with the association's agreement, and even then with care. A camera on your house that records your own steps and the edge of your lot is yours to set up; one aimed across the association's beach records people who have not agreed to it, and a light aimed that way puts glare onto common property at night. If the association wants coverage of the dock or the boat racks, the better route is a camera the association installs and controls, with its own notice to members. On your own camera we draw privacy masks over the shared land and keep the light's motion zone on your side.

We shut off the power at the panel when we close the lake cottage for winter — can a floodlight camera do anything while we're gone?

Not on that circuit. With the panel off, a wired or plug-in floodlight is just a fixture on the wall. If you want something watching through the winter there are two choices. Leave one circuit live — the one that feeds the floodlight, the router and modem — and turn off the rest, which keeps the light, the camera and the alerts working while the water and heating stay off. Or use a battery or solar camera with a small light for the winter months, accepting that it records shorter clips and needs charging or sun. Whichever you pick, tell the Sheriff's Office if you are using its Dark House Check while you are away, which it describes as extra patrols and an exterior check that do not guarantee protection, and set up someone nearby who can respond when the camera alerts.

Cars, Driveways and Footage Police Can Use

After the Mahopac car break-ins, police asked for driveway camera footage — what makes a driveway clip actually useful to them?

Three things: a face or a clear figure, a time that is right, and a file you can hand over. A camera looking down a long drive from the garage corner often shows a small shape at the far end and nothing usable near the car. Aim it instead at the spot where the car sits and the path to its doors, from a height and distance where a person fills a good part of the frame, and let the floodlight switch on as they reach the car so the camera records colour rather than grey infrared. Check that the camera's clock is set and keeps time after outages. Know how to export a clip from the app or recorder to your phone or a USB drive, because that is what investigators ask for. We show you that export before we leave.

Where should the light go so a car parked in our driveway isn't just a silhouette on the footage?

To the side of the camera and a little in front of the car, not behind it. A floodlight mounted directly over the camera and pointed down the drive lights the far side of the car and leaves anyone standing on the near side as a dark outline against a bright background. Better to light the space where people stand — beside the driver's door, at the trunk, at the garage door — from a light that faces the same way the camera does, so faces are lit from the front. On many Putnam houses that means the light on the garage wall and the camera at the house corner looking along it, or the reverse.

Snow, Trees, Wetlands and Rock

Snow slides off our metal roof right where the floodlight would go — where else can it mount?

Not under that eave. A sheet of snow and ice coming off a metal roof will knock a floodlight's lamp heads out of line at best and tear the fixture off the wall at worst, and it hits hardest at the corners and above doors, which is where people want the light. The usual answers are to mount on a gable end, where nothing slides off; under a porch roof or a solid overhang that is not itself shedding snow; on the side wall well away from the drip line; or on a post a few feet out from the house. We look at the roof line from the ground before choosing the spot, and on houses with metal roofs we ask where the snow piled up last winter.

Can a floodlight camera be mounted on a tree instead of a post?

We advise against it. A tree moves in the wind, so the picture shakes and the motion sensor sees movement where there is none, which in Putnam's woods means the light flashing on all night. Trees also grow, and screws or straps that are tight now will either be swallowed by the bark or pull loose in a few years. And a tree on a lot line may not be entirely yours. A treated or metal post set in concrete beside the parking area does the same job steadily for years, and the cable can run underground to it.

Our driveway crosses a wetland buffer — does trenching a cable out to a light post need a town permit?

It may. Carmel regulates wetlands of an eighth of an acre or more together with a 100-foot buffer around them, and Kent's building permit guidance flags work within 100 feet of ponds, streams and watercourses. Digging a trench for a power or network cable inside those areas can count as regulated activity, even when it is short and refilled the same day. Before anything is dug we check the property against the town's mapping and, where the run would cross a buffer, look for a route around it — along the edge of the existing driveway, for example, or to a post that does not need a trench at all. Where there is no way around, the owner applies to the town first. A light that waits a few weeks for a permit is better than a stop-work notice.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Floodlight Camera Installation in Putnam County

Google's AI summary was not reachable from the tools used to build this page, so we took the claims from the cost guides, maker manuals and install blogs those summaries lean on. They hold up as national shorthand; on a wooded or lakeside Putnam lot each one bends.

1. “Budget around $450 to have a floodlight put in”

The national figure most answers repeat comes from Inch Calculator, whose 2023 guide lands near $450 and spreads typical jobs from $250 to $800, with labor of $150 to $350 a light; HomeCamCafe adds that hiring an electrician for a floodlight camera runs about $650. Both assume a subdivision house where the box is already on the wall and the garage is steps away.

In Putnam our installation for a straight swap onto an existing sound box runs $435 to $834 plus the camera, reflecting the county's distance and travel. Where the light has to go somewhere with no power — a detached garage, a post at the parking pad — it runs $834 to $2,175, depending on the route and the length, and whether a Putnam-licensed master electrician needs to set a new box.

2. “Skip the junction box and plug it in instead”

For walls with no box, install blogs point owners to plug-in kits that run a weatherproof (though unarmored) cord to an outdoor GFCI outlet, promising “no electrical work required beyond confirming you have a working outdoor outlet nearby.”

Putnam's licensing law actually supports half of that. Plugging an ordinary device into an existing circuit without splicing is outside the chapter. But adding the outdoor outlet you are missing is electrical work, and so is any adapter that splices into a fixture box. And many Putnam outlets on garages and porches are old, not weatherproof, or on a circuit that trips every time it rains.

3. “Wired floodlights are always on, even when the power goes out”

Hardwired floodlight cameras are often described as more reliable than battery models because they never need charging. That is true until the grid fails, and in Putnam the grid fails at county scale: Daily Voice reported 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers out on the morning after Isaias in 2020, and 1,733 still out four days later.

During an outage a hardwired floodlight camera is dark and silent like everything else in the house, and when the power returns the camera may come back before the router does and sit offline. Battery-backed network equipment, a generator circuit chosen on purpose, and local recording are what make a wired light reliable here.

4. “Brighter is better — look for the most lumens”

Floodlight cameras are marketed on light output. Lorex lists one Wi-Fi floodlight with two 2,000-lumen light arrays, and Reolink lists 1,800 lumens on a PoE model. On a dark Putnam drive that sounds like the right answer.

Several Putnam towns say otherwise in their codes. Philipstown restricts anything above 2,000 lumens to full-cutoff fixtures and requires floodlights to be shielded or baffled; Patterson caps light at a residential property line at 0.2 foot-candle; Carmel at 0.1. A bright, unshielded light on a wooded hillside is also visible a long way off, and a white wall of lit leaves gives the camera glare instead of detail.

5. “There's no contractor licensing in New York State, so anyone can install it”

It is true that New York has no single statewide license for this trade, and online license guides often say so. Some AI answers stop there.

Putnam is one of the places where that stops being the whole story. The county's Electrical Licensing Law requires a county license for master electricians and for low-voltage and limited-data technicians, defines the technician's work to include video surveillance cabling, and attaches civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation. The Consumer Affairs office separately requires home-improvement contractors to register.

So in Putnam the right question is not whether a license exists, but whose license each part of the job falls under. Our written quote names the parts and who does them.

6. “Put it where the Wi-Fi is strong”

Arlo's own installation guide recommends a location with a strong Wi-Fi signal, which is good advice and usually the last thing anyone checks.

On a wooded Putnam lot the best spot for the light is often the detached garage or the parking pad, a long way from a router in the middle of the house, with trees in between whose leaves soak up signal in summer. A camera that works during the October install can start dropping out in June.

7. “Cover the whole perimeter”

Generic security advice says to light and watch every approach to the property. On a three-acre wooded lot that would take a dozen lights and leave the hillside glowing all night.

People approach a house where it is easy to: the drive, the parking area, the doors, the path from the lake. Those are what we light and record. The rest of the lot is better left dark, with motion zones that ignore it, which also keeps the camera from alerting every time a deer walks the tree line.

What Changes Between Putnam's Towns

The county licensing law is the same everywhere in Putnam. The lighting codes, the ground and the houses are not. This is how the six towns differ for a floodlight job.

Carmel and Mahopac

The county's most populous town, with Mahopac's lake, Carmel Hamlet's main street and a long Route 6 corridor. Carmel's outdoor lighting section is written to keep light inside the property, with 0.1 foot-candle at the line and lights that do not shine above the horizontal; how far it reaches an existing single house, as opposed to a site under Planning Board review, is a question for the building department. Its wetland law covers wetlands of an eighth of an acre and a 100-foot buffer, which matters for trenching. And it is where police asked for driveway footage after the August 2026 car break-ins near Lake Casse, Lake Drive and Ridge Road.

Kent

Lake Carmel, Ludingtonville, Farmers Mills and the land around the West Branch and Boyds Corner reservoirs. Kent's building department says it does not issue electrical permits and points owners to the county's Consumer Affairs office, and its permit guidance flags work within 100 feet of ponds, streams and watercourses. After Isaias, Daily Voice counted 5,815 Kent customers without power on the first morning, so outage planning is part of every Kent job.

Patterson

Putnam Lake, Patterson hamlet and the farms and yards off Route 22 and Route 311. Patterson has the most detailed residential lighting rule we found in the county: single-family fixtures must meet the standard, 0.2 foot-candle at a residential property line, full shielding, no flood or spot aimed at a neighbor's house, and an exemption for motion lights that are normally off and stay on under four minutes. On Putnam Lake's narrow lots that rule and good manners point the same way.

Southeast and Brewster

The village of Brewster, Peach Lake, Tilly Foster Farm and the I-84 and Route 312 business area. Commercial lots and older village buildings sit close to houses here, so the rule is to light the doors and the lot, not the neighbors.

Putnam Valley

Lake Peekskill, Tompkins Corners and steep, wooded roads toward the Westchester line. The town's property maintenance code says required exterior lighting must be kept working and that no offensive glare may be transmitted so as to endanger the public.

Philipstown, Cold Spring and Nelsonville

The Highlands side of the county: Garrison, Continental Village, the villages of Cold Spring and Nelsonville, and Central Hudson rather than NYSEG on most of the power lines. Philipstown's code is the strictest on brightness — half a foot-candle at the line, shielded or baffled floodlights, no PAR lamps or wall packs, full cutoff above 2,000 lumens. In Cold Spring, the historic district's review board looks at changes visible from the street. The Highlands Current reported break-ins on Old Albany Post Road and Indian Brook Road in September 2024, a reminder that the quietest roads are not exempt.

FAQ

Putnam County Floodlight Camera Installation FAQ

Can the floodlight stay off when our own car comes up the drive at night?
+

Often, yes, though how depends on the model. Many floodlight camera apps can use your phone's location to switch modes when you arrive home, and some let you set a schedule or a short pause from the app before you turn into the drive. A simpler route on a long Putnam driveway is placement: if the light covers the parking spot and the door rather than the whole drive, your headlights pass by long before the sensor sees anything. What we do not recommend is turning motion lighting off for the whole evening, because that is when the car break-ins police warn about happen. We set up whichever option your camera supports and show the household how it behaves.

Our house sits on a slope and the best wall is two storeys up — do some jobs need a lift?
+

Some do. Most Putnam floodlights go in from an extension ladder on level ground, but a walk-out basement on a hillside, a gable above a steep bank or a lake-side wall over rocks can put the spot out of safe ladder reach. Usually we find a lower spot that covers the same door and parking area; otherwise we bring a second person and the right ladder setup, or rarely a lift. The quote says which, so access is no surprise on the day.

Can the floodlight also light our house number sign so emergency crews find us?
+

It can help, if the sign is near the house. Many Putnam houses sit well back from the road, so the number that matters to an ambulance is the one on the mailbox or a post at the bottom of the drive, and a floodlight at the house will not reach it. For that sign, a small solar or low-voltage light aimed only at the numbers is the better answer. Where the number is on the house or the garage, we can place the floodlight so its light falls across it on motion, and some owners leave a low glow on all night for that reason. Keep the light aimed down at the sign, not out at the road.

Can we switch the floodlight on from the app when we hear something outside?
+

With most floodlight cameras, yes. The app usually has a button to turn the light on manually, and many also let you sound the siren or talk through the speaker. That is useful when you hear something at the garage at night and want to look before you open the door. The manual switch is separate from the motion setting, so you can keep the light's motion timer short to suit the town code and still turn it on for longer when you choose. We show everyone in the household where the button is, and check that it works from outside the house on mobile data, not just on the home Wi-Fi.

How do we keep the lens and lamp covers clean when spring pollen and road dust coat them?
+

Gently, and from the ground where you can. A yellow film of pollen in May or dust from a gravel drive softens the picture and scatters the light, so a wipe every few weeks in season helps. Use a soft, clean microfibre cloth, lightly dampened with water, on the lens and the lamp covers; avoid paper towels, glass cleaners and anything abrasive, which scratch the coating. Many owners use a cloth on an extension pole rather than a ladder. We can add lens cleaning to a maintenance visit if the light is out of reach.

Can you give us the fixture details and settings in writing for our insurance company?
+

Yes. At the end of the job we can give you a short written summary: the floodlight make and model, where each one is mounted, what it covers, whether it records to a card, a recorder or the cloud, and the main settings such as motion zones and the light timer. Where a licensed electrician installed a new box, keep the inspection certificate with the summary. Ask your insurer what they need before the visit and we will include it.

We are re-siding the house next year — should the floodlight wait until then?
+

If the new siding is definitely happening, it is usually worth waiting, or at least planning the two together. New siding means new mounting blocks, and a floodlight fitted to the old siding has to come off and go back on, which costs a service visit. If you need the light now, we can mount it so it comes off cleanly, leave the cable with enough slack, and note exactly where the box and the cable entry are, so the siding crew can fit a proper block around them. Tell the siding contractor about the floodlight before they start, because removing and reconnecting a hardwired fixture is electrical work.

Can the light stay at a soft glow all night and only go to full brightness when someone walks up?
+

Some models can. A number of floodlight cameras offer a dusk-to-dawn low setting that brightens to full on motion, and on a dark Putnam lot a soft glow over the door can be friendlier than a light that snaps on. Two cautions. A light that stays on all night, even at a low level, is no longer a motion light, so check it against your town's limits at the property line; a dim, shielded glow aimed at your own step is usually fine. And the glow attracts insects, which then trigger motion. We set the low level as dim as it can usefully go and keep the full-brightness timer short.

Can the floodlight's built-in siren be turned off so it never sounds by mistake?
+

On nearly every model, yes. Sirens on floodlight cameras are usually set in the app: off, manual only, or triggered automatically with motion or person detection. On a quiet Putnam road a siren that goes off at 2 a.m. for a deer wakes the whole lake, so we normally leave it on manual, which means you can sound it from your phone after looking at the live view, and it never fires on its own. Some owners turn automatic siren on only when they are away. Whatever you choose, test it once in daylight so everyone in the house knows how loud it is and how to stop it.

Can the steps down to our dock be lit without throwing light across the lake?
+

Yes, with low light rather than a floodlight. On a lake lot the stairs and the dock gate are where people trip and where an intruder would come up from the water, but a floodlight aimed down the slope ends up shining across the lake at the houses opposite. Better to use small, shielded step or path lights pointed straight down, on a timer or motion, and to put the camera on the house looking down the stairs, using its own infrared or a dim built-in light. Keep anything on the dock itself low and aimed at the boards, never out over the water.
