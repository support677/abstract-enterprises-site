Ring vs Nest:
Which Security Camera System Is Better?

Doorbells, Cameras, Floodlights, Plans, Video History and Alexa vs Google Compared · Also Searched as Nest vs Ring · Updated November 2026

Ring vs Nest comes down to the assistant your home already uses: Ring is the better system for Alexa homes, months of saved video and doors or walls that can take a network cable, while Nest is the better system for Google homes, 2K wired cameras and a few hours of free clips. Both keep real history behind a plan.

Quick answer: Pick Ring for Alexa, a wired or Power over Ethernet front door, and up to 180 days of saved clips on one plan for the whole address; pick Nest for Google Home, Gemini search of your footage, and 2K wired cameras that keep a few hours of clips for free. Installed in NYC by our crews, four Ring cameras start from $1,350 and four Google Nest cameras start from $1,750 on our current price lists, including mounting, a wire run up to 100 ft per camera and app setup; recording is on the brand's own plan. It arrives as a Forest Hills house where the old chime transformer is too weak for the doorbell the owner picked, or a Staten Island driveway where a battery camera keeps going quiet in February. This page compares Ring and Google Nest the way those visits do: power, cold, Wi-Fi, how long video is kept, what the plans cost for a whole house, which speakers each one talks to, and what still works two winters later. If you only read one part, read this. Ring belongs to Amazon and Nest belongs to Google, and that ownership shapes almost everything else. Both sell video doorbells, outdoor and indoor cameras and a floodlight camera; both stream live view and send motion alerts with no plan; both sell a monthly plan that unlocks saved history and the smarter features. The differences that change what we install are these. History: Ring's plans page offers up to 180 days of event history, while Google Home Premium keeps 30 days on its Standard plan and 60 days on Advanced. Free clips: Google's newest wired Nest cameras and doorbell keep up to 6 hours of short event previews with no subscription, and its battery models keep up to 3 hours of event history; Ring's plans page treats saved history as a plan feature. Power: Ring sells hardwired, plug-in, battery and Power over Ethernet versions, including a doorbell that runs on PoE, and no Nest model on Google's spec page lists PoE. Resolution: Ring's top doorbells and outdoor camera are 4K, and Nest's newest doorbell and wired cameras are 2K. Smart home: Ring lists Alexa; Nest lives in the Google Home app and, according to Google's own help page, also works with Alexa.

So our short rule is this. If the household talks to Alexa, if you want to scroll back months rather than weeks, or if the camera can be fed by a network cable, we lean Ring. If the household talks to Google, if you want Gemini to search your footage and write summaries, or if you would like a few hours of clips without signing up for anything, we lean Nest. The installed cost is a little lower for Ring on our lists, the monthly plan cost is almost identical, and the rest of this page shows the detail behind each part of that rule.

Our Verdict by Household

Who Should Pick Ring and Who Should Pick Nest

On paper both brands cover a whole house. In practice the right choice depends on the speakers already in the home, how long you need to look back and whether there is power where the camera goes. This is how we split it after putting both on row-house stoops, two-family driveways, co-op doors and shopfronts across the five boroughs, Long Island and the Hudson Valley.

Choose Ring if

  • Alexa runs the house. Ring's outdoor camera support page lists "Works with Alexa," and seeing who is at the door on an Echo Show in the kitchen is the feature Ring owners use most after the doorbell button itself.
  • You want history measured in months. Ring's plans keep motion events for up to 180 days. Google's plans keep 30 or 60. For a building owner who gets asked about an incident weeks later, that gap decides it.
  • The door or wall can take a cable. Ring's Wired Doorbell 4K Elite is powered natively over Ethernet, and a PoE+ edition of the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro exists too. One network cable powers and connects the device, with no battery and no Wi-Fi guesswork.
  • You want 4K. Ring's top doorbells and outdoor camera are sold as 4K; Nest's newest models top out at 2K.
  • An alarm is on the list. Ring Alarm lives in the same Ring app, and the Ring Pro plan adds 24/7 professional monitoring for it.

Choose Nest if

  • Google runs the house. Nest cameras and doorbells sit in the Google Home app next to the thermostat, the speakers and the Nest Hub, and they answer to Gemini for Home on a Google Home Premium plan.
  • You would like some clips with no subscription. Google's newest wired Nest camera and doorbell keep up to 6 hours of short event previews for free, and the battery models keep up to 3 hours of event history. For a renter who only wants to see who rang this afternoon, that may be enough.
  • You want to search your footage by asking. On the Advanced plan Google lists searching your video history with Gemini, event descriptions and a daily summary of what happened.
  • Your stoop is deep and the packages land low. The 3rd gen Nest Doorbell records a square picture across a 166-degree diagonal, which frames a visitor's face and the mat in the same shot.
  • The Wi-Fi is merely average. Google says 2K on its newest doorbell and outdoor camera needs at least 3.3 Mbps; Ring recommends 15 Mbps upload for its 4K devices. On a modest building connection, the lighter stream is easier to keep steady.

When we would install neither

A walk-up with a dozen units, a store that must keep two weeks of round-the-clock video from eight cameras, or a parking lot that needs to read movement at a distance is not a job for either cloud brand. A wired system that records to a recorder inside the building keeps everything without a plan per device, and a Ring or Nest doorbell can still handle the front door. The recorder brands we fit are weighed against each other on our best security camera brands hub, and our house-brand systems are described on the security camera installation NYC page.

Front Door

Ring vs Nest Doorbells: Where Most Owners Start

Most of our Ring and Nest jobs begin at the door. Someone has a package stolen, or a parent wants to see who is buzzing while they are at work, and the doorbell becomes the first piece of a system. The two brands approach the doorbell very differently: Ring sells a long list of models at several power levels, while Google's spec page covers four generations of Nest doorbell and the Google Store currently offers two of them, the wired 3rd gen and the battery model.

Ring: a model for every kind of wiring

Ring's comparison page lists battery doorbells, hardwired doorbells and a Power over Ethernet doorbell side by side. The Battery Doorbell 4K Pro carries what Ring calls Retinal 4K video with up to 10x Enhanced Zoom, a head-to-toe field of view of 140 degrees horizontal by 140 degrees vertical, Wi-Fi 6 on both bands and a Quick Release battery pack. Ring recommends at least 15 Mbps of upload speed for its 4K devices and rates this doorbell from −4 °F to 122 °F. If you wire it in, Ring asks for a standard doorbell transformer of 8 to 24 VAC, 40 VA at most. The Wired Doorbell 4K Pro gives the same 4K picture without the battery, and the Wired Doorbell 4K Elite runs on native PoE and adds remote gate access. Ring's 3D Motion Detection, which Ring describes as pinpointing both motion and distance, appears on the 4K Pro and Elite models. Ring's product FAQ for the Battery Doorbell 4K Pro also says it supports end-to-end encryption.

Nest: fewer doorbells, a taller frame

Google's current wired model is the Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen). Its spec sheet lists a 2K sensor of 2048 by 2048 pixels, video up to 2K at 30 frames per second with HDR, a 1:1 square frame, a 166-degree diagonal field of view, 6x digital zoom, infrared night vision lit up to 10 ft, IP65 water and dust resistance and an operating range of −4 °F to 104 °F. It needs a transformer of 16 to 24 VAC and 10 to 40 VA, connects on 2.4 or 5 GHz Wi-Fi, and Google says 2K quality needs at least 3.3 Mbps. The Nest Doorbell (battery) is the older-style option: HD video at 960 by 1280 in a 3:4 frame, a 145-degree diagonal view, IP54, a 6 Ah built-in battery and the ability to run on bell wire of 8 to 24 VAC for continuous charging.

What those numbers mean on a New York stoop

Ring's 4K doorbells win on raw detail, which helps when a visitor stands back on the sidewalk or the stoop is several steps up from the street. Nest's 3rd gen doorbell wins on shape: its square frame shows a person's face and the doormat in the same picture, which is exactly where packages end up on a brownstone landing. Both see more than enough of a typical vestibule. Where they part is the cost of the picture: Ring's 4K stream asks a lot more of the upload connection, and in a basement-fed brownstone the Wi-Fi at the front door is often the weakest in the house.

Old transformers and chimes

This is the part that catches people. The Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen) needs at least 16 volts AC from the transformer, while Ring's Battery Doorbell 4K Pro accepts 8 to 24 VAC and also runs on its battery. Many older New York homes still have small chime transformers from decades ago, and we meter every one before mounting anything. If the voltage is short for the doorbell you picked, the fix is a new transformer, which we fit or arrange before the doorbell goes up, so it never sits half-powered and dropping off Wi-Fi.

Battery doorbells in winter

Google is unusually direct here: its spec sheet says a wired installation of the Nest Doorbell (battery) will still need charging with a cable when temperatures fall below 32 °F. For a door that faces the weather from December to March, that means bringing the doorbell inside to charge. Ring's battery models can also be hardwired to keep the battery topped up, and its removable Quick Release pack can be swapped for a spare. If you want a doorbell you never think about, either brand's wired model is the better buy.

Apartment doors and building intercoms

Neither brand replaces a building's intercom. In a co-op or rental hallway, a battery doorbell on a no-drill mount can work if management allows it, and Ring sells a peephole camera for renters. Where the building door is the real problem, our doorbell camera installation NYC crews look at the intercom and the apartment door together rather than adding a second button nobody uses.

Outdoor Cameras

Ring vs Nest Outdoor Cameras: Detail, Power and Cold

Past the front door, a typical New York house needs eyes on a driveway, a back door and sometimes a side alley or a garage. Here is how the two brands' main outdoor cameras compare on the points that matter on a real wall.

Resolution: 4K against 2K

Ring's Outdoor Cam 4K Pro is sold as Retinal 4K with Enhanced Zoom. Google's Nest Cam Outdoor (wired, 2nd gen) records 2K, 2560 by 1440, with HDR and night vision with HDR. On a driveway twenty or thirty feet long, 4K gives more room to zoom into a recorded clip; on a narrow side yard the difference is hard to see. Remember too that resolution is only as good as the upload: Ring wants 15 Mbps for its 4K cameras, Google 3.3 Mbps for 2K.

Field of view and night vision

Google lists a 152-degree diagonal view for the 2nd gen outdoor camera and infrared night vision lit up to 20 ft by two high-power LEDs. Ring describes the 4K Pro as super wide-angle with night vision, and its spec page, as we read it on November 6, gives 140 degrees horizontal by 85 degrees vertical without stating a night-vision range. Because one brand measures diagonally and the other horizontally and vertically, the numbers cannot be compared directly. In practice both cover a typical backyard from a corner.

Sirens and deterrence

Ring lists a remote-activated 85 dB siren on the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro, measured at 1 m. The Nest Cam Outdoor spec rows we read cover picture, night vision, power and weather rather than a siren, so we do not promise one. If you want a camera that can sound off at someone on the driveway, Ring states it plainly, and the floodlight models of both brands add light as a deterrent.

Power: the real split

Ring sells the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro three ways: hardwired, with a plug-in adapter, or on PoE+. Google's Nest Cam Outdoor (wired, 2nd gen) runs on a 12 V, 0.75 A supply through an 18 ft outdoor-rated cable, which means an outlet within reach or a cable brought through the wall, and the Nest Cam (battery) runs on a 6 Ah built-in battery. No Nest model on Google's spec page lists Power over Ethernet. For us that is the biggest practical difference between the brands outside: a PoE camera can sit 80 ft from the router on one cable with no outlet nearby, while a Nest wired camera needs power close to the mount.

Cold: read the charging line

Every outdoor Nest model is rated to operate from −4 °F, and Ring's outdoor camera from −5 °F. The difference is in charging. Google lists a charging range of 32 °F to 104 °F for the Nest Cam (battery), so a battery camera that runs low in a cold snap cannot recharge outdoors until it warms up. And the Nest Cam Indoor (wired, 3rd gen) is indoor only, rated from 32 °F, so it does not belong in an unheated garage or enclosed porch in January.

Wi-Fi through brick

Every current Nest model sold in the U.S. lists both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz Wi-Fi. Ring's Outdoor Cam 4K Pro and its better doorbells are dual-band too, but some of Ring's lower-priced devices, including the Floodlight Cam Plus, connect on 2.4 GHz only. Through a brick party wall, 2.4 GHz usually reaches further, and 5 GHz carries more data when the signal is good. We measure both bands at every mounting spot before recommending either brand.

Indoor Cameras

Indoor Cameras: The Quiet Half of the Comparison

Most Ring vs Nest arguments are about the front door, but plenty of our customers start inside: a camera on the stairs to see the kids come home, one by the back door of a ground-floor apartment, or one watching a dog walker let themselves in.

Nest Cam Indoor (wired, 3rd gen)

Google lists a 2K sensor at 2560 by 1440, video up to 2K at 30 frames per second with HDR, night vision with HDR lit up to 15 ft, a 152-degree diagonal view, a 10 ft cable ending in USB-C with a 7.5 W adapter, dual-band Wi-Fi and an operating range of 32 °F to 104 °F. It is marked indoor use only. Like the other newest Nest models, it keeps up to 6 hours of event previews with no plan and can hold about an hour of events if the Wi-Fi drops.

Ring's indoor cameras

Ring sells several indoor cameras, including pan-tilt and 4K models, and our own Ring price list includes a Power over Ethernet indoor version. We did not read Ring's indoor spec sheets for this page, so we are not quoting their resolution or field of view here; the Ring price page lists the models we install.

Our view inside the home

Indoors, power is easy and the Wi-Fi is usually good, so the choice comes down to the app you already use and the plan you will pay. A Google household with a Nest Hub gets the most from a Nest indoor camera; an Alexa household with Ring at the door is better keeping everything in the Ring app. Point indoor cameras away from bedrooms and bathrooms, and use the privacy settings each app offers when the family is home.

Floodlights

Ring Floodlight Cam Plus vs Nest Cam With Floodlight

A floodlight camera replaces an existing outdoor light, so it is usually the second device people add after the doorbell. Both brands make one, and both need a proper electrical box and 120-volt power from a licensed electrician when hardwired.

Light and picture

Ring's Floodlight Cam Plus pairs 1080p HD video and color night vision with two 2000-lumen floodlights at a warm 3000K, across a 140-degree horizontal by 80-degree vertical view. Google's Nest Cam with floodlight also records 1080p, with HDR, across a 130-degree diagonal view, with two adjustable lights reaching 2400 lumens at most at 4000K and infrared night vision lit up to 20 ft. Google lists two floodlight motion sensors covering 180 degrees horizontally out to about 25 ft, plus the camera's own sensor.

Power, siren and Wi-Fi

The Floodlight Cam Plus comes in a hardwired version for 110 to 240 V and a plug-in version, connects on 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only, and carries a 105 dB siren. The Nest version hardwires at 100 to 240 V AC onto an electrical box, while the camera itself is a Nest Cam (battery) that rides on the floodlight; Google lists 2.4 and 5 GHz Wi-Fi for the U.S. model and an IP54 rating. Ring rates its floodlight from −5 °F to 122 °F and Google rates its from −4 °F to 104 °F.

Which floodlight we would put up

For a driveway or back door in an Alexa home, the Ring floodlight is simple: one device, one plan, and a loud siren if you want it. For a Google home, the Nest floodlight blends into the Google Home app with the doorbell and indoor cameras. Either way, the outdoor box and the circuit come first. We mount, connect and set up the camera; the 120-volt feed comes from your electrician, and we will tell you before the visit if the existing light box will not carry the weight.

Video History

Where Ring and Nest Keep Your Recordings

Both brands are cloud-first: the camera uploads clips, and the plan decides how long they stay. Neither takes a memory card in the camera itself. The details below are where the two brands differ most, and they matter more than resolution once something actually happens.

Paid history: up to 180 days against 30 or 60

Ring's plans page promises up to 180 days of event history on Ring Solo, Ring Multi and Ring Pro, so the clip count grows with the plan but the window does not shrink. Google Home Premium keeps 30 days of event-based history on the Standard plan and 60 days on the Advanced plan. For a homeowner who checks the app the same evening, 30 days is plenty. For a landlord, a small business or anyone who might be asked about an incident long after it happened, Ring's window is up to six times longer for about the same monthly price.

Free history: Nest gives you a few hours

This is Nest's quiet advantage. Google's help article on video history says the Nest Cam Indoor (wired, 3rd gen), the Nest Cam Outdoor (wired, 2nd gen) and the Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen) keep up to 6 hours of event video previews with no subscription, in clips up to 10 seconds long. The Nest Cam (battery), the Nest Doorbell (battery), the Nest Doorbell (wired, 2nd gen) and the Nest Cam (wired) keep up to 3 hours of event video history. Older Nest models get 3 hours of snapshots instead. Ring's pages list live view, real-time alerts and two-way talk without a plan and treat saved video as part of the plan. If you never intend to pay a subscription, Nest leaves you with more.

Round-the-clock recording

Ring sells 24/7 Continuous Recording as an add-on for eligible cameras, priced per camera on top of a plan, with recordings kept for up to 14 days. Google includes 24/7 history on the Advanced plan, up to 10 days, but only for wired installations: the Nest Cam (battery) has to be connected to power to record continuously, and Google says 24/7 history is not available on the Nest Doorbell (battery) even when it is wired. If continuous recording on several cameras is the goal, both get expensive quickly, and that is usually the point where we price a recorder-based system as well.

When the internet goes down

Google's article on how Nest cameras store video says its current cameras and doorbells have enough internal memory to record up to one hour of events while offline, then upload them when the connection returns. That covers a router reboot or a short outage, not a power cut: if the camera loses power it cannot record, and the Nest Cam (wired) and the Nest Doorbell (wired, 2nd gen) have no battery to carry them. Ring's answer is Ring Edge, which stores video on a microSD card inside a Ring Alarm Pro base station; Ring requires a compatible plan, linked Ring and Amazon accounts and the cameras on the Alarm Pro's own eero Wi-Fi network.

When the plan ends

Ring's support pages warn that you lose access to recorded videos when a plan ends and advise downloading anything you want first. Google's subscription help says you can only have one Google Home Premium subscription per home and tells owners to save any video history they want to keep before changing it. The habit we teach on every handover is the same for both: the moment something important is recorded, download it to your phone or computer.

Plans and Fees

Ring Protect vs Google Home Premium: What You Pay Each Month

Both plans are optional, both bill monthly or yearly, and both are paid to the brand, not to us. They are built differently, though. Ring prices by device or by location. Google prices by home and folds its speakers and displays into the same subscription.

Ring Protect

  • Ring Solo, $4.99 a month or $49.99 a year: video recording, smart alerts and up to 180 days of event history for one device.

The two side by side

For one doorbell and two cameras at one address, the cheapest full plan on each side is almost the same money: Ring Multi at $9.99 a month or Google Home Premium Standard at $10. Ring keeps clips far longer; Google throws in Gemini on your speakers and the free-tier clips if you ever cancel. Step up a level and it is $19.99 against $20 again: Ring Pro adds professional alarm monitoring and AI alerts, while Google Advanced adds 24/7 history and Gemini search. If you only have one camera, Ring Solo at $4.99 is the cheapest paid option on either side.

The plan over three years

Cameras usually stay up for years, so the plan ends up costing more than people expect. Using the brands' own yearly prices: Ring Multi at $99.99 a year comes to about $300 over three years, and Google Home Premium Standard at $100 a year comes to $300. One level up, Ring Pro at $199.99 a year and Google Advanced at $200 a year each come to about $600 over the same three years. In other words, the monthly bill is a wash between the brands; what you are really choosing is what the bill buys. Ring spends it on a long history window and alarm monitoring, Google spends it on Gemini, 24/7 history for wired cameras and smart-speaker features. Paying yearly instead of monthly saves roughly two months on both, according to their own pages.

What still works with no plan

Without a plan, both brands give you live view, motion alerts and two-way talk from your phone. Nest adds its few hours of free clips; Ring, as its plan pages put it, provides certain features without a plan but keeps saved history behind one. We set up every system so the free features work on day one and the owner decides about a plan with the trial period in hand, not on the doorstep.

Small print worth knowing

Ring says each plan covers one device or one location and plans renew until cancelled. Google says that switching between monthly and yearly billing means stopping auto-renewal, waiting for the cycle to end and subscribing again. Plan names and prices have both moved recently: Nest Aware is now Google Home Premium, and Ring's own pages describe its plans under the Ring Protect name. Treat the numbers here as correct for November 2026 and check the brand's page before you sign up.

Before You Buy

Six Questions to Answer Before Choosing Ring or Nest

We ask these on every first call. The answers usually pick the brand for you.

1. What voltage is on the old doorbell wire?

The Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen) needs 16 to 24 VAC. Ring's Battery Doorbell 4K Pro takes 8 to 24 VAC and can fall back on its battery. If you do not know, a photo of the chime box and the transformer label tells us most of it.

2. Which voice assistant is already in the house?

Alexa points to Ring. Google points to Nest. Both, or a household that may switch, points to Nest, because Google documents Alexa support for its cameras and Ring does not document Google support.

3. How far back will you ever need to look?

A few hours: Nest's free tier may be enough. A month: either brand's entry-level whole-home plan. Several months: Ring.

4. Is there power, or a cable path, at each spot?

An outlet within 18 ft suits Nest's wired outdoor camera. A cable path back to the router suits Ring's PoE models. Neither, and you are choosing a battery camera, which in our climate means a charging routine.

5. How good is the Wi-Fi at the mounting spot?

Ring's 4K devices want 15 Mbps of upload; Nest's 2K models need 3.3 Mbps. Test at the actual spot with your phone on the same band, not in the living room.

6. Do you own the walls?

Renters and co-op shareholders often need permission for anything drilled into a door frame or facade. A battery doorbell and a battery camera on small screws are easier to take with you, on either brand.

Year Two and Beyond

Living With Ring or Nest After the First Winter

Unboxing reviews stop at week one. Our calls start around month eighteen. These are the things owners of both brands tell us about once the novelty wears off.

Charging becomes a chore

A battery camera that needed charging twice a year in a mild spring needs it far more often over a cold winter with busy foot traffic. Google's help page on Wi-Fi problems says plainly that cold weather can shorten battery life. Ring's swappable packs make the job quicker; Nest's built-in batteries mean taking the camera down. Either way, the camera you can wire is the camera you will still trust in year three.

The router changes

A new internet provider, a new router or a renamed network knocks every Wi-Fi camera offline until it is set up again. Write down which devices you have before the technician arrives, and keep the app logins handy. A PoE Ring device keeps its connection through the cable to the switch, which is one more reason we like them for doors that matter.

Plans change under you

Nest Aware became Google Home Premium, and Ring's plan names have shifted too. Over the life of a camera you should expect the plan to be renamed, repriced or reshaped at least once. Look at the price the brand charges today and decide whether you would still pay a few dollars more.

Older models lose ground

Google's own video history article shows how this plays out: the newest wired Nest models keep 6 hours of free event previews, while older ones such as the Nest Cam IQ and the original Nest Doorbell (wired) get only 3 hours of snapshots without a plan. Google even publishes a help page for upgrading from the 2nd gen wired doorbell to the 3rd gen. On either brand, buy the current model rather than a cheap old one.

Adding devices later

Ring Multi already covers every Ring device at the address, and Google Home Premium covers the whole home, so a fourth or fifth camera adds no plan cost on either. What it does add is load on the Wi-Fi, which is where a mesh point or a cable earns its place.

"Nest records nothing without Nest Aware"

Out of date twice over. Nest Aware is now Google Home Premium, and Google's current wired models keep up to 6 hours of event previews for free, with the battery models keeping up to 3 hours of event history.

"Ring only works with Alexa and Nest only works with Google"

Half true. Ring's pages list Alexa. Google's help center says Nest cameras and doorbells are compatible with Alexa too.

The battery camera and the floodlight camera still are, but the Nest Cam Outdoor (wired, 2nd gen), Nest Cam Indoor (wired, 3rd gen) and Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen) record 2K.

Not always. Google says a wired Nest Doorbell (battery) still needs cable charging below 32 °F.

Only when the upload keeps up. Ring wants 15 Mbps for 4K; a weak front-door signal can make a 4K camera look worse than a steady 2K one.

FAQ

Ring vs Nest FAQ

Is Ring or Nest better for a New York City home?
+

It depends on the speakers in the house. For an Alexa home, for history measured in months, or for a door or wall that can take a network cable, we lean Ring: its plans keep up to 180 days of events and it sells Power over Ethernet models. For a Google home, or for anyone who wants a few hours of clips without a subscription, we lean Nest.

Is Nest vs Ring the same comparison as Ring vs Nest?
+

Yes. It is the same two brands searched in the opposite order, so everything on this page answers both. We keep a single page for the pair so the facts are maintained in one place.

Which keeps recorded video longer, Ring or Nest?
+

Ring. Its plans page offers up to 180 days of event history on Ring Solo, Ring Multi and Ring Pro. Google Home Premium keeps 30 days on the Standard plan and 60 days on the Advanced plan, which also adds up to 10 days of round-the-clock history for wired cameras.

Do Ring or Nest cameras save any video without a subscription?
+

Nest does, briefly. Google says its newest wired camera and doorbell keep up to 6 hours of event previews in clips of up to 10 seconds, and its battery camera and doorbell keep up to 3 hours of event history. Ring gives live view, alerts and two-way talk without a plan, with saved history as a plan feature.

Will Ring work with Google Home, or Nest with Alexa?
+

Google's help center says Nest cameras and doorbells are compatible with Alexa, including live view on Echo devices with a screen and doorbell announcements. The Ring pages we read list Alexa only, and Ring's community forum says Google Home is not a supported integration.

Can a Ring or Nest doorbell run on my existing doorbell wiring?
+

Usually, if the transformer is strong enough. The Nest Doorbell (wired, 3rd gen) needs 16 to 24 volts AC and 10 to 40 VA. Ring's Battery Doorbell 4K Pro accepts 8 to 24 volts AC up to 40 VA when wired in. Many older New York chime transformers fall short, so we meter it before mounting.

Do Ring or Nest outdoor cameras handle a New York winter?
+

Their outdoor models are rated to run from about minus 4 or minus 5 degrees Fahrenheit. The catch is charging: Google lists 32 degrees as the lowest charging temperature for its battery models, and a wired Nest Doorbell (battery) still needs cable charging below freezing. Wired cameras avoid the problem on both brands.

Can Ring or Nest cameras use a network cable for power?
+

Ring's can. The Wired Doorbell 4K Elite runs on native Power over Ethernet and the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro comes in a PoE+ version. No current Nest model on Google's spec page lists PoE; the wired outdoor Nest camera uses a 12-volt supply on an 18 ft outdoor cable.

Do Ring or Nest cameras keep recording if the internet goes out?
+

Current Nest cameras and doorbells store up to one hour of events in internal memory and upload them once the connection returns, as long as they still have power. Ring's local option is Ring Edge, which records to a microSD card in a Ring Alarm Pro base station with a compatible plan.

Which has the sharper picture, Ring or Nest?
+

On paper, Ring. Its top doorbells and its Outdoor Cam 4K Pro are 4K, while Nest's newest doorbell and wired cameras are 2K. Ring recommends 15 Mbps of upload for 4K and Google needs 3.3 Mbps for 2K, so on a weak connection the steadier stream can look better.

How much does Ring or Nest cost installed in NYC?
+

On our current NYC price lists, four Ring cameras installed start from $1,350 and four Google Nest cameras from $1,750, including mounting, a wire run up to 100 ft per camera and app setup. Single Ring cameras run $260 to $1,200 and single Nest cameras $330 to $480. Hidden drywall wiring is $200 per camera, prices are plus sales tax, and the plan is paid to the brand.
