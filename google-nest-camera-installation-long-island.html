The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Google Nest Camera Installation

Long Island is not one neighborhood, so this page starts where the island's busiest rail hub is: Hicksville. Wikipedia records that the man the hamlet is named for bought land there in 1834 and turned it into a stop on the Long Island Rail Road in 1837, and that the presence of a major LIRR hub is why Hicksville grew into a major bedroom community of New York City. A short drive south is Levittown. EBSCO's history of it says Levitt and Sons announced on May 7, 1947 a plan to build a neighborhood called Island Trees on old potato farms in Nassau County, and kept expanding it until 1951, when it held 17,447 homes. Encyclopedia.com goes further and describes Levittown as a development at Hicksville itself, built after the war for returning servicemen. Those houses, and the thousands of ranches, capes, split-levels and colonials that followed them across Nassau and into Suffolk, are where most Long Island Nest cameras end up. They were built for families and fresh air. They were not built for doorbells that need steady power, cameras that need steady Wi-Fi, or cables that need a path from the eave to an outlet.

The numbers explain why so much of our work here comes back to the age of the house. The Census Bureau's American Community Survey for 2023 counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau, 364,529 of them one-unit detached houses, with a median year built of 1955. Suffolk has 579,790 units, 463,095 of them detached, with a median year of 1970. A Newsday analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026 found that more than 5,000 of the houses were built in the 1950s and 1960s. In plain terms, most Long Island homes are standalone houses that are between fifty and seventy-five years old, owned by the people living in them, and changed many times by many owners. That is good news for a Nest install, because the owner decides where a camera goes and nobody needs a board's approval. It is also the reason we test everything, because the doorbell wiring, the transformer, the chime and the Wi-Fi in a 1955 house have usually been touched by three or four people before us.

The Levitt houses deserve a paragraph of their own, because they shape a Nest install more than any other building type on the island. The Living in L-Town slab report describes them plainly: slab-on-grade, wood-frame construction, no basements, the house sitting on a concrete slab. A history of the area's radiant heat on Substack explains what was inside that slab: copper coils that carried heated water from the oil furnace. Wikipedia notes the original houses were small, about 750 square feet, with no basement and no porch. Each of those facts changes where a camera's power can go. With no basement, nobody can drop a cable down and run it under the floor to a camera on the far side of the house. With heating pipes poured into the concrete, nobody should be drilling into that floor at all. With no porch roof, a doorbell camera at the front door takes rain, snow and low winter sun straight on the lens. Heating Help adds a twist: only about five percent of the houses still have their original radiant systems, so the coils in a given slab may be dead, replaced or still live. We treat every slab as if it is live.

Long Island homeowners also have recent reasons to want a camera at the door and the driveway. News 12 Long Island reported a doorbell camera in Massapequa Park recording a thief taking a package off a porch on Atlantic Avenue while the homeowners and a UPS driver were in plain sight, and four new iPhones stolen from a home on Walters Avenue in Wantagh within minutes of delivery. CBS New York reported the Suffolk District Attorney charging 14 men in a porch theft ring that worked 31 communities including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, allegedly targeting deliveries of phones and tablets. ABC7 reported a multi-agency burglary task force in January 2024, with police saying burglars are attracted by cars in people's driveways. In June 2026 ABC7 reported three suspects walking up a driveway in East Hills, going through a car and then entering the home before the alarm sounded. NBC New York reported in November 2025 that security cameras in Jericho, the hamlet next to Hicksville, showed how quickly a crew of four reached the second floor of a house. LI Life Politics reported that Nassau burglaries rose five percent, from 234 to 245, for January through May of 2026, even as overall property crime fell.

None of those stories proves that a camera stops a crime, and we will not pretend it does. A Long Island Patch reader put the limit honestly: "I have a Ring camera, but I understand it would just let me watch someone steal my package." That is the right expectation to start from. What a well-installed Nest system does is give you a clear, timely look at your door, your driveway and your yard, alerts on your phone while something is happening, and footage that is good enough to be useful afterward. What a badly installed one does is drop offline on cold nights, miss the driveway because the Wi-Fi at the garage is weak, or record a faceless top-of-the-head view because it was mounted too high on a slab ranch with low eaves. The difference is almost entirely in the installation.

There is also a newer reason people call: Google has changed a lot of things at once. Google Home Help says Dropcam and Dropcam Pro stopped being supported starting April 8, 2024, and BGR lists the Nest Protect, the Nest x Yale lock, the Nest Secure alarm and the Nest Cam IQ series among discontinued products. 9to5Google reported that Google Home Premium, in Standard and Advanced tiers, has taken over from the old Nest Aware plans. ConsumerAffairs carries complaints from owners who moved from the Nest app to the Google Home app and found it freezing and slow, and one Queens reviewer whose Nest doorbell went offline every other day despite paying for professional installation. On Long Island, where many families bought their first Nest devices years ago, we get calls to replace a Dropcam on a garage shelf, to move an old account into the Google Home app, or to work out why a doorbell installed by someone else will not stay connected.

Finally, we want to be plain about what our research did not find. We looked for Reddit threads on r/longisland and similar boards about Nest installs and found none we could read directly; press coverage in TechHive reports Reddit complaints about Nest cameras that will not charge in freezing weather, but we did not see the threads themselves. We could not retrieve Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches, Bing or DuckDuckGo results, or Yelp reviews of Long Island Nest installers. We found no Town of Oyster Bay, Town of Hempstead or Suffolk town rule that deals specifically with low-voltage camera work. Where this page talks about what we find in walls, attics and doorbell circuits, it is drawing on the building record above and on what we see in houses of these ages generally, not on a survey of Long Island homes.

Long Island facts that shape a Nest install

  • Old, detached and owner-occupied: 364,529 of Nassau's 477,860 housing units and 463,095 of Suffolk's 579,790 are one-unit detached houses; median years built are 1955 and 1970 (ACS 2023).
  • Slabs with pipes inside: Levitt houses sit on concrete slabs with copper heating coils embedded, and have no basement, so floor drilling is out and cables travel through walls, attics or outside.
  • Big counties, many governments: Nassau has two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 hamlets over 453 square miles; Suffolk has 10 towns over 2,373 square miles (ny.gov).
  • Nearly three million people: the 2020 Census counted 1,395,774 people in Nassau and 1,525,920 in Suffolk.
  • Cold that matters to batteries: Google says Nest battery models will not charge below 32°F, while a Nest Cam (battery) on wired power keeps working down to −4°F.
  • A free police registry in Suffolk: SCPD lets residents and businesses register camera locations; the department says it does not get access to the cameras.

After a Long Island house has a Nest Doorbell and a driveway camera, the next request is usually the Wi-Fi itself: an access point for the detached garage or the back of a long ranch, because the camera that sees the yard is the one furthest from the router. If you already know the signal is weak out there, say so when booking so we bring the right equipment and do it in the same visit, rather than mounting a camera that will struggle for months.

From 1947 to the 1970s: How a Long Island House's Build Era Decides Where a Nest Cable Can Travel

If you drove from a Levittown cape down the Wantagh Parkway, across to a split-level near Hofstra and out to a 1970s colonial in western Suffolk, you would pass houses from at least four building periods, and each one hides its own puzzle for a Nest install. The puzzle is almost never the camera. It is power: where it comes from, how far it has to travel, and what is in the way. The table below lines up the eras with the facts the public record gives us and with what we expect to find. The last column is our working assumption, not a fact about any particular house; we confirm it on site.

The original Levitt capes

The first houses of Island Trees were capes on quarter-acre lots, and Wikipedia notes they were about 750 square feet with no basement and no porch. In houses like that, the attic is usually our best friend and the only practical way across the house. A camera on the gable end can often be fed from an outlet in the room below by going up through a closet ceiling, across the attic and back down behind the soffit, all without opening a finished wall. A camera at the front door is harder, because there is no porch roof to hide a cable behind and no overhang to keep weather off the doorbell. In that case we often choose the battery Nest Doorbell, or a wired doorbell on the existing bell circuit if the transformer passes our test, rather than running new power to the door. Capes that have been extended, dormered or opened up since 1949 have walls of every age, so we check each one before we pick a route.

The ranches that came after 1949

Encyclopedia.com records that in 1949 Levitt and Sons discontinued the Cape Cod model and introduced ranch-style houses, and those ranches, along with the many copies built by other developers across Nassau, are the single most common house we expect to see on a Long Island Nest call. A ranch is long and low. That is good for attic runs, because the attic covers the whole house, and bad for Wi-Fi, because a router in the front room may be forty or fifty feet of walls away from the camera that watches the back yard. The ranch's original doorbell circuit is the other issue. Bell transformers in houses this age were sized for a ringing chime, not for a camera that runs all day, and they have often been swapped for whatever was on the hardware-store shelf. We measure the voltage, read the label, and only then decide on the doorbell.

Split-levels and hi-ranches

The split-levels and hi-ranches of the 1950s and 1960s add a vertical puzzle. Part of the house may sit on a slab, part over a crawlspace or a garage, with half-flights of stairs between them. A camera on the garage corner of a split-level may be easiest to feed from inside the garage, where an outlet is often close and an exterior wall is easy to pass through. A camera over the front entry of a hi-ranch may be fed from the upper floor's attic. Where the levels meet, there is usually a framed wall that runs all the way from one floor to the next, and that is the route we look for first. If the house has a basement section, we can use it, but we never assume one exists until we have seen it.

Suffolk's 1960s and 1970s houses

Move east and the median house is younger, the lots are often larger and the driveways longer. Many of these houses have full basements, which makes power runs easier, and garages that may be attached, detached or set far back from the house. The challenge here is distance. A Nest Cam that watches the end of a long driveway or a detached garage has to reach the home Wi-Fi from wherever it is mounted, and a router on the first floor in the middle of the house may not cover it. We survey the signal at the exact mount point with the router where it lives day to day, not with the router carried outside for the test. If the signal is weak, we fix the Wi-Fi before mounting the camera.

The remodeled house, whatever its age

The US History Scene essay on Levittown notes there has been so much remodeling that very few original Cape Cods exist, and the same is true of houses of every era on the island. That remodeling is why we never trust a wire just because it is there. A two-conductor cable at the front door may be the original bell wire, a later replacement, an abandoned alarm loop or a phone line. A cable in the attic may lead to a camera that someone took down years ago. We test what we find, trace it with a tone generator and probe when we have to, and only reuse a wire when we know where both ends go and what it carries.

Front Door to Back Fence: What to Check in a Long Island House Before We Arrive

Most of the time lost on a Nest job is lost before the first screw: a doorbell that turns out to need a transformer nobody brought, a garage camera with no Wi-Fi, a Google account password nobody remembers, or a camera bought for a spot that faces straight into the afternoon sun. Stand where the camera will stand

The best camera position is decided by what it needs to see, not by where it is easiest to mount. Stand at the spot you have in mind and look at what is in view: the walk, the driveway, the side gate, the neighbor's windows. Then look at the time of day you most care about. A camera facing south toward a driveway in a Massapequa winter can be staring into low sun for part of the afternoon, and a doorbell on a slab ranch with no porch catches rain on the lens. Take one photo from the camera's height in the direction it will face, and send it with your request. It tells us more than a sketch.

Find where the power actually comes from

For wired Nest cameras, the question is always which outlet will feed it. Google's own guidance says you can run the power cable to an outdoor outlet, or to an outlet inside the home through an existing vent or hole. Walk around the outside of the house and note any weatherproof outlets, dryer vents and old cable entry holes. Inside, note the outlet closest to each camera on the other side of the wall. In a slab house there is no basement route, so those outdoor outlets and attic paths usually decide the plan. If an outdoor outlet looks damaged or has no cover, tell us; that is an electrician's fix before any camera plugs into it.

On slab floors and heating coils. The single rule we never break in a Levitt-era house is that the floor stays whole. The Substack history of Levittown's radiant heat says copper coils carrying hot water from the oil furnace were embedded in the slab, and Heating Help says only about five percent of the houses still have their original systems. That means some slabs still have live water pipes inside them, some have dead ones and some have been abandoned for baseboard heat, and from the surface you cannot tell which. A drill bit through a live coil means a leak inside concrete. So every Nest cable in a slab house goes up, not down: through a closet ceiling to the attic, through an exterior wall at a sealed point, or along the outside in a tidy run. When a camera needs power at a spot with no attic above it, we look at the battery model on a solar or weatherproof wired option rather than reach for the floor.

On older walls and what might be in them. A restoration contractor writing about postwar Cape Cods and ranches in Uniondale warns that houses from that boom frequently contain asbestos materials. That is a contractor's statement, not a survey, but it matches what anyone working in houses of that age has to keep in mind. We keep holes small and few, prefer existing openings such as vents and old cable entries, and avoid disturbing old siding, old plaster and old ceiling texture where we can. If a house shows signs of materials that need testing before they are drilled or cut, we stop and tell you, and the drilling waits until you have an answer.

On rentals. NeighborhoodScout reports that about 38 percent of homes in Uniondale are rented, and parts of Hicksville are adding hundreds of new apartments around the station. A tenant can usually put an indoor Nest Cam on a shelf and a battery doorbell on the door trim with the landlord's agreement. Wiring into the bell circuit, drilling the siding or mounting a camera that covers a shared driveway is a different conversation. We ask who owns the building at booking, and if the landlord has not agreed in writing, we keep the install to what can be removed without a trace.

What a shop or office Nest camera has to do

A Nest camera in a living room watches a quiet room. A Nest camera in a deli on North Broadway watches a register, a door that opens hundreds of times a day, and a sidewalk full of passing people, all of which trigger events. The Morning Delights story shows what a business owner actually wants from a camera like this: a motion alert that arrives when the place is closed, with a clear enough picture to decide what to do. Getting there takes more than putting the camera on the wall. Activity zones have to ignore the street and focus on the door and the register. Notification schedules have to be quiet during business hours and loud after closing. The Wi-Fi has to stay up when the shop's own customers are using it. We set those up with the owner, screen by screen, before we leave.

When Nest is the wrong tool

The flex and warehouse buildings along Route 110 and inside the Hauppauge park, the office towers at the Nassau Hub, and the larger stores around Roosevelt Field usually need cameras that record continuously to a local recorder, cover large areas with many cameras and do not depend on one Wi-Fi network. Nest cameras record events to Google's cloud, and without a plan the event history is short. Security.org reports three hours of event video without a subscription for the Nest Cam (battery) and Floodlight, and six hours for the wired indoor and outdoor cameras. For a business that needs weeks of footage from a dozen angles, we will suggest a wired system instead and say why. For a two-door shop that wants alerts and a quick look, Nest can be exactly right.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Licensing. The NYS Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and New York's rules under 19 NYCRR 195.2 bring CCTV used for detecting or monitoring intrusion or theft within that licensing. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • County and town licensing. Nassau County's Office of Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement contractors and fingerprints them; the county says work only on commercial buildings is exempt. Suffolk County Code Chapter 563 licenses regulated trades, except in towns and villages with their own licensing.
  • Floodlights and new outlets. A floodlight camera connects to line voltage, and a new outdoor outlet is new electrical work. Both are a licensed electrician's job, and the quote says so.
  • Audio. Justia's New York answer advises disabling audio unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation. In a shop full of customer conversations, we set Nest cameras up with audio recording off unless the owner has taken advice and decides otherwise.
  • Privacy areas. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy. Restrooms and changing areas never get cameras.
  • Registries. Suffolk businesses can register camera locations with SCPD for free; the Garden City Police Department runs its own voluntary private camera registry. Neither gives police access to the cameras.

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes Outside a Long Island Ranch

We do not publish stories about a customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at the kind of house Long Island is full of: a long slab ranch, a doorbell button at a door with no porch roof, a chime somewhere in the hall, and a box of Nest devices on the kitchen table.

We leave the boxes closed and walk the outside first. We stand at the door and look at the button and the trim. We find the chime and take its cover off with the doorbell circuit off. We look for the transformer, which in a house with no basement might be in the furnace closet, the attic or behind a panel in the garage, and read its label. We put a meter on the bell wires and see what voltage actually arrives at the door. Then we walk to every camera spot with a phone and check the Wi-Fi there, at the height the camera will sit. We look up at each eave and find the nearest attic access. We note every outdoor outlet and every vent. Only after that walk do we sit down with you and say: this doorbell will run on your wiring, or it will not; this camera will reach the Wi-Fi, or it needs help; this is the route the power will take. In houses this age, that walk is what separates a doorbell that works in February from one that goes dark in the first freeze.

Your Questions Answered

Google Nest Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Doorbells, transformers and chimes

Our ranch still has the small doorbell transformer that came with the house. Is 16 volts at 10VA why the wired Nest Doorbell keeps restarting?

It may well be. Google's store page lists a 16–24VAC, 10–40VA transformer for the wired Nest Doorbell, so a 16V, 10VA unit sits at the very bottom of the range. On the Google Nest Community forum, many owners of the 2nd-gen wired doorbell report that a 10VA transformer was not enough and that upgrading to a 30VA transformer fixed it. In an older Long Island house the real voltage at the door can also be lower than the label says, because of long or tired bell wire. We measure the voltage at the doorbell under load, check the transformer's label, and if it is marginal we recommend a higher-rated transformer before we hang the doorbell. Changing a transformer that is hard-wired into a junction box is line-voltage work, and we tell you when that needs an electrician.

We upgraded the transformer. Does our twenty-year-old chime have to go as well?

Not automatically. That exact question appears on the Google Nest Community forum, and the answer depends on the chime type. Many mechanical chimes work with a Nest Doorbell; some older or unusual ones buzz, hum or ring weakly once the doorbell is drawing constant power through the circuit. Electronic chimes are a separate case and are sometimes the ones that cause trouble. We test the chime with the new transformer and the doorbell connected. If it rings cleanly, it stays. If it hums or fails, we show you and talk through a replacement chime or a setup where the doorbell announces through a Google speaker instead.

What has to be in place at the door before a wired Nest Doorbell (3rd gen) can go up?

Google's own answer is a doorbell transformer in the 16–24VAC, 10–40VA range and a chime, both sold separately from the doorbell. For houses without existing doorbell wires or the right transformer, Google points to its Indoor Power Adapter or to the battery Nest Doorbell. On Long Island that means three checks at most doors: is there bell wire at the button, does the transformer meet the range, and does the chime work with it. We also look at the trim, because the original door casings on many 1950s houses are narrow or rounded, and a doorbell needs a flat, solid place to sit with a seal around the wire hole.

Is one doorbell camera enough to stop packages walking off a Long Island stoop?

One camera rarely tells the whole story. The Massapequa Park footage News 12 aired shows why: the thief was in frame, but a doorbell at the door sees faces up close and very little of the street, the car or which way the person went. A blog FAQ on package theft asks the same question and argues for more than one view. On a typical Long Island house we suggest the doorbell for the face at the door, plus one camera at a corner or the garage that sees the walk, the driveway and the street. A camera is also not a lock; a delivery box or a pick-up point does more to keep packages, and the camera is there to show you what happened.

Cold, power and wiring

Our battery Nest Cam stopped charging in January. Is the camera broken?

Probably not. Google Home Help says Nest battery models have a minimum charging temperature of 32°F, so on a freezing Long Island morning a battery camera running on its own cell will drain and not refill until it warms up. TechHive reported owners complaining about exactly this. The same Google page says a Nest Cam (battery) on wired power can operate down to −4°F. So the fix for a camera that matters in winter is wired power: the Nest Cam weatherproof cable to an outlet, routed through a vent or a sealed hole. Google's installer blog makes the same point about doorbells, saying the wired model is a better fit where temperatures are likely to drop below freezing.

With no basement under our slab house, how does an outdoor Nest Cam get its power?

From above or from outside, never from below. Google says the power cable can run to an outdoor outlet, or to an outlet inside the home through an existing vent or hole. In a Levitt-era slab house, the most common route is up through a closet ceiling into the attic, along the attic to the eave, and down behind the soffit to the camera. Where there is no attic over the spot, a short sealed run on the outside wall to an outdoor outlet is the next choice. If no outlet is close, an electrician adds one. What we never do is drill through or into the slab, because Levitt slabs were poured with heating coils inside them.

Police, privacy and the law

Does registering our cameras with Suffolk police cost anything, and can they watch them?

It costs nothing, and no, they cannot watch. On SCPD's Nextdoor post about the program, a resident asked how much it costs and the answer was "Nothing." SCPD says the program lets citizens and business owners register the locations of their video systems and that the department does not have access to the cameras. SCPD SHIELD describes the department asking participants to check their video for a particular date and time, with retrieval at the owner's convenience. Suffolk's separate alarm registration charges a fee, but that covers alarm systems, not cameras. We found no county-wide camera registry run by Nassau County police; Garden City runs its own.

In New York, can our Nest Doorbell record the conversations of people walking past?

Be careful with audio. A New York answer on Justia advises disabling audio recording unless you know a consenting participant, such as you, will be part of any recorded conversation. Video of your own front door is a different matter from recording the talk of people on the sidewalk. On Long Island houses close to the street, especially capes set near the sidewalk, we show you where the audio setting lives and set it the way you decide after reading that guidance. We are not lawyers, and for a specific situation you should ask one.

What if our backyard camera also sees into the neighbor's yard or window?

Aim it away and mask what remains. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and an installer guide recommends digital privacy masking on exterior cameras so you do not capture a neighbor's backyard. On quarter-acre Levitt lots, houses are close, and a camera aimed down the side yard can easily see a neighbor's bedroom window. We aim each camera from live view with you, adjust activity zones, and where the Nest app's settings allow it, keep the neighbor's windows out of what is recorded.

Does whoever installs cameras on Long Island need a New York State license?

For security camera work done as a business, it is worth checking. New York General Business Law Article 6-D says no person shall engage in the business of installing security or fire alarm systems without a license, while noting that passive, non-detecting devices do not require one, and the state's rules bring CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion or theft into that licensing. A homeowner installing their own camera is not running a business. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and on top of that Nassau and Suffolk have their own contractor licensing for home improvement work.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Google Nest Camera Installation in Long Island

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or the People Also Ask boxes for Long Island Nest searches from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Nest cameras in general, set against what Google's own pages, local reporting and our field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers; they help with a rough idea, but they are not a quote for a 1955 ranch in Nassau.

"Any doorbell transformer will run a wired Nest Doorbell."

Many quick answers treat existing bell wiring as a green light. Google's own requirement is a 16–24VAC transformer rated 10–40VA, and owners on the Google Nest Community forum report that 10VA was not enough for the 2nd-gen wired doorbell until they moved to 30VA.

On Long Island, where the median Nassau house dates to 1955, the transformer on the bell circuit has often been there for decades or was replaced with whatever was cheapest. A transformer at the bottom of the range, plus long old bell wire, can leave the doorbell rebooting, dropping offline or making the chime hum.

The fix starts with a meter, not a guess. We measure the voltage at the door, read the label, and recommend an upgrade, the Indoor Power Adapter or the battery model before the doorbell goes on the trim.

"Battery Nest cameras work fine outdoors all year."

The cameras keep running for a while, but they will not recharge in the cold. Google says the minimum charging temperature for its battery models is 32°F. TechHive reported owners on Reddit complaining that their Nest doorbells and cameras would not charge in freezing weather.

Long Island winters routinely bring nights below freezing, and some of the most important cameras, on a north-facing driveway or a seasonal house on the East End, sit in the coldest spots.

Google's own answer is wired power: a Nest Cam (battery) on wired power works down to −4°F, and Google's installer blog recommends the wired doorbell where temperatures drop below freezing. We plan wired power for any camera you cannot afford to lose in February.

"Just drill through the floor and run the cable underneath."

Advice written for houses with basements tells people to drop cable through the floor and run it below. In a Levitt-era house there is no below. The Living in L-Town report describes slab-on-grade houses with no basements.

Worse, those slabs were poured with copper heating coils inside them, and Heating Help says only about five percent of the houses still have their original radiant systems. That means you cannot tell from the surface whether the pipes in a given slab are live.

In slab houses we route up through the attic, through an existing vent or hole as Google suggests, or along a sealed exterior run. The floor stays untouched, every time.

"A Nest camera will deter thieves on its own."

Many answers imply that a visible camera solves package theft. The Long Island record says otherwise. News 12 aired a Massapequa Park doorbell video of a thief taking a package in plain sight of the homeowners and a UPS driver, and CBS New York reported a Patchogue thief waving at a camera on Rider Avenue.

A Central Islip man was caught on video using a knife to destroy a doorbell camera, according to ABC7. Cameras get seen, ignored and sometimes attacked.

What a well-placed Nest system does is show you, in real time and afterward, what happened at the door and in the driveway. Pair it with a delivery plan, good lighting and a second view of the street, and set expectations honestly, as that Long Island Patch reader did.

"Wi-Fi cameras can't be interfered with."

Quick answers rarely mention that Wi-Fi can be disrupted. Spectrum News reported in December 2025 that New York authorities warned some package thieves use Wi-Fi jammers to disrupt doorbell camera footage.

A Nest camera relies on Wi-Fi to send events to Google's cloud. If the signal is already weak at a garage corner or the far end of a ranch, it takes very little to knock it out.

We cannot make any Wi-Fi camera jam-proof, and we will not say we can. What we can do is make sure the signal at each camera is strong, add access points where it is not, prefer wired power so the camera never sits on a low battery, and tell you plainly when a wired recording system would suit a property better.

"You still need Nest Aware to record anything."

Older articles, and answers trained on them, still talk about Nest Aware. 9to5Google reported in October 2025 that Google Home Premium, in Standard and Advanced tiers, has taken its place.

Without any plan, Nest cameras still record short event clips. Security.org puts the free event history at three hours for the Nest Cam (battery) and Floodlight and six hours for the wired indoor and outdoor cameras.

So the honest answer is that you can use Nest cameras without a plan, but you get a short window of history. Google sets and changes the plan prices; we show you what each tier adds in the Home app and let you decide.

"One price covers Nest installation anywhere around New York."

National cost guides and AI answers tend to give a single range for the whole region. Our own Nest price list varies by area. Nassau addresses are priced at 1.15 times our city base and Suffolk at 1.20 times, reflecting the drive and the spread of the two counties.

Within a Long Island job, the devices, the power routes and the Wi-Fi change the total more than the county does. A battery doorbell and one indoor camera is a different job from a wired doorbell with a transformer upgrade, two attic-fed outdoor cameras and an access point for the garage.

That is why our price pages list each line separately, and why this page does not print any number that is not on our verified list.

+
