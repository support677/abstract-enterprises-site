The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Properties Need eufy Camera Installation

Brooklyn was a city before it was a borough. The Dutch West India Company chartered the village of Breuckelen in 1646, and the old town of Brooklyn was one of six in Kings County, alongside Bushwick, Flatbush, Flatlands, Gravesend and New Utrecht. It joined New York City on January 1, 1898, and by the 2020 census it held 2,736,074 people, more than any other borough. What matters for a camera installer is what those people live in. The Census Bureau's 2023 survey for Kings County counts 175,805 homes in two-unit buildings and 170,655 in three- and four-unit buildings, so roughly 346,000 households live in small multi-family houses. Another 93,812 live in one-family attached houses, the classic rowhouse, and only 52,359 in detached houses. Put simply, most Brooklyn front doors open onto a stoop, a shared vestibule or a sidewalk within a few feet of a neighbor's door, and most back windows look across a yard at the back of the next row.

Those houses are old. The same survey puts the median Kings County home at 1943, with more than half a million built in 1939 or earlier. The brownstone belt, which covers Brooklyn Heights, Stuyvesant Heights, Fort Greene, Park Slope, Carroll Gardens, Cobble Hill, Boerum Hill, Clinton Hill and Vinegar Hill, is the famous part, but Brownstoner's architectural history makes a point that changes how we mount a camera: most brownstones are really brick houses with a four- to six-inch facing of brownstone on the street front. Behind that soft stone, and along every side and rear wall, is brick. In Bay Ridge, Bensonhurst, Sunset Park, Flatbush and East Flatbush the fronts are often brick or limestone without the brownstone skin, and in Ditmas Park, Prospect Park South and Kensington, which were laid out as suburbs of detached houses, wood frame and clapboard come back. A eufy camera that simply sticks to vinyl siding on Long Island needs a masonry bit, a sleeve anchor and a careful choice of joint in most of Brooklyn.

The reasons people call are just as local. CBS New York has run footage of package thieves on an apartment building on Eldert Street in Bushwick, inside a lobby on Lawton Street, and walking up the steps of a house near Montgomery Street and New York Avenue in Crown Heights. In Cypress Hills, a homeowner on Crescent Street between Atlantic Avenue and Fulton Street was slashed by a package thief. News 12 Brooklyn reported in December 2025 that around 90,000 packages are stolen across the city every day, using a city Department of Transportation figure, and Security.org's national survey found apartment residents more than three times as likely as single-family residents to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent. The New York Attorney General's own advice to residents is to use a doorbell camera or video system to deter thieves or help police identify them. In a borough of stoops and shared vestibules, that advice lands on a lot of doors.

The back of the house matters as much as the front. In June 2018 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that police were looking for a man linked to at least a dozen burglaries in the 61st and 62nd Precincts, covering Bensonhurst and Gravesend, since August 2017, and that in every known case he had forced open a window at the rear of the house. A CBS report from October 2019 described two people burglarizing a Bushwick home near Central Avenue and George Street while the resident was in the backyard, and the incident was caught on surveillance footage. A rear yard in attached Brooklyn housing is dark, enclosed by fences and the backs of other houses, and invisible from the street. A battery eufy camera is one of the cheapest ways to watch it, but it is also where the HomeBase signal has the furthest to travel through brick, and where a solar panel may see only a few hours of sun between the walls.

Then there is trust. In January 2025, Attorney General Letitia James announced that three companies distributing eufy cameras would pay $450,000 after her office found that, in certain situations, video sent over the internet from eufy products was not protected by end-to-end encryption and at least part of the connection used no encryption at all; one outlet reported a $640,000 figure, so the reports differ. The distributors agreed to proper encryption, regular penetration testing and a vulnerability management program. Earlier, Tom's Guide reported eufy owners seeing live and recorded feeds from strangers' cameras. And on March 2, 2026, Representative Elise Stefanik and Senator Rick Scott asked the FCC and the Commerce Department for a formal investigation into Anker, eufy's parent company. None of that means a eufy camera cannot be set up responsibly, but it does mean the settings matter, and Mozilla's privacy review notes that on at least one indoor model, end-to-end encryption for cloud video is available but not on by default. Turning it on is part of our installation, not an afterthought.

Six Brooklyn facts that shape a eufy installation before we pick a mount

  • Small multi-family buildings dominate. About 346,000 Kings County homes are in two- to four-unit buildings, by the 2023 census survey. In those buildings the front door, the vestibule and the yard are usually shared, so a camera aimed there affects more than one household.
  • More than half a million homes predate 1940. With 513,629 units built in 1939 or earlier, solid brick behind plaster or brownstone is the wall we expect first, and a camera bracket has to go into the brick, not the soft mortar between bricks.
  • Brooklyn holds the most landmarked buildings in the city. LPC's 2018 newsletter put the borough at nearly 15,900 designated buildings and sites, more than any other.   • Outdoor outlets are scarce. eufy's plug-in floodlight runs on an ordinary three-prong cord, but it still needs an outdoor outlet within reach, and those are rare on the bare brick back walls of Brooklyn yards and alleys.

Same-Day Repair in Brooklyn

Need Repair in Brooklyn? A eufy Camera That Went Offline, Fell Off the Brick, or Stopped Recording the Back Yard

The eufy repair calls we get from Brooklyn tend to fall into four kinds, and the buildings explain most of them. The first is a camera that keeps dropping off the app. In a rowhouse, that is usually a signal problem rather than a broken camera: the HomeBase or router is on one floor, the camera is on the outside of a solid brick wall two floors away, and on a cold or wet evening the link fades just enough to disconnect. Moving the HomeBase, adding a properly placed extender or swapping to a wired unit at that position usually fixes it without buying a new camera. The second is a camera on the ground under its bracket. Adhesive pads that held on a smooth painted wall in spring let go on rough, cold brick in winter, and plastic plugs pushed into a mortar joint work loose as the joint crumbles around them.

The third kind is a wired doorbell that never worked, or worked for a week. We often find it connected to a building buzzer line, which eufy says its wired doorbell cannot use, or to an old doorbell transformer too weak for the 16 to 24 volts and 30VA the doorbell needs. The fourth is a system that records but nobody can see: the person who set it up moved out, the account belongs to a former tenant or a relative, and the current owner cannot sign in. For all four, call (347) 934-8335 or send a photo of the camera and the app screen on WhatsApp. A setup-only visit for equipment you already own is $225 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks, on our list, and if the fix needs a new mount on masonry we tell you the wall add before we start. If a camera has fallen, do not stick it back on with new pads until we have looked at why the first ones failed; the same wall will usually drop it again.

Building Specialists

eufy Camera Installation for Brooklyn's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Brooklyn eufy call. They differ in what the outside walls are made of, how far a wireless signal has to travel from the router to the camera, and who besides you gets a say in what goes on the front. Those three things settle the plan long before anyone compares models.

The brownstone rowhouse

A brick house with a soft stone face, a high stoop, a garden floor below and a yard behind. Often inside a historic district, often with the router on the parlor floor.

The brick two-family

The single most common small building in the borough by census count. Two households share the street door, sometimes the vestibule, and nearly always the yard.

The three- and four-family house

Stacked flats with a shared entrance and a buzzer panel, common in Bushwick, Ridgewood's Brooklyn edge, Sunset Park and Bay Ridge. The owner may live in one unit.

The walk-up apartment building

Prewar buildings of five or six stories with a lobby, mailboxes, a cellar and rear yard. Common areas belong to the landlord, and many tenants want a camera on their own door.

The detached house with a driveway

Ditmas Park, Prospect Park South and Kensington's frame houses, and the detached and semi-detached homes of Mill Basin and southern Brooklyn, with driveways and side yards.

The new condo or rental

Towers and mid-rises in Downtown Brooklyn, Williamsburg, Greenpoint and Gowanus, with glass, concrete and a management office that controls every door but your own.

The brownstone rowhouse: soft stone in front, brick everywhere else

A brownstone is the building where the wrong drill bit does the most damage. Brownstone is a sandstone that weathers and flakes, and on most Brooklyn rowhouses it is only a facing a few inches thick over a brick structure, which is why we do not anchor a camera into the face of the stone if there is any alternative. On the front, the usual answer is a doorbell at the door frame or the vestibule, which needs no masonry fixing at all, and a camera tucked under the stoop, inside the areaway or at the door opening where it is hard to see from the street. The rear wall, by contrast, is almost always plain brick, which holds an anchor very well when the hole goes into the brick itself. That front-and-back split also matches what several Brooklyn installers describe for landmarked houses: cameras concealed at the rear, cabling through the yard, the interior stair or the roof bulkhead, and a single discreet unit, often a doorbell, at the front stoop.

Inside, the brownstone works against wireless. The router usually sits on the parlor floor, the rear camera hangs off the back wall of the garden floor or the floor above, and between them are plaster walls, a brick bearing wall or two and a floor. We test the signal at each camera position with a meter before choosing between a HomeBase on the parlor floor, a HomeBase closer to the back of the house, or a short wired run for the hardest spot. If the house is in one of Brooklyn's historic districts, the plan for the front is discussed before the visit, because the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules cover cameras and intercom panels on a primary façade, and we would rather put the front camera in a place that needs no application than in one that does.

The brick two-family: two households, one stoop, one yard

Brooklyn's two-family houses hold about 175,805 homes by the 2023 census count, and almost every eufy question in them comes back to sharing. The street door, the stoop and the yard usually serve both units, so a doorbell at the street door records the upstairs tenant's visitors as well as the owner's. That is not a reason to skip it; it is a reason to decide, before installation, who gets access to which camera in the eufy app and whether the tenant is told. We set up the owner's account as the main account, share only the cameras each person should see, and leave a short written note of who has access to what. If the tenant has their own entrance, such as a garden-floor door under the stoop, that door can have its own doorbell on the tenant's own account.

The construction of a two-family is usually simpler than a brownstone: brick front or a limestone or brick facing, brick rear, plaster inside. Front cameras go on the door frame, under the cornice or at the side of the entrance, and the rear camera on the back wall above the yard door. Because the owner often lives downstairs and the tenant upstairs, the HomeBase may end up in the owner's unit on the ground floor, which is close to the yard and the front but far from any upstairs window camera. We place it for the cameras that matter most to the owner and check the signal to the others before deciding.

The three- and four-family house: a buzzer at the door and an owner in the building

Three- and four-unit buildings hold another 170,655 Brooklyn homes, and they are where eufy's doorbell limits show up most. Many have a buzzer panel at the street door wired to each apartment, and eufy states plainly that its wired doorbell does not work with intercom power and signal systems, although the wiring can be reused if the intercom is retired. Few owners want to retire a working intercom for one doorbell, so the usual eufy answer here is a battery doorbell on the owner's own door inside the building, a camera above the street door that watches the stoop and the parcel spot, and the intercom left alone. Where the intercom is already dead and the owner wants it gone, we check the old pair of wires for a proper transformer before reusing them.

The owner of a three- or four-family is also a landlord, and that changes how cameras are aimed. A camera at the street door, in the vestibule or over the mailboxes covers a common area that every tenant uses. Courts and New York's housing agency take any landlord action that could be read as harassment of a rent-stabilized tenant seriously, and hallway footage has been used in stabilized housing disputes. We point common-area cameras at doors, mailboxes and parcels, not at the doors of individual apartments, keep audio off, and suggest the owner tells tenants in writing what the cameras cover. The wall work itself is ordinary brick, and the cellar usually gives an easy indoor route for any wired camera.

The walk-up apartment building: the landlord owns the hallway, the tenant owns the door

In a prewar walk-up we often work for two different customers in the same building: the owner who wants the lobby, mailboxes, cellar and yard watched, and the tenant who wants to see who is outside their own door. For the owner, the job looks like a small commercial system: cameras at the street door and lobby, the mailbox wall where packages pile up, the cellar door and the rear yard, with a HomeBase in a locked closet and wired runs where the brick is too thick for Wi-Fi. For the tenant, the rules are different. Brick Underground's guidance is that a tenant needs the landlord's permission to install equipment in common areas, which includes the hallway outside the apartment, and a doorbell camera on the unit door sits in a gray zone that most landlords tolerate but that a lease's alteration clause technically covers.

So for tenants we usually start with options that touch nothing the landlord owns. An indoor eufy camera facing the inside of the apartment door shows anyone who comes in. A window camera mounted flat against the glass works well on a first- or second-floor front window, as eufy itself recommends, and can watch the stoop and sidewalk. A battery doorbell on the unit door is possible once the landlord agrees, and is easy to remove and patch when you move. The construction of these buildings is masonry outside and often plaster on hollow tile or block inside, which is another reason we prefer not to drill a tenant's hallway walls at all.

The detached house with a driveway: Victorian Flatbush and the southern shore

Brooklyn has only about 52,359 detached homes, but they produce some of our largest eufy jobs. Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park and Kensington were developed as suburbs of freestanding houses, and many are wood frame with clapboard or shingle walls, porches and side driveways leading to a rear garage. In southern Brooklyn, a neighborhood guide to Mill Basin describes mostly detached and semi-detached homes, many with driveways, private outdoor space or waterfront access near Jamaica Bay. These houses need more cameras, cover longer distances and have the daylight that battery and solar cameras need, which makes eufy's wireless line a better fit here than on a narrow rowhouse.

The challenge in a detached house is distance rather than brick. A camera on a rear garage may be fifty feet or more from the router, through an exterior wall and across open air, and a camera on the far side of a porch roof may lose sight of the HomeBase behind a chimney. We plan the HomeBase position for the outermost cameras, not the nearest, and test the signal at the garage before mounting. Frame walls are easy to fix into, so the wall add-ons that apply to brick usually do not apply here, but porches and trim boards mean we choose fixing points carefully so a camera does not end up on a rotting piece of fascia. If the house is in the Ditmas Park Historic District, designated in 1981, the front is handled as a landmark front.

The new condo or rental: glass, concrete and a management office

Since the 2004 Downtown Brooklyn rezoning, the Downtown Brooklyn Partnership counts more than 22,000 new homes in that district alone. The Greenpoint and Williamsburg waterfront was rezoned in May 2005, and Greenpointers reported that 2,306 units were added in that community board in 2023, the most in Brooklyn that year. Gowanus followed with its neighborhood plan adopted on November 23, 2021. Tenants and owners in these buildings almost never control a façade or a hallway wall, and the building usually has its own camera system in the lobby, garage and amenity spaces. The eufy question here is nearly always the apartment door and the inside of the apartment.

That makes these jobs quick and mostly no-drill: an indoor camera facing the door, a battery doorbell on the unit door where the condo board or management allows, and a careful Wi-Fi setup in apartments where concrete floors and glass walls do strange things to signal. A board or management office may ask for a certificate of insurance and set working hours, and we send the certificate before the visit. If the building has a rule against anything on the corridor side of the door, we stop at the inside of the apartment and say so plainly, rather than mounting something that will be removed by the super the next morning.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes at a Brooklyn eufy Job

These notes describe how our technicians start a eufy installation in Brooklyn's attached houses. They are our routine, not a story about any one address.

Walk to the back fence first. Before unpacking anything, the technician goes through the house to the rear yard and looks back at the building. The rear wall is where the trouble usually is: the furthest point from the router, the wall with the least light for a solar panel, and the side of the house that faces twenty or thirty other windows across the yards. Standing at the fence shows what the camera will see and what the neighbors will see of it.

Find the router and the power. Next comes the router, usually in the front room of the parlor floor, and every outlet near the front door and the yard door. A eufy plan in a rowhouse is built backwards from those two things. If there is no outdoor outlet in the yard, a plug-in floodlight is off the table unless an electrician adds one.

Meter the signal at each spot. With the HomeBase or the router in its proposed position, the technician stands where each camera will hang and checks the signal on the phone. Brick party walls and floors make the numbers drop fast, and a camera that connects in the yard at noon may not hold on a wet night. If the number is marginal, the HomeBase moves or the plan changes before any drilling.

Read the stoop and the door. At the front, the technician opens the doorbell or buzzer button and looks at what is behind it. A pair of thin wires to a chime and a small transformer is a doorbell. A multi-wire cable to a panel in the vestibule is an intercom, and eufy's wired doorbell will not run on it.

Mark, check on the phone, then drill. Each camera position is marked with tape, the camera is held there and the live view checked, and only then does the masonry bit come out. Moving tape costs nothing. Moving a hole in a hundred-year-old brick does.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Brownstone, Mortar and Siding: How a eufy Camera Is Fixed to a Brooklyn Wall

eufy cameras are light. A doorbell or a battery camera weighs very little compared with a TV, and that leads many people to treat the mount as an afterthought. The problem is not the weight; it is time, weather and hands. A camera on a Brooklyn stoop gets pulled, knocked by deliveries and soaked by winter rain that freezes in every crack. A camera on a rear wall is baked by summer sun and frozen in January. The fixing has to survive that for years, on walls that are often a century old.

Solid brick. The rear and side walls of almost every Brooklyn rowhouse, two-family and walk-up are brick, and brick is an excellent base when the anchor goes into the brick itself. The common mistake is to drill the mortar joint because it is softer and easier. Mortar in older Brooklyn walls is often crumbly lime mortar, and a plug in a joint works loose as the joint erodes around it. We drill into the face of the brick with a masonry bit, keep the hole away from the brick's edges, and use anchors sized for the bracket. On our list, a brick or stone wall add is $65 where it applies, because the work needs a hammer drill, the right bits and more time than a wood wall.

Brownstone facing. Brownstone is soft, layered sandstone, often only four to six inches thick over the brick behind it. Drilling it risks spalling, where the face flakes away around the hole, and a damaged brownstone face is expensive to repair. On a brownstone front we prefer fixing points that avoid the stone entirely: the door frame, the vestibule, the underside of the stoop or the areaway. If a fixing into masonry is unavoidable, we look for a mortar joint in a part of the front where the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidance favors it, and we discuss that with you before the visit, especially in a historic district.

Mortar joints, when they are the right choice. There is one situation where the joint is preferred: when the masonry itself is historic and the goal is to make the fixing reversible. LPC's guidance for landmarked buildings generally favors attaching equipment through mortar joints so a later removal can be repaired by repointing rather than by replacing stone. That is a preservation choice, and on a landmarked front we follow it, using anchors suited to the joint and checking the mortar is sound before trusting it.

Plaster inside. Indoor eufy cameras usually sit on a shelf, but some customers want one high in a corner or over the apartment door, and in a prewar Brooklyn building that wall is plaster on lath, plaster on hollow tile, or plaster on brick. A plaster wall add is $45 on our list where we mount into it. Plaster cracks if it is drilled fast, and a plastic plug in plaster alone loosens over time, so we use a slow pilot hole and an anchor that reaches the framing or masonry behind.

Wood siding and trim. In Ditmas Park, Kensington and the frame houses of southern Brooklyn, siding and trim are easy to fix into, but the timber behind a porch ceiling or a fascia board is sometimes rotten. We probe the wood before trusting it, use stainless screws that will not rust streaks down painted siding, and avoid mounting on thin trim that flexes in wind.

Adhesive and clamp mounts. eufy sells adhesive and clamp options and says they can work on brick without drilling, while still recommending drilling for a more secure installation. We agree with both halves. Adhesive can be the right answer for a renter who cannot drill, on a smooth, clean, dry surface such as a painted window frame or the inside of glass. On rough, cold, dusty brick outdoors, adhesive is the most common reason a camera ends up on the ground.

Getting power to a eufy camera on a bare brick wall

Most eufy outdoor cameras are battery powered, which is why they are popular in Brooklyn: no cable has to come through the wall. The trade-off is charging. A camera that faces a busy sidewalk wakes up every time someone walks past, and a battery that would last months in a quiet yard can drain much faster on a stoop facing a commercial street. Solar panels help where the light reaches, which on a north-facing rear wall between tall rows may be only a short part of the day. We test where the sun actually lands before placing a panel, and trim motion zones so a camera is not waking for every passer-by.

Wired doorbells need a transformer delivering 16 to 24 volts AC at 30VA or more, according to eufy. Older Brooklyn doorbells sometimes have transformers below that rating, and walk-ups often have buzzer lines instead of doorbell circuits. Hardwired floodlights need a weatherproof junction box with 120-volt power, and if there is no fixture there already, eufy says a qualified electrician must install the box and wire. Our license covers the camera and network side; we do not run new 120-volt circuits. Plug-in floodlights need an outdoor outlet, and if your yard has none, that is a conversation with an electrician or a reason to choose a battery camera with a solar panel instead.

Renters, Owners and Landmarks

Leases, Boards and the Landmarks Commission: Who Has a Say in a Brooklyn eufy Camera

In most of the borough, the person who wants a camera and the person who owns the wall are not the same. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what public guidance says and what we have learned from doing this work, so you can ask the right question before booking.

Tenants and their own door. Brick Underground's guidance is that tenants need the landlord's permission to install equipment in the common areas of a building, and that the hallway and the area outside your apartment count as common areas. A doorbell camera on the unit door itself is, as one New York security firm puts it, a gray area: generally tolerated by most landlords, but technically subject to whatever the lease says about alterations. The practical path is to read the alteration clause, ask in writing, and say exactly what the device is and how it is mounted. A battery doorbell held by two small screws and easy to remove is a much easier request than a wired unit.

Landlords and common areas. If you own a two-family, three-family or walk-up, the common areas are yours to protect, and cameras on the street door, lobby, mailboxes and yard are common. But the way they are aimed matters, especially in rent-stabilized buildings. One New York firm notes that any landlord action that could be construed as harassment of a stabilized tenant is taken seriously by the courts and by the state housing agency, and another notes that hallway footage has been used in stabilized housing disputes. In 2018 a Brooklyn landlord's attempt to put a facial recognition camera at the entrance of a rent-stabilized building drew tenant opposition, and New York City Council materials say the landlord backed off. Brownsville tenants fought a similar facial recognition entry plan at two brick high-rises in 2019. Cameras that watch doors, mailboxes and parcels, without audio and without face matching, are the version that keeps the building safer without starting a fight.

Notice and signs. New York's Penal Law section 250.65 exempts security systems from the state's unlawful surveillance offense where a written notice is conspicuously posted or the cameras are installed so they are clearly and immediately obvious. For a building owner, a small sign at the entrance and cameras that are visible rather than hidden is the simplest way to be on the right side of that.

Co-ops and condos. Boards and managing agents usually have house rules covering anything mounted on the corridor side of an apartment door and any contractor working in the building. They may ask for a certificate of insurance and limit working hours. We send the certificate before the visit, and if the board allows nothing outside the door, we keep the camera inside.

Landmarked fronts. Brooklyn has more landmarked buildings than any other borough, nearly 15,900 by LPC's 2018 count, out of 38,119 buildings and sites citywide. The districts include Brooklyn Heights, the city's first, designated November 23, 1965, which runs from Atlantic Avenue to Fulton Street and from the East River to Court Street with more than 600 buildings; Stuyvesant Heights (1971); Park Slope (1973, extended in 2012); Carroll Gardens (September 25, 1973); Fort Greene (1978); Ditmas Park (1981); DUMBO (2007); Prospect Heights (2009); and Willoughby-Hart on Willoughby Avenue and Hart Street between Nostrand and Marcy Avenues (2024). LPC's Rule 2-20 treats security cameras, lighting and intercom panels as common safety equipment, and its 2018 rule amendments would generally prohibit installing many of these objects on a primary façade unless there is no feasible alternative or another agency requires the location. One security vendor describes a limit of two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings; we have not checked that against the rule text, so we treat it as a vendor's summary.

What that means in practice is that the rear of a landmarked house, which is usually not visible from the street, is far easier than the front. LPC's permit guidebook has a section on security cameras, and some changes on a landmarked front, including camera mounts and conduit, may need a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter. We plan landmarked fronts around a doorbell at the door, a camera under the stoop or in the areaway, and nothing new on the stone, and if you want something more visible, we tell you to check with LPC first.

City permits for the camera itself. A Brooklyn security vendor states that low-voltage camera installation in a residential brownstone typically does not need a Department of Buildings permit, and a New York low-voltage guide notes that wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits does not need an electrical permit when done by a qualified person. State law, under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, requires a license for security and CCTV installation, which is what our NYS license #12000287431 covers. New 120-volt wiring for a hardwired floodlight is electrician's work.

Encryption switched on. Mozilla's review of a eufy indoor camera notes that end-to-end encryption for cloud video is offered but not on by default. We set storage so the recordings you care about are kept, and we explain what you lose if the internet goes down, because live viewing and remote access need the app and a connection.

Aim and mask. In attached Brooklyn housing, nearly every rear camera can see into somebody else's windows. eufy Camera Installation in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brooklyn owners, tenants and shopkeepers describe when they call about eufy: stoops and parcels, soft stone and hard brick, yards with no outlet, buildings shared with tenants, and rear windows nobody can see from the street. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Stoops, vestibules and packages

Where should a eufy camera sit on a Brooklyn stoop so it actually catches someone taking a package?

Higher and closer than most people think, and aimed at the spot where boxes land, not at the street. On a stoop, couriers usually leave parcels at the top step, inside the vestibule or against the door, so the camera's job is to see that spot and the face of anyone bending over it. A doorbell at chest height beside the door catches faces well but can miss a box left low and to one side; a camera above the door frame looking down sees the box but only the top of a head. On many Brooklyn stoops the best answer is both, or a doorbell with a downward-facing second lens where the model has one. The CBS footage from Crown Heights showed a thief simply walking up the steps, which is the moment you want in frame. We test the view with a box placed where your deliveries actually go.

Packages pile up in our walk-up's lobby every day. Can a eufy camera watch the mailbox wall without filming tenants' apartment doors?

Yes, and it is one of the most common building-owner requests we get. CBS New York has reported thefts from a lobby on Lawton Street in Bushwick, and the pattern in small walk-ups is the same: boxes stacked by the mailboxes, a street door that does not always latch, and no one watching. A eufy camera high on the lobby wall, aimed at the mailboxes and the inside of the street door, records who comes in and who picks up what. We keep the view off the doors of individual apartments and off the stairs leading to them, leave audio off, and suggest a small notice at the entrance. A wired or plug-in unit is better than a battery here, because a busy lobby triggers motion all day.

Brownstone, brick and mortar

Will drilling for a eufy mount crack the brownstone on the front of our house?

It can, which is why we avoid drilling brownstone wherever there is another option. Brownstone is a soft, layered sandstone, and on most Brooklyn rowhouses it is a facing only four to six inches thick over brick. A hammer drill on a weathered face can spall the stone, leaving a flaked patch around the hole that is costly to restore. On a brownstone front we use the door frame, the vestibule, the underside of the stoop or the areaway, none of which needs a hole in the stone. If the house is in a historic district, those locations are also the ones least likely to need anything from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The brick on the rear and side walls is a different material and takes an anchor well.

Do you fix the bracket into the brick itself or into the mortar line, and how deep does the hole go?

On an ordinary brick rear or side wall, into the brick. Old Brooklyn mortar is often soft lime mortar that crumbles, and a fixing in a joint loosens as the joint wears around it. We drill into the face of a sound brick with a masonry bit, keep away from its edges, and set the hole only as deep as the anchor needs, which for a light eufy bracket is a short depth into a wall that in older Brooklyn houses is much thicker. The exception is a landmarked front, where preservation guidance generally favors fixing through mortar joints so the masonry can be repaired by repointing if the camera is ever removed. In that case we check the joint is sound first. Where the list's brick or stone wall add applies, it is $65.

Is double-sided tape or a clamp strong enough to hold a eufy camera on Brooklyn brick?

Sometimes indoors or on glass, rarely outdoors on brick. eufy says adhesive strips and clamp mounts can work on brick without drilling, and in the same article recommends drilling for a more secure installation. Our experience on Brooklyn rear walls matches the second part: brick is rough, porous and dusty, it gets wet and freezes, and adhesive that grips on day one often lets go after the first cold snap. If you cannot drill, a clamp onto a gutter bracket or a railing you own, or a camera inside a window, is usually more reliable than tape on masonry. If you can drill, a small anchored bracket is cheap insurance against a camera on the ground.

How do you get the cable to a wired eufy camera on a brick rowhouse without it showing on the outside?

By starting indoors and coming out where the camera is. On a Brooklyn rowhouse, the usual routes are through the cellar ceiling to the rear wall, up the inside of the stair to a top-floor window head, or through the roof bulkhead for a camera under the rear cornice. Several Brooklyn installers describe the same approach for landmarked houses: cabling through the rear yard, interior stairwells or roof bulkheads, so nothing new appears on the front. The hole through the brick sits directly behind the camera's base, so the cable is hidden the moment it leaves the wall, and it is sealed against water. Long visible runs of conduit or cable stapled to a façade are what we try hardest to avoid.

Power, batteries and the rear yard

Our rear yard has no outdoor outlet anywhere. Can a plug-in eufy floodlight still go back there?

Not without an outlet, and a long extension cord through a window is not a safe substitute. eufy's plug-in floodlight runs on a standard three-prong cord, which avoids a junction box, but it still needs an outdoor outlet within reach of the cord, and bare brick rear walls in Brooklyn yards and alleys rarely have one. You have three honest choices. An electrician can add a weatherproof outdoor outlet, after which we mount and set up the floodlight. A battery eufy camera with a solar panel can watch the yard without any power at all, though without the floodlight's brightness. Or, if there is already a light fixture on the rear wall on a junction box, a hardwired floodlight may be able to replace it. We look at your wall and tell you which applies.

Our eufy battery camera faces a busy Brooklyn sidewalk. Why does it need charging so often, and what can we change?

Because it is waking up all day. A battery eufy camera sleeps until motion wakes it, records a clip and goes back to sleep, and on a stoop facing a commercial street or a block near a subway stop, people walk past constantly. Each wake-up costs battery, so a camera that would last a long time in a quiet yard drains much faster out front. We reduce the drain by trimming the motion zone to your stoop and door so the sidewalk is ignored, lowering sensitivity, shortening clip length, and turning off features you do not use. If the camera still drains too fast, a solar panel helps where there is light, and a wired or plug-in unit is the real fix for the busiest spots.

Our garden-floor back window faces the yard. Is a camera on the rear wall worth it if nobody can see the back from the street?

That is exactly why it is worth it. The rear of a Brooklyn rowhouse is hidden from the street and from passing police, and the Brooklyn Eagle's 2018 report of a dozen burglaries in Bensonhurst and Gravesend described every known break-in as starting with a forced rear window. A camera on the rear wall, high enough that it cannot be reached from the yard and angled to see the garden-floor windows and the yard door, records anyone who comes over a fence. Pair it with motion zones that stop at your own fence line so the neighbors' yards and windows are not filmed. On a north-facing rear wall, we check where the sun actually falls before relying on a solar panel.

Shared buildings and shared access

We own a two-family. Can our upstairs tenants see the front-door camera too, while the yard camera stays private?

Yes. eufy's app lets the account owner share individual cameras with other people, so the tenant can be given the front-door camera and nothing else. We set up the owner's account as the main account, share only the cameras each household should see, and show both of you how to view clips on your phones. If the tenant later moves out, removing their access takes a moment in the app and does not require changing the owner's password. Can one eufy system mix wired and battery cameras across a small Brooklyn multifamily building?

It can, and for a building with a busy front and a quiet back it often should. One Brooklyn handyman's case study describes a multi-family owner who chose eufy cameras, both wired and wireless, and that matches what we see. Wired or plug-in units belong where motion never stops, such as the lobby, the street door and the mailbox wall. We check each model's compatibility with your HomeBase before you buy, because not every eufy camera pairs with every base.

We own a rent-stabilized walk-up. What should we avoid when aiming eufy cameras at the hallways and doors?

Avoid anything that looks like watching a particular tenant. One New York firm notes that courts and the state housing agency take seriously any landlord action that could be read as harassing a stabilized tenant, and another notes that hallway footage has been used in stabilized housing disputes. Brooklyn eufy Camera Installation FAQ

Can eufy cameras really cut down on package theft at a Brooklyn front door?
+

They help in two ways: a visible camera deters some thieves, and clear footage helps police identify the ones who are not deterred, which is why the New York Attorney General recommends doorbell cameras for package theft. They do not stop every theft, so pair the camera with a delivery note asking couriers to leave parcels inside the vestibule where possible.

We already have eufy cameras up. Can you just fix the settings and the app without remounting anything?
+

Yes. That is a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks on our list. We update firmware, move or re-pair the HomeBase if the signal is weak, switch on encryption and two-step sign-in, trim motion zones and set up sharing.

Do you cover every Brooklyn neighborhood at the same price?

Is there an extra charge when a camera goes on brick?

Your Wi-Fi name and password, access to the email you want the eufy account under, your phone for two-step sign-in, and keys to the yard, cellar and roof if cameras or cable go there. Clear the area under each camera spot.
