The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

East Williamsburg is one of the few places in Brooklyn where people sleep, cook, weld, rehearse, store inventory and throw parties inside the same kind of building. The city's North Brooklyn Industry and Innovation Plan describes the industrial area that runs through Greenpoint, East Williamsburg and Bushwick as 1,066 acres, the third largest industrial hub in New York City by employment, with about 19,500 jobs, 77 percent of them industrial. It also found that the mix is changing: between 2010 and 2016, non-industrial businesses in the study area grew by 219, or 61 percent, and more than a third of new jobs were in offices, retail, arts and entertainment. The plan describes the area's historic loft buildings as generally three to five stories. For fire detection, that mix is the whole story. A detector chosen for an apartment will false-alarm in a woodshop, a detector chosen for a woodshop will sleep through a smoldering mattress, and a building that holds both needs a plan that knows which room is which.

The residential side of that mix has its own history. The Loft Board was created under the 1982 Loft Law, Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law, to oversee the legal conversion of former commercial and manufacturing buildings into what the Board calls safe, rent-stabilized residential units, and its coverage was extended in 2010, 2013, 2015 and 2019. Buildings in that process are interim multiple dwellings, or IMDs. The Loft Board lists smoke and carbon monoxide detectors among the services an IMD owner must provide to protected occupants, alongside heat, hot water, lit stairs and secure doors. Separately, the Multiple Dwelling Law gives the owner eighteen months after the alteration permit to bring the residential parts of the building up to the standards of safety and fire protection in Article 7-B. East Williamsburg has buildings at every stage of that process. In December 2024, PincusCo reported plans filed for 43 residential units to legalize an East Williamsburg IMD, with 57 Thames Street named in its photo caption.

Then there are the ordinary apartment buildings along the avenues, the ones with a store on the ground floor and two floors of apartments above. On Sunday night, May 3, 2026, a fire started at 927 Grand Street, a three-story building with a deli downstairs and apartments upstairs at the corner of Catherine Street, and spread to 931 and 933 Grand Street. North Brooklyn News reported that more than 200 emergency responders came, three firefighters were hurt, 18 residents escaped, and the Department of Buildings ordered 927 demolished the next day. News 12 Brooklyn reported that residents said the fire alarms did not sound. That has not been confirmed, and we do not know what detection the building had. But a resident's words to News 12, "It all happened in like 60 seconds," describe exactly the time a detector exists to buy.

Carbon monoxide is the other half of the job here, and the neighborhood had a hard lesson in it this year. At about 3:30 a.m. on February 18, 2026, firefighters answered reports of smoke from a manhole near 101 Humboldt Street, found high levels of carbon monoxide inside the seven-story building, and later fought a transformer vault fire that burned for hours, ABC7 reported. Nearly 200 people were evacuated. A resident, Ruben Burgos, told the station: "One confusing thing to me was everybody has carbon monoxide tests, not one went off in the building." We explain further down why household CO alarms can stay quiet while a firefighter's meter reads high, and what that means for where detection goes in a building like that.

Interim multiple dwellings: detection owed today, a fire protection standard owed later

An interim multiple dwelling is a building the Loft Law recognizes as housing people while it is still, on paper, a commercial or manufacturing building. The Loft Board's page on owner maintenance is short and direct: the owner of an IMD must provide protected occupants with a list of basic services, and smoke and carbon monoxide detectors are on it, next to heat, hot water, electricity, lighting for public halls and fire stairs, secure entrance doors and window guards. That duty does not wait for legalization. It applies to the building as it is, with its tenant-built rooms, its freight elevator and its long corridors.

The second duty is larger. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 284, an IMD owner files an alteration application, takes the steps to get an approved permit, and then has eighteen months to meet the standards of safety and fire protection in Article 7-B for the residential parts of the building, before going on to a residential certificate of occupancy. The Loft Board says that compliance is certified by a registered architect or professional engineer on the Board's own form, or shown by a temporary certificate of occupancy for the residential units. In practice that means two kinds of fire alarm work can happen in the same building in the same year: the detectors the owner owes the tenants right now, and whatever detection the legalization drawings call for. We do the first directly. On the second, the architect or engineer designs and certifies, and we install, label and test what is on the drawings.

The physical layout of an IMD is what makes detection here unusual. Units were often framed out by the people living in them, sometimes decades ago, so a bedroom may be a sleeping platform behind a partition that stops short of the ceiling, a room with no door, or a space with no window. The floor plan the owner has may not match any of it. So we walk every unit before writing a device list, mark where each person actually sleeps, and place detection for that, not for the drawing. The Multiple Dwelling Law also obliges occupants to give reasonable access for compliance work and the inspections that check it, which helps when a unit is hard to get into, but the smoother route is almost always a written notice with a clear date, a two-hour arrival window and a short explanation of what we will do inside.

Legalized loft conversions: the same duties as any apartment house, in a factory shell

Once a loft building has its residential certificate of occupancy, it stops being a special case for detector purposes and becomes a multiple dwelling like any other. The duties that HPD sets for apartment buildings apply: approved smoke detection near every sleeping room, carbon monoxide detection where fuel burns, notices to occupants, posted notices in the entrance area, and records of when each device went in and when it expires. What does not change is the building. A legal loft still has deep floors, wide open plans, high ceilings, heavy masonry walls and, often, a ground floor still in commercial use.

Two things come up in almost every legalized loft we see. First, the detection that went in during legalization was designed around the units as they were drawn at the time, and owners and tenants have changed things since: a wall added to make a second bedroom, a kitchen moved, an office closed in. Each of those can leave a sleeping room without a detector inside the distance the rules expect. Second, the records from the legalization often sit with the architect who certified the work, not with the building. When we take over a legalized loft, we ask for those drawings and certifications first, then compare them with what is actually on the ceilings, and we give the owner a device list they can keep in the building from then on.

Three-story buildings over stores: a closed business under families who are asleep

The avenue buildings are the neighborhood's oldest housing type, and they are the ones that have been burning. The building where the May 2026 Grand Street fire began was a three-story structure with a deli on the ground floor and apartments above. In January 2025, CBS New York reported a fire on Graham Avenue in three-story buildings with apartments above a pharmacy, restaurants and a salon, which the FDNY chief at the scene described as wood row-frame buildings; it started on the top floor of one and spread to two neighbors, and a resident said, "My smoke alarm went off, I ran outside." Two fires, two lessons: a detector inside the apartment works for the people in that apartment, and attached old buildings let fire travel to the people next door.

The plan for an avenue building has three layers. Inside each apartment, the HPD detectors with their notices and dates. In the shared stair and hall, detection that sounds where every household can hear it, because the stair is the one way out for everyone and the first place smoke from a store fire will collect. And in the store itself, detection chosen for what the store does: a deli's back room with its slicers, fryers and compressors, a salon full of aerosols and dryers, a pharmacy stockroom full of cartons. The store is usually closed from evening to morning, which is exactly when the families upstairs are asleep, so the store's detection has to reach someone, either the apartments directly or a monitoring center that can send the FDNY.

Multi-tenant studio buildings: many leaseholders, one panel, nobody who owns the whole picture

The studio buildings near Morgan Avenue are some of the most recognizable in the neighborhood. Hyperallergic described 56 Bogart Street, across from the Morgan Avenue L stop, as a building with artist studios mainly on the second and third floors, some occupied by the same artists for at least ten years, and galleries mostly on the ground floor. Buildings like that hold dozens of separate leaseholders on a few floors, each with their own hours, tools and habits, and one landlord whose building fire alarm, if it has one, ties all of them together.

The weak point in these buildings is change. Every time a studio is split, joined, closed in with a new wall or turned into an office, the detection pattern on that floor shifts, and the person making the change is usually a tenant's contractor who does not know where the building's devices are or that covering one with a new ceiling matters. Over a few years, a building that was laid out properly can end up with detectors inside closets, detectors removed and never replaced, and zones that no longer describe the floor. We start these jobs by walking the whole building with the landlord's records, if any exist, and writing down every device on every floor with its condition. That list becomes the base for every future change, and it is the one thing that keeps a twenty-tenant building's detection from drifting apart.

Studio buildings also raise the question of who lives there. In much of the industrial zone residential use is not allowed, and the Loft Board notes that units in most industrial business zones are not covered by the Loft Law. Some tenants sleep in their studios anyway. We do not design detection for an illegal residence, and an alarm does not make a studio a legal home. If we see signs that someone is living in a space that is not permitted for it, we tell the owner plainly, because it changes the building's risk and its legal position. We go into more detail on that in the questions section below.

One-story warehouses and shops: big rooms, real work, and stock that burns for a long time

The warehouse blocks around Johnson Avenue, Meserole Street, Gardner Avenue and Varick Avenue are mostly one- and two-story block or steel buildings with high, open roofs, roll-up doors and very little separation inside. The North Brooklyn plan lists the kinds of businesses that operate in buildings like these: manufacturing, wholesale distribution, construction supply, recycling and film and production, plus restaurants and food producers. Each one creates something a smoke detector does not like: welding fumes, sawdust, flour, steam, exhaust from trucks idling at an open door. A detector that goes off every afternoon gets covered with a bag or disconnected, and then the building has no detection at all.

So device choice in a warehouse starts with the work. Heat detection belongs where dust, fumes or steam are part of the day. Smoke detection belongs in offices, storage areas and places where a slow fire in stock would grow before anyone noticed. CO detection belongs near fuel-burning heaters and wherever vehicles run indoors. When a building system is required, the design comes from a registered design professional and goes to the FDNY, and we install to the approved drawings. When it is not required, a smaller system chosen for the space, with monitoring, is often the right answer for a business that sits empty every night. The 2024 Morgan Avenue warehouse fire, where the FDNY called hazmat because lithium-ion batteries were present, is a reminder that what you store also changes what the fire department meets when it arrives, and the alarm is what decides how early they arrive.

Large residential campuses: the building system belongs to the corporation

East Williamsburg also has large residential developments that work very differently from a loft or a walk-up. Lindsay Park, the Mitchell-Lama cooperative described on Wikipedia as seven buildings and 2,702 apartments completed in 1965, is the largest. In developments like that, the building fire alarm system belongs to the corporation or the housing authority, is maintained by their contractors, and is changed only through their management. A private installer does not touch it unless hired by that management.

Inside an individual apartment, the household smoke and CO alarms are a different matter, and shareholders and residents sometimes ask us to look at those. We are glad to, as long as the work stays inside the apartment, follows the corporation's alteration rules, and does not connect to or interfere with the building's system. If a resident reports that something in the halls or the building panel is wrong, the right call is to the development's management, and we will say so.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find Every Bed, Then Find Every Machine

Ask where people sleep, then go and look. In a converted factory, the owner's list of units and the real layout rarely match. Before any device is chosen, the technician walks each unit with whoever lets us in and marks where every person sleeps: bedrooms, sleeping platforms, rooms behind partitions that stop short of the ceiling, a couch in a studio somebody stays in during deadline weeks. Detection is planned for those spots. Nothing else on the survey matters as much.

Then list everything that makes heat, dust or fumes. The second pass is about the work. Kilns, welders, table saws, ovens, fryers, spray booths, laser cutters, 3D printers, banks of battery chargers, and space heaters all go on the sheet with the room they sit in, because each one decides whether a room gets smoke, heat or CO detection and whether a device needs to sit farther from it.

Walk the way out, not just the rooms. We follow the route each unit's occupants would use to leave: the corridor, the stair, the fire escape, the street door. Anything stored in that route goes in the notes, including bikes and charging scooters, because the Loft Board's egress rule bars storage in the means of egress and names lithium battery charging devices, and because detection in a stair is worth little if the stair is blocked.

Sound one alarm with the machines on. Last, with unit doors shut, a second person sounds an alarm at the far end of the floor while the building runs as it normally does: compressors, dust collectors, music, a band rehearsing two doors down. If the alarm cannot be heard clearly in the farthest sleeping spot or the loudest shop, the plan gets another sounder, a strobe or a linked device, whatever the minimum says.

Loft Law Buildings

Where Fire Detection Fits on the Road From Interim Multiple Dwelling to Certificate of Occupancy

Owners of East Williamsburg loft buildings often ask us the same thing in different words: if the building is going to be legalized anyway, why put money into detection now, only to have the architect redo it later? The honest answer is that the Loft Law treats the two as separate obligations on separate clocks, and that the detectors owed today are cheap compared with what happens if a unit goes without them while the paperwork moves. Here is how the pieces line up, in the order an owner meets them.

Right now, before any permit: the owner's maintenance duties. The Loft Board's owner maintenance page lists smoke and carbon monoxide detectors among the services the owner of an IMD must provide to protected occupants, with a reference to the Board's rules at 29 RCNY section 2-04. That applies to the building as it stands. It does not depend on whether an alteration application has been filed, and it does not wait for the legalization drawings. If a protected occupant's unit has no working smoke detector, or has fuel-burning equipment and no CO detector, the gap is the owner's to close now. This is the work we do most often in IMDs: a device in each sleeping area as the tenants have built it, CO where any fuel burns, a dated record for each unit, and a short sheet left with the occupant on how to test it.

The alteration application and permit. Section 284 of the Multiple Dwelling Law sets the sequence for legalization: the owner files an alteration application, takes all reasonable steps to get an approved permit, and from there the clock runs for fire and safety compliance. The plans are prepared by an architect or engineer, and they will show whatever fire protection the residential parts of the building need under Article 7-B and the Building Code. The DOB's code notes for Loft Law projects point to Building Code sections 907, 908 and 909 for smoke and CO alarms and note that a fire alarm application is required if the building has four or more stories. For a five-story loft, that usually means a building system with its own filing. For a three-story loft, the design may rely on unit alarms and other protections instead. That call belongs to the design professional, and we do not make it for them.

Eighteen months for Article 7-B. Once the permit is in place, the Multiple Dwelling Law gives the owner eighteen months to meet the standards of safety and fire protection in Article 7-B for the residential portions of the building. The Loft Board says this phase is certified either by a registered architect or professional engineer on the Board's own compliance form, or by a temporary certificate of occupancy for the residential units, and that one or the other must be filed with the Board. The Board can extend the later occupancy deadline, but the extension provisions in the law are tied to proof of fire and safety compliance, which is one more reason fire protection is the part of legalization owners should not leave for last.

Access during the work. Section 284 also says occupants must give reasonable access for compliance work and for the inspections that verify fire safety compliance, and that refusing a Loft Board access order can be grounds for eviction. We would much rather never get near that point. In practice, the units that are hard to get into are hard because nobody explained what the visit is for, how long it takes, or whether a tenant's work and equipment will be disturbed. We send a plain notice in advance, give a two-hour arrival window, keep each unit visit short, and keep the tenant's space as we found it apart from the device on the ceiling.

Residential certificate of occupancy, and after. At the end of the process the owner obtains a Class A residential certificate of occupancy. From then on the building is a legal multiple dwelling, and the ordinary detector duties for apartment buildings apply: devices near every sleeping room, CO where fuel burns, notices to occupants and in the entrance area, and records of install and expiration dates. The work done during legalization does not end the job. Detectors have useful lives, tenants change layouts, and the building's records have to keep up.

Why it pays to do the first part well. The detectors an owner puts in now are not wasted when the architect's design arrives. Unit detection that is already placed for the real sleeping areas, recorded by unit and room and dated, gives the design professional an accurate picture of the building to start from, and in many buildings the in-unit devices stay where they are while the building-level work is added around them. The expensive version is the opposite: an owner who did nothing, an architect who has to survey every unit from scratch, and a legalization schedule that slips while tenants live without working detection.

927, 931 and 933 Grand Street, May 3, 2026: three buildings from one fire. North Brooklyn News reported that the fire began around 10 p.m. at 927 Grand Street, at the corner of Catherine Street, and spread to 931 and 933 Grand Street. The Department of Buildings found 927 structurally unstable and issued a full demolition order the next day; 931 had significant structural damage and 933 was placed under a full vacate order. 1st Responder News described it as a fire in a multiple dwelling with extension to the exposures that went to a fourth alarm. The decision it points to: in attached avenue buildings, an alarm inside one apartment protects one apartment. Detection in the shared stair and in the store below, sounding where every household will hear it, is what gives the whole building warning, and nobody can count on the building next door having any.

101 Humboldt Street, February 18, 2026: carbon monoxide from under the street. ABC7 reported that firefighters called to smoke from a manhole found high levels of carbon monoxide inside the seven-story building, that a transformer vault fire later scorched two parked cars and burned for about seven hours, and that nearly 200 people were evacuated. An FDNY battalion chief suggested winter salt and utility overload may have compromised wires. A resident said not one of the building's CO alarms went off. The decision it points to: carbon monoxide in a building is not only a boiler question. CO can arrive from outside, and the lowest floors and the cellar, closest to the street and the vaults, are where it shows up first. It also points to a fact about household CO alarms that surprises most people, which we explain in the FAQ: they are built to wait before sounding at lower levels.

254 Boerum Street, September 29, 2025: a construction site fire next to a neighbor. ABC7 reported a two-alarm fire at a construction site at 254 Boerum Street, between Bushwick Place and White Street, that extended to 252 Boerum Street, with 25 units and more than 100 fire and EMS personnel responding and two people treated for minor injuries. The cause was under investigation. The decision it points to: an occupied building beside an active construction site should have its own detection in working order, tested and recorded, and an owner who learns that a neighboring lot is about to be built on is wise to check the side of the building that faces it, including the cellar, the roof space and any windows that open onto the site.

Morgan Avenue near Meserole Street, 2024: batteries in a warehouse. News 12 reported an overnight warehouse fire on Morgan Avenue near Meserole Street in which fire officials requested the FDNY hazmat team because of the presence of lithium-ion batteries. The cause had not been determined. The decision it points to: what a business stores and charges overnight should be on the survey sheet, because it changes which detectors make sense, where charging should happen, and how much warning matters to the people who work there and the firefighters who respond.

Graham Avenue, January 25, 2025: a working alarm and a quick exit. CBS New York reported a fire in three-story wood row-frame buildings on Graham Avenue with apartments above a pharmacy, restaurants and a salon, which started on the top floor of one building and spread to two others, displacing twelve people. One resident told the station, "My smoke alarm went off, I ran outside." The decision it points to: the simplest device on this page, a working smoke alarm near where someone sleeps, is the one that gets people out of an old frame building before the stair fills.

Our Lady of Pompeii, Easter 2024: old buildings and what the code does not require. News 12 reported that the East Williamsburg church burned and that it was not required to have sprinklers, because buildings standing for more than 90 years are exempt from the sprinkler requirement in the 2008 code revision. The decision it points to: in a neighborhood full of old buildings, what the code requires and what the building needs are not always the same. Compliance notes for East Williamsburg businesses and mixed-use buildings

  • Filed systems start with a design professional. The FDNY's tips guide for fire alarm approval says a New York State registered professional engineer or registered architect prepares the plans and completes the filings, and that installation follows plan approval and is carried out by an FDNY-certified fire alarm company. Where your building needs that, we install to the approved drawings alongside the design professional and the company of record.
  • Plan review takes months, not weeks. The same guide says initial review can take up to eight weeks and that the average from plan submission to final inspection and approval is about twenty to twenty-four weeks. A shop or venue planning an opening date should start the design long before it signs for its first delivery.
  • Objections do not always mean a new fee. The guide says no filing fee applies if corrected plans are resubmitted within six months of the objection date.
  • Monitoring comes before inspection. The FDNY guide says the central station monitoring connection with an approved monitoring center must be active before the inspection is scheduled, and that the installation must be 100 percent complete. The inspection is requested with form B-45, and approved plans are brought to the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit in Brooklyn on Tuesdays or Thursdays between 9 a.m. and noon.
  • Adding to an existing system needs its paperwork. The guide says a letter of approval for the existing fire alarm system is needed for additions or modifications, and a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings where there is no certificate of occupancy or approved Schedule A. In a converted factory with a complicated history, that second point comes up often.
  • Loft buildings with four or more stories. The DOB's code notes for Loft Law projects say fire alarm applications are required if the building has four or more stories. Mixed buildings with a commercial ground floor and residential lofts above are designed as a whole, not floor by floor.
  • Exits stay clear. In an IMD, the Loft Board's fire egress rule bars storage in corridors, stairs and other means of egress, names lithium battery charging devices among the things that do not belong near them, and requires the owner to post a lobby notice saying so. A fire alarm plan for a loft building assumes those routes are open.
  • Who is licensed matters. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and its page says the license requires 81 hours of qualifying education, fingerprinting and an exam. AESS works under New York State license #12000287431. We do not service kitchen hoods or their suppression systems, and the 120-volt electrical side of hardwired work is done by a licensed electrician.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in East Williamsburg: Questions Answered

These are the situations loft owners, studio landlords, residents and business owners in East Williamsburg bring to us. Each is answered in full here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Loft Law buildings and legalization

Our building is an interim multiple dwelling and the architect has only just started on the alteration application. Can the smoke and CO detectors go in now, or should they wait for the legalization design?

Put them in now. The Loft Board lists smoke and carbon monoxide detectors among the services an IMD owner must provide to protected occupants, and that duty applies to the building as it stands today, not after the permit. The legalization design is a separate obligation on its own clock: under Multiple Dwelling Law section 284, the eighteen months for Article 7-B fire and safety compliance start from the permit, which may be a long way off. Waiting means tenants live without required detection for that whole stretch, and it leaves the owner exposed if something happens. Doing it now is rarely wasted. We place unit detectors for the sleeping areas as they really exist, record each device by unit and room with its install and expiration dates, and give the owner a list the architect can use as a starting survey. If the eventual design moves a device or adds a building system around it, the in-unit detectors usually stay. The one thing we ask is that the architect sees our list before finalizing drawings, so the two pieces of work fit together instead of overlapping.

Who signs off that the fire protection part of a Loft Law legalization is done, and how is that different from an FDNY letter of approval for a fire alarm?

They are two different sign-offs from two different authorities, and a building may need both. The Loft Board says Article 7-B compliance is certified by a registered architect or professional engineer on a form the Board prescribes, or shown by a temporary certificate of occupancy for the residential units, and that one or the other is filed with the Board. That certification covers the building's fire and safety standards as a whole, of which detection is one part. An FDNY letter of approval is narrower and comes from the fire department: it is the record that a specific fire alarm system was designed, filed, installed, inspected and accepted. The FDNY's tips guide says a letter of approval for the existing system is what you need before anyone can add to or modify it. In a five-story loft where a building system is required, the owner may end up with an FDNY letter of approval for the alarm and an architect's Article 7-B certification for the building. We are not the signer of either. What we provide is the installation and testing record that both the design professional and the FDNY inspection rely on: every device, its location, its test result and its date.

Tenants built the partitions, lofts and kitchens in our units years before we bought the building, and the Loft Law lets the owner buy some of those improvements. Whose detectors are they, and who keeps them working?

For the purposes of the detector duty, treat them as the owner's to provide and maintain, whatever happens to the improvements. The Loft Board's maintenance page puts smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on the list of services the IMD owner must provide to protected occupants, and that does not depend on who paid for the walls they are mounted on. In practice, many IMD units have detectors the occupants bought and put up themselves, sometimes old ionization alarms near the door of a unit whose sleeping area is forty feet away behind a partition. When we survey a unit like that, we leave the tenant's devices in place until new ones are tested, note them on the record, and install owner-provided detection placed for the real layout. If a tenant wants to keep an extra alarm of their own, that is fine, but it does not replace the owner's device. When an owner buys out a unit's improvements, the device list is worth updating at the same time, because a sale is often followed by a renovation that moves walls and sleeping areas.

Our loft building finally has its residential certificate of occupancy. Is the Loft Board still the agency that cares about our detectors, or does that move somewhere else now?

Once a building has finished legalization and holds a residential certificate of occupancy, it is a legal multiple dwelling, and the ordinary detector rules for apartment buildings apply. Those are HPD's rules: approved smoke detection within the distance HPD sets from each sleeping room, carbon monoxide detection where fuel-burning appliances serve the building, natural gas detection where there is gas piping from January 1, 2027, a notice to an adult in each unit when devices are installed, a posted notice in the building's entrance area, and records of install and expiration dates. How long the Loft Board keeps a role for a particular building depends on where it is in the Board's process, which the owner's attorney or the Board itself can confirm. For the fire alarm plan, the practical point is that the building now has the paperwork duties of an apartment house on top of the physical realities of a factory. We help with the physical side and give you the records in the form HPD's rules expect.

The Loft Board makes us inspect and certify our fire escapes and stairs every August. Should the smoke and CO detector check happen at the same time, and does the Board want to see it?

The Loft Board's fire egress rule asks for something specific: that the owner keep fire escapes, corridors and other means of egress in good working order, inspect them annually, and file a certification that they were inspected within 14 days before submission, with any defects repaired and a new certification filed no later than August 30. Detectors are not part of that certification as the rule is written. But combining the two visits makes sense. Both require getting into halls, stairs and sometimes units; both depend on the same access notices; and a detector in a stair hall is part of the same picture as the stair's lighting and clear path. A summer check of every unit's smoke and CO detectors, with dates and results written down, gives the owner a yearly record of the maintenance duty the Loft Board lists separately. If the Board or an inspector asks, that record is there. If not, it still protects the owner, and the people asleep in the building.

Story counts, the industrial zone and how a building is used

We heard that a loft needs a fire alarm application if it has four or more stories. Our building has three. Does that mean we only need apartment-style alarms?

It means the four-story line is not the reason you would need a filed system, but it is not the only reason one can be required. The Department of Buildings' code notes for Loft Law projects list "Fire alarm applications required if four or more stories" alongside the Building Code references for smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, which is why the question comes up so often in a district of three- to five-story lofts. A three-story building can still need a building system because of how it is used, how many people occupy it, whether it has a commercial or assembly space on the ground floor, or what the legalization design calls for. That determination belongs to the architect or engineer working on the building. What a three-story loft certainly needs is the in-unit detection every residential unit is owed, and usually detection in the shared stair, halls and cellar so the households in the building hear a fire that starts outside their own walls. We can install that now, and if the design professional later specifies a building system, we will tell you what of the existing work carries over.

Our building is fully commercial and sits inside the industrial business zone, and one of our studio tenants has started sleeping in the space. What does that do to the fire alarm plan?

It changes the building's risk, and it is a problem a detector cannot solve. The Loft Board says units in most industrial business zones are not covered by the Loft Law, so a studio in a commercial building inside the zone generally cannot become a legal home through that route. Someone sleeping in a space built for work is sleeping in a room that was never planned for it: possibly with one way out, no window anyone could climb through, heaters and equipment running, and detection, if there is any, chosen for a workplace rather than for waking a person up. We do not design detection for an illegal residence, and we will not tell an owner that adding alarms makes the arrangement acceptable. What we will do, if asked to work in the building, is protect the space as what it legally is, and tell the owner in writing that we saw signs of someone living there, because the owner needs to deal with it through the lease and, if necessary, their attorney. The Department of Buildings' 2014 partial vacate of the basement at 255 McKibbin Street, which The Real Deal reported the agency called imminently perilous to life, shows how quickly the city can act when it finds people living where it is not safe.

We rent out our warehouse floor for one-night events a few times a year. Does each event need its own fire safety setup, or does the building's alarm cover it?

An event changes what the building is being used for, and that is a question for the Department of Buildings and the FDNY before it is a question for us. The DOB's place of assembly guide says a place of assembly certificate of operation is required where the certificate of occupancy shows 75 or more members of the public may gather indoors, and a warehouse whose certificate of occupancy describes storage or manufacturing does not become an assembly space because someone rents it for a night. The 2016 vacate orders DNAinfo reported at 111 Gardner Avenue cited, among other things, missing exit signs and emergency lighting, unsafe exits, structures that were not fire-rated and no evacuation plan, which is a fair picture of what inspectors look for. If you are planning events, start with an architect or expediter who can tell you what the building's certificate of occupancy allows and what an event would require. If the answer includes fire alarm work, we can install it to the approved design. What we will not do is set up temporary alarms to make an unpermitted event look covered.

We park our food trucks inside a Johnson Avenue garage overnight, with propane cylinders and generators on board. What detection makes sense for that room?

Plan for heat, not smoke, over the vehicles, and plan for carbon monoxide wherever engines or generators run indoors. A garage that holds trucks sees exhaust every time one starts, and a smoke detector above the parking area will false-alarm on a cold morning when every engine warms up at once. Heat detection reacts to a fire instead of to exhaust. CO detection belongs near any place where a generator or engine runs inside, and anywhere people work in the room, at the height and spacing its listing calls for. Propane adds its own concerns: how many cylinders are stored, where, and under what conditions is governed by the FDNY's rules on fuel gas storage, and an owner should confirm what the building and the FDNY allow before deciding where trucks with cylinders spend the night. If the garage shares a building with offices or studios, detection in the garage should reach those occupants too, so a fire in an empty bay at 3 a.m. is not discovered by the first person who opens the door in the morning. News 12's May 2026 report about generators stolen from two food trucks outside 599 Johnson Avenue is a reminder of the other side of the same decision: trucks parked indoors are safer from theft, so the indoor space has to be safe for them.

After a fire or demolition on the block

The building next door was demolished after a fire, and our side wall is now open to an empty lot. Does that change anything about our detection?

It can, and it is worth a look soon after the lot is cleared. When an attached building comes down, the wall it shared with yours becomes an exterior wall that was never built to be one. Openings that used to lead into the neighbor's building, old vents, abandoned pipe sleeves and gaps at the roof line can now open onto the weather, and to anyone on the lot. Rooms along that wall may be colder, damper and dustier than before, which can push detectors there toward nuisance alarms or early failure, and cockloft or roof spaces that used to be shared may now be open at one end. We check the rooms along the exposed wall, the cellar and the roof space, test the detectors there, and look at whether any device needs to change type or move. If your building has a panel, we also look for cable that once crossed into the neighbor's building, which happens in old attached rows more often than anyone expects. The May 2026 fire at 927 Grand Street, which News 12 and North Brooklyn News reported led to a demolition order for that building and vacate orders next door, is exactly the situation where neighbors end up asking this question.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in East Williamsburg

AI answers and national guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for a typical American house or office park. These are the shortcuts that mislead East Williamsburg owners and tenants most often.

"A loft is just an apartment with high ceilings"

Not here. Many East Williamsburg lofts are interim multiple dwellings under the Loft Law, buildings the law recognizes as homes while they are still legally commercial or manufacturing buildings, overseen by the Loft Board rather than handled like any rental.

That gives the owner two sets of fire duties: the smoke and CO detectors the Loft Board says must be provided now, and the Article 7-B fire and safety standards the Multiple Dwelling Law requires within eighteen months of the legalization permit.

A plan written for "an apartment with high ceilings" misses both, and also misses the tenant-built sleeping areas that no generic placement rule anticipates.

"Carbon monoxide only comes from your own boiler or stove"

The February 2026 evacuation at 101 Humboldt Street began with smoke from a manhole, and ABC7 reported that firefighters found high carbon monoxide inside the seven-story building before a transformer vault fire broke out on the street.

In a neighborhood of utility vaults, loading docks, idling trucks and garages, CO can enter a building from outside, and the cellar and lowest floors are often where it arrives first.

CO planning in East Williamsburg starts with the building's own fuel-burning equipment but does not stop there. We look at the street side, the cellar and anywhere engines run.

"A household CO alarm sounds the moment there is carbon monoxide"

It is designed not to. BRK, the maker of First Alert and BRK alarms, explains that under UL 2034 a residential CO alarm must sound within 4 to 15 minutes at 400 ppm, within 10 to 50 minutes at 150 ppm, and within 60 to 240 minutes at 70 ppm.

The same page says the unit is designed not to alarm at a constant 30 ppm for 30 days. Those delays exist so the alarm reacts to dangerous exposure rather than every brief whiff, but they mean a firefighter's meter can read CO while household alarms are still silent.

That is one possible reason residents of a building can hear later that CO was found and that no alarm went off. It is also why expired or missing alarms, and alarms mounted far from where CO arrives, matter as much as the device itself.

"A three-story building never needs a fire alarm system"

The DOB's code notes for Loft Law buildings say fire alarm applications are required at four or more stories, and AI answers tend to turn that into a promise that anything shorter is exempt.

A three-story building can still need a building system because of its use, its occupant load, a commercial or assembly ground floor, or what its legalization design requires. That is a design professional's determination, not a rule of thumb.

And even where no system is required, every residential unit is owed its detectors, and a shared stair over a closed store is the place a few linked devices do the most good.

"Wireless alarms work anywhere"

Wireless interconnected alarms are often the neatest answer in an occupied building, but East Williamsburg's lofts and warehouses are built of heavy masonry, concrete, steel and timber, with long distances between units on a floor.

Radio links that work across a frame house can fail across a factory floor or through a masonry fire wall, and the failure is silent until the day one alarm sounds and the others do not.

We test every wireless link in place, with doors shut and machines running, before relying on it, and run cable where radio does not reach reliably.

"FDNY approval is a formality you can finish in a couple of weeks"

The FDNY's own guide says initial plan review alone can take up to eight weeks, and that the average from plan submission to final inspection and approval is about twenty to twenty-four weeks.

Monitoring has to be live before the inspection is scheduled, the installation has to be 100 percent complete, and plans that draw objections go back for another review.

A studio building, shop or venue that needs a filed system should start the design months before its target date. Owners who start late end up paying for delays that no installer can make up.

"More alarms always means a safer building"

In mixed buildings the wrong alarm in the wrong room is worse than useless. A smoke detector above a welding bench, a fryer or a row of idling trucks will false-alarm until someone bags it or pulls it down, and then the room has nothing.

The FDNY treats repeated unwarranted alarms from monitored systems as an enforcement matter, so a building whose devices are wrong for its rooms can end up in trouble for trying to be careful.

Better is the right device for each room: heat where the work makes smoke or steam, smoke where people sleep and stock sits, CO where fuel burns, all linked so the warning reaches the people who need it.

FAQ

East Williamsburg Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

How can we tell whether our building is an interim multiple dwelling covered by the Loft Board?
+

Ask the Loft Board or check its records. The Board keeps the records of buildings covered by the Loft Law, and its process pages describe each stage of legalization through to a residential certificate of occupancy. Owners and tenants should confirm coverage there rather than relying on how a building is described in a listing, because the detector duties and the legalization timeline both depend on it.

In a loft that is not legal yet, is the owner still the one responsible for smoke and CO detectors?
+

Yes. The Loft Board lists smoke and carbon monoxide detectors among the services the owner of an interim multiple dwelling must provide to protected occupants, along with heat, hot water, lit public halls and fire stairs, and secure entrance doors. That duty applies while the building is still going through legalization, not only after it finishes.

What is a temporary certificate of occupancy, and does getting one mean the fire alarm has been approved?
+

A temporary certificate of occupancy documents that a use of the building is legal for a period while work is completed. The Loft Board treats a TCO for the residential units as the equivalent of Article 7-B compliance. It is not the same as an FDNY letter of approval for a specific fire alarm system, which is a separate record from the fire department.

Does every unit in a Loft Law building get detectors, or only the units of protected occupants?
+

The Loft Board's maintenance list is written around protected occupants, but a fire does not check anyone's status. A building where some units have working detection and others do not is a building where a fire in an unprotected unit can grow unnoticed next to everyone else. We plan detection for every unit where people live, and we let the owner and their attorney sort out the legal status of each tenancy separately.

Why might a building's CO alarms stay silent when firefighters' meters are reading carbon monoxide inside?
+

Household CO alarms are built to wait. BRK explains that under UL 2034 an alarm must sound within 4 to 15 minutes at 400 ppm, 10 to 50 minutes at 150 ppm and 60 to 240 minutes at 70 ppm, and is designed not to alarm at a constant 30 ppm. A meter reads instantly. Expired alarms, missing alarms and alarms far from where the gas enters are other common reasons.

Our building was evacuated for gas or CO from the street. Once we are allowed back in, should the detectors be checked?
+

Yes. After an evacuation, test every smoke and CO alarm, check the dates on the back, and replace any that are past their useful life or did not respond as expected. Pay special attention to the cellar and the lowest floors, closest to the street, and add CO detection there if the building has none. Then keep a dated record of what was tested and replaced.

We just signed a lease on a studio in a converted factory. What should we check about fire safety on day one?
+

Find the nearest two ways out and walk them, check that the stair and corridor are clear, look for a detector or device in your space and ask the landlord whether it is connected to a building system, and find out who to call when an alarm sounds. Before you build any wall or ceiling, ask the landlord how the building's detection will be adjusted.

The Loft Board says nothing may be stored in the means of egress. Can a fire alarm panel or power supply be mounted in the stair hall?
+

A permanently mounted panel is building equipment, not storage, and panels are often placed near the entrance where firefighters look first. But the location is set by the design or by what the building already has, and it must never narrow the path or block a door. Boxes, bikes and chargers stacked around a panel are storage, and they come out.

Our studio runs a laser cutter and 3D printers overnight with nobody in the room. What detection makes sense?
+

Smoke detection in the room, placed away from the machines' exhaust and fans so routine fumes do not trigger it, with the alarm reaching someone outside the room, either through a building system or monitoring. Unattended equipment is exactly where early warning matters, because nobody is there to smell smoke. Keep the machines on a clear area and never block the way out.

Our photo studio uses hot continuous lights for long shoots. Will they set off the detectors above the set?
+

Hot lights rarely trigger photoelectric smoke detectors on their own, but they can set off heat detectors mounted close above them, and dust burning off a fixture can trip smoke detection. We place devices for the set's lighting positions, choose heat ratings with the fixtures in mind, and keep detectors working rather than covered during shoots.

In a building with twenty studio tenants, who should hold the fire alarm panel's keys and codes?
+

The landlord or managing agent, plus the fire alarm company that services the system and whoever the building names as its on-site contact. Not every tenant. A panel that tenants can silence or reset is a panel that will be silenced at the wrong moment. We leave the owner a short written note of who holds access and how to reach the service company.
