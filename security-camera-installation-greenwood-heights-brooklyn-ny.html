The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Greenwood Heights is small, steep and surrounded by edges. CityNeighborhoods describes it as a compact, hilly neighborhood between Park Slope, Sunset Park and the grounds of Green-Wood Cemetery, roughly from 15th Street to 36th Street and from Third Avenue to Eighth Avenue, barely two dozen blocks. Wikipedia notes that the borders are much debated, with the Prospect Expressway on the north, the Gowanus and the harbor to the west, Eighth Avenue on the east and the cemetery's southern edge near 39th Street. Inside those lines you find wood-frame and brick rowhouses built for working families at the end of the nineteenth century, three- and four-story store-and-apartment buildings on Fifth Avenue, one-story warehouses and workshops on the big lots near Fourth Avenue, and a scattering of new condo and rental buildings that went up after the 2005 rezoning.

A camera does not watch a house. It watches whatever is in front of its lens, and in Greenwood Heights that is often unusual. From 24th Street to 34th Street, Fifth Avenue has the cemetery on one side and homes on the other, as the Brooklyn Eagle put it in its 2020 tour of the cemetery's perimeter, so a front camera on those blocks looks across a wide avenue at an iron fence and grounds that are locked and dark at night. On Fourth Avenue the view is the curb, where trades park flatbeds and vans in front of their shops. Behind houses near the expressway, the view is one of the small public parks the city built on leftover land, separated from back yards by a chain-link fence. In the newer buildings it is a gated lot or a ground-floor garage.

The local record shows why the details matter. When Green-Wood suffered what the Brooklyn Eagle called unprecedented vandalism in August 2012, with more than 40 monuments toppled and about $100,000 in damage, the image police released came from a security camera and showed a man climbing at the main entrance at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street. When a flatbed was stolen from in front of a Fourth Avenue ironworks near 22nd Street in April 2016, with tragic results for its owner, ABC7 reported that the suspect was identified from surveillance footage recovered from the area. In both cases the camera that mattered was one that happened to be pointed at the right spot, at the right height, with enough detail to identify a person.

The numbers from the 72nd Precinct, which covers Greenwood Heights from its station house at 830 Fourth Avenue, point the same way. Its 2026 CompStat report for the 44th week of the year shows burglary down slightly from 2025, 86 reports against 90, and down more than 60 percent over 16 years. Petit larceny, the category that covers small thefts such as a package taken from a stoop or a bag lifted from a counter, is up 7 percent, 645 against 603. Grand larceny of autos is up a little too, 92 against 89. So the camera work that pays off here is less about a dramatic break-in and more about the everyday: who walked up the stoop, who leaned into a car, who left with something from a shop.

Greenwood Heights facts that change where a camera goes

  • Frame houses, many under siding: Brownstoner wrote in 2014 that Greenwood Heights is better known for its wood-framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding. PropertyShark lists houses such as 347 20th Street, a two-family frame house from 1899 with aluminum or vinyl walls, and 204 22nd Street, a two-unit frame house from 1910. Camera mounts on these houses go into the wood behind the skin, never into the siding alone.
  • Two households, one door: those PropertyShark records are two-family buildings, and a two-family usually shares one street door. Cameras there need two sets of accounts and a clear agreement about which views each household sees.
  • A cemetery across the avenue: Green-Wood covers 478 acres, is a National Historic Landmark and is ringed by an iron fence. Its main entrance is at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street, with other gates at 20th Street and Prospect Park West and at 34th Street and Fourth Avenue.
  • Busy days at the gate: the Green-House at Green-Wood, the restored 1895 Weir Greenhouse and a new building across from the main arch, opened on April 18, 2026 and is open free Thursday to Monday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., according to the city's Department of Cultural Affairs.
  • New buildings with lots and garages: New York YIMBY reported a seven-story, 13-apartment building at 643–645 Fifth Avenue with a seven-car lot, a 10-apartment building at 814 Fifth Avenue with a seven-car garage on the ground floor, and permits in September 2026 for a three-story building at 156 21st Street with manufacturing space and four enclosed parking spaces.
  • Parks behind the fences: Untapped Cities counted 15 small parks along the sunken Prospect Expressway and noted that a chain-link fence is usually all that separates them from the back yards of neighboring homes.

Many owners who call about cameras here pair them with a video intercom at the shared street door or with an alarm for the back of the house; we quote those on the same visit if you want them, and the cameras are planned so that one does not duplicate the other.

What the Camera Faces

Cemetery Fence, Work-Truck Curb, Pocket Park or Condo Lot: Planning Greenwood Heights Cameras by the View in Front of Them

Before we talk about brands or resolution, we ask one question about every camera: what will be in front of it at two in the afternoon, and what will be in front of it at two in the morning? In Greenwood Heights the answers fall into six patterns. Most properties have two or three of them at once, and the quote is built camera by camera from these.

Fifth Avenue facing Green-Wood: a camera looking at the dark

On the blocks of Fifth Avenue between about 24th and 34th Streets, the houses and storefronts face the cemetery's fence across a wide avenue. By day that is a pleasant view and an easy one for a camera. After the cemetery closes, the grounds behind the fence are unlit, and a camera on the front of a house there is looking from a lit stoop toward a large black area. Cameras set to automatic exposure try to average the whole frame. With a big dark field filling the top half of the picture, the camera opens up, the stoop light and anyone standing under it blow out to white, and the face you wanted is lost. When the camera switches to infrared, its own light has nothing to bounce off across the street, so the picture of the avenue goes flat and grey while the nearby railing flares.

We solve that in three ways, often together. The face camera is aimed down at the stoop and door, not out across the avenue, so the frame is mostly lit steps and doorway rather than dark grounds. A second camera, with a narrower lens, is set for the sidewalk and the curb in front of the house, where the street lights do fall, and its exposure is fixed for that light rather than left to guess. And where the stoop light is weak, a warm, steady light or a motion-switched light on the house does more for the night picture than any camera upgrade.

The main gate corner at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street

The corner around the main entrance is the busiest stretch of the cemetery's edge. The gate is where the 2012 vandal was photographed climbing, according to the security still that Bklyner and News 12 reported that September. It is where monk parakeets nest in the spires, as Wikipedia notes. And since April 2026 it faces the Green-House at Green-Wood, which opened across from the arch with free visiting hours from Thursday through Monday. For a shop, house or building near that corner, the camera problem is not darkness but movement: visitors, tour groups, birds and traffic all crossing the frame. A camera there should record continuously, so nothing is lost between motion triggers, and its phone alerts should be limited to a zone drawn around your own door, steps or counter.

Fourth Avenue curbs: the work truck and the shop door

Fourth Avenue is the working edge of the neighborhood. NYC Planning's 2005 rezoning study described its edge as large lots with one-story warehouse and commercial buildings, and that is still where trades, workshops, garages and delis sit. Trucks and vans park at the curb in front of the businesses all day. The April 2016 case near 22nd Street showed what happens there: police said the flatbed's keys were in it or it was running while parked, a man drove it away at about 9:30 in the morning, and he was later identified from surveillance video gathered from the area. For a business on the avenue, that is the model of a useful camera: one that shows the cab door of a vehicle at the curb close enough to see the face of the person getting in, and a second view of the direction the vehicle leaves.

So a Fourth Avenue curb plan usually has a camera mounted low on the building front, aimed along the curb at the cab of the truck you park most often, with a lens set so a person at the driver's door fills a good part of the frame. A second camera, higher, takes the wide view up or down the avenue toward the nearest corner, which on a side street like 22nd Street is the way a vehicle turns.

Side streets of frame two-families: the stoop, the shared door and the package

On the numbered streets between Fourth and Fifth Avenues and up the hill toward Seventh, most houses are two- and three-story rowhouses, many of them wood frame and many converted to two or three apartments. The view in front of a camera there is close and narrow: a few steps, a door, a small front yard behind an iron fence, the sidewalk and the parked cars. A face camera at the door, mounted no higher than about the top of the door frame and angled slightly down, gives the identifying picture; a wider camera covering the steps, the front yard and the sidewalk shows how someone arrived and left. If deliveries are left in the vestibule or on the steps, the face camera's view includes the spot where they land, so a clip shows the package and the hand that takes it in one frame.

On a two-family, the shared door is the camera both households care about, and the yard camera usually belongs to whoever has the garden apartment or the owner. We set that up on the recorder with separate accounts from the start, rather than sharing one login around the house.

Back yards against a pocket park or the cemetery fence

Some yards in Greenwood Heights end at somebody else's land. Near the expressway, the back fence may be the chain link of one of the small parks the city built along the trench, such as Detective Joseph Mayrose Park. On a few blocks near the cemetery, and behind the houses of Roosevelt and Woodrow Courts at 30th Street and Fifth Avenue, yards look toward the cemetery. For a camera, those are public or institutional spaces, not a neighbor's back yard, and people use them at all hours in the case of a park. A yard camera there is aimed at your own rear door, windows and the inside of your fence line, with a privacy mask over the part of the picture that shows the park or grounds, so the recording stays on your property and the alerts do not fire every time someone walks a dog on the other side.

New buildings: gated lots, garages and lobbies

The buildings that went up after the 2005 rezoning brought views the older houses never had: a seven-car lot behind a gate at 643–645 Fifth Avenue, a ground-floor garage at 814 Fifth Avenue, enclosed parking planned at 156 21st Street, and lobbies, mailboxes and bike rooms. A lot or garage camera faces headlights, roll-up doors and cars that pull in fast. It needs a camera that handles bright light against dark, mounted so headlights do not shine straight into the lens, and a second camera on the pedestrian door so that whoever walks in behind a car is seen on foot. Lobby and mailbox cameras face glass street doors, where afternoon light turns people into silhouettes unless the camera is set for it.

Frame houses under siding: find the wood, seal the hole

Brownstoner's 2014 look at 207A–209 18th Street noted that on 1888 maps wood-framed row houses dominated both sides of that block, and that Greenwood Heights is better known for wood houses than for brownstone. Many still wear the aluminum or vinyl siding added in the twentieth century. That skin is thin. A camera bracket screwed only into siding will loosen as the siding expands and contracts, and the camera's view will creep. So on a sided frame house we locate the studs or solid sheathing behind the siding, mount the camera on a mounting block made for siding so the bracket sits flat, drive the fasteners into wood, and seal the cable entry with a gasket and sealant so water cannot run behind the panel. The cable then goes straight through the wall into a hollow stud bay, which a frame house usually has, and down to the cellar.

The same hollow walls make frame houses forgiving for indoor cable. Between floors we can usually drop a line inside a wall cavity or follow a plumbing chase from the cellar, so a camera at the top of the house rarely needs exposed raceway.

Restored frame houses: keeping the wood clean

Wikipedia notes that recent years have brought gut renovations and restorations of neglected wood-frame houses from around 1900. Owners who have stripped the siding and repainted the clapboard do not want a cable stapled across their work. On those houses we mount cameras on blocks painted to match the trim, place them under eaves or door hoods where a small dark camera reads as a shadow, and bring every cable through the wall right behind the mount.

Brick and brownstone rows: anchors in the joints

Greenwood Heights has masonry rows too: the red-brick rowhouses on 27th Street between Fourth and Fifth Avenues that the Brooklyn Eagle pointed out, the Queen Anne houses on 18th Street, and brick houses mixed into most blocks. On brick we drill anchors into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so a camera can be moved later and the hole repointed without a scar on the brick. Cable passes through one cored hole near the camera, sloped down to the outside and sealed.

Fifth Avenue store buildings: two owners, two systems

NYC Planning described Fifth Avenue's buildings as three- and four-story mixed buildings, and the avenue still works that way: a cafe, a bakery or a bar on the ground floor and two or three apartments above. The store's cameras watch its door, counter and stock room and belong to the business. The building's cameras watch the residential street door, the stair and perhaps the cellar, and belong to the landlord.

New condo and rental buildings: raceways and board approval

The post-rezoning buildings are concrete and steel with finished, closed ceilings, and their developers often installed a few cameras at the door and the lot. Adding to those cameras means following the raceways the electrician left, or using surface raceway painted to match where none exists. We do not core through a slab without the board's written approval and its engineer's say-so.

Rules that apply to Greenwood Heights business cameras

License. New York's General Business Law section 69-m requires a state license to install or service security and fire alarm systems. The Department of State's own application notes that installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require licensing, so the rule for cameras alone is not as clear as some installers claim. We hold NYS license #12000287431 either way, which covers cameras installed alongside alarms and access control.

Permits. Since December 21, 2025, the city's Construction Codes, in section 28-105.4.7, exempt low-voltage work under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits from an electrical permit when a licensed or qualified person does it. A camera system on low-voltage cable fits that rule. A new outlet for a camera's power supply is electrical work and goes to an electrician.

Audio. New York allows recording a conversation when at least one party consents. A camera recording sound at a counter where staff are not part of the conversation is a different matter, so we leave audio off on business cameras unless you have a clear reason and have taken advice.

Exits and fire safety. Cameras and cable never block an exit, a sprinkler head or a fire alarm device, and any hole through a fire-rated wall or floor in a mixed building is closed with rated firestop.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Standing on the Sidewalk Before Touching the House

This is how we approach a Greenwood Heights property, not the story of one particular job. We start across the street. From the far sidewalk you see what a burglar or a package thief sees: which door is lit, which is in shadow, where the parked cars block the view of the steps, and whether the house next door has a stoop that hides yours. On Fifth Avenue facing the cemetery, we stand with our backs to the fence to see how the street lights fall on the fronts.

Then we walk up the steps and hold a phone at the height a camera would go, to see what a face looks like from there. On many of these houses the door hood or the cornice over the stoop is the natural place for a camera, and from that height a cap brim hides a face; the IPCamTalk forum's advice to rowhouse owners, not to mount face cameras higher than about eight feet, matches what we see. After that we go to the cellar to find where the cable can come down, and to the back yard to see what is beyond the fence. If we can, we come back after dark, or ask the owner to send a photo from the stoop at night, because the night picture is the one that decides most camera choices here.

Across Fifth Avenue from our house there is only Green-Wood's fence and unlit grounds. With nothing lit on the far side, what does our front camera need to work after dark?

It needs to stop trying to see the cemetery. A camera that takes in the whole view across the avenue will spend its exposure on a black field and wash out the lit stoop where the useful detail is. We aim the face camera down at your steps and door so the frame is mostly the area your own light covers, fix its exposure for that light, and give the sidewalk and curb their own camera tuned to the street lights.

The 2012 Green-Wood vandal was caught on a security camera climbing at the main gate on Fifth Avenue and 25th Street. Should houses and shops near the gate point cameras at the cemetery too?

No, and it would not help much if you did. The image police released in 2012 came from a camera close to the gate, and the cemetery protects its own grounds. A camera on your building looking across the avenue at the fence is too far away to identify anyone at night, and it records a place that is not yours. What helps the neighborhood is a camera that covers your own door and the sidewalk in front of your building well, so that if something happens on your stretch of Fifth Avenue, your clip shows a clear face and a direction of travel.

Monk parakeets nest in the spires of the cemetery gate, and birds swoop past our shop sign all day. Can the camera stop alerting us every time one flies by?

Yes. Birds trigger the simplest kind of motion detection, which reacts to any change in the picture. Most current recorders and cameras can instead detect people and vehicles, and they let us draw an alert zone around your door and window only. We switch on person detection, draw the zone tight, and leave continuous recording running so the birds are still on the recording if you ever need it, just not on your phone.

A Fourth Avenue business owner was killed in 2016 chasing his own flatbed, and police found the thief through surveillance video from the area. We park a work truck in front of our shop near there. What should our curb camera be set up to catch?

The person at the driver's door. ABC7 reported that police said the truck's keys were in it or it was running when it was taken, which is common for trades loading during the day, and the suspect was identified from footage nearby. A close camera, mounted low on your building front and aimed along the curb at the cab of the truck you park most, gives a face. A second, wider camera aimed toward the corner shows which way the vehicle went and when. We set both for moving subjects, with a faster shutter than a camera watching a quiet stoop, and we label them so you can pull the right clip quickly.

Our two-family has one street door that both households use. Can the tenant upstairs see the front door camera on their phone without seeing the camera in our garden?

Yes, and that is how we set up most two-families here. The recorder holds every camera, and each person gets their own login with only the cameras they should see. The tenant gets the street door, the vestibule and perhaps the sidewalk; the owner or the garden apartment gets the yard and rear door as well.

We rent in one of the newer buildings that lists security cameras among its amenities. If a package disappears from the lobby, can we make the landlord show us the footage?

Usually not. Brick Underground's landlord column has explained that a landlord is not obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant. Many managers will review it and some will share a clip, but it is their system. The practical steps are to ask management quickly, before the recording loops, and to file a police report, because the police can request the video.

A small public park along the Prospect Expressway is on the other side of our back fence. Can our rear camera record people in the park?

Your camera can see what anyone standing in your yard could see, but there is no reason to record the park. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a residential owner or tenant sue someone who puts a camera on adjoining property to record the recreational activities in their back yard, without consent and with intent to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. A city park is not a back yard, so that section is not about you here, but your neighbors' yards on either side are. We aim the rear camera at your own door, windows and the inside of your fence, and put a privacy mask over the park and the neighboring yards.

A man was robbed outside a storefront on 24th Street between Fourth and Fifth Avenues in August 2026. Our shop is on a block like that. How much of the sidewalk can a storefront camera actually identify people on?

Less than most people expect. Hoodline reported that robbery at about 9:45 at night on 24th Street. At night, a typical storefront camera identifies a face reliably only within a few steps of the door, and beyond that it shows what happened rather than who did it. For a shop, we use one camera at the door for faces and one camera covering the length of your frontage and the curb for events, mounted where the store's own lights and the street lights help rather than glare.

Burglary is down in the 72nd Precinct this year, but petit larceny is up. Does that change where our cameras should go?

It changes the priorities. The precinct's 2026 CompStat report shows 86 burglaries so far against 90 last year, and petit larceny at 645 against 603. Petit larceny covers small thefts: a package off a stoop, a bike from a front yard, a bag or phone from a counter. Those happen in the open, in daylight, in the few seconds someone is at your door or your car. So for most houses we put the money into a sharp face camera at the door and a view of the front yard and curb before adding cameras at the back.

The store burglary at Fourth Avenue and 17th Street in 2019 came through a side door at about two in the morning. Our store has a door on the cross street too. Should the camera go outside it, inside it, or both?

Both, if the budget allows one more camera. Outside, a camera over or beside the side door shows who approached and how long they worked at the lock; that is often the clearest picture you will get, because people stop at a door. Inside, a camera facing the door from across the room shows who came through and where they went, and it cannot be tampered with from the street. If you can only have one, put it inside, aimed at the door, because the inside camera is protected and still catches the face as the person enters.

We filed plans for a new building between Third and Fourth Avenues with a workshop on the ground floor and enclosed parking. At what stage should the camera layout be drawn?

Before the walls and ceilings close, ideally when the electrical drawings are being finalized. New York YIMBY reported permits in 2026 for exactly that kind of building at 156 21st Street, with manufacturing space and four enclosed parking spaces. At that stage, conduit or a pull string to each future camera position costs little, a recorder spot with power and ventilation can be planned in the office, and the parking door and workshop roll-up can be lit with cameras in mind.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Greenwood Heights

AI summaries and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr answer camera questions for the country as a whole. Some of what they say is fine. Some of it does not fit a hilly neighborhood of frame two-families, a cemetery across the avenue and a working Fourth Avenue. Here are seven common claims and how they look on the ground here.

"Security camera installation in New York costs about $1,500."

Angi gives an average of $1,509 for New York, with a range of $797 to $2,222, and HomeAdvisor's national guide gives $1,288, with $25 to $500 per camera. Those are averages of jobs elsewhere, mostly simple ones. They do not know whether your house is under aluminum siding, whether the cable has to go from a cellar up three floors of a frame house, or whether a shop's side door needs its own run.

A Reddit thread on r/homesecurity shows the spread from the other side: one installer said $300 a camera was not unreasonable if it included wire, labor and a recorder, and that his company bills by the hour plus equipment.

That is why we do not publish a per-camera price. We write a line-by-line quote after photos or a visit, at our Brooklyn rate, and the fixed prices we do publish are listed in the pricing section.

"Mount cameras as high as possible so nobody can reach them."

Height protects a camera and ruins a face. From the top of a stoop or under a cornice, a camera looks down on cap brims and hoods. Forum advice on IPCamTalk to owners planning cameras for a brownstone was not to mount face cameras higher than about eight feet, and to make sure each camera is in another camera's view so one cannot be knocked out unseen.

On Greenwood Heights houses we usually use a high camera for the overview and a lower one, near the door frame, for faces. The low one is small and tough; the high one watches it.

At a shop counter or a workshop door the rule is the same: one camera shows what happened, and a lower one shows who did it.

"Night vision means you will see everything after dark."

Infrared works by lighting what is near the camera. Point it across Fifth Avenue at the cemetery and there is nothing within range to light, so the far side goes black while the railing in front glows.

On fronts facing the cemetery, color low-light cameras aimed at the lit stoop and curb work far better, and a steady light on the house helps more than any camera feature. Brick Underground's package-theft advice makes the point that a camera paired with a motion light is the stronger deterrent.

Before we call a job finished, every camera is checked after dark, or you send us screenshots from the first night and we adjust them.

"Wireless cameras are simpler and just as good."

For one door in a rented apartment, a Wi-Fi camera can be the right choice. For a two-family with cameras at the front, the yard and the curb, wireless means batteries to charge or outlets to find outside, and a signal that has to get through old walls and floors.

A cable carries power and picture together, does not drop out, and in a frame house usually runs inside a hollow wall to the cellar without much trouble. The IPCamTalk owners redoing a brownstone facade were told to run solid copper cable while the walls were open, for exactly that reason.

We use wireless where cable truly cannot go, and we tell you where that is before quoting.

"You need a city electrical permit to install security cameras."

Since December 21, 2025, New York City's Construction Codes, in section 28-105.4.7, exempt low-voltage signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under 50 volts from an electrical permit when a licensed or qualified person installs it. A camera system on low-voltage cable fits.

What still needs an electrician is a new 120-volt outlet, for example for a recorder in a spot with no power. And in a condo building, the board's approval matters more than any permit.

Greenwood Heights' houses are not in a historic district, as far as our searches of the Landmarks records show, so Landmarks approval is not usually part of a camera job on a residential block here; the Weir Greenhouse and the cemetery gates are individual landmarks.

"A camera stops package theft."

Brick Underground is blunt about it: getting images of a culprit will not stop them in the act. What a camera does is make the theft less attractive, especially with a light, and give you a usable record afterward.

With petit larceny up in the 72nd Precinct in 2026, that record matters. A face camera that shows the package and the hand taking it in one frame is the piece most kits miss, because their doorbells are set to see the visitor, not the step where boxes land.

Where thefts keep happening, a lock box, deliveries to work, or a video intercom that lets you buzz the courier in can prevent what a camera only records.

"Greenwood Heights is just part of Park Slope or Sunset Park, so the same package fits."

Many summaries fold Greenwood Heights into its neighbors. Its housing is different: Brownstoner called it better known for wood-framed houses, restored or covered in aluminum siding, rather than the brownstone rows north of the expressway.

Its views are different too. No other neighborhood has so many fronts facing the cemetery's fence, and few have Fourth Avenue's mix of trades, trucks and new buildings on one strip.

A plan built for a Park Slope brownstone with a lit block across the street will miss the dark-field problem on Fifth Avenue and the curb problem on Fourth.
