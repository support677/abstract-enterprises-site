The Neighborhood

Why Homecrest Properties Need Access Control

Homecrest is a neighborhood of shared front doors. Wikipedia places it between Kings Highway and Avenue X, from Coney Island Avenue to Ocean Avenue, inside Brooklyn Community District 15 and the 61st Precinct, with the ZIP code 11229 and about 44,000 residents in 2010; Brick Underground draws its southern line at Avenue U, and CityNeighborhoods stretches it north to Avenue P. Whichever map you use, the blocks between East 12th and East 16th Streets are lined with apartment buildings, and the people inside most of them come and go through a lobby, a garage or a street door that dozens or hundreds of other people also use. That is the situation access control was invented for.

The biggest of those buildings are mid-century elevator co-ops. StreetEasy lists 1840 East 13th Street as a six-story co-op built in 1954 with 125 apartments, an elevator, a laundry room, a live-in super, a package room and a garage. Brick Underground’s 2022 guide describes 2299 East 13th Street as a six-story co-op of 1960 with 66 apartments, an upgraded lobby, a security system and a live-in super, and 1750 East 14th Street as a six-story building of 1961 with 64 apartments, an elevator and parking. A OneKey listing for 1730 East 14th Street describes a stock cooperative “built in 1961” with “on-site laundry facilities” and “a garage with a waiting list.” None of those records mentions a doorman. In buildings like these the super, the board and the managing agent are the security staff, and the lock on the lobby door is the only thing standing between the sidewalk and every hallway.

Many of those buildings still run on the keys they were given when they opened, more than sixty years ago, plus every copy cut since. A key does not know who is holding it. It cannot be switched off when a shareholder sells, when a tenant moves out, when a porter leaves the job or when a dog walker stops coming. A co-op with sixty or a hundred and twenty-five apartments, a garage with its own list of drivers, a package room, a laundry room and storage cages is carrying hundreds of keys it cannot account for. Access control does not make a building secure by itself, but it is the only tool that lets a board say, at any moment, exactly who can open which door, and change that answer in a minute instead of a locksmith’s afternoon.

The newer buildings ask different questions. Homecrest’s early-21st-century condominiums, most of them on or near Ocean Avenue, were sold on their amenities. Brick Underground lists 2381 Ocean Avenue, an eight-story building of 2006 with 22 apartments, and 2131 Ocean Avenue, eight floors and 16 apartments, opened in 2019. A OneKey listing describes 1769 East 13th Street, built in 2018, as an “11-unit boutique building” with an elevator, “a private spa with an indoor pool, sauna, steam room, and showers” and “a large common roof deck.” A small board running a spa and a roof deck is running doors that need hours, rules and a record, and the rules for a sauna or a rooftop are not the rules for a lobby.

The smallest buildings are the prewar walk-ups and the houses. Brick Underground describes 1717 East 14th Street as a three-story building of 1932 with 24 apartments, and Wikipedia notes that many of the neighborhood’s two-story houses have been rebuilt into much larger homes, and that south of Avenue V one-story brick rows share community backyards and driveways. A walk-up’s street door serves two dozen households and often a landlord who does not live there; a rebuilt house may have three or four entrances and a family that wants a code for the cleaner and none for the contractor who has finished.

And then there are the stores. The city’s Department of Small Business Services, working with the Brooklyn Alliance, surveyed Homecrest’s shopping streets between September 2021 and June 2022 and counted 508 storefronts along Avenue U, Coney Island Avenue and Gravesend Neck Road, 10.6 percent of them empty. In the merchant survey, 88 percent of business owners rented their space and the median business had two full-time employees. Small, rented, family-run shops are exactly the businesses that hand out back-door keys to relatives and part-timers and then lose track of them, and that cannot afford a locksmith every time someone leaves. Homecrest also has community organizations with real door problems: the City Council announced more than $2.5 million in October 2025 to move Homecrest Community Services into an owned headquarters with “privacy protections for clients” and “separate work zones for staff and partners,” which is, in plain words, an access control specification.

On buildings of the 1950s and early 1960s the cause is often mechanical before it is electronic: a closer that no longer pulls the door home, a strike that has worked loose in an old steel frame, or a door that has dropped on its hinges. On newer systems it is more often a power supply on a circuit someone switched off, a backup battery at the end of its life, or a controller that lost the internet when the building changed providers. What the materials mean for the install

The co-ops of the 1950s and early 1960s were built with steel door frames set in brick, and the frames are often filled solid with mortar. That is good for strength and slow for cutting: a strike pocket in a grouted frame takes careful work, and we price it as such. The lobby doors themselves are frequently original or second-generation aluminum and glass, with narrow stiles that leave little room for a lock in the door edge, so the lock often goes into the frame as a strike, or into an exit device on the door if that door is also a way out. Cable usually follows the original intercom path from the cellar.

The walk-ups and houses are mostly brick with wood or steel doors. A wood street door that has been painted for decades often binds in summer and gaps in winter, which is exactly when an electric strike starts missing; we plane, rehang or adjust before we wire. Newer condos are the opposite case: their doors and frames were bought with electric hardware in mind, but the cable may be buried in finished walls with no record of where it runs, so the first job is finding the developer’s drawings or tracing the runs.

Sixty Years of Keys: Moving a Homecrest Co-op Onto Fobs

A co-op that opened in 1954 or 1961 has had a lot of keys. Every apartment was given some when it was first sold, every new shareholder since has received a set at closing, and every household has had copies cut for children, parents, cleaners, aides and neighbors who water the plants. Porters, supers, managing agents and contractors have carried masters. Nobody has a list, and nobody can make one. The only way to take that exposure back is to stop the keys from working, and the practical way to stop them working without changing the locks again every few years is to put the street door, and then the doors behind it, on credentials that can be counted and switched off.

The conversion has more steps than it seems. First the board decides which doors join the system and in what order: almost always the street door and the vestibule door first, then the garage and the package room, then laundry, storage and the cellar. Then the board decides who gets what. In our experience the cleanest rule is one credential per person, not per apartment, so a fob that leaves with a former au pair can be cancelled without touching the parents’ fobs. The board also decides whether phone keys are offered alongside fobs, whether residents can request extra fobs for helpers, and who in the office or among the staff may issue and cancel them. Then come the house rules, the notice to shareholders, and a schedule for handing out fobs that does not leave a line in the lobby.

Only then does the hardware matter, and the hardware choices are mostly about the doors. The street door gets an electric strike or an electrified exit device, a door position contact that tells the system whether the door is shut, and a reader that accepts encrypted credentials rather than the older 125 kHz fobs that a cheap copier can clone. The key cylinder stays, because someone will always need it: firefighters with a lockbox key, the super in a long outage, a resident whose phone died. What changes is that the key becomes a fallback rather than the everyday way in, and that the building can see when it is used.

Keeping the key without keeping the old problem

If the old key keeps opening the door, the building has not taken back its exposure; it has added fobs on top of it. So the cylinder is changed when the fobs go live, to a new key held by very few people: the super, the managing agent, the board president and the fire department’s box if the building has one. Where the board wants to know whenever that key is used, the lock or exit device can report a key override separately from a fob entry, so a door that opens with no credential and no request-to-exit signal shows up as an exception instead of passing silently. We explain how that reporting works on the specific hardware before the board chooses it, because not every lock reports a key in the same way.

What the city expects in a building with residents

Once a building with apartments uses fobs, apps, cards or other smart access, New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to it. HPD’s summary says owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access must obtain consent, keep a written privacy and data retention policy, collect only the minimum data needed, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and may not require smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment. Co-ops and condos with renters among their residents should read that page with their attorney; the practical result for us is that the system we set up keeps as little personal data as it can, for as short a time as it can, and never becomes the only way into anyone’s home.

Rent-Stabilized Walk-Ups: What a DHCR Ruling Asked of One Fob Conversion

Homecrest’s prewar walk-ups and some of its larger rental buildings include rent-stabilized apartments, and in those buildings a change from keys to fobs is not only the owner’s decision. New York’s housing agency, DHCR, has heard tenant complaints that replacing keys with fobs reduced their services, and its rulings show what it has been willing to accept. A summary on LandlordvTenant of one ruling, docket DR430034RT dated July 29, 2016, lists the conditions under which DHCR allowed a building to replace its entry keys with a fob system. We are installers, not lawyers, and one ruling about one building is not a rule for every building, but the list is the clearest picture we have found of what a careful conversion looks like.

Among the conditions the summary lists: “the building must still be accessed by metal keys in the event of emergency”; “the system could record entrances, but not departures”; “the system must have a 48-hour battery backup in case of power outage”; “occupants, including children, would receive free key fobs,” and tenants “could also get up to four free key fobs for guests or employees”; “key fob replacement costs couldn’t exceed $25”; management “would accommodate religious observers who couldn’t operate electronic devices on the Sabbath”; individuals had to show proof of identity “but landlord couldn’t record this information”; the tenant’s name, address and photo would not be listed on the fob; and “no additional equipment would be installed in tenants’ apartments.”

Several of those conditions are hardware questions, and we design for them from the start. A 48-hour battery means sizing the power supply and batteries for the strike, the reader and the controller together, and choosing a fail-secure strike that draws power only when it releases, so the battery is not spent holding a door locked. Recording entrances but not departures means a reader on the outside and a request-to-exit device on the inside that does not log a person. A Sabbath accommodation usually means a mechanical key path or a scheduled arrangement agreed with the residents who need it. And keeping equipment out of apartments means the system lives at the doors, in the cellar and in the cloud, never in the units. An owner who wants a conversion to survive a complaint should plan it with counsel; we build to whatever plan they settle on and document it.

Spas, Saunas and Roof Decks: The Amenity Doors in Homecrest’s Newer Condos

A small condo with a spa, a steam room or a roof deck has doors that behave nothing like a lobby. The lobby should open for every owner at every hour. The spa should open only during posted hours, perhaps only for owners who are current on an amenity charge, and never with a lock that could hold someone inside a hot room. The roof deck should open in the evening and stop admitting people at the hour the house rules set, while anyone still upstairs can always get back down. Each of those is a different door program, and a board that treats them all as “fob doors” usually discovers the difference the first warm night someone props the roof open.

We plan amenity doors in three parts. The first is the way in: a reader on the corridor side with its own schedule and its own list, so the spa can close at ten while the lobby stays open, and a single owner’s spa access can be paused without touching their front door. The second is the way out, which must never depend on a credential. A spa or sauna suite, a gym or a roof deck has to be leavable at any hour by anyone inside, with free egress hardware on the inside and nothing that relies on power, a phone or a fob to release. The third is the record: a contact on each amenity door so the board knows when one was left standing open, and an entry log the board’s privacy policy says it may keep.

The equipment matters more here than at a lobby. Heat and steam are hard on electronics, so we keep readers and controllers out of the hot rooms themselves and mount the reader for a spa suite on the dry corridor side, with the lock on the suite door rather than on the sauna or steam room door, which should have no electric lock at all. On a roof deck the reader faces the weather, so it must be rated for outdoor use, and its cable must enter through a sealed penetration that will not leak into the top-floor ceiling. A roof door is also part of the building’s exit path under New York law, so the stair side always opens freely; the reader only decides who may step out onto the deck from inside, and the deck side is fitted so nobody can be shut out on the roof.

Fob Rollouts That Went Wrong, and What a Homecrest Board Can Learn From Them

Fob systems fail in public, and the failures are instructive. In April 2025 CBS New York reported on Queensbridge Houses in Queens, where NYCHA had switched building doors to electronic fob entry and residents said the fobs often did not work and some doors jammed. The president of the residents’ association told the station that 78 building-door complaints had been filed in one month on the north side of the complex alone, that repairs took “between one and three days,” and that residents were holding doors open with duct tape so they would not be locked out. A door taped open is worse than a door on an old key.

A smaller failure is just as common. In a November 2018 column, Brick Underground answered a tenant whose landlord had installed a fob system that stopped working whenever the internet went down, and in the rain, while the landlord refused to provide any other way in. A tenant lawyer quoted in the column put it plainly: “If you’re being deprived of the ability to get into your own apartment, your lease is being breached.” The column suggested complaints to 311 and HPD and, if that failed, an HP action in Housing Court.

Neither was a Homecrest building; we cite them for the three mistakes we design against here. The first is a door that depends on the internet, so our controllers keep the credential list on site. The second is hardware chosen without the door, so readers are rated for rain and closers are fixed first. The third is a rollout with no fallback, so a key still works for the few who need it, someone can always issue a fob, and every household, including those who do not read English easily, gets time to collect one.

The one-story rows and bungalows south of Avenue V, with scattered corner businesses and community spaces. Single-door premises where one good keypad and a door that latches every night is most of the job.

Small, rented and family-run

The numbers in the city’s survey describe the typical Homecrest business door better than any sales brochure. Eighty-eight percent of the merchants surveyed rented their space and only six percent owned it. Rules that apply to Homecrest business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path must unlock when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock must be released by hardware on the door that works with one hand. A shop, office or classroom exit always opens from inside without a key, code or card.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking, and a building with eight or more apartments needs an intercom with voice and remote release. A store that shares a street door with apartments cannot leave that door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to the residential doors. A shop’s own staff door is not covered; a shared residential door is.
  • Electrical licensing. The city’s electrical code treats signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under 50 volts as low-voltage work, outside its electrical permit section. The 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician; AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431.
  • Your lease. Most commercial leases require the landlord’s written consent for alterations. We give you a one-page description of the work, the devices and the holes, to send with the request.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Homecrest: Questions Answered

Mid-century elevator co-ops

Our new fob system will keep a key cylinder on the street door for emergencies. Can the system tell us when someone opens the door with that key instead of a fob?

Usually, yes, with the right hardware. A door position contact tells the controller whenever the door opens. If it opens without a fob read, without a request-to-exit signal from inside and without a release from the super’s desk, the controller logs it as an exception, which on most systems is called a forced door. On a street door whose only other way in is the emergency key, that exception almost always means the key was used. Some exit devices and mortise locks go further and report the key switch itself, so the log says plainly that the key opened the door.

Two things make the report useful. The new cylinder should be keyed to a restricted key held by very few people, so a key entry points to a short list, and the alert should go to the super and the managing agent, not to every shareholder. We set the rule, test it with the actual key on handover day, and show the board what the entry looks like in the log.

Several shareholders keep the building fob on the same key ring as a gym tag and a work badge. Will the lobby reader get confused?

It can. Many building fobs, gym tags, transit cards and office badges use the same 13.56 MHz radio band, and when two or three of them reach the reader together it may read the wrong one, read none, or take a second or two to sort them out. Residents notice this as a reader that “works on the second try.” It is not a fault in the reader, and it gets worse with fast readers that are trying to be helpful.

The fix is mostly habit: tap the fob on its own, or keep it on a separate ring from other badges. For a building where many residents carry badges, a phone key is a good alternative, and so is a card the size of a credit card kept in a separate pocket. When we hand out fobs we include a one-line note about this in each language the board needs.

Newer condos on Ocean Avenue and the side streets

Our eleven-unit building has a spa with a sauna and a steam room. Can the spa lock at night without any chance of locking someone inside?

Yes, if the lock goes on the right door and the way out never depends on electricity. The reader and electric lock belong on the spa suite’s entrance from the corridor, which can stop admitting owners at closing time. The sauna and steam room doors inside the suite should carry no electric lock at all, and the suite door must open from inside at any hour with ordinary free-egress hardware, so whoever is still in the steam room when the schedule ends simply walks out and the door relocks behind them.

Keep the electronics out of the heat. The reader goes on the dry corridor side, the controller in a closet elsewhere, and any contact on the suite door is a type rated for damp areas. If the board wants the spa to open only for owners who are current on an amenity charge, that is an access list the managing agent can change, separate from each owner’s lobby access, so nobody loses their front door over a spa bill.

Our condo’s roof deck is popular, but neighbors complain about late noise. Can the deck stop letting people up after ten at night while anyone already there can still get down?

Yes. The reader goes on the stair side of the roof door and runs on a schedule, so fobs open the door to the deck until the hour the house rules set and stop working after it. From the deck side, the door is set so nobody can be shut out on the roof: a person who is already up there can always come back in, either through hardware that opens from the deck side or a reader that keeps admitting fobs inward at every hour.

The stair side must also stay a free path, because a roof door is part of the building’s exit plan. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, roof doors in the buildings it covers must be self-closing but not self-locking, and openable from inside without a key, so we coordinate with the building’s plans and its fire safety documents before choosing hardware. A contact on the deck door, with an alert if it stands open after hours, usually does more for the neighbors’ sleep than the lock does.

The duplex at the top of our Ocean Avenue condo has a private terrace reached through a door off the common stair. Can that stair door open only for that owner?

Yes. That door gets its own reader with a list of one household, plus whoever the owner authorizes, and it can be set to open freely from the terrace side so nobody is ever trapped outside. Other owners’ fobs do nothing at that door, while the stair itself stays open as an exit. The owner can be given control of that one door’s list in the app, so a cleaner or a contractor can be added for a week without asking the board.

Check two documents first. The condo declaration and bylaws say whether that door and the terrace are common elements or the owner’s, which decides who pays and who approves. And the building’s plans say whether the stair door is part of a fire-rated enclosure, which narrows the hardware choices. We read both before quoting.

Stores on Avenue U, Coney Island Avenue and Neck Road

Our shop keeps the delivery van keys and the safe key on a hook in the back office. Can access control keep track of keys as well as doors?

It can, with a key cabinet: a steel cabinet that opens with a code, card or fob and records who took which key and when it came back. Some cabinets lock each key in its own slot, so the van key comes out only for a driver on shift and the safe key only for the owner or manager. Where the cabinet ties into the same system as the back door, the owner sees the door entries and the key removals in one place, and can be alerted if a van key has not come back by closing.

For a two- or three-person shop the simpler answer is often enough: a keypad on the office door with personal codes, and the safe key kept somewhere that door protects. We price both and say which we would choose for your store.

Community offices and shared space

Our nonprofit lets partner agencies use some of our rooms on certain weekdays. Can a partner’s card open only those rooms, and only on those days?

Yes. Each partner becomes its own group, with the street door and its rooms on the agreed days and hours. A legal-aid clinic that uses two counseling rooms on Tuesdays gets those rooms on Tuesdays; a health partner that uses the multipurpose room on Saturday mornings gets that room then. Your own staff keep their own groups, and the room where client files are kept is on nobody’s list but the people who need it.

Partners change people more often than you do, so give each partner a coordinator who can ask you to add or remove a person, and set every partner card to expire at the end of the agreement unless it is renewed. The entry log then shows which organization used which room, which is what most funders and partners want to see, without showing your own staff’s movements to anyone outside the organization.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Homecrest

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Homecrest door actually needs. We do not attribute a specific claim to any of those sources unless we quote it.

“Fobs are more secure than keys, full stop.”

A fob system is only as secure as its credentials and its door. Older 125 kHz fobs carry no encryption, and copiers that duplicate them are sold openly online, so a building that swaps uncounted keys for copyable fobs has traded one unknown for another.

What actually improves security is encrypted credentials that cannot be cloned, one credential per person rather than per apartment, a list someone reviews, and a door that closes and latches every time. A fob read on a door that hangs open is a log entry, not a lock.

On a Homecrest co-op of the 1950s or 1960s that usually means fixing the closer and the strike before anything else, and choosing readers that will still be supported when the building orders fobs ten years from now.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

That is the price of a lock in a box. Installed access control includes the strike or electrified lock, the reader, the cable, a controller, a power supply and battery, programming and the time to make the door close properly.

On our Brooklyn list a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500, a keypad on a personnel or service door is $1,500 to $2,000, and a multi-door building system managed from the cloud is $4,000 to $15,000. A grouted steel frame in a 1961 lobby takes longer to cut than a hollow frame in a new condo, which moves a job within its range.

We put the figure in writing after one visit, with sales tax added, and we do not change it on install day.

“A fob system works anywhere with Wi-Fi.”

Many quick answers describe access control as an internet product. If a building’s doors open only when the internet is up, the building has a single point of failure on its front door, and Brick Underground’s 2018 column described a tenant locked out every time the connection dropped.

The controllers we install keep the list of enrolled credentials on site. If the internet goes down, everyone already enrolled still gets in, the log keeps recording, and the cloud catches up when the connection returns. What you lose for that time is the ability to change the list from a phone.

Wi-Fi is also a poor link through thick brick, so we run cable wherever a cable can go.

“Switching to fobs is a weekend job.”

The hardware for a lobby door can go in within a day. The conversion of a building with sixty or more households takes longer, because people have to be enrolled, informed and given time to collect their credentials, and the old keys have to stop working on a date everyone knows.

When residents are left without a working way in, they tape doors open, as the Queensbridge story above shows. The same mistake looks the same at any size.

We plan a handout schedule with the board or manager, keep the old cylinder working through a short overlap if the board wants one, and set a firm cut-over day.

“An owner can switch the building to app-only entry.”

Some answers treat phone entry as the new standard. In a New York City building with apartments, the Tenant Data Privacy Law, as HPD summarizes it, says owners may not require smart access, which includes apps and fobs, for a tenant to enter their own apartment, and it requires consent, a written privacy policy and limits on the data kept.

In rent-stabilized buildings DHCR rulings add their own expectations. The ruling summarized by LandlordvTenant, for example, required metal keys for emergencies and free fobs for occupants, and capped replacements at $25.

We set up phone keys for the residents who want them and fobs or cards for everyone else, and we never make the app the only way through a residential door.

“A magnetic lock is the easy answer for any door.”

Magnetic locks are simple to mount, which is why they are suggested so often. But a maglock holds a door only while it has power, and on an exit door the NYC Building Code requires release hardware on the door itself that works with one hand and releases the lock immediately, plus release on loss of power and on fire alarm.

Those rules are there because a maglock that fails to release traps people. On amenity doors such as a spa suite or a roof door, the stakes are obvious.

On most Homecrest doors an electric strike or an electrified exit device is the better choice: the door stays locked in an outage while it always opens from inside, and nothing depends on a sensor deciding to let someone out.

“Small shops don’t need access control, just a good lock.”

A good lock is the start. But a store with two employees, relatives who help on weekends and a landlord with a key has the same problem as a building: keys it cannot count and cannot switch off. The city’s survey found that 88 percent of the Homecrest merchants it surveyed rent and the median business has two full-time employees.

A single keypad on the back door with a personal code per person, a schedule that ends staff codes after closing, and an alert if the door opens at night is a modest job that removes the need to rekey every time someone leaves.

We install hardware that comes out cleanly when a lease ends, and we put the landlord’s consent in writing before we drill.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask the Super for His Key Ring

This describes how we start on a mid-century co-op, not a particular customer. Before we look at a single door, we ask the super to show us the key ring he carries and to name what each key opens. On a building of this age the answer is usually a long one: the street door, the vestibule, the garage, the package room, the laundry, the storage cages, the boiler room, the roof, the elevator machine room and a few nobody can identify. Then we ask who else has each of those keys. The silence after that question is the whole case for access control, and it tells us which doors the board will want on the system and in what order.

Then we go to the cellar, because the cellar decides the price. We look for the path the old intercom cable took to the lobby, a dry wall for the controller and its battery, the panel that will feed the power supply, and how far the garage door is from all of it. Only after that do we stand at the street door, open it, let go, and watch whether it closes and latches by itself. If it does not, that is the first thing we fix. A reader on a door that drifts open protects nothing.

Homecrest Access Control FAQ

What would a sixty-apartment Homecrest co-op pay to put its lobby, garage door and package room on fobs?
+

How many spare fobs should a co-op or condo keep on the shelf after the changeover?
+

A good rule of thumb is about one spare for every ten households, plus a few for staff, so a lost fob or a new helper never waits for an order. Spares should be stored blank and locked in the management office or the super’s office, and enrolled only when they are handed to a named person. A board that keeps a short log of who received each spare will never wonder later where one went.

I spend the winter away from my apartment. Can my own fob be paused until I come back?
+

Yes. The super or managing agent can suspend a credential without deleting it and switch it back on the day you return, so a fob lost or borrowed while you are away opens nothing. The same can be done for anyone you have authorized, such as a cleaner who only comes while you are home. Tell the office the dates and leave one contact who can approve a change if something comes up.

Who holds the encryption keys for our building’s fobs, and can we take them to another installer later?
+

That depends on the credential program, and it is worth deciding before anything is ordered. Some encrypted fobs can only be bought through one dealer or one manufacturer’s program, which ties the building to that channel for every future order. We prefer credentials the building can reorder from more than one source, and we put in writing who holds the site keys and how the board gets them if it ever changes installers.

How long do encrypted fobs programmed for our building take to arrive?
+

Fobs and cards keyed to a particular building are a special order, not something that sits on a shelf, so shipping time is set by the manufacturer and is outside our control. We order them once the deposit is paid, give you the arrival date as soon as the supplier confirms it, and book the cut-over only once the credentials are in hand. Hardware for the doors can go in before the fobs arrive.

Can the system point out fobs that have not been used for months, so the board knows which to cancel?
+

Yes. Most access software can list credentials with no activity in a chosen period, such as ninety days. That list is the quickest way to find fobs belonging to people who sold, moved out or stopped working in the building. A building that keeps little personal data can still run this check, since it needs only the date of last use.

If there is trouble on the block, can the super lock every controlled door at once?
+

Yes. A lockdown command, from a button in the super’s office or from the app, can stop every reader from admitting anyone except the people named for an emergency, and cancel any scheduled unlock. It never stops the doors opening from inside, because they are exits, and it does not affect the fire alarm release. We set it up so only a few people can trigger it and it is easy to undo.

Does every controlled door need its own cable all the way back to the controller?
+

Usually, yes, or at least to a nearby door module that then shares one cable back to the controller. Each door has a reader, a lock, a door contact and an exit device, and wiring them all to a point near the door keeps the system reliable. In a long co-op where the garage is at the far end, a small module near the garage saves a long run. We avoid wireless links for doors in cellars or behind thick brick.

Can a reader work from inside, through the glass beside our lobby door, so nothing is mounted outside?
+

Sometimes. Some readers are made to read through glass of a limited thickness, and mounting one inside a sidelight keeps it out of the weather and away from vandals. It works best with plain glass; wired or laminated safety glass and window films can block the signal. We test the actual reader against your glass before recommending it, and if it does not read reliably we mount it outside on the frame instead.

Will cutting a strike into our new lobby door frame void the door maker’s warranty?
+

It can, if the door or frame was not ordered for electric hardware. Before we cut anything on a recently installed door, we ask for the door supplier’s details and check whether a factory prep is available or whether the supplier allows field preparation. On a door still under warranty, the safest route is often a strike or exit device the manufacturer lists for that frame, installed to its template.
