The Neighborhood

Why Beverley Square Properties Need TV Installation

Beverley Square was built to be looked at. The developer Thomas Benton Ackerson laid out the western half on old farmland he bought in 1901, renamed East 13th, 14th and 15th Streets as Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads, and filled them with freestanding houses; a 1906 sale advertisement for one of them on Rugby Road called it a "fine modern" house. The eastern half, from East 16th to East 19th Street, started a few years earlier with larger houses and finished with simpler ones. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's research found that 114 of the 119 houses in the western blocks were complete by 1906 and the last five went up by 1910. When the commission voted on November 25, 2025 to designate the Beverley Square West Historic District, its research director called it the area with "the highest quality architecture and most cohesive streetscapes not yet designated in Victorian Flatbush." For people who live here, that pride shows inside as much as outside. Owners keep the stair, the woodwork, the stained glass and the tiled fireplaces, and they want a TV that does not wreck any of it.

That is the first reason a TV job here is different from one in a newer apartment. The rooms were designed around a fireplace, a bay window and tall double-hung windows, not around a screen. Listing write-ups show the pattern clearly. At 293 Rugby Road, a Colonial Revival house finished by 1906, the old dining room now serves as the living room and its focal point is a corner fireplace with a columned mantel and a green-tile surround. At 327 Marlborough Road, a deep-porched house first sold in 1905, the living room has a gas fireplace and a bay window facing the side yard, and the dining room has a second fireplace with its original tile under a stained-glass window. In rooms like those, the TV usually has to choose between the chimney breast, the one plain wall left between doorways, or a stand that does not need a wall at all. Each choice has a different cost, a different risk to the original fabric and a different answer for where the wires go.

The second reason is the walls themselves. Houses of this age were finished with plaster, usually over wood lath on wood framing, and in places over brick, most often around the chimney. A stud finder that works perfectly on new drywall gives confusing readings on a century-old plaster wall: one Brownstoner owner of an 1899 house said hers worked poorly on plaster and asked how else to find the studs without drilling holes. Plaster over lath can hold a TV very well, but only when the fixing goes through the plaster and the lath into the framing or into an anchor rated for that wall. A plastic plug pushed into plaster alone may feel solid on the day and loosen over months. Chimney breasts add another layer, because the plaster sits on brick that may be thinner or softer than it looks, and a decorative fireplace that has not been lit in decades is still a masonry flue behind the wall.

The third reason is distance. In a freestanding house of three floors plus a cellar, the cable or fiber line usually comes into the basement, the router often lives there or in a front hall, and the TV goes in a parlor, a converted sleeping porch, a top-floor suite or a finished basement den. Getting a wired connection, a cable-box feed or a second TV's signal from one end of the house to the other is part of the job in a way it rarely is in an apartment. The Q train's open cut, which runs along the eastern edge of the western district and gives the two halves their separate identities, also means streets end at the tracks and the walk from a parked van to a back door can be longer than it looks on a map. And the houses are only half the picture. Along Ocean Avenue at the eastern edge, apartment buildings from the 1910s, such as the roughly thirty-unit building at 726 Ocean Avenue on the corner of Beverley Road, bring prewar partitions, a super and building rules into the same zip code.

Finally, the designation changed what people ask us. Since late 2025 we hear the same question in different words: now that the block is landmarked, can we still put a TV on the wall? For an inside wall the answer is yes, with nothing to file; the porch and the outside of the house are a different matter, and we set out the line between the two further down this page.

Six Beverley Square facts that change how a TV goes up

  • Every original house is old enough for plaster. The western blocks were built from 1894 to 1910, and the eastern blocks started in 1898. We expect plaster first, priced at +$45 on our list, and we confirm it with a single pilot hole the bracket plate will hide.
  • Fireplaces are everywhere, and many are not lit. Listings on Rugby and Marlborough Roads describe fireplaces in living rooms and dining rooms, gas inserts and original tile surrounds. Same-Day Repair in Beverley Square

Need Repair in Beverley Square? A Screen Tilting Off a Chimney Breast, Anchors Loosening in Old Plaster, or a Dead Cable to the Parlor TV

The urgent TV calls from houses of this age come in three kinds. The first is a screen that has started to droop or tilt forward, most often on a full-motion arm that gets pulled out every evening. In plaster over lath, that usually means the fixings went into the plaster and lath alone, or into small anchors meant for drywall, and each pull has worked them a little looser. Above a fireplace, it often means the anchors went into the mortar between bricks rather than the brick itself, or into a thin brick facing that was never meant to carry a load. The second is a bracket left behind by a previous owner, with a set of holes, a cord poking out of the plaster and no one sure what is holding what. The third is a TV that is fine on the wall but has lost its picture or its internet because the cable from the cellar was stapled along a joist, crushed, or cut during other work.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back up and do not let anyone pull on it. If two people can lift it down safely, unplug it, lift it off and lay it face up on a blanket, then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our list, and putting the same or a new TV back up on a properly anchored bracket is priced as a new mount on that wall: $179.99 plus $45 for plaster or $65 for brick, and $130 more if it sits above the fireplace. Where the old holes are in the way, we move the bracket enough to reach sound plaster and framing, and we tell you before we start whether the wall itself has a problem, such as plaster that has come away from its lath, which is work for a plasterer rather than a TV installer. For a lost signal, we trace the run from the TV back to the cellar and tell you whether the cable can be replaced along the same route or needs a new one.

TV Installation for Beverley Square's Architecture

Six kinds of home cover almost every TV call we get from between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. What separates them is not the style on the outside but three things inside: what the TV wall is made of, how far the cable has to travel, and who else has a say in what gets drilled.

The 118 designated houses on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads, built 1894 to 1910: plaster on lath, tiled fireplaces, bay windows and porches the commission now oversees.

Prewar apartment houses

Ackerson houses west of the cut: a landmarked street and an ordinary interior wall

The houses on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads were designed by a small group of Brooklyn architects, among them John J. Petit and Benjamin Driesler, mostly in Queen Anne and Colonial Revival styles, with some Tudor Revival and Shingle-style touches. Inside, most follow the same plan: a large entry hall with the original stair, a parlor to one side, a dining room behind it, and the kitchen at the back. The parlor often has a bay window, the dining room often has the fireplace, and in many houses the owners have swapped the two uses so the bigger room with the fireplace is now the living room. That swap is where TV decisions start. A dining room built for a sideboard and a fireplace has very little uninterrupted wall: the fireplace sits on one wall, the windows on another, and the third and fourth walls carry a wide opening to the hall and a door to the kitchen.

For the TV wall itself, the landmark designation changes nothing. The plaster is the same plaster it was in 2024, and a bracket fixed to it is interior work that needs no filing. What the designation does change is how owners feel about holes, and we share that instinct. The plan we follow on these houses is to touch as little original material as possible: one small pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate, fixings that go into framing rather than into decorative moldings, cables that follow existing cavities instead of new channels cut across the plaster, and no fixings into a tile surround, a mantel shelf or a picture rail. If the only good TV wall has wainscoting or a plate rail, we mount above it on the plaster rather than through the woodwork, and if that puts the screen too high, we say so and suggest a stand instead.

The larger houses east of the cut: wide front rooms and tall ceilings

East of the Q train, on East 16th to East 19th Street, the houses began earlier and, on the eastern edge, larger. Early sales were slow, according to the neighborhood's history, and the rest of the eastern blocks were filled with simpler houses by around 1901. These streets were not part of the 2025 designation, so exterior changes follow ordinary city rules rather than the commission's, but the interiors are of the same age and the same construction. The difference we notice most is scale: wider front rooms, higher ceilings and, in some houses, a front parlor and back parlor joined by a wide opening. A tall room changes the viewing math. People sit farther from the screen, the windows are taller and throw more light across it, and there is a temptation to hang the TV high because the wall is high. We mark the screen at seated eye height with painter's tape and let you look at it from the sofa before we decide anything.

Tall rooms also make cable runs longer, because the router is often in a back room or the cellar while the TV is at the front. We look for the route the house already offers, up from the cellar ceiling or along a closet, and if a cavity is blocked we say so before cutting anything; the fallback is a baseboard cord cover or a wireless link, never a channel cut into the plaster.

Bungalows with a full upper floor: two fireplaces, one TV

Some Beverley Square houses are called bungalows for their deep porches and broad eaves even though, as Brownstoner said of 327 Marlborough Road, a full second story "stretches the definition of bungalow." That house is a useful example of a problem we see often: it has a gas fireplace in the living room with a bay window facing the side yard, and a second fireplace in the dining room with its original tile and insert under a stained-glass window. Two fireplaces and a bay leave very little plain wall, and the obvious place for a TV is over one of the mantels. Above a gas fireplace that is used, heat is a smaller worry than above a wood fire, but the screen still sits higher than is comfortable, so the conversation is about height first: a tilting bracket, or a pull-down mantel-style mount that brings the screen to eye level when you watch and lifts it out of the way when you do not.

Houses with a top-floor suite: a second household's TV, two floors from the router

The listing for 293 Rugby Road describes a legal two-family that is lived in as one house, with a top-floor guest suite that has a separate bedroom and a full kitchen, "ideal for mother-daughter living." Houses set up like that ask for a TV plan that treats the top floor as its own home. The person living there wants their own TV, their own remote and their own streaming accounts, and does not want to depend on a box in the parlor downstairs. That usually means a TV on the suite's own wall, a streaming device or a provider app on the TV, and a network connection strong enough for it, which in a three-story house may mean a wired run from the cellar or a second Wi-Fi access point on the top floor.

When the house might someday go back to being two separate homes, it is worth placing the cable runs as if it already were. A run that goes up a common stair wall can serve either floor; a run that goes through the downstairs owner's bedroom closet becomes awkward the day that bedroom belongs to someone else. We draw the route on a sketch before we start and leave it with you, along with a label on each end of each cable, so the next person who works in that house knows what went where.

Houses already renovated: a new wall beside an old one

Plenty of houses here have had a kitchen opened to the dining room, a parlor skimmed over with drywall, or a cellar finished into a den. Renovations leave a mix. A wall that looks like new drywall may be drywall laid over the original plaster, which means a longer screw and a different feel when we drill; a wall that was opened and rebuilt may have new wood or steel studs where the old framing was; a finished cellar may be drywall on furring strips over a stone or brick foundation. We do not assume anything from how a wall looks. The pilot hole and the scanner tell us, and the price follows what is actually there, not what the wall looks like from the sofa.

Prewar apartment houses: the super, the partitions and the long hallway

The apartment buildings along Ocean Avenue and at the ends of the two main roads are a different job altogether. Brownstoner's Building of the Day on 726 Ocean Avenue, at Beverley Road, dates it between 1912 and 1918 and notes it still has about the thirty apartments it was built with, which means large rooms by today's standards. Buildings of that era often used hollow clay tile or gypsum block for the partitions between rooms, with plaster over them, and masonry for the outside walls and the walls around stairs and shafts. Hollow tile is the wall that catches out handymen: it sounds solid, a standard masonry anchor can break through its thin face into the void behind, and a plastic plug spins. The fix is an anchor made for hollow masonry, set with a slow drill so the face does not shatter, and more fixings at lower load each.

The building itself has rules. The super or the managing agent may want to know who is coming and when, may ask for a certificate of insurance before the visit, may set hours for noisy work, and may say that no wall can be opened for wiring. We ask about all of that when we book, and if the building will not allow an in-wall kit, the cables go into a paintable cord cover or drop straight into a console below the screen. Bring us the building's rules sheet if there is one; it saves a phone call on the day.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Walk Through a 1900s Beverley Square House Before a TV Goes Up

These notes describe how our technicians work in houses like the ones between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. They are our routine, not a story about a particular address.

Start at the bottom. Before looking at the TV wall, the technician asks to see where the internet and cable come into the house, usually in the cellar near the front. That tells us how long the run is, whether there is a cavity under the TV wall to come up through, and whether an old coax line is still in use or just left behind. It takes five minutes and changes the plan more often than anything upstairs.

Look at the fireplace before the wall beside it. If the TV is going near a fireplace, we ask whether it is used, whether it is wood, gas or purely decorative, and we look for the chimney breast's edges. The flue runs up the middle of the breast; the sound brick for anchors is usually toward the sides and well above the firebox. A decorative fireplace that has not been lit in years still has a flue, and we treat it as one.

Knock, scan, then drill once. On plaster over lath, a scanner in deep mode finds most of the framing, but not always, and old houses rarely have studs at modern spacing. A knock along the wall at screen height tells us where it sounds hollow and where it sounds dead. Then one small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it, tells us the rest: white powder and a give into wood for plaster on lath, red grit for brick, a sudden drop for a cavity.

Tape it and sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen on the wall, and we ask you to sit where you watch. In a room with tall windows we check the glare at the time of day you watch most, and in a room with a mantel we check the angle from the sofa. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket costs plaster.

Protect what cannot be replaced. Inlaid floors, stained glass and original woodwork get covered or kept clear of the ladder. Dust from the pilot hole and the bracket holes is caught at the wall with a vacuum, not swept off the mantel afterward.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Chimney Brick and Frame Walls: What Holds a TV in a Beverley Square House

Our Brooklyn base price, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on a bracket on drywall over wood studs, labor only. Hardly any original room in Beverley Square has drywall over wood studs, which is why we explain the wall charges before anything else. Each one exists because that wall takes different tools, different fixings and more time.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). This is the wall of almost every original room here: thin wooden strips nailed across the studs and covered with coats of plaster. It holds a load well when the fixing reaches the framing, and poorly when it relies on the plaster alone, because old plaster is hard on the face and crumbly behind it. A Brownstoner commenter put the difference simply, noting that on a non-party wall with plaster over wood lath you can often screw into the lath and framing without an anchor, while a party wall of brick "rarely" has studs at all. We drill the plaster slowly with a sharp bit so it does not crack outward, then drive the fixings through the lath into the stud, or use a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Studs in these houses are not always where a modern tape measure expects them, so we find them one at a time.

Brick behind plaster, mainly chimney breasts (+$65). In a freestanding frame house, brick usually means the chimney. Plaster applied straight onto brick feels and sounds different from plaster on lath, and the pilot hole confirms it with red dust. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor goes into the brick, not into the softer mortar joint, and if the brick is the solid kind and not a thin facing. In the same Brownstoner thread, a contractor warned an owner that the brick behind her fireplace "might be shallow," and another commenter recommended long lead shields through the plaster into the brick for heavy loads. We test the depth with the pilot hole, keep the anchors away from the flue in the middle of the breast, and on old, soft brick use more fixings at lower load each.

Above the fireplace (+$130, plus the wall charge). A mount over a mantel costs more because it means drilling a chimney breast, working higher on a ladder over a mantel shelf and a hearth that must not be scratched, and almost always using a tilting or pull-down bracket so the screen does not end up uncomfortably high. On our list the fireplace charge sits on top of the wall charge, so a 55-inch TV over a brick chimney breast comes to $179.99 plus $130 plus $65, or $374.99 before the bracket.

Outside walls of a frame house. Because the houses stand alone, the long wall of a living room is often an outside wall: plaster on lath on the inside, framing, then sheathing and clapboard or shingle outside. It mounts exactly like any other plaster wall, at the plaster rate, but it is the wall where we are most careful about cables, because a cavity on an outside wall may hold old insulation, and it is the wall where any new line from the street would come through. We do not drill through to the outside without your say-so, and on a house in the western blocks, an exterior opening is a Landmarks question, as the next section explains.

Hiding the wires in an old house without breaking the electrical code

The most common request after "put it on the wall" is "make the wires disappear," and old houses tempt people to do it the wrong way. The wrong way is pushing the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and out of a hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls (section 400.12 in recent editions), because a cord in a wall can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and cannot be inspected. A StreetEasy discussion about mounting TVs in prewar walls made the related point that an outlet cannot simply be buried behind a wall, and one poster warned that the plastic-sheathed cable supplied in some power kits is not what New York City expects inside walls: "Must use metallic armored cable ("BX") in NYC walls." Whatever is hidden has to be hidden with materials made and listed for it.

The right way, on a framed plaster wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It gives a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into an existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a clear cavity between the two openings, so it suits plaster on lath between studs and does not suit a chimney breast. Where a cavity is blocked, or the wall is brick, the choices are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly on the surface and takes your wall color, or a TV placed over a console so the cables drop straight down behind it. The Historic District

Inside and Outside the Line: What the Beverley Square West Designation Means for a TV

The Beverley Square West Historic District is new, and a lot of owners are still working out what it covers. We are installers, not preservation lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice; it is our reading of the commission's own published guidance, so you know what to ask before booking. The commission calendared the district on June 17, 2025, heard testimony on October 21, 2025 and designated it on November 25, 2025, covering 118 houses on the three blocks between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road west of the subway cut. A few properties were left out; Brownstoner reported that 359 Rugby Road was removed because of significant alterations.

Inside the house. The commission's list of permit types says its permits are needed for interior work in three cases: work that needs a Department of Buildings permit, work that affects the exterior, such as "HVAC louvers and vents," and work in a designated interior landmark. A TV bracket on a parlor wall, a soundbar, a cord cover and low-voltage cables inside the walls are none of those. So for an indoor TV in a house on Argyle, Rugby or Marlborough Road, the designation does not add a step.

Outside the house. The same page says permits are needed for "any project that affects the exterior of the building, even parts of the building that are not visible from the street." That is the sentence that matters for three requests we hear. A weatherproof TV under the porch roof is fixed to the house's exterior. A new cable or fiber line brought in through the clapboard, or a new hole in the foundation for one, changes the exterior. An antenna or a satellite dish on the roof or a chimney is on the exterior, whether or not it shows from the sidewalk. None of those is necessarily refused, and the commission issues many permits at staff level for work that meets its rules, but they are the owner's application to make, not something an installer should quietly do. We will tell you what the work involves and give you photos and dimensions for the application, and we will not drill the exterior of a house in the district until you tell us the permit is in hand.

East of the cut. East 16th to East 19th Street were not included in the 2025 designation. Exterior work there follows ordinary city rules, which for a TV under a porch roof usually means none at all. Owners on those streets still often ask us to treat their houses with the same care, and we do, because the clapboard and the porch posts are just as old.

Temporary setups need no permit anywhere. A TV on its stand on a porch table for a summer evening, unplugged and carried in afterward, fixes nothing to the house. On our list a TV placed on its stand and connected is $119.99, and it is the simplest way to watch a game outside without touching a landmarked porch.

Compliance notes for Beverley Square businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Beverley Square: Questions Answered

These are the situations Beverley Square owners, renters and organizations describe when they call: fireplaces set in awkward places, rooms that were porches a century ago, a router three floors from the TV, a brand-new historic district and apartment walls a super calls terra cotta. Each one is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Mantels, chimneys and the one wall everybody wants

The fireplace in our living room is set diagonally across a corner, the way the one at 293 Rugby Road is. Can a TV go above a corner fireplace?

Sometimes, and it is worth checking before you buy a bracket. A corner fireplace has a chimney breast that is angled across the room, so the face above the mantel is narrower than a straight chimney breast of the same house, and it often ends close to the ceiling or a crown molding. We measure that face first: a TV wider than the flat part of the breast would overhang its edges, which looks wrong and leaves the outer bolts with nothing sound to bite. If the face is wide enough, the TV goes on a tilting or pull-down bracket anchored into the brick on either side of the flue, and on our list it is $179.99 plus $130 for above the fireplace and $65 for brick, or $374.99 for a TV up to 55 inches before the bracket. If the face is too narrow, a corner of a room with a diagonal fireplace usually leaves two good straight walls on either side, and one of them often gives a better view from the sofa anyway.

A contractor told us to rest the TV on the mantel shelf instead of drilling the chimney breast. Is that a good idea?

It can work for a light TV on a deep, solid shelf, but it is usually the weakest of the options. The contractor in a Brownstoner discussion gave the same advice to an owner whose fireplace was decorative, and other commenters replied that anchors through the plaster into the brick would hold without relying on the mantel. The problems with the shelf are depth, height and safety: most period mantels are only a few inches deep, a TV standing on its feet up there sits even higher than a wall-mounted one, and a screen on a shelf can be tipped forward by a child, a pet or a slammed door unless it is strapped back to the wall, which means drilling anyway. If the reason for avoiding the wall is protecting the chimney breast, the fixings for a wall bracket go into a handful of small holes that the bracket hides and that can be filled later. If the reason is the height, that is a reason to look at another wall, not to put the TV even higher on the shelf.

We still burn wood in the parlor fireplace a few times a winter, and we also have a gas insert in the dining room. Which mantel is the safer one for a TV?

The gas insert, in most houses. Homes & Gardens quoted one designer saying that "placing a TV above a wood-burning fireplace can be risky since the heat and smoke emitted can damage it," and noted that gas and electric fireplaces pose little risk by comparison. A mantel helps by pushing rising heat away from the wall, and a deeper mantel helps more. Before choosing, look at the TV's manual for its operating temperature, and on a fire night put your hand flat on the wall above the mantel where the bottom of the screen would be: if it is uncomfortable to hold your hand there, it is too warm for electronics. The other thing to compare is viewing height. A dining-room mantel is often set lower than a parlor one, which makes a big difference to a screen that has to start above it. Whichever you choose, we use a bracket that keeps a gap behind the TV for air, and the wall charge and the $130 fireplace charge are the same for both.

Rooms that were never meant for a screen

Our old sleeping porch is now a home office lined with bead-board. Can a monitor or a TV go on bead-board, and how does it get power out there?

Bead-board is thin tongue-and-groove paneling, and it cannot carry a TV on its own, but it is usually nailed to framing that can. On a sleeping porch that was enclosed later, the outer wall is often the original porch framing with windows added, and the inner wall is the old exterior wall of the house, sometimes with the original clapboard still behind the paneling. We find the framing behind the bead-board, fix the bracket through the paneling into it, and keep the screws in the grooves where they show least. Power is the harder part, because enclosed porches often have only one or two outlets. A paintable cord cover ($59) running along the baseboard or a door casing is the usual answer on paneling, since cutting into bead-board for an in-wall kit leaves seams that are hard to hide. The listing for 293 Rugby Road describes exactly this kind of room: a sleeping porch turned into an office with double-hung windows and white bead-board.

Our front bedroom is inside a rounded turret. Can a TV go on a curved wall?

Not directly on the curve, but often close to it. A flat bracket on a curved wall touches the plaster only along a vertical line in the middle, so the outer screws either pull the bracket out of shape or sit in air. There are three ways around it. If the turret meets a flat wall, the TV usually goes on the flat wall facing into the turret, which gives the bed the view and keeps the windows free. If the curve is gentle and the framing is sound, a single-stud or narrow wall plate on a full-motion arm can be fixed to the framing at the center of the curve, with spacers behind it so it sits true. And if neither works, a TV on a low dresser opposite the bed, placed on its stand for $119.99, avoids the curved wall entirely. In every case we scan the turret first, because the framing in a curved wall is closer together and harder to read than in a straight one.

Getting a signal from the cellar to the room

The internet comes into our cellar at the front of the house, and the new TV is going in the top-floor suite. How does a wired line get up three floors without opening walls?

By following the house rather than fighting it. In many houses of this age, we can get up the first stretch through the cellar ceiling into a wall cavity that runs to the floor above, often inside a closet or beside the stair, and then continue up through stacked closets or along the stair wall. Where a cavity is blocked by framing, we use a small hole in a closet ceiling or floor rather than in a room's plaster, and every hole is sized for the cable and filled afterward. Before deciding on a wired run at all, we check whether a streaming device on the TV plus a Wi-Fi access point on the top floor will do the job, because in a lot of houses it will, and it means no run at all. If the suite belongs to a separate household, we label the cable at both ends and give you a sketch of its route. The run is quoted from your photos before we book, so there is no surprise on the day.

Will a century-old plaster wall and a chimney breast between the router and the parlor weaken the Wi-Fi a streaming TV depends on?

They can, and it is easy to check before blaming the TV. Old plaster is dense, a chimney breast between the router and the TV blocks a lot of signal, and in some houses earlier renovations added metal lath or foil-backed insulation that blocks Wi-Fi far more than wood lath does. We look at where the router actually sits, often in a cabinet or on the floor of a back room, and test the signal at the TV. The fixes, cheapest first, are moving the router somewhere higher and more central, adding an access point, or running a wired connection to the TV, which is the most reliable for streaming and the one we test with a cable tester before the wall plate goes on. If the problem turns out to be the provider's line into the house rather than anything inside, we tell you so and you call them; the line outside the house is theirs.

No. The Beverley Square West Historic District covers the houses west of the subway cut, on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads; East 16th to East 19th Street were not included. Outside, your house follows ordinary city rules rather than the commission's, which matters only if you want a TV under the porch roof, a new cable entry or an antenna. We want a weatherproof TV under our wraparound porch roof, on the side that faces the yard. If nobody can see it from the street, does Landmarks still need to approve it?

If your house is inside the Beverley Square West district, yes, as we read the commission's published rules. Its list of permit types says a permit is needed for "any project that affects the exterior of the building, even parts of the building that are not visible from the street," and a bracket bolted to a porch wall or post is exterior work. The application is the owner's to file; we can give you the TV's dimensions, the bracket model, the fixing points and photos of the spot to include with it. Our provider has to run a new line into the house for the TV room. On a landmarked house, who deals with the outside?

The provider brings its line to the house and through the exterior; we handle everything inside from where it enters. On a house in the western blocks, a new hole through the clapboard or the foundation, a new box on the outside wall and a cable run up the facade all affect the exterior, so ask the provider before the appointment where they intend to enter and whether they can reuse an existing entry point. Reusing the old entry, often near where the original phone or cable line came in on the side or the back of the house, is usually the least visible choice. Once the line is inside, we take it from the cellar to the TV room through the house's own cavities, as described above, and label both ends. If the provider's plan involves anything new on the outside of a landmarked house, that is the owner's conversation with the commission, not something either installer should decide alone.

Buying, renting and the apartment buildings

We are buying a legal two-family that the sellers used as one house. Should the TV wiring plan assume it might become two homes again?

It is worth planning that way, because it costs almost nothing now and saves a lot later. Listings here describe this arrangement often; 293 Rugby Road, for example, is a legal two-family used as a single home, with a top-floor suite that has its own kitchen. If the upper floor might be rented or given to family as a separate household, run its TV and internet cables up a shared wall, such as the stair or a common closet, rather than through a downstairs bedroom, and give each floor its own path from the cellar. Put each floor's TV on a wall that belongs to that floor, not on the partition by the stair that both floors share. And label everything at both ends. When you get the keys, send us the floor plan from the listing and photos of each room, and we will mark where we would put each TV and each run before you buy any furniture.

The super in our Ocean Avenue building says the wall we want is "terra cotta." What does that mean for a TV?

It almost certainly means hollow clay tile, a common partition material in New York apartment buildings of the 1910s and 1920s: blocks of fired clay with hollow cells inside, plastered over. It is strong in compression but has thin faces, and the wrong anchor either spins in the void or cracks the face of the tile. We use anchors made for hollow masonry, drill slowly without hammer action until we are through the face, and spread the load across more fixings than we would on brick. On our list it is priced as masonry, $179.99 plus $65 for a TV up to 55 inches. The other thing a super's comment tells us is that he knows the building, so ask him whether the wall has a pipe chase or riser inside it, and whether the building lets residents open walls for an in-wall kit. If it does not, the cables go into a paintable cord cover ($59) or straight down into a console.

FAQ

Beverley Square TV Installation FAQ

Can drilling into an old chimney breast bring soot or dust out of the flue into the room?
+

It can if a hole goes too deep or lands in the flue, which is why the anchors go into the solid brick toward the sides of the breast and only as deep as the anchor needs. Dust from drilling is caught at the wall with a vacuum, and the mantel and hearth are covered first.

Should we take the clock, candles and photos off the mantel before the technician comes?
+

Yes, please. Clear the mantel shelf and anything fragile within about six feet of the TV wall. The technician works from a ladder over the shelf and covers the hearth, and it is safer for your things to be in another room.

Will the test hole in our plaster show once the TV is up?
+

No. The single pilot hole that tells us what is behind the plaster is drilled inside the outline of the bracket plate, so the plate covers it and the TV covers the plate. If the TV ever comes down, it is filled along with the bracket holes.

What happens if the plaster on our chosen wall turns out to have come loose from its lath?
+

The technician shows you before going further. Plaster that has lost its grip on the lath should not carry a TV until a plasterer has reattached or replaced it, so we either move to a sound part of the wall, choose another wall or put the TV on its stand for now.

Should we paint the parlor before or after the TV is mounted?
+

Before, if you can. Painting around a mounted screen is awkward and paint on a bracket rarely looks good. If the TV has to go up first, the bracket can be removed for painting later, and a paintable cord cover can be painted with the wall at the same time.

Our power went out during the Beverly Road manhole fire in June 2025. Should the new TV be on a surge protector?
+

It is a sensible precaution in any house. News 12 reported that the fire left at least 1,700 Con Edison customers without power, and the moment power returns is when electronics are most exposed. Have a surge protector ready and we will plug the TV, soundbar and streaming device into it during setup.

While the technician is there for the TV, can the router be moved out of the cellar to a more central spot?
+

Often, yes, if your provider's line allows it and you tell us when you book, so the time is included in the quote. Moving the router higher and closer to the middle of the house is the simplest way to strengthen Wi-Fi for a streaming TV in an old plaster house.

Our house has a driveway. Can the technician park in it during the visit?
+

Yes, and it helps. A driveway means the TV, the ladder and the tools come in through the side or back door without a long carry from the corner. Tell us when you book whether the driveway will be clear and which door is easiest.
