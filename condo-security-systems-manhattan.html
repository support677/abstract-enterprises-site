The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Condominium Buildings Need a Security Plan the Board Can Defend

Manhattan condominium security is shaped by one fact that buyers notice and boards sometimes forget: in a condo, each owner holds a deed to a unit, and the board of managers holds responsibility for everything the owners share. The lobby door, the garage ramp, the gym on the amenity floor, the package room and the riser cable that carries every intercom call are common elements. When one of them fails, nobody owns it personally, so the repair waits for whoever has authority to approve it. A condo security plan exists to shorten that wait and to make sure the next decision about the building's security is made deliberately rather than in a hurry on a Friday night.

New York's Condominium Act puts that structure in writing. Real Property Law §339-v requires every condominium's bylaws to cover the election of a board of managers, the board's powers and duties, whether it may hire a managing agent, and the "operation of the property, payment of the common expenses and determination and collection of the common charges" (NY Senate, RPL §339-v). The same section lets bylaws set up funds for "major and minor maintenance, repairs, additions, improvements, replacements." Security hardware lives inside those clauses. Who approves a new entry panel, whether it comes out of reserves or a special assessment, and which house rules govern fobs and footage all trace back to the declaration and bylaws your offering plan described when the building was sold.

The buildings themselves raise the stakes. Much of the Manhattan condo stock built since 2005 is new development: glass lobbies with a concierge desk, amenity floors with pools and screening rooms, valet or self-park garages, and package rooms sized for a building that orders everything online. Those features add doors, and every door is a decision about who gets in and when. Developers often hand over a building with a security system chosen to sell units, and the board inherits it at turnover along with service contracts it never negotiated. Other condos are conversions: prewar rentals sold off as condominiums, lofts in Tribeca and SoHo, and now offices turned into homes. Each brings old wiring, odd entrances and a mix of residential and commercial owners who all need different access.

Then there is volume. Packages are the most visible security problem in a Manhattan condo lobby because the city ships so many of them. The New York Times reported in December 2019 that an estimated 90,000 packages a day disappear in New York City without explanation (The New York Times, Dec 3, 2019). A doorman building can absorb some of that. A non-doorman boutique condo with a vestibule, or a tower whose desk closes at midnight, needs the intercom, the package room door and the camera over it to do the work instead. Property managers running several buildings can read our property management security in Manhattan page.

This page is written for condo boards of managers, condo association and HOA members, unit owners and managing agents who want one contractor to look at the whole building. It starts with the entry, because that is where residents feel failures first and where the law sets a floor, then moves through access control, elevators, cameras, garages and amenity spaces. Every section ends by pointing to the Manhattan service page that goes deeper. If your building is a cooperative rather than a condominium, the governance is different, and our co-op security systems page for Manhattan is written for shareholders and co-op boards instead.

Manhattan condo numbers worth knowing before the board meets

  • Condos price per foot well above co-ops: in June 2026 the average Manhattan condo signed at $2,054 per square foot, while co-ops averaged $1,384, and co-op contracts rose 16% year over year against 9% for condos (Corcoran, Jul 9, 2026).
  • New development sets the expectations: the median Manhattan new-development condo sale reached $2.5 million in the second quarter of 2026, against a $1.25 million median for condos and co-ops combined (Brick Underground, Jul 2, 2026).
  • Thousands of new units still unsold: about 3,000 new-development units were on the Manhattan market in the third quarter of 2026, and 240 new-development contracts were signed that quarter, down from 331 a year earlier, per Brown Harris Stevens Development Marketing data (CRE Daily, Oct 7, 2026). Buildings still in sponsor sell-out often run with sponsor-controlled boards.
  • Conversions keep adding condos: Corcoran Sunshine projected 317 condo units from conversions of offices, hotels and industrial buildings reaching the city market in 2026, alongside nearly 2,000 rentals (Bisnow, Dec 17, 2025).
  • Intercoms are a legal baseline: class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a lobby intercom that lets each apartment talk to the door and release its lock (NY Senate, MDL §50-a).
  • Smart entry has data rules: NYC Local Law 63 of 2021 requires owners of class A multiple dwellings that use smart access systems to get written or in-app consent and to destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized (Local Law 63 of 2021).

Intercom First

Condo Intercom Replacement in Manhattan: When the Board Should Stop Repairing

Most Manhattan condo boards call us about the intercom before anything else, and the call usually starts with a repair. A lobby panel that drops calls to the upper floors, a garage station that rings nobody, residents on one line of units who hear the visitor but cannot be heard back. Those are fixable, and a repair visit starts with the $250 service call. The harder question is the one boards ask on the third or fourth visit: is this condo intercom replacement time, or are we still in repair territory? Our answer rests on a few plain tests rather than on the age of the system alone.

The first test is parts. If the manufacturer has discontinued the lobby panel or the in-unit stations, every failure becomes a hunt for used inventory, and a building can end up with three generations of handsets that do not behave the same way. The second is pattern: one failure a year is maintenance, but repeated failures across different floors usually point to a tired riser, a failing power supply design or a controller near the end of its life. The third is fit. A legacy audio intercom built around a landline or a single door cannot answer a garage, a side entrance, a package room and a resident's phone at once, and new-development condos and conversions often need all of those. When two of the three tests fail, we tell the board it is looking at a condominium intercom replacement rather than another repair, and we put the reasoning in writing so the managing agent can share it.

Replacement does not have to mean tearing the building open. Manhattan condo intercom modernization usually takes one of three shapes. The lightest reuses the existing riser cable with new equipment designed to run over it, which keeps work inside apartments short. The middle path puts a new IP entry panel at the door and sends calls to residents' phones through an app, so units need little or no new wiring and owners who want an in-unit screen can add one. The heaviest runs new network cabling up the building for full video stations in every unit, which suits gut-renovated conversions and buildings where the old riser is damaged or impossible to trace. Which one fits depends on what we find on the walk-through, especially in the riser closet and a sample of units on different lines.

The board's side of an intercom replacement is mostly paperwork, and we write the scope to make it easier. A condo board of managers usually wants three things before voting: a clear statement of what is being replaced and what is reused, a schedule that shows how long the lobby will be without a working panel and which units need access, and a price it can compare against other bids from an RFP or informal bid request. We provide all three in one document. Funding is the board's call. Some boards pay from reserves, some fold the work into a capital plan, and some levy a special assessment; the declaration and bylaws decide what the board may do, and its attorney and accountant advise on it. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Unit access is the part residents feel. A modernization that keeps in-unit stations means a technician in each apartment, and repairs that trace a fault along a riser need access to every affected unit on that line because the units share the cable. We give the managing agent a floor-by-floor schedule in 2-hour arrival windows and confirm each one. Owners who are away can leave a key with the resident manager. When a building chooses app-based answering, most owners never need a visit at all; they accept an invitation and the lobby panel rings their phones.

The equipment we install covers the major video intercom platforms boards compare, including Akuvox, ButterflyMX, Aiphone, Comelit, 2N and DoorBird, and a non-branded house system by default unless the board asks for a specific brand. Some of these carry a monthly subscription from the maker and some do not, and we say which is which in the proposal. Our Akuvox vs ButterflyMX comparison lays out two of the most common choices side by side. For hardware detail and Manhattan installation pricing, see our page on intercom installation in Manhattan. If the system you have simply stopped working, the right first stop is intercom repair in Manhattan. Buzzers & Door Releases

Condo Door Buzzers and Releases That Stop Working in Manhattan

The intercom gets the attention, but a large share of condo entry complaints are really door complaints. A resident presses the button, the panel says the door is open, and the visitor is still standing outside. That is usually the release hardware: an electric strike out of alignment after a winter of the door being slammed, a closer that no longer pulls the door tight enough for the strike to catch, a magnetic lock that lost power, or a transformer that has aged out. In glass-front new-development lobbies the frame and hardware are often specified by the architect, so replacement parts have to match what is already there.

We diagnose the buzzer and the door together on one service call. If the strike or lock is the problem, we replace or adjust it; if the closer is the problem, we tell the managing agent, because a closer that will not latch can defeat any access system. When the release circuit needs new power, we use low-voltage supplies and leave any new line-voltage circuit to the building's electrician. For a condo with an older buzzer system and no video, a buzzer repair is often the cheapest way to buy the board time while it plans a fuller replacement. Our door buzzer repair page for Manhattan covers the common failures and how the $250 service call applies.

Key Fobs & Access

Condo Access Control in Manhattan: Fobs, Amenity Floors and Who Holds the Keys

Condo access control is where the difference between a deeded unit and shared space becomes practical. An owner's front door is the owner's business. The gym, the pool, the roof deck, the children's playroom, the storage cages and the bike room belong to everyone, and the board has to decide who may use them and when. A key fob system makes that decision enforceable: readers on each shared door, a credential for each resident, and a schedule that can open the pool at seven and close it at ten without anyone holding a key ring.

The best condo key fob installation is planned around how the building actually moves. Residents need the front door, the elevator and the amenity floors. Staff need service entrances, the mechanical room, the trash room and the loading dock. Contractors working on a renovation need one floor and one service door for a fixed number of weeks. Dog walkers, cleaners and caregivers need narrow, time-limited access that the owner can request and the managing agent can approve. A modern system handles all of those as separate credential groups, and when an owner sells, every fob tied to that unit can be turned off in one step. That last point is worth stating plainly because it is the most common gap we see in older condo systems: nobody knows how many active fobs belong to units that changed hands years ago.

Mobile credentials are now common in Manhattan condos. Residents open doors with a phone app instead of, or alongside, a fob, and guests can receive a time-limited pass. Short-term guests deserve a careful word. The access system can issue a temporary credential to anyone the building approves and expire it automatically, but whether a condo permits short stays at all is a matter for the bylaws, the house rules and city law, and the board's attorney should answer it. We build the system to whatever policy the board adopts.

Privacy rules apply once a building goes keyless. New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021 covers any class A multiple dwelling that uses a smart access system, meaning keyless entry by fob, card, app or biometrics. It requires the owner to give residents a plain-language written policy covering what data the system collects, who it is shared with, how it is protected and how long it is kept; it bars collecting a user's reference data without express consent in writing or through a mobile app; and it requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized (Local Law 63 of 2021). How those duties apply to a particular condominium is a question for its counsel, but we set up systems so the logs, retention period and credential lists can match the policy the board writes.

Existing hardware does not always have to go. If a condo's readers are working and the controller is still supported, we can often keep them, replace only the software or controller, and add doors over time. If the building inherited a closed system from the developer and cannot get administrator access, we look at what is recoverable before recommending new equipment. Elevator Access

Elevator Floor Access for Manhattan Condo Towers

In a Manhattan condo tower, the elevator is often the real front door. Full-floor and half-floor residences open straight off the elevator landing, penthouse owners expect nobody else to reach their floor, and amenity levels need to be reachable by residents but not by a delivery driver who slipped in behind someone. Elevator access control handles that by putting a reader in the cab and letting each credential select only the floors it is authorized for, while the lobby desk or the intercom can grant a single visitor one ride to one floor.

This work always involves the building's elevator maintenance company, because the reader has to talk to the car controller and only the elevator contractor touches that equipment. Newer controllers in recent towers usually have a clean interface; older ones in converted buildings may need the elevator company to add one first, which it prices on its own. We arrange a joint visit before the board approves anything, set the floor schedules with the managing agent, and make sure fire service operation still overrides restrictions as the elevator code requires. More on readers, destination-dispatch systems and Manhattan scheduling is on our elevator access control installation page for Manhattan.

Condo Cameras

Condo Security Cameras: What a Manhattan Board Should Cover First

Condo security camera installation in Manhattan comes down to a short list: the entrance and vestibule, the package room or mailroom, the garage entry lane, service and loading doors, and the amenity spaces where expensive equipment sits. A board that covers those spots with cameras that record faces clearly has most of what it needs to answer the questions residents actually ask, which are almost always about a missing package, a damaged car or a stranger who got in behind someone.

Footage policy matters as much as coverage. A 2023 Habitat Q&A described a Midtown East condo where the board president installed cameras above the doorman's desk and in the passenger and freight elevators, with only the president and the superintendent able to view them. Attorney Andrew Lieb said the board's authority depends on the declaration and bylaws, and that "a house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable"; the article also noted that recording conversations without a party's consent is unlawful in New York (Habitat, Aug 28, 2023). For that reason we install condo cameras for video only by default, and we recommend the board adopt a written policy on who can view, who can export, and how long recordings are kept before the system goes live.

Unit owners raise a related question about video doorbells on their own apartment doors, which look into a common hallway. Habitat reported in February 2023 that boards were having to write policies on placement and recording because hallways are common space; Manhattan lawyer Debra Guzov, who represents co-op and condo boards, put it simply for co-ops: residents "can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways" (Habitat, Feb 20, 2023). A building intercom with an app often removes the reason owners want their own doorbell camera, because they can already see the lobby visitor on their phone.

This page keeps the camera discussion short on purpose. Equipment choices, recorder sizes and Manhattan camera pricing live on our apartment building security camera installation page for Manhattan, and condo-specific camera work has its own page: condo security camera installation in Manhattan.

Garages, Packages & Amenities

Garages, Package Rooms, Amenity Floors and the Edges of Our Scope

Manhattan condos have more shared spaces than most residential buildings in the city, and each one has its own failure mode. Garages are the classic example. A self-park or valet garage under a condo has a vehicle door, a pedestrian door, a ramp and often a direct elevator to the residential floors, which makes it a second lobby with no doorman. We put a reader on the pedestrian door, a credential on each resident vehicle where the garage operator allows it, an intercom station at the vehicle entry so a visitor can be let in, and a camera on the lane that reads plates in daylight and records clearly at night. The gate operator itself is mechanical and electrical work by the garage's door company; we connect our readers and intercom to it.

Package rooms are the second. A condo that receives hundreds of deliveries a week needs the room locked, a record of who opened it, and a way for couriers to get in without a resident or staff member present. Options include a fob-controlled door with courier codes issued at the intercom, a camera over the shelves and the door, or a smart locker system from a locker provider that we integrate with the access control. We install and connect the security side; the locker hardware and its subscription belong to the locker company.

Amenity floors are the third: gyms, pools, spas, screening rooms, lounges and roof decks. Here the questions are hours, guests and liability. We set readers to the schedule the board approves, add a camera where valuable equipment sits, and link the door to the intercom so a resident can buzz a guest up to the floor. Clubhouse-style rooms and tennis or sport courts on larger campuses get the same treatment.

Fire alarm work is separate, and we describe it only within our licensing scope. Code-required fire alarm systems in Manhattan condos are designed from engineer-sealed drawings and filed with the FDNY, and access control has to release on alarm where the code requires it. Our fire alarm installation page for Manhattan explains how that work is done and quoted per building. We do not bundle cameras with fire alarm work, and we do not do electrical work or patch and paint walls; a contractor of the board's choosing handles finish repairs after a cable run.

Building Specialists

Condo Security for Manhattan's Architecture

Manhattan condominiums come in more shapes than any other kind of housing in the borough, and the right security design follows the building rather than a catalog. These are the types we work in most and what changes from one to the next.

New-development glass towers

Concierge desk, amenity floors, garage and package room. The intercom and access system is usually networked from day one, and the board's first job at turnover is getting administrator control of it.

Boutique condos of 8 to 30 units

No full-time staff, a single vestibule, sometimes a keyed elevator. The entry panel, app answering and a package solution carry the whole security load.

Prewar condo conversions

Former rentals sold as condos, with plaster walls, original risers and service entrances off side streets. Reusing the riser is often possible; finding it can take a careful trace.

Loft conversions

Tribeca, SoHo, NoHo and Chelsea loft condos with freight elevators, keyed-elevator floors and cast-iron or masonry facades where outside mounting is limited.

Office-to-residential conversions

Former office floors with commercial-grade ceilings and risers, new residential entries and sometimes retained commercial tenants on lower floors.

Mixed-use condos with retail

Residential and commercial units under one declaration, with separate entrances, separate hours and owners whose access must never overlap.

How the method changes by building type

Glass and curtain-wall lobbies. Floor-to-ceiling glass is beautiful and hard on cameras and entry panels. A camera aimed at the glass sees daylight glare and records silhouettes; a lobby camera placed back from the glass and angled toward the desk sees faces. Entry panels on glass vestibules mount on a stanchion or the frame rather than the glass itself, and the cable has to reach them through the floor or the frame, so we plan that path with the managing agent before anything is ordered.

Finished interiors. New-development lobbies and amenity floors are designed spaces with millwork, stone and coffered or plaster ceilings. Readers and cameras go where the design allows, in the smallest housings that do the job, and cable is fished through existing chases rather than surface-run. Where there is no concealed path, we say so and show the board what a surface raceway would look like before installing it.

Older risers. In prewar conversions the original intercom riser may still run up the building, sometimes with abandoned cable alongside it from earlier systems. We test what is there before deciding whether a new system can reuse it. Abandoned cable in a riser is common, and removing it is a separate line item when space is tight.

Loft and conversion floors. Freight elevators and keyed elevator floors are common in loft condos, and owners often control their own landing. Elevator readers and intercom calls that land directly on a unit's floor need careful planning with the elevator company and the owners affected.

Landmarked facades. Many Manhattan condo conversions sit in historic districts. We mount entry stations inside the vestibule or recess wherever we can so the facade is not altered; anything that would change it goes through the building's architect or expediter and the required approval first.

Commercial Condo Security

Business Condo Security in Manhattan: Retail Units, Office Condos and Mixed-Use Buildings

Many Manhattan condominiums are not purely residential. A tower on a busy avenue may have retail condo units on the ground floor owned by investors and leased to shops or restaurants, a medical or office condo on the second floor, and residences above. Downtown, office-to-residential conversions sometimes keep commercial tenants on lower floors for years after the residential floors open. Each owner group needs its own entrances, hours and credentials, and the board, or the separate boards some declarations create for commercial and residential sections, needs a security plan that keeps them apart.

The commercial work we see most in Manhattan condos falls along a few corridors. On Broadway and the side streets of SoHo and NoHo, retail condo units below loft residences need their own cameras and alarms that do not share a recorder or a login with the residential side. Along Madison and Lexington Avenues on the Upper East Side, medical and professional office condos in mixed-use buildings need patient and staff access separate from residents. In the Financial District around Wall, Water and Broad Streets, converted office buildings combine new residential lobbies with remaining office floors. On the West Side near Hudson Yards and along Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, new mixed-use towers put restaurants and fitness clubs at the base of residential condos. In Harlem along 125th Street, newer condo buildings sit above ground-floor retail.

For commercial condo owners we install cameras, intrusion alarms with central-station monitoring through our provider on the two monitoring tiers listed on our site, access control on their own doors and an intercom station at the commercial entrance where it is needed. For the board, we make sure the residential system and the commercial systems connect only where the building wants them to, such as a shared loading dock or a fire-alarm door release, and stay separate everywhere else.

Compliance points boards and commercial owners raise

  • Egress first: locked doors on paths of egress have to release on fire alarm or power loss where the building and fire codes require it; we design door releases to the building's approved egress plan and coordinate with its fire alarm contractor.
  • Accessible entry: entry panels and readers are mounted at reachable heights with visual call indication, and we discuss accessible operation with the board when a station is replaced.
  • Video only, by default: cameras record video without audio unless the board's counsel approves otherwise, given New York's one-party consent rule on recorded conversations.
  • Insurance paperwork on award: commercial owners and managing agents usually require a certificate of insurance; we issue it naming the condominium, the managing agent and any listed parties once the job is awarded.

Board Checklist

Before the Board of Managers Votes: A Manhattan Condo Security Checklist

Condo security decisions go faster when the board has its own facts in order before contractors arrive. This is the list we suggest a Manhattan condo board, or the managing agent working for it, run through first. None of it is legal advice; where a question turns on the governing documents, the condo's attorney is the right person to answer it.

  1. Find the governing documents. The declaration and bylaws say what the board may approve, how it funds repairs and replacements, and how house rules are adopted. The New York Attorney General's guide to condo boards notes that copies of the declaration, bylaws, floor plans and rules "shall be available for inspection" at the board's office (NY Attorney General, condo board Q&A).
  2. Know who controls the board. In a building still in sponsor sell-out, the sponsor may control the board. The same AG guide says the Attorney General's office may intervene where a sponsor still controls the board or breaks offering plan promises. Security changes during that period are worth documenting carefully.
  3. Inventory what exists. List every entrance, the intercom make and age, who holds the administrator login, the access control software and who hosts it, the camera recorder location, and any service contracts inherited from the developer.
  4. Write down the problems. Which units have intercom complaints, which doors get propped, where packages go missing, which amenity rooms are used after hours. Specific complaints produce specific scopes.
  5. Decide policies before equipment. Who may view footage, how long it is kept, what the access system logs, who gets guest and contractor credentials and for how long. Local Law 63 of 2021 makes a written data policy part of running a smart access building.
  6. Set the budget path. Reserves, capital plan or assessment; the board and its advisers decide. Ask each bidder for phasing options so the work can match the budget year.
  7. Compare bids on the same scope. Give every contractor the same list of doors, units and expectations, and ask each to say what it reuses, what it replaces, what needs unit access and what carries an ongoing subscription.
  8. Plan the owner notice. Unit owners need the dates, the access windows and what they must do, whether that is being home for a station swap or accepting an app invitation.
Maintenance

Keeping a Manhattan Condo Security System Working After Installation

A condo security system rarely fails all at once. It drifts. A door closer weakens until the strike no longer catches, a camera lens collects grime from the garage exhaust, a recorder drive fills and starts overwriting sooner than the footage policy says it should, firmware on the entry panel falls several versions behind, and the list of active fobs grows longer than the list of residents. None of those shows up as an emergency, which is why condo boards tend to discover them only when someone asks for footage that is not there or a former renter's credential still opens the gym.

Condo security maintenance plans exist to catch the drift. On a scheduled visit we check every entry panel and door station for audio, video and release; walk each reader door to confirm it locks, unlocks and reports its position; review the recorder for drive health, retention length and camera images; update firmware where the manufacturer has released fixes; and give the managing agent a short written report with anything that needs a repair quote. We also run the credential list against the managing agent's current resident roster so stale fobs can be cancelled.

Seasonal timing helps in Manhattan. Garage and exterior equipment takes the most abuse in winter, when salt, slush and slammed doors work against strikes and readers, so a check before the cold sets in is worth scheduling. Year-end is also when many boards set the next budget, and a maintenance report gives them real numbers for the repairs and replacements to plan rather than guesses.

Associations & HOAs

Condo Associations, HOAs and Multi-Building Communities in Manhattan

Not every Manhattan condominium is a single tower with a single board. Some are campuses of several buildings under one declaration, some share a garage or a courtyard with a neighboring building under an easement, and some residential condos sit inside larger developments with a master association that governs shared plazas, parks or loading areas. In those communities the security question becomes whose system controls which door. A resident of one building may need the shared garage and the shared fitness center but not the other tower's lobby, and the master association may want cameras on the plaza that each building's board can view but not change.

We design those systems so each board or association keeps control of its own doors and recordings while shared spaces use credentials both sides can issue. In practice that means one access platform with separate administrator roles, or two platforms connected at the shared doors, depending on what each party already owns. The governing documents, and the agreements between the associations, decide who pays for shared equipment and who approves changes, so we price shared and private portions separately in the proposal.

Your Questions Answered

Condo Security Systems in Manhattan: Questions Answered

These are the questions Manhattan condo boards, managing agents and unit owners raise most often before a project, grouped by where they come up: the building's turnover from the developer, the day-to-day running of the system, and the costs and contracts behind it.

After the developer hands over the building

Our sponsor installed the intercom and we have no administrator login. Can the condo take control of it?

Often, yes. Many networked intercom and access platforms let the hardware be re-registered to a new account once the building proves ownership, sometimes with the manufacturer's help. Others are tied to the original installer's dealer account and can be moved only with that dealer's cooperation. We check the make and model on the walk-through and tell the board which path applies before it spends anything on new hardware.

Should a new condo board replace the developer's security system right after turnover?

Not automatically. Systems in recent Manhattan towers are frequently sound; what boards lack is documentation, logins and a service relationship. We recommend an assessment in the first year that lists what works, what is under warranty, what the developer's contracts cost and where coverage is missing, so the board replaces only what earns it.

Who should hold the master credentials for a Manhattan condo's security systems?

The condominium, not a contractor. We set up administrator accounts in the name of the condo or its managing agent, give the board a written record of every login and device location, and keep our own access limited to what service work requires.

Running the system day to day

When a Manhattan condo unit closes, how quickly can the seller's credentials be shut off?

The managing agent cancels every credential tied to the unit on the closing date and issues new ones to the buyer. A properly set-up system groups credentials by unit, so this takes minutes. Under Local Law 63 of 2021, reference data for a resident who has permanently left must be removed or anonymized within 90 days, so the building's process should cover that step too.

Can renters in condo units get their own credentials?

Yes, if the building's rules allow leasing and the owner requests it. Renters' credentials can carry an end date matching the lease, and the owner keeps or loses access according to the board's policy. The access system enforces the policy; the declaration and house rules set it.

How do couriers get into a non-doorman Manhattan condo without a resident letting them in?

Usually through delivery codes at the entry panel or courier access built into the intercom platform, opening only the vestibule or the package room door during set hours. Each entry is logged and, with a camera on the door, recorded. Some boards add a locker system so couriers never enter the package room at all.

Can the concierge desk and residents' phones both answer the same lobby call?

Yes. Most current platforms route a visitor call to the desk first during staffed hours and straight to the resident's app or in-unit station after the desk closes. The schedule is set in software, so the board can change it without new equipment.

Costs, contracts and scope

Is a condo security system a common charge or the unit owner's cost?

Building-wide systems in the common elements are generally a common expense, but the answer for any specific item, such as an in-unit monitor an owner wants upgraded, depends on the condominium's declaration and bylaws. The board's attorney is the right person to say; we price the work both ways if the board asks.

What ongoing costs come with a modern condo intercom?

Some platforms charge a monthly or per-unit subscription from the manufacturer for cloud service and the resident app; others run locally with no maker subscription. Service visits are separate. We list every recurring cost in the proposal so the board sees the full picture before choosing.

Will you work under our managing agent's vendor rules and insurance requirements?

Yes. Manhattan managing agents usually have vendor onboarding forms, insurance requirements and building work rules. We complete their forms, follow their hours and protection rules, and issue the certificate of insurance once the job is awarded.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems in Manhattan

Boards and managing agents increasingly start research with an AI answer or a cost site such as Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr. Those answers are a useful start, but they are written for a generic suburban condo complex, and several of their common claims do not hold in a Manhattan building. These are the ones we correct most often.

"A condo security system is something each owner buys for their own unit"

AI summaries often blend condo security with home security, suggesting each owner pick an alarm or doorbell camera for their own space. In a Manhattan condominium that misses the point. The entrances, garage, elevators, package room and amenity floors are common elements, and the systems that protect them are chosen, paid for and governed by the board of managers under the declaration and bylaws.

Unit owners can and do add their own in-unit alarms, and we install those too. But an owner cannot mount a camera on the hallway ceiling or swap the lobby panel, and the common hallway outside a unit door is shared space where the board's rules apply, as the Habitat reporting on video doorbells made clear.

The practical result is that condo security in Manhattan is a board project first. The owner's role is to report problems, follow the building's rules and give access when the board's contractor needs to reach a unit.

"Intercom replacement costs a fixed amount per unit"

Cost guides like to quote a per-apartment number for an intercom replacement. In a Manhattan condo that number hides most of what matters. Whether the existing riser can be reused, how many entrances need stations, whether units get screens or app answering, how many days the building rules allow for work and how many apartments need visits each move the price more than the unit count does.

Two buildings with sixty units each can differ widely: one a postwar conversion with a sound riser and a single front door, the other a new tower with a garage, two lobbies and an amenity floor. A per-unit average applied to either one gives the board a figure it cannot use.

We do not publish per-unit prices for that reason. Repairs start at the $250 service call; replacements get a free assessment and a written price for the building in front of us.

"Wireless systems mean no wiring work in the building"

AI answers often say a modern wireless or cloud intercom can be installed without touching the building's wiring. Partly true. App-based answering removes the need for new wiring to each unit, but the entry panel, the readers and the cameras still need power and a reliable network connection, and in a Manhattan high-rise that usually means cable in the riser and the ceilings.

Wi-Fi in a concrete tower is also less reliable than the summaries suggest. Lobby panels and garage readers that depend on Wi-Fi drop off at exactly the wrong moment, so we cable anything that has to work every time and keep wireless for convenience devices.

The honest version is that many upgrades can avoid entering apartments, but no serious condo system avoids cabling entirely. We confirm which applies after the site survey.

"Any licensed electrician can install condo security"

Several AI answers treat security installation as electrical work. In New York, intercom, access control and camera work is low-voltage work, done by licensed low-voltage contractors; Abstract Enterprises Security Systems holds NYS license #12000287431. Line-voltage circuits are a separate trade.

That division matters on a condo job. We do not do electrical work, so where a new panel location or gate operator needs a 120-volt circuit, the building's electrician runs it, and we handle everything from the low-voltage side onward. The scope should say who does what so the managing agent can schedule both trades.

Fire alarm work is a third category with its own drawings, filings and approvals, and we describe it only within our licensing scope.

"Cameras in condo common areas need every owner's permission"

Some AI answers suggest a condo needs consent from each owner to install common-area cameras. The Habitat Q&A on a Midtown East condo cited a New York Times column stating that cameras capturing images in common areas of residential buildings for security purposes are lawful, while noting that the board's authority comes from the declaration and bylaws and that audio recording without consent is not.

The more useful point for a board is process rather than permission. Install video only, put the decision on the record, adopt a written footage policy, and limit who can view and export recordings. Those steps protect the board when an owner questions a camera later.

We are not lawyers, and this is not legal advice; the condo's counsel should review the policy. Our part is building a system that can enforce it, with user accounts, export logs and retention settings that match what the board decides.

"A virtual doorman replaces the need for a security contractor"

AI answers often present a virtual doorman service as a complete security solution for non-doorman condos. A remote attendant service still runs on hardware in the building: an entry panel, cameras, door releases and network gear, all installed and maintained by someone.

We do not operate a virtual doorman or managed security service. What we do is install and integrate the equipment such a service uses, or the equipment the board's own staff use, and keep it working. Boards considering a remote service should ask who maintains the hardware and what happens when a release fails at night.

For many boutique Manhattan condos, a well-configured app intercom, delivery codes and a locked package room solve the problem a virtual doorman was meant to solve, without a new monthly contract.

"You can get a same-day install from any security company"

Summaries sometimes promise same-day or round-the-clock installation from local companies. Building-wide condo work does not happen that way in Manhattan. The board approves the scope, insurance paperwork goes to the managing agent, equipment is ordered after the deposit, and work follows the building's permitted hours.

What we do promise is response time. We book within 2 hours of a request, schedule seven days a week with early-day visits, and take jobs first come, first served once paid. Repairs often move quickly because the visit itself is the first step.

For special-order equipment we give no date until it arrives, because shipping is outside our control. Boards plan better with an honest schedule than with a promise that slips.

Field Notes

From Our Job Library: Reading a Condo From Its Recorder Screen

Two small photos on this page say a lot about how we assess a condo's cameras. One is a recorder view along a sidewalk and curb; the other is a night view of a service door standing open. When we walk a building, we ask to see the recorder before we look at a single camera, because the screen shows what the building actually captures, not what the installer meant to capture. A street view that shows the curb but not faces at the door, or a night image where an open door is visible but the person walking through it is not, tells the board exactly where its coverage fails.

The second habit is checking doors before cameras. The open service door in that night view is the most common condo security failure we find: a door propped for a delivery or a move and never closed. A door position contact that alerts staff, a closer that works and a camera aimed along the door's path usually do more than another lobby camera. In Manhattan condos with loading docks and garages, we walk those doors with the resident manager first.

The third is the closet. Recorders, intercom controllers and access panels end up in whatever closet the developer had free, sometimes beside the trash room or under a leak. Before quoting we note where the equipment lives, whether it has battery backup and whether the closet locks, because a system is only as reliable as the room it sits in.

Manhattan Condo Security Systems FAQ

Does a Manhattan condo intercom have to meet a state requirement?
+

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have an intercom at the self-locking lobby door that lets each apartment speak to a visitor and release the lock. Older buildings must install one when tenants occupying a majority of apartments request or consent in writing. Whether and how that applies to your condominium is a question for its attorney; this is general information, not legal advice.

Can the condo keep its existing fob readers when it replaces the intercom?
+

Frequently. If the readers use a common credential format and the access controller is still supported, a new intercom can run alongside them or connect to them. If the access software is locked to a previous vendor or no longer updated, the board may choose to replace both at once. We test the readers and controller on the walk-through and price both options.

Can unit owners open the lobby door for guests from their phones?

Yes, with an app-based intercom. A visitor presses the owner's name on the entry panel, the call rings the owner's phone with video, and the owner taps to release the door from anywhere. Can the security network be kept separate from residents' Wi-Fi?
