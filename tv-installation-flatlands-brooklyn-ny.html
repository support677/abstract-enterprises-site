The Neighborhood

Why Flatlands Properties Need TV Installation

Flatlands is one of the oldest names in Brooklyn and one of the youngest housing stocks in this part of it. The town began as the Dutch farming settlement of Nieuw Amersfoort in 1636, but Brooklyn Community Board 18's own history notes that it grew slowly, because for a long time it had neither the roads nor the transit that pulled houses into Flatbush and Bushwick. The result is a neighborhood that was filled in mostly between the 1920s and the 1950s, in neat blocks of brick and frame one- and two-family houses, with bungalows and semi-detached brick homes taking the last empty lots after the Second World War. Very little here is the 1890s brownstone that most Brooklyn TV advice is written for. A Flatlands living room is much more likely to be a front room in a brick pair on East 39th Street, a ground floor in a frame house off Avenue L, or an apartment in a postwar building near Kings Highway, and each of those hides a different wall behind its paint.

That matters because the wall is the whole job. A semi-detached brick house usually puts its longest living room wall against the house next door, and that wall is brick under the plaster. A detached frame house from the 1920s or 1930s has plaster on wood lath over studs that were not always placed on modern spacing. A bungalow that has been renovated may have new drywall on one wall and its original plaster on the next. Postwar apartment buildings tend to mix plaster partitions with masonry around the outside and the shafts. And Glenwood Houses, the NYCHA campus built in the early 1950s, brings its own rule book before a bracket can go up. A handyman who treats every wall as drywall gets about half of Flatlands right. We price each wall honestly: $45 extra for plaster, $65 extra for brick or stone, nothing extra for drywall on wood studs, and you see which applies before the visit.

Ownership shapes the work as much as construction does. The NYU Furman Center's profile of Flatlands/Canarsie, the community district that takes in Flatlands, put the homeownership rate at 61.2 percent in 2024, against roughly a third for the city as a whole, and counted 75,575 housing units. That is unusual for Brooklyn, and in practice it means many of our Flatlands calls come from owners who can decide for themselves where a TV goes, including owners who live in one half of a two-family and rent out the other. It also means plenty of tenants rent from a landlord who lives downstairs and hears every drill. The same profile shows public housing at 9.8 percent of the district's rental units in 2025, and a renter in Glenwood Houses works under NYCHA's written rules rather than a private lease. So our first question on a Flatlands call is not the TV size. It is who owns the wall.

The third thing that shapes a Flatlands TV job is what happens to a screen after it goes up. News 12 Brooklyn reported in March 2026 that four people entered a home on East 34th Street and Avenue N, on the Marine Park side of the 63rd Precinct, and "removed two smart T.V.'s before escaping from the home." A big new screen is one of the easiest things in a house to sell, and how it is mounted, what can be seen through the front window, and whether the household has a record of the serial number all matter after a break-in. News 12 also reported in June 2026 on tenants at 1155 East 35th Street describing ceiling leaks and open HPD complaints, which is the other side of older housing: a TV and its outlet should never go on a wall that is getting wet. We look at both before we choose a wall, and we say so when the right answer is to wait for a repair.

Same-Day Repair in Flatlands

Need Repair in Flatlands? A Bracket Sagging Off the Party Wall, a Screen Left Behind by the Last Owner, or a Cord Pushed Through the Plaster

The urgent TV calls we get from houses like Flatlands' follow a pattern. The first is a bracket on a brick party wall that was screwed into the mortar between the bricks rather than the bricks themselves. Mortar in a house from the 1930s or 1940s is softer than the brick around it, and MantelMount's own installation guidance warns that "mortar joints are much softer than the bricks themselves." A fixing in a joint can feel tight on the day and then loosen over a few winters, especially under a full-motion arm that gets pulled out every evening. The second is a screen hung on plaster with plastic plugs. Plaster is hard on the face and brittle behind it, and a plug that only grips the plaster slowly crushes its own hole. The third is what new owners find after a sale: an old TV still on the wall, an empty bracket, and a power cord disappearing into one hole and coming out of another.

If a screen is leaning or one corner has dropped, do not push it back into place. If two people can lift it off safely, take the weight off the bracket and unplug it; if not, keep everyone clear of it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV then goes back up properly anchored, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, so a 55-inch screen re-hung on brick is the $179.99 base plus the $65 brick add-on, with the take-down added. A cord that has been fished through the wall gets pulled out, because the National Electrical Code does not allow a flexible cord to be concealed inside a wall, and its job is taken over either by an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) or by a paintable cord cover on the surface ($59). If what we find is a problem in the wall itself, such as cracked plaster, a loose brick or a stain from a leak, we say so plainly and stop, so the owner can call the right trade first.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Flatlands's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every Flatlands TV call. They differ in three ways that matter far more than the brand of the TV: what the wall is made of, who has to agree before anyone drills, and how a large box gets from the curb to the room.

The semi-detached brick pair

Two houses built as one, sharing a center wall and often a driveway. Brick outside and on the shared wall, plaster inside, and the living room usually at the front.

The detached frame house

Wood-framed one- and two-family houses from the 1920s and 1930s on the side streets, with plaster on lath, stairs along one wall and porches at the front.

The postwar bungalow

Smaller one- and one-and-a-half-story houses from the years after the war, many of them renovated more than once, with low rooms and walls of mixed age.

The two-family with an owner downstairs

Stacked apartments, a shared entrance or a side door, and an owner who lives in one unit, decides what happens to the walls of both and hears the drill.

The apartment building

Postwar walk-ups and elevator buildings on and near Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue, with a super, a managing agent and masonry around the outside of every unit.

Glenwood Houses

The NYCHA development from the early 1950s, where a resident needs the management office's written consent before adding a fixture, and most walls are masonry.

The semi-detached brick pair: the strongest wall in the house is the one you share

Walk down most of the blocks between Avenue J and Avenue N and you see pairs: two houses built together, mirror images of each other, joined by one center wall. In a pair, the long side wall of the living room is often that shared wall, and it is the obvious place for a TV because it has no windows, no radiator under a window and no door. It is also almost always masonry, with plaster laid over brick. That is good news for a heavy screen, because a properly set anchor in solid brick holds far more than any TV weighs. It is also the reason a handyman's drywall toggles fail there, and why the brick or stone add-on, +$65 on our list, applies to most TVs on a shared wall.

Two cautions apply to every shared wall. The first is depth. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, and we keep every hole in the face of a brick rather than in the mortar joint, because the joint is the weak part of the wall and the part a neighbor's own fixings may already have disturbed. The second is the neighbor. If the family on the other side has their TV on the same wall, a bracket placed directly back-to-back with theirs is not a structural problem in solid brick, but the sound of both TVs and both soundbars travels through it. We ask what is on the other side if you know, offset the bracket where we can, and suggest a soundbar on the wall rather than on a hollow console. The driveway side of the house, by contrast, is an outside wall with windows, and it tends to bring glare onto a screen placed opposite it in the afternoon.

The detached frame house: plaster, lath and studs that were not laid out for a stud finder

The detached houses on Flatlands' side streets, especially the older ones from the 1920s and 1930s, are mostly wood-framed, and their original interior walls are plaster on wood lath. That wall is strong under a load pressing into it and weak under a load pulling out of it, and a TV on a bracket pulls out. A screw into lath alone, or a plastic plug in plaster alone, can feel fine on the day and loosen within a year. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into a stud, with the pilot hole started slowly so the plaster does not crack outward. A Brownstoner forum thread on mounting TVs in older Brooklyn houses put it simply: on a wall that is not a party wall, "you can often screw through plaster into wood lath" and reach the framing behind it.

The difficulty is finding the studs. In houses of this age they are not always on a regular spacing, the plaster can be close to an inch thick, and electronic scanners often read the lath and the plaster keys as noise. So we find framing one stud at a time: scan, knock, and confirm with a single small pilot hole placed where the bracket plate will cover it. If the TV has to be centered on a spot that falls between studs, we use a bracket with a wider wall plate, or toggles rated for the load into the plaster, depending on the weight. Frame houses also tend to have a front porch, sometimes enclosed, and a front room with windows on two sides, so the TV usually ends up on the inside wall that backs onto the stair or the dining room. Plaster on framing is the +$45 line on our list.

The postwar bungalow: small rooms, low ceilings and three renovations' worth of walls

Cityneighborhoods.nyc describes bungalows and semi-detached brick homes filling the last lots of Flatlands after the Second World War, and many of those bungalows have been updated more than once since. That is what makes them interesting for a TV. One living room wall may still be its original plaster, the next may be drywall put up in the 1980s, and a third may be paneling over something else entirely. Each one is priced by what it really is, so a TV on the renovated drywall wall can come in $45 cheaper than the same TV on the plaster wall beside it, and we tell you both prices before you choose.

The rooms themselves are smaller and the ceilings lower than in a two-story house, which changes the height. A 55-inch screen in a compact bungalow front room is usually best mounted lower than people expect, with its center close to the eyes of someone sitting on the couch, because there is less distance from the seat to the wall. Low ceilings also mean the screen and a soundbar can crowd a window head or a beam, so we mark the outline with painter's tape and let you look at it from the couch before any drilling.

The two-family with an owner downstairs: one house, two households and two sets of rules

Two-family houses are everywhere in Flatlands, and many of them have the owner living on one floor and a tenant on the other. For a TV job that sets up two very different calls. When the owner books, the questions are about the house: which wall, whether to use an in-wall kit, and whether the rental unit should get a bracket the owner chooses so that every new tenant does not drill a fresh set of holes. When the tenant books, the first question is whether the owner has agreed, and the second is how much noise the owner will hear. We suggest the tenant sends a two-line text before the visit naming the wall, the size of the TV and whether the wires will go inside the wall or in a cover. Owners who are told first rarely object.

The apartment building: a super, a managing agent and walls that change from room to room

Flatlands' apartment buildings stand mostly along and near its main avenues, with an occasional building on a side street that Forgotten New York describes as otherwise blocks of detached homes. For a TV they bring a set of practical questions before the technical ones. Is there an elevator, and does it need to be reserved or padded? Does the managing agent want a certificate of insurance naming the building? Are there working hours for contractors? We settle all of that before the visit, because a technician turned away at the lobby is a wasted morning for you.

Inside, postwar apartment walls are rarely all one thing. Partitions between rooms may be plaster on framing or on hollow block, and walls along the outside of the building, beside the shafts and between apartments are commonly masonry. A wall shared with the apartment next door may be the most solid one in the unit and also the one most likely to carry your neighbor's TV on its other face. Where the wall is masonry, an in-wall kit is not possible because nothing can be fished inside it, so the cables either go into a paintable cord cover or drop straight down behind a console. If the building's rules forbid any opening in a wall, the cord cover is the answer regardless of the wall type.

Glenwood Houses: written consent first, then a wall that will not take a drywall anchor

Glenwood Houses, built by NYCHA in the early 1950s, houses many Flatlands renters, and its residents live under the NYCHA Resident Handbook rather than a private lease. The 2024 edition is direct: "Residents are not allowed to alter their apartments or add any fixtures or fittings in their apartments" without prior written consent from the property management office, and residents are responsible for any resulting damage. A wall-mounted TV bracket is a fixture. So the first step for a Glenwood household is a written request to the development's management office, and we will not drill a NYCHA wall without the consent in hand. We can describe the bracket, the number and size of the holes and the cable plan in writing so the request is easy to approve.

The same handbook says cable TV installation "is the responsibility of the resident," and that satellite dishes may only be used inside the apartment, with nothing attached to window frames and no antennas on the roof. Some NYCHA developments also receive basic cable and internet through the city's Big Apple Connect program, which ABC7 reported includes "a cable box and remote control" for participating developments; we connect whatever box the household already has. If consent is refused or slow, a TV set on its own stand with a tidy cable layout is a proper job on our list at $119.99, and the soundbar can be placed in front of it for $79. If consent is given, expect masonry: the brick or stone add-on usually applies, and the cables run in a paintable cover because the wall cannot be opened.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Flatlands Front Room Before the Drill Comes Out

These notes describe how our technicians work in the kinds of houses and apartments Flatlands has. They are our standing practice, not a story about any one address.

Find the shared wall from the street. Before going inside a semi-detached or attached house, the technician looks at the front of the building to see which wall is shared with the house next door and which faces a driveway or a side yard. That one look usually tells us which living room wall is masonry before the scanner comes out.

Look up for water. On any ceiling above the chosen wall, a brown ring, bubbling paint or a soft patch is a reason to stop. A TV and its outlet do not belong under a leak, and we would rather move the screen or come back after the repair than mount it and hope.

One hidden pilot hole. The technician drills one small hole where the bracket plate will cover it and reads the dust: white powder and then wood for plaster on lath, red grit for brick, grey grit for block. If the result changes the add-on from the quote, you hear it before any further drilling.

Tape, then sit. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen. You sit where you watch, and we check the height, the glare from the front windows and the swing of every nearby door. Moving tape is free. Moving a bracket costs a wall.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Plaster, Block or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Flatlands Wall and What It Costs

The $179.99 base on our Brooklyn list covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. Plenty of Flatlands walls are not drywall over wood studs, which is why the wall add-ons come before anything else. Each one exists because the wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time, not because an older house is an excuse to charge more. Screens larger than 55 inches are priced from the TV's model number when you send it, so the weight and the bolt pattern are known before the quote.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). Common where a Flatlands owner has renovated, in additions and finished basements, and in newer construction. It is the easiest wall to mount on and the easiest to hide cables inside, provided the screws land in the studs or the anchors are rated for the load. The one thing we always check is what the drywall was hung over. Many renovations in houses of this age put new drywall straight over the old plaster or onto furring strips fixed to brick, and that changes both the anchor and the price.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The original wall of most frame houses and bungalows built before the 1950s. Plaster is brittle, and MantelMount's guidance for plaster is to "always pre-drill and let the anchors do the work." We drill slowly, reach the stud behind the lath wherever the bracket allows, and use toggles rated for the weight where it does not. A plaster wall that is already cracked, bulging or loose from the lath is a repair job for a plasterer first, and we will tell you so rather than hang a TV on it.

Brick or stone (+$65). The shared walls of semi-detached and attached houses, the outside walls of brick houses and many apartment walls. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor sits in the brick itself and not in the softer mortar joint. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and we keep holes away from the edges of each brick. Plaster laid directly over brick is priced as brick, because it is the brick that does the holding. A stone accent wall or a stone fireplace surround takes the same add-on and slower, more careful drilling.

Concrete and concrete block (quoted from your photos). Block appears in basements, garages, some apartment partitions and most industrial buildings, and poured concrete in some larger buildings. Hollow block has thin faces with voids behind them, so anchors go into the solid webs or use hardware made for hollow block. Poured concrete is hard drilling, and a hidden steel bar stops a hole dead, in which case we shift the bracket slightly rather than fight it. Because block and concrete vary so much from one building to the next, we price them from your photos before booking, and the price you are given is the one you pay.

Above a fireplace (+$130). Some Flatlands houses, particularly the larger detached ones, have a fireplace in the front room, working or decorative. Above-fireplace mounting is +$130 on our list, and a brick or stone chimney breast adds the +$65 masonry line as well. HomeAdvisor's guidance is to check the temperature at the spot where the TV would hang and avoid anything above 100°F, and a Brownstoner forum thread on Brooklyn fireplaces warned that a TV above a mantel "invites a sore neck." We check the heat with the fireplace in use if it works, and we will tell you if the room has a better wall.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common request after "put it on the wall" is "make the wires disappear," and there is a wrong way that many people have seen done. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out of a hole near the floor. National Electrical Code section 400.12, as EC&M's code column quotes it, says flexible cords and power-supply cords "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings, or located above suspended or dropped ceilings." Gizmodo's widely shared summary put it in one line: "running power cords through the walls is not a substitute for permanent wiring." A cord inside a wall can be nicked by a fixing, can overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It has its own rated cable inside the wall, a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, a recessed outlet behind the TV, and a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It is $169 on our list including the kit, and it needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits drywall and plaster on lath and does not suit brick, block or concrete. We do not do electrical work. The NYC Department of Buildings says that "no installation and modification to any electrical systems can be performed without a permit," and that work belongs to a licensed master electrician. If you want a new hard-wired outlet added behind the TV, that is an electrician's job, and we can mount the TV once it is in.

For masonry walls, and for any wall a lease or a management office says must not be opened, the choices are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match, or a layout that drops the cables straight down behind a console placed directly under the screen. On a brick party wall in a semi-detached house, the second option often looks cleanest of all, because the console hides everything and the wall needs no channel at all.

Owners, Renters and NYCHA

Who Owns the Wall: Mounting a TV in a Flatlands House, Rental or NYCHA Apartment

Flatlands has more homeowners than most of Brooklyn, a large number of tenants renting from an owner who lives in the same house, and a NYCHA development with its own rules. Permission comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and what we have learned doing this work, so you can ask the right question before booking.

If you own the house. You decide, and the choices are about the long term. If you expect to sell in a few years, a bracket and a cord cover leave the least to patch. If you are staying, an in-wall kit on a framed wall gives the cleanest result. If you rent out the other unit, consider choosing and installing the tenant's bracket yourself, on a sound wall with a proper anchor, and writing into the lease that tenants use the existing mount. That one decision stops a new set of holes appearing every time a tenant changes.

If you rent from a private landlord. Start with the lease. Brick Underground put the question to a New York real estate attorney in 2022, who said the lease's alteration clause decides whether a landlord can bar wall hanging. Steven Kirkpatrick, the attorney quoted, said: "If it's not included in the lease, then this wouldn't be enforceable." The same article quoted property manager Arik Lifshitz: "If the hole is larger than the size of a nail, then it's considered damage," which is exactly what a TV bracket leaves, and both advised asking before installing a bracket. A landlord who knows a licensed, insured installer is coming and that no cord will be pushed through the wall usually says yes. Keep the yes in writing.

If you live in Glenwood Houses or another NYCHA development. The handbook requires the management office's prior written consent for any fixture, so the request comes first and the booking second. Ask the office what it will accept: some residents are allowed a bracket with the cables in a surface cover, and some are told to keep the TV on a stand. Either way, we follow the consent exactly as written.

Consider not drilling at all. A TV on its own stand on a good console, with a soundbar placed in front of it and the cables tidied behind, is $119.99 plus $79 for the soundbar on our list. It needs no consent, leaves no holes and moves with you. The CPSC's 2023 tip-over report counted 217 tip-over deaths reported between 2013 and mid-2023, 103 of them involving a falling television, so a TV on a stand in a home with children should be secured with an anti-tip strap. Ask us to fit yours during the stand setup, and in a rental its one small screw goes only where the landlord agrees.

Compliance notes for Flatlands businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, an object mounted on a wall with its leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a corridor or waiting room can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. NEC 400.12 does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings, including above a dropped ceiling. Hidden power for a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable or a cord cover on the surface.
  • New outlets are electrician work. The NYC Department of Buildings requires a permit and a licensed master electrician for changes to a building's electrical system. If a screen needs a new circuit or a new hard-wired outlet, the electrician comes first and we mount after.
  • Exits and life-safety devices stay clear. We do not mount screens where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and cables stay off the floor in any route people use to leave.
  • Insurance paperwork before the visit. Commercial landlords and shopping centers often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent. We send it ahead of the visit.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Flatlands: Questions Answered

These are situations Flatlands owners, renters and businesses actually bring to us: renovated bungalows with two kinds of wall, brackets bought online, a TV coming home in the family car, a break-in a few blocks away, a NYCHA office that says no, and a metal building in the industrial zone. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Security, theft and the TV in the front room

Two smart TVs were taken in a daytime break-in near East 34th Street and Avenue N in February. Can a mounted TV be made harder to lift off its bracket?

Yes, within limits. Most flat and tilting brackets hold the TV on two hanging arms that drop onto a wall plate, and they lock with a pair of screws or pull straps at the bottom so the TV cannot bounce off. Many brackets also accept a small padlock or security bolts through those same points, which turns a five-second lift into a job that needs tools. It will not stop a determined burglar, but it slows one down, and News 12's report of the February 23 break-in described a group that was in and out quickly. Two other things help more than the bracket: keep the screen out of a direct line of sight from the sidewalk through the front window where the room allows it, and write down the TV's serial number and photograph it on the wall. We point out the serial label to you before the TV goes up, because once it is on the wall the sticker is hard to reach.

We are selling our Flatlands house next spring. Should the TV and its bracket stay behind for the buyer?

That is your call, but plan it now rather than at the closing table. A bracket on a sound wall with a proper anchor can stay and is often welcome. A TV that stays is a negotiation item, so list it or leave it out of the contract. What buyers and their inspectors tend to notice is the cable work: a cord pushed through the wall looks like a code problem, because it is one under NEC 400.12, while an in-wall power kit or a neat cord cover looks finished. If you plan to take the bracket with you, the brick or plaster holes it leaves will need filling, and if you plan to leave it, ask us to note its make and model so the buyer knows what it is rated for.

Renovated houses and walls that are two things at once

When I knock along the living room wall of our renovated bungalow, it sounds hollow at one end and solid at the other. What is behind it?

Most likely two different constructions under one coat of paint. In a postwar Flatlands bungalow, a renovation often put new drywall over part of a room, sometimes on wood studs, sometimes on thin strips fixed straight onto an old masonry or plaster wall. The hollow end is drywall over a cavity; the solid end may be drywall laid tight over plaster or brick, or the original plaster itself. That matters for both the anchor and the price. Drywall over wood studs is our $179.99 base; plaster is +$45; drywall over brick is priced as brick (+$65) because the anchor has to reach the brick to hold. The technician confirms which with one small pilot hole under where the bracket plate will sit, and if your chosen spot straddles the two, we may shift the screen a few inches so the whole bracket lands on one kind of wall.

Our bungalow front room is small and the ceiling is low. How high should a 55-inch TV go there?

Lower than most people guess. The usual rule of thumb in TV mounting guides is to put the center of the screen roughly at the eye level of a seated viewer, and in a small room where the couch is only a few feet from the wall that matters more, because looking up at a screen from close range strains the neck faster. A low ceiling also tempts people to push the TV up toward it to clear a console or a radiator, which puts the screen too high. We mark the outline with painter's tape, you sit where you watch, and we adjust before drilling. If the only workable spot is a little high, a tilting bracket angles the picture down a few degrees. In a compact front room we also look at the windows on the street side, because afternoon light across a low room lands right on the screen.

The bracket we ordered online came with long lag bolts for wood studs and nothing else, and our TV wall is brick. Can you still use it?

Usually, yes. Lag bolts packed with a bracket are meant for wood studs, and on brick they are the wrong fixing, so we replace them with masonry anchors sized for the bracket and the TV. MantelMount's guidance is that total anchor capacity should be at least four times the TV's weight, and that is the margin we work to. What we check first is the bracket itself: that it is rated for your TV's weight and screen size, and that its bolt pattern matches the back of your TV. If both are right, we install your bracket at the labor prices on our list, and the brick add-on of $65 applies. If it is under-rated for the TV, we will tell you before anything goes on the wall.

Getting the TV here, and when the answer is no

We are picking the TV up from the store in our own car. Can it lie flat on the back seat until you come?

Ideally not. Flat-screen TVs are built and packed to travel upright, and lying a large screen flat lets its own weight flex the panel, especially over bumps. If it has to go in a car, keep it upright in its original box, wedged so it cannot tip, with nothing stacked against the screen side. With no subway inside Flatlands, plenty of households bring a TV home by car from a store along Flatbush Avenue or farther away, and screens of 65 inches and up often simply do not fit, so delivery is worth the fee for those. Once it is home, leave it in the box, standing up, until the technician arrives. The box is the safest way to move it into the room, and keeping the inserts lets you store or return it if anything is wrong.

The Glenwood Houses management office did not give consent for a wall bracket. What is the best way to set up a big TV without one?

A stand setup done properly. The NYCHA Resident Handbook requires written consent before a resident adds a fixture, so without it the TV stays off the wall, and that is a perfectly good result if it is done well. We set the TV on its own stand, on a console wide and deep enough for the stand's footprint, connect the cable box and any streaming device, tidy the cables behind the furniture, and place a soundbar in front if you have one. That is $119.99 for the TV on its stand and $79 for a soundbar placed, on our list. Because the CPSC's 2023 report found that 103 of 217 tip-over deaths it counted involved a falling television, a stand TV in a home with children should be secured with an anti-tip strap, and fixing a strap may itself need the office's agreement, so ask for that in the same request. If the office later gives consent for a bracket, the TV can go up then, priced by the wall.

Businesses in the industrial zone and on the avenues

Our dispatch office in the Flatlands/Fairfield zone is a prefab steel building with corrugated metal walls inside. Can a TV hang on that?

Not on the sheet metal alone. Corrugated or ribbed steel siding is thin, and a bracket screwed into it would be held by the metal around a few small holes, which tears under the pull of a screen. In a prefab building there is a frame behind the skin, usually steel girts and columns, and a TV can be fixed to that framing, or a plywood backing panel can be bolted across two framing members and the bracket fixed to the panel. Either way we need to see the inside wall, the frame members if they are visible, and the nearest outlet in your photos before quoting, and we price it from those photos rather than from the house list. If the building is a trailer or modular office, the same rule applies: we find what is structural and fix to that.

Our bakery on Avenue N has tile from the floor to about six feet and painted plaster above it. Where should the menu screen go?

Usually above the tile line, on the plaster, if the screen can sit high enough to be read over the counter. That keeps every hole out of the tile, which can crack when it is drilled and is hard to patch in a matching color, and it puts the screen above the steam and the splashes around a counter. Plaster on framing is the +$45 line on our list; if the plaster turns out to be on brick, the brick line applies instead. A screen mounted high for a counter usually needs a slight downward tilt so customers in line are not looking at reflections from the ceiling lights. If the only workable spot is on the tile, we price that from your photos, drill with a tile bit and a slow start, and tell you beforehand that a hole in tile is there for good.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Flatlands

These are claims that show up in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor, Fixr and Thumbtack, paraphrased or quoted, each followed by what Flatlands' houses and buildings actually show.

"Mounting a TV in Brooklyn costs about $280"

Angi's Brooklyn page gives an average of $280 for TV wall mounting, with a typical range of $172 to $376 and a low-to-high spread of $100 to $670. Fixr's national figure is $200, and Thumbtack's national average for the service is $100 to $130.

An average does not know what your TV wall is made of. Fixr itself prices brick, concrete and stone at $200 to $800, far above drywall, and in Flatlands a large share of TV walls are brick party walls in semi-detached houses or old plaster in frame houses and bungalows.

Our list makes the wall visible in the price: $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, +$45 for plaster, +$65 for brick or stone, so you can add up your own total before you call.

"Any surface can take a TV"

TaskRabbit's Brooklyn page says Taskers can typically mount a TV on any surface, and many AI answers repeat that every wall is fine with the right anchor.

Most walls are fine, but not all of them on the day. A plaster wall that is cracked or loose from its lath, a ceiling with a fresh water stain above the spot, or the thin metal skin of a prefab building in the industrial zone is not a surface to trust with a screen until something is fixed or added.

We look before we drill, and when the honest answer is to repair the wall, add a backing panel or wait for the landlord, that is what we tell you.

"Run the TV's cord through the wall to hide it"

Plenty of how-to answers suggest cutting two holes and feeding the power cord through the cavity.

NEC 400.12, quoted by EC&M, says flexible cords "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord in a wall cannot be inspected and can be damaged by a fixing. On a brick party wall, nothing can be fished inside at all.

On a framed wall the right answer is an in-wall kit with its own rated cable ($169); on masonry it is a paintable cord cover ($59) or a console directly below the screen.

"Public housing tenants are not allowed to mount TVs"

Some answers flatly say NYCHA residents can never put a TV on the wall; others ignore NYCHA's rules entirely.

The NYCHA Resident Handbook says residents may not add fixtures or fittings without prior written consent from the property management office. That is a consent rule, not a ban, and it applies to Glenwood Houses as much as any other development.

Ask the office in writing first, and we follow its answer exactly: a bracket if it agrees, a stand setup at $119.99 if it does not.

"Use a stud finder and screw into the studs"

The standard advice is to run a stud finder along the wall, mark two studs and fix the bracket into them.

In a semi-detached brick house the shared wall has no studs at all, just plaster over brick, as a Brownstoner forum contributor put it about older Brooklyn houses. In a 1920s frame house the plaster and lath confuse a scanner, and the studs are not always on regular spacing.

We confirm the wall with one pilot hole under the bracket plate and choose the anchor for what is really there.

"A TV on a stand is the safe option"

Many answers present a stand as the no-risk alternative to drilling.

The CPSC's 2023 tip-over report counted 217 tip-over deaths between 2013 and mid-2023, and 103 of them, 47 percent, involved a falling television. A stand TV in a home with small children is not automatically safe.

A stand setup is a good choice for renters and NYCHA households, and it should come with an anti-tip strap where children are in the home.

"Any handyman can add an outlet behind the TV"

Generic answers treat adding an outlet behind the screen as a small extra any installer can do.

The NYC Department of Buildings says changes to a building's electrical system need a permit and are work for a licensed master electrician. In NYC, that is not a TV installer's job, and it is not ours.

Our in-wall kit plugs into your existing outlet and needs no new wiring. If you want a new hard-wired outlet, an electrician puts it in first and we mount the TV after.
