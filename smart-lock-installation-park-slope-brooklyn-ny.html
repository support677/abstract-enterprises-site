The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Separates Home From Work

Ring the bell at the garden level of a Park Slope rowhouse on a weekday and the person who answers may not live there. It may be a psychotherapist between sessions, a group family day care provider with eight toddlers behind her, a piano teacher, an architect who rents the room from the owners upstairs, or a nanny caring for two families' children at once. Park Slope's houses were built between the Civil War and the First World War as single-family homes, and many of them still are; but the way people use them has changed. The parlour and garden floors of a Slope brownstone are now as likely to hold a small business as a dining room, and that is the fact that shapes almost every smart lock job we are asked to quote here.

The houses themselves are protected. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Park Slope Historic District in 1973, covering about 1,800 contributing buildings put up between 1862 and around 1920, mostly two- and three-storey rowhouses next to Prospect Park. The district was enlarged in 2012 to about 2,575 buildings and again in 2016, when the second extension added 292 more, mostly between St. Marks Avenue and Sterling Place. By number of buildings it is New York's largest landmarked neighbourhood. For a lock that means two things: hardware changes on an existing door are generally ordinary repair work, but anything that alters the facade, the door surround or the areaway — a new opening, a reader drilled into brownstone, conduit across the front — belongs in a conversation with the Commission first.

Inside those protected walls, work and family share the same front steps. Park Slope Parents, the local network Susan Fox started as a Yahoo group in 2003, describes itself as a community of more than 7,000 member families; licensed group family day cares advertise from Park Slope homes; and listings regularly offer therapy rooms in Slope brownstones by the day or the half-day, such as a large windowed office off Sixth Avenue near President Street. New York City's zoning rules allow this: a "home occupation" may take up to 49 percent of a dwelling unit's floor area, to a maximum of 1,000 square feet, and a professional may employ one person who does not live there. The same rule forbids clients from queuing or waiting outside the dwelling unit — which is, in practice, a rule about the front door.

So the questions we hear in Park Slope are less about which brand is best and more about where the lines fall. Can a client reach the consulting room without being able to wander up the stairs to where the children sleep? Can a day-care parent have the "unlimited and on demand access" to their child that New York's day care regulations promise, while the door still keeps strangers out? Can the front parlour, with its original pocket doors, be closed and locked as an office during the day and opened as a living room at night? Can a nanny who works for two families on the block have a code that stops working when the share ends? A good smart lock plan answers those questions door by door, and it starts with a rule the state writes into its day care regulations and that we apply in every house: every exit opens from the inside without a key.

The avenues are a different world with some of the same tensions. The Park Slope Fifth Avenue BID, set up in June 2008, covers 62 block faces and 521 ground-floor businesses; Seventh Avenue is lined with restaurants, boutiques and specialty food shops, many with apartments above. In the autumn of 2021 Patch reported about 30 burglaries at Park Slope businesses in two months, with the BID noting that many of the Fifth Avenue break-ins hit shops without roll-down gates and the precinct urging commercial-grade door locks and cameras; at one Eighth Avenue restaurant near 11th Street, the burglar simply broke the front door at a quarter past three in the morning. Shopkeepers here need staff codes, schedules and a front door that resists more than a shove — and, like the brownstones behind them, a door that never traps anyone inside.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Park Slope's Architecture

Park Slope was built in one long burst, mostly between the 1860s and about 1920, and then, a century later, rebuilt along Fourth Avenue. Six building types cover nearly every door we are asked about in the neighbourhood, and each one splits between home and work in its own way.

Brownstone and Brick Rowhouses

Two- to four-storey rows with a high stoop to the parlour floor and a garden entrance beneath it. Original front doors are often heavy, panelled and glazed, sometimes in pairs with a vestibule behind. The garden level is where practices, day cares and rentals usually live.

Limestone and Romanesque Rows

The later rows of the 1880s and 1890s — Queen Anne and Romanesque Revival fronts in limestone, terra cotta and tan brick, like the 1891 George P. Chappell rowhouse and the 1892 Montgomery Place houses. Deep reveals, carved surrounds and doors that were made to measure.

Prospect Park West Pre-War Co-ops

Apartment buildings from the 1920s, such as the 16-storey Emery Roth co-op completed in 1929 at 35 Prospect Park West. Doorman lobbies, uniform unit doors, house rules on alterations and boards that want a key or code on file.

Flats Over the Avenues

Three- and four-storey mixed buildings on Fifth and Seventh Avenues with a shop at street level and apartments above, reached through a narrow side door that the shop does not use but every tenant does.

Fourth Avenue New Buildings

Condominiums and rentals built after the City Council adopted the Park Slope rezoning on April 30, 2003, which mapped higher-density R8A zoning along Fourth Avenue. Developer-chosen lobby entry, steel-framed unit doors and often a ground-floor commercial space.

Conversions and Institutions

Former factories such as the Ansonia Clock Company works on Seventh Avenue and 12th Street, built in 1880–81 and residential since 1982; churches, synagogues and schools whose side doors serve many groups through the week.

How each building type changes the lock work

Rowhouse front doors. Original Slope doors are frequently thicker than the range many retail smart deadbolts list, and many were bored decades ago for a mortise lock or a rim lock rather than the tubular deadbolt a box-store smart lock expects. We measure thickness, backset and the existing preparation before anyone buys anything. Where a standard smart deadbolt will not fit honestly, an interior retrofit unit on a compatible single-cylinder deadbolt, a longer through-bolt kit from the manufacturer, or a mortise-format electronic lock is the answer — not a new hole in a 130-year-old door.

Garden entrances. The under-stoop door is usually sheltered from rain but not from damp, and it sits behind an iron gate in the areaway. Battery locks there need a check on condensation and on how far the keypad sits from the gate a client must pass through. If the practice or day care needs a buzz-in from inside, the door gets a strike and a small power supply, and the cable route is planned through the cellar rather than across the front of the house, so nothing new appears on the protected facade.

Interior doors. Pocket doors, parlour doors with original glass, and the stair door between the garden and parlour floors were never meant to carry security hardware. We use privacy-function and edge-mounted hardware that leaves the door's original pulls in place where possible, and we never fit anything that needs a key or a powered release to get out of a room.

Co-op and new-building unit doors. The building's alteration agreement or house rules come first. Many boards accept an interior retrofit unit that leaves the hall side and the building's key untouched; others require their own cylinder or a duplicate code. Steel unit doors in Fourth Avenue buildings usually take a standard smart deadbolt without trouble once the building agrees.

Avenue shops and conversions. Aluminium storefront frames, steel back doors and narrow stiles call for commercial hardware: keypad levers or readers with strikes rated for the traffic, and an exit that works with one push from inside even when the electronics are off.

Rules that apply to Park Slope business doors

  • Exit without a key. Doors on the way out of a business, a day care or a rented practice room must open from the inside without a key or special knowledge. Double-cylinder deadbolts that need a key inside have no place on these doors, and HPD states plainly that they are illegal on apartment doors.
  • Electrified locks release on the egress side. Where a strike or magnetic lock is used, the door must still open from inside by ordinary action or by a release that does not depend on knowledge or a credential; a magnetic lock needs a sensor and a manual release, and in some settings a fire alarm tie-in.
  • Landmarks. Hardware replaced on an existing door is generally a no-permit repair in the Commission's guidance; a new storefront door, a reader or camera drilled into a historic front, or conduit across it is a matter for the Landmarks Preservation Commission first.
  • Day care licensing. New York's group family day care rules require egress doors to open from the inside without using a key, and the state's egress policy prohibits keyed doors and gates on the path out. Providers in the city answer to the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene as well; we describe the hardware, they and their licensor decide what meets their rules.
  • Home occupations. Under the zoning definition, clients may not wait outside the dwelling unit and a home occupation may not make external structural alterations that are not customary for residences — another reason the practice entrance stays a normal-looking door with discreet hardware.
  • Privacy. A practice keeping health information on site has its own federal obligations on physical access, and a residential owner with three or more units who adds smart access falls under the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act. We set the systems up to support those obligations; the obligations themselves stay with the business or owner.

Smart Lock Installation in Park Slope: Questions Answered

The practice on the garden floor

The only thing between my garden-floor consulting room and the family's stair is an old door with no lock — what should go on it?

Something mechanical, simple and lockable from one side only. There are really two doors in play. The first is the entrance clients use, usually the under-stoop door, which gets whatever credential suits your sessions: a code, a buzz-in from your consulting room, or both. The second is the door between the practice and the family — typically the door at the foot of the inside stair or between the garden-floor hall and the kitchen. That second door is the one that keeps a client in the waiting area from wandering upstairs, and it is the one most people forget. We fit it with a lock that is locked from the practice side and always opens from the family side and from the practice side by hand, so nobody can be shut into either part of the house. During the day it stays locked; in the evening, when the practice closes, the family can leave it open. Where the stair has no door at all, a new door in the existing opening is carpentry first and hardware second, and we will say so plainly at the walkthrough.

Could the lock's entry history give away who is in therapy with me, and how do we stop that?

It could, if it is set up carelessly, because a smart lock's history is a timestamped list of named codes, and a list of names arriving at a therapist's door every Tuesday is sensitive in itself. Three habits prevent it. Label client codes with initials or a reference number that only your own records decode, never full names. Keep the practice entrance in an account the practice controls, so family members, a partner's phone or a nanny with app access never see the practice's history. And set the history to the shortest retention the lock allows that still serves you, deleting old entries rather than keeping years of them. Where you would rather have no client records at all, a buzz-in from the consulting room through a video panel and an electric strike lets you admit people without anyone holding a code. Avoid one shared code for every client: it cannot be withdrawn from one person and tends to end up written on appointment cards.

Does seeing clients at home change what the city expects of our front door?

Indirectly, yes. New York's Zoning Resolution allows a home occupation in a dwelling unit within limits — currently up to 49 percent of the unit's floor area and no more than 1,000 square feet, with one non-resident employee allowed in connection with a profession — and it adds conditions that land right at the door: clients may not queue or wait outside the dwelling unit, there may be no exterior display, and the home occupation may not make external structural alterations that are not customary for a residence. In Park Slope the house will usually be in the historic district as well. Put together, the practice entrance should look and work like a house door: discreet hardware on the existing door, a waiting area inside rather than a queue on the stoop, and no new signage or equipment drilled into the front without checking with the Landmarks Preservation Commission. We are installers, not zoning lawyers; for a specific question about your practice, ask the Department of City Planning or your own adviser.

A licensed day care in the house

We run a licensed group family day care on the garden level — how should parents get in at drop-off and pick-up?

Give each family its own credential, and make the door open for them without anyone leaving the children. New York's group family day care regulations give the parent of a child in care unlimited and on-demand access to their child and to the caregivers during hours of operation, and the right to inspect the parts of the home used for care. A parent code or fob on the garden entrance, active during your operating hours and labelled with the family name, delivers that access without the provider having to stop and answer the door twenty times in the morning rush. Outside operating hours the parent codes switch off, so a parent cannot walk into your home on a Sunday. Grandparents and sitters who collect a child are added as separate, time-limited codes once the family has authorised them in writing, and removed just as easily. The lock records each code's use, but the paper sign-in sheet the rules require for visitors stays exactly as it is.

Does the state's rule against keyed exits affect the gate under our stoop and the back-garden door?

Yes, and those two openings are where providers most often get caught out. The regulations require egress doors from the residence to open from the inside without a key, and the state's egress policy for family and group family day care goes further: doors and gates at the ends of passageways must be readily openable from the side people are leaving from, without a key, and doors and gates with keys are prohibited on that path. In a Park Slope rowhouse the path out from the garden floor often runs through the areaway gate to the sidewalk, and the second way out usually runs through the back-garden door into the yard. A padlock on the areaway gate, a keyed deadbolt on the garden door or a double-cylinder lock on either one breaks that rule. We fit gate latches and door hardware that open by hand from the inside, put any keypad on the outside face only, and position latches where an adult can operate them at once but a toddler will not by accident. Your licensor makes the final call on any specific door.

Lines inside the house

Can the pocket doors between our front and back parlours lock, so the front parlour works as a closed office?

They can, with the right hardware and some care. Pocket doors slide into the wall on rollers hung from a track, and on original parlour doors that track is often still inside the wall behind the casing; the Brownstone Boys, who restore them, note that you may need to remove part of the trim to reach the rollers. A meeting pair of pocket doors can take a flush hook-and-strike lock in the edges, operated by a small flush pull, or a privacy set designed for pocket doors; a single door can lock into the jamb. A full smart lock is rarely the right tool on a pocket door, because there is nowhere for the inside unit to go when the door slides away, so for a home office we usually fit mechanical pocket-door locks on the parlour doors and put the smart lock on the door into the hall instead. Before anything is cut, we check that the doors still run freely; a door that binds in its pocket will defeat any lock.

Our live-in au pair's room needs a lock of its own — should it be a smart lock we can open, or a privacy lock only she controls?

A privacy lock she controls is usually the right answer. The room is her home while she lives with you, and a lock that the family can open from a phone at any moment is not much of a lock to the person behind it. What works well is a quality privacy or keyed-entry lockset on her bedroom door, with the key in her hands and a spare kept somewhere she knows about for emergencies. If you want her to have her own way in and out of the house at hours that suit her, that belongs on the house door: her own code on the front or garden entrance, set up with her, and removed on her last day. We avoid fitting app-controlled locks on bedroom doors in a family home at all, because a lock whose history and controls sit with someone else is the wrong tool for a private room.

A nanny share means another family's caregiver brings their child to our house most mornings — what access should she get?

Her own code, on one door, for the hours of the share, and nothing more. A nanny share creates an unusual situation: the person caring for your child may be employed by another family, and when the share ends, the arrangement ends with it. We set her code to the days and hours the share runs, on the door she actually uses — usually the garden entrance with the stroller, not the parlour door — and give it an end date matching the share agreement. The other family should not be an administrator of your lock, and the caregiver does not need the app; a code is simpler. If the share moves between the two houses week by week, each house runs its own lock and its own codes, and nobody needs to know the other family's.

We're opening our house on the Park Slope House Tour — how should the locks be set for a day of strangers walking through?

Set them so the tour's route is open and everything else is shut. The Park Slope Civic Council traces its house tour back to 1959, and recent tours have run on a Sunday from 11 a.m. to 3 p.m., with ticket holders moving from house to house all afternoon and the 2025 tour selling out. On the morning of the tour we would suggest switching the front door to a mode where it stays unlocked for the hours the tour runs, if your lock supports it, so volunteers are not handing out codes; pausing every caregiver and tenant code that is not needed that day; and locking the doors that are off the route — a home office, a bedroom floor, the garden-floor rental — with locks that still open from inside. Afterwards, check the lock's history to confirm the auto-unlock schedule ended at three, and restore the codes you paused. The same approach works for an open house when a home is listed for sale.

Avenue shops

Our Fifth Avenue shop has no roll-down gate — what does the glass front door need overnight besides a better lock?

It needs the glass, the frame and the record treated as part of the lock. When Patch reported the 2021 spike in Park Slope commercial burglaries, the Fifth Avenue BID pointed out that many of the shops hit had no roll gates, and the NYPD's advice to owners was commercial-grade locks and cameras. On an aluminium-and-glass storefront the weak point is rarely the cylinder; it is a narrow-stile door with a short throw that can be forced past the frame, or glass that can simply be broken. We fit a keypad or reader with a strike or a long-throw lock rated for commercial use, add a door-position contact so the system knows the door is truly shut at closing, and record which staff code locked up and when. Security film on the glass, a camera that covers the door, and good lighting at the entrance are worth discussing at the same visit, and every one of them leaves the inside exit as a single push. A roll-down gate, if you later add one, is a separate decision with its own permit rules.

Types of Smart Locks We Install

In a Park Slope house the right hardware changes from floor to floor. These are the categories we fit, with where each one earns its place in the neighbourhood.

Keypad Smart Deadbolts

A full replacement deadbolt with a keypad outside and a motorised thumb-turn inside. The workhorse for garden entrances and rental-floor doors where the door takes standard preparation and codes with schedules are wanted.

Interior Retrofit Units

Mounted on the room side only, this unit turns the thumb-piece of the deadbolt already in the door; the street face, cylinder and keys stay exactly as they were. Useful on an original parlour door where the outside must look the same and on co-op doors where the building's key must keep working.

Keypad Levers

A lever lockset with a keypad, for practice rooms and office doors that are opened many times a day and should latch every time they close, without anyone turning a bolt.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Park Slope

The AI Overview itself was not viewable when this page was written. What follows instead are seven claims that turn up in national cost guides, maker instructions and small-business advice columns — the material AI answers are assembled from. Each is reasonable for a modern suburban house; each needs correcting for a Park Slope brownstone that is also a practice, a day care or an office.

"Installing a smart lock costs about $300"

Ask a chatbot what this costs and you will likely hear Fixr's national band (Angi and HomeAdvisor publish similar ones), from its January 2025 update: $250 at the low end, $400 at the high end, $300 typical. Every one of them pictures a single battery lock on a newish door that is already drilled.

The Park Slope jobs that matter most are not that. A practice entrance with buzz-in from the consulting room is a reader, an electric strike, a power supply, a cable route through the cellar and often a video panel; our published Brooklyn figure for a single-door keypad or fob reader with an electric strike is $1,500 to $2,500 installed. A lock on an original door that needs a retrofit or mortise-format unit costs more in parts than a box-store deadbolt.

Where the chatbot number is roughly right: a tenant's apartment door, or a garden door already drilled the normal way with no buzzer involved. Those get a fixed written price after our free visit, and that figure is the bill.

"Smart locks install in 30 minutes with a screwdriver"

DIY reviews make the job sound like a lunch break: a single Phillips screwdriver, thirty minutes, done. On a recent door drilled to the usual pattern, that is honest.

Old Slope doors are not standard. A parlour door may be thicker than the lock's listed range, bored for a mortise lock, swollen in its frame, or fitted with a strike that has been moved three times. An hour spent measuring, planing and realigning before the lock goes on is normal, and on a door that drags, the motor of a smart deadbolt will strain and drain its batteries until the door itself is fixed.

The screwdriver estimate also ignores the second door — the one between the practice and the house, or between the day care and the stair — which is often the more important door and the harder carpentry.

"A home business can only use 25 percent of the home"

Business-advice sites still repeat that a New York City home occupation may use no more than 25 percent of a home's floor area and no more than 500 square feet. That was the old rule.

The current text of Zoning Resolution section 12-10 allows a home occupation to occupy up to 49 percent of the dwelling unit's floor area and in no event more than 1,000 square feet, and still forbids clients queuing or waiting outside the unit, exterior displays and external structural alterations that are not customary for a residence.

For a lock plan, the conditions matter more than the percentages: a practice entrance that looks like a house door, a waiting area inside the line of the front wall, and no new equipment on the facade. Check your own situation with the Department of City Planning or an adviser before relying on any summary, including this one.

"Lock every door to keep children safe"

General childproofing advice often says to lock doors so small children cannot get out, and in a family home a high latch on a door to the street is sensible.

In a licensed day care, the rules point the other way on the path out. New York's group family day care regulations require egress doors to open from the inside without a key, and the state's egress policy prohibits keyed doors and gates on that path, including gates at the end of a passageway such as an areaway gate.

The way to square that circle is placement and type, not more locks: latches an adult can operate in one motion, set where toddlers do not reach them by accident, keypads on the outside only, and a written description of each door for the provider's own file.

"One code for the whole household is simplest"

Setup guides often start by suggesting a single family code. In a one-person apartment that is fine.

In a Park Slope house with clients, day-care parents, a nanny, a dog walker and a tenant, a shared code means nobody can be removed without changing it for everybody, and the lock's history tells you nothing. Clients end courses of treatment, day-care families graduate to kindergarten, nanny shares end, tenants move.

Every person who needs access gets their own code with its own schedule and end date, and the household's own codes stay separate from the practice's. It takes an hour longer to set up and saves the household from re-keying every time an arrangement ends.

"Any Wi-Fi smart lock works anywhere in the house"

Manufacturers quote short distances for their bridges: August's own support pages ask for the Connect bridge to sit within 10 to 15 feet of the lock. Park Slope residents on r/parkslope ask what Wi-Fi setups actually work in multi-level brownstones, because a router on the parlour floor often reaches the garden floor badly through brick and plaster.

A garden-floor practice door with its bridge two floors away will drop offline, and its schedules and remote unlocks will fail at the worst moment. We place the bridge or hub on the same floor as the door, use a Thread border router or Z-Wave hub where they suit better, and test from a phone on mobile data out on the sidewalk.

If the practice keeps its own network, the practice lock goes on it; the family's front door stays on the household network.

"In a historic district you need permission to change a lock"

Some summaries go further and suggest a Park Slope owner must apply before swapping a deadbolt. Read the Commission's permit guidebook and you find locks and handles in the list of hardware whose repair or replacement may need no permit at all.

What does need the Commission is work that changes the protected fabric: a new door at the front, a reader or intercom drilled into the stone, conduit across the facade, changes to the areaway. In the city's largest historic district, that is a line worth respecting.

We keep new hardware on the existing door and the cable inside the house, and where an owner wants a reader or video panel beside the garden entrance, we say at the walkthrough that the Commission should be asked first and provide cut sheets for the application.

For Home Practices

Running a Practice From a Brownstone Without Opening the House

Therapists, tutors, music teachers, speech and occupational therapists, architects and accountants all see people in Park Slope homes. The details differ, but the access plan that works is remarkably consistent.

  • One entrance for clients, never the family's. Clients use the garden door; the family uses the parlour door. Even when both doors are on the same lock app, they are separate locks with separate codes.
  • A waiting area inside. Zoning bars clients from waiting outside the dwelling unit; a code or buzz-in that lets the next client into a waiting area on time, without interrupting a session, meets that rule and keeps the stoop clear.
  • Codes that end on their own. Each client's code carries the session days and an end date; a client who finishes a course of work simply stops having a code, with no awkward conversation.
  • Minimal names in the history. Initials or a reference number only, as explained in the questions above.
  • A business account for the business door. The practice entrance sits in an account the practice owns, so it can move, close or change hands without touching the family's front door.
  • Health information is a separate obligation. A practice that keeps health records on site has federal rules on controlling physical access, including visitor control and documenting repairs to locks and doors. The lock history helps; the practice's own policies are what satisfy the rule.

Practitioners who rent a room by the day in someone else's brownstone have a narrower version of the same need: their own code, for their own days, on the entrance and the room, removed when the arrangement ends. The owner stays the administrator; the practitioner never needs the app.

When the practice leaves. Practices move, retire or outgrow a garden floor, and the house should not carry their access forward. At the end of an arrangement the practice account is removed from the garden lock, every client code still on it is deleted rather than paused, and the stair door goes back to whatever the family wants it to be. If the practice owned the lock outright, it can take the lock with it and we refit the house's own hardware; if the house owns the lock, a factory reset and fresh administrator set-up take less than an hour. Either way, the written register is updated the same day, so the next practitioner starts from a clean list.

For Day Care Providers

Licensed home day cares in Park Slope operate in the same rowhouses and apartments as their neighbours, under rules written for safety first and convenience second. The door plan has to respect that order.

Two ways out, both keyless from inside. The state's egress policy expects a first-floor family or group family day care to have two means of egress, with an approved window as one of them only where the provider has applied for that arrangement. In a garden-floor day care the two ways out are usually the under-stoop entrance and the back-garden door. Both get hardware that opens from inside in one motion; neither gets a double-cylinder deadbolt; and the areaway gate at the end of the path keeps a latch, not a padlock.

Parents in, strangers out. Each enrolled family has its own code or fob for the garden entrance, live only during operating hours. Grandparents and regular sitters are added once the family authorises them in writing, each with their own credential. Because the rules give parents unlimited and on-demand access to their child during operating hours, we never set parent codes so tightly that a parent arriving early for a sick child is shut out.

Paper and electronic records side by side. The written visitor sign-in and sign-out sheet stays at the door. The lock's history adds an automatic record of each opening, which helps if a time on the sheet is ever questioned, and the written description we provide of each door's hardware can go in the provider's own file.

The house above. Where the provider's own family lives upstairs, the stair door between the day care and the house gets the same treatment as a practice's: locked from the day-care side so children cannot climb into the house, openable by hand from both sides so no adult or child is ever shut in.

Before an inspection. Shops After Dark

Closing Up on Fifth and Seventh Avenues

An avenue shop's lock problem is usually a closing-time problem. The last person out has to be sure the door is locked, the owner wants to know who that was, and the next morning the first person in needs to get in without waiting for the manager with the key. A keypad or reader with individual staff codes answers all three: the system logs which code locked the door, a door-position contact confirms it is truly shut rather than resting on the latch, and the opener's code works only from the hour the shift starts.

Two Park Slope events shape where the hardware goes. The first is the burglary spike of autumn 2021, when the Fifth Avenue BID counted about 30 business burglaries in two months and noted that shops without roll-down gates were often the ones hit; a glass front door with a short-throw lock is the place to spend money. The second is water. After storm water flooded Fifth Avenue in autumn 2023, the BID told Brooklyn Paper that more than 30 businesses on the avenue were directly affected and that up to six feet of water filled basements, destroying equipment. A door controller and power supply put in a shop cellar on Fifth Avenue belong high on the wall, or on the ground floor behind the counter, not on the cellar floor.

Back doors deserve the same care as front doors. Many avenue shops open at the rear onto yards that back onto the gardens of the brownstone blocks behind them, and those doors are easy to forget because customers never see them. A reinforced strike, a latch guard and a staff code on the outside, with a plain lever or push bar inside, protects the shop without turning the back door into a trap for the staff working at the far end of a narrow store.

Where a shop shares its building with apartments above, the tenants' entrance and the shop's doors are separate systems with separate codes. The owner of the building, not the shop, decides what goes on the tenants' street door, and the shop's staff never need a code to the flats.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Park Slope Houses That Are Also Workplaces

The order we walk a Slope house in before writing a price. This describes our usual method; it is not an account of any one client's home.

We start downstairs, not at the front door. Who comes in through the garden entrance on a weekday, and who should never get further than the room they came for? We stand at the foot of the inside stair and ask what is on the other side of it.

Then we test every way out. We open the garden door, the back-garden door and the areaway gate from the inside, by hand, as a parent carrying a child or a client in a hurry would. If any of them needs a key, a second hand or a hunt for a hidden latch, that is the first thing on the written plan, before any smart lock.

Only then do we go up to the parlour door, measure it, look at what its bore was made for, and check that it closes without being pushed. We write down every person who needs a code, their days and hours, and when the arrangement ends. The lock choice comes last, because by then it is usually obvious.

FAQ

Park Slope Smart Lock Installation FAQ

What does a keypad and electric strike for a garden-floor practice entrance cost in Park Slope?
+

Our published Brooklyn figure for a single-door keypad or fob reader with an electric strike, installed, is $1,500 to $2,500, and Park Slope is priced at the Brooklyn base. That covers the reader, the strike cut into the frame, a power supply, the cable run and programming. Where the door only needs a new strike for an existing buzzer, the published figure is $250 to $450. A battery smart deadbolt on the practice door, which is often all a one-room practice needs, has no published figure and is quoted flat in writing after the free walkthrough.

Can a client's code be set to stop working by itself when their course of sessions ends?
+

Yes, on most current smart locks and on every access-control reader we fit. A code can be given a start date, an end date and a weekly schedule, so a client who comes on Tuesdays at four for twelve weeks has a code that works only then and expires on its own after the last session. You can still delete it early, extend it, or turn it into a one-time code. We set the first few up with you so the pattern is clear before you do the rest yourself.

Should the lock's app account be in the practice's name or the household's?
+

If the practice is a real business with its own email and records, put the practice entrance in an account the practice owns, and keep the family's front door in a separate household account. That keeps client entry history out of the family's phones, keeps the children's comings and goings out of the practice's records, and means the practice can be handed over, closed or moved without touching the house. One person can be an administrator on both accounts; they simply stay separate.

Do you cover Windsor Terrace, Prospect Heights, Gowanus and the South Slope as well?
+

Yes. The same Brooklyn crews and the same Brooklyn prices cover the South Slope below the Prospect Expressway, Windsor Terrace across the park's southwest corner, Prospect Heights across Flatbush Avenue and Gowanus down the hill toward the canal. Each is a short drive from our Troy Avenue office, and the walkthrough, written quote and installation work the same way in all of them.

Can you give us a written description of the door hardware for our day-care licence file?
+

Yes. After installation we can send a short written description of each door we worked on: the lock or strike fitted, how it opens from the inside, whether it needs power to release, what happens in a power cut, and who holds administrator access. Day-care providers have found it useful to keep that with their own records for inspections. It is a description of the hardware, not an approval; only the licensing agency can say whether a home meets its rules.

Should the practice's smart lock run on a separate Wi-Fi network from the family's?
+

It is a good idea where the practice already keeps its own computers apart from the household's. Most routers can run a separate guest or business network, and the practice lock's bridge or hub can join that one, so a child's tablet and the practice's door never share a network. Where the lock uses Thread or Z-Wave rather than Wi-Fi, the hub is the piece that matters, and it can sit on the practice side of the house.

Can a day care's door log replace the paper visitor sign-in sheet?
+

No, and it should not try to. New York's group family day care rules require visitors to sign in and to sign out in writing with the time they leave, so the written log stays. What the lock's history adds is a second, automatic record of when the door was opened and by which code, which is useful if a time on the sheet is ever questioned. We set the lock's history to name each parent's code clearly so the two records are easy to compare.
