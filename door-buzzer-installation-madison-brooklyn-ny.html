The Neighborhood

Why Madison Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Madison sits in southern Brooklyn between Kings Highway to the north and Avenue U to the south, with Ocean Avenue on its western side and Nostrand Avenue on its eastern side, although some descriptions carry it east to Gerritsen Avenue. Wikipedia describes it as "a purely residential subsection of Sheepshead Bay, located just south of Midwood," named for James Madison High School on Bedford Avenue, and it shares the 11229 ZIP code and Community Board 15 with its neighbors. Untapped New York dates the start of its development to 1877 and calls it one of the last parts of Brooklyn to be built up; City Neighborhoods says most of its houses went up between 1910 and 1930. Both agree on what a visitor sees today: tree-lined side streets of two-family brick houses, limestone row houses and detached brick and stucco homes with driveways, and larger apartment buildings along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue.

For a buzzer installer, that split is the whole story. On the side streets, the door belongs to a family, sometimes with a tenant upstairs, and the question is how many buttons the house needs and how the release reaches the people who answer it. On the avenues, the door belongs to a building of thirty-five, forty-seven or fifty-three apartments, and the question is whether a wired intercom that was installed long ago, repaired many times and never fully documented can carry on, or whether it has reached the point where testing every apartment one by one costs more than replacing it. The records Openigloo keeps for five Madison buildings show the pattern clearly. 2197 Ocean Avenue has 35 apartments on six floors and was built in 1928. 1575 East 24th Street has 53 apartments on six floors and dates to 1930. 2528 Kings Highway has 47 apartments on six floors, built in 1936. 1680 East 22nd Street has 49 apartments on six floors, built in 1941. 2523 Ocean Avenue, a 2005 building, has 15 apartments on six floors.

Four of those five buildings are prewar elevator houses of between thirty-five and fifty-three apartments. That puts them in a legal position that surprises many owners and tenants. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law requires a self-locking main door and an intercom that lets tenants talk to a visitor and release the door in every class A building of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968; buildings that went up before then must install both only when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask or consent in writing. Almost every one of Madison's six-story apartment houses predates that line. Many have had intercoms for decades anyway, put in by an earlier owner or after a tenant request, and once a building has one, Habitat magazine's February 2024 article on the subject notes that the city's Housing Maintenance Code "also requires a functioning intercom," and that failing to maintain it "is a breach of the all-important warranty of habitability under state law."

The practical result is that many Madison buildings are not deciding whether to have an intercom. They are deciding what to do with one that has been failing a few apartments at a time. Openigloo shows open HPD violations on more than one of the buildings above without saying what they are for, and one review of 1680 East 22nd Street says the super "can be hard to reach." Put those together and you have the most common call we get from a building like this: some tenants can hear visitors and some cannot, the release works from the third floor but not the fifth, and nobody is sure whether the fault is in the lobby, the riser or the apartments. Brick Underground describes a renter who waited three months for an inspector after a 311 complaint about a broken intercom and a propped door, which is what happens to the front door of a building while everyone waits: residents start leaving it open for visitors they cannot buzz in.

The houses have problems of their own. A two-family brick house with one bell for two floors means the downstairs household answers the door for everyone. A detached house where the bell wire was lost in a renovation ends up with a battery doorbell camera that shows a picture but opens nothing. A limestone row house rented to a second household has a tenant who has to come down a flight of stairs to let in every courier. None of those is a legal matter in the way the apartment houses are; they are about making the door work for the people behind it. Community District 15, which takes in Madison, recorded 26,319 residents aged 65 and over in the 2010 census, 16.5 percent of the population, and 8,459 householders 65 or older living alone, so in a good share of homes the person answering the door is someone for whom an extra trip down the stairs is not a small thing.

Repair or Replace in Madison

Need Repair in Madison? Diagnose the Lobby, the Riser and the Apartments Before Anyone Says "New System"

The first question on almost every Madison apartment-house call is whether the old intercom can be saved. Owners have usually heard two opinions already: a handyman who says it only needs a new transformer and a salesman who says the whole thing is obsolete. Both can be right about different buildings. ButterflyMX's guide to small-building buzzers puts it plainly: "Do not replace a system based on age alone." What decides it is where the faults are and how many there are, and that takes testing, not a look at the brass plate.

The symptoms tell us where to start. The same ButterflyMX guide lists the usual clues, and they match what we find in Madison's six-story houses. Continuous buzzing "may indicate a stuck button or another entry-panel issue," which points at the lobby. "No audio or one-way audio may point to an apartment station, audio path, connection, or shared-equipment fault," which can be anywhere from one handset to the common amplifier. "Static, humming, or intermittent calls may indicate wiring or interference problems," which usually means the riser. And a release that fails everywhere is normally the strike, the relay or the power supply, while a release that fails from a few apartments is the line to those apartments. The guide also notes that how many apartments are affected can matter more than the noise itself, and that is the key observation: one bad handset is a repair, one dead floor is a riser repair, and a third of the building failing in different ways is usually the start of a replacement conversation.

We sort Madison buildings into three groups after testing. The first is a sound system with a few failed parts: a power supply in the cellar, a worn strike, a cracked lobby speaker, a handful of handsets. Those are repaired, and we will not sell a new system to a building that a few parts would fix. The second is a system whose lobby equipment and riser test well but whose apartment stations are failing or unavailable; there the riser stays, and the lobby panel and stations are replaced with a modern system built to run on the existing wire. The third is a building where the riser itself has failed in several places, or where earlier repairs have left splices nobody can trace; there a new system with new cable on the worst lines is the honest answer. If a system has to be tested in person to tell which group it is in, that is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 goes toward whichever path you choose. This page is about installing and replacing; straight repairs are handled on our repair pages.

Six-story prewar apartment houses: the riser is the building's real intercom

In a Madison apartment house, the part of the intercom residents see, the lobby panel and the handset by each apartment door, is the smaller part of the system. The bigger part is the riser: the bundle of wire that runs from the cellar up through the building, floor by floor, with a branch to every apartment. In a 1930s building it is usually inside the walls of a stair hall or a stack of closets, it was installed by someone long gone, and it has been spliced every time a handset or a floor had trouble. Whether that riser is sound decides whether the building's next system costs a few thousand dollars or a great deal more.

So the riser gets tested before anything is chosen. We find where the apartment lines meet in the cellar or the lobby, identify each line, and test each one for continuity, shorts between conductors and shorts to ground. The result is a list: which apartments' lines are good, which are marginal and which have failed. With that list, an owner or a board can see the real choice. If most lines pass, a system built to run on existing wire keeps the walls closed and the cost down. If a whole section of the riser has failed, it is replaced in a painted raceway along the stair hall or through the closets, and only that section.

The mistake we see most in these buildings is a new lobby panel installed on an untested riser. The panel works on the day it goes in for the apartments that happened to be working, and the apartments that were already failing stay silent, now on a system that cost far more. Testing first costs a fraction of that, and it is the only way to give a building a price that does not change halfway through.

Co-op apartment buildings: the board decides, and shareholders want to know why

Several of Madison's prewar apartment houses are cooperatives. In those buildings, the entry system is the co-op's responsibility, and Habitat's February 2024 article quoted Nancy Kourland of the Lasser Law Group: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The same article said a shareholder is "very unlikely" to be responsible for it under the proprietary lease, although the lease should be checked. That makes the board's decision a building-wide one, and shareholders will want to understand it.

What a board needs from us is a written report it can share: the test results line by line, what each option includes, what it costs, whether any option carries a monthly fee and who pays it, and how residents will answer the door afterward. We separate the common parts, the lobby panel, the strike, the power supply and the riser work, from anything inside individual apartments, so the cost can be allocated the way the building's documents say. And we plan the installation so the lobby door keeps locking throughout and no shareholder has to leave an apartment open for a technician.

Newer mid-size buildings: the system came with the building

Madison has some buildings from the last few decades, such as 2523 Ocean Avenue, built in 2005 with 15 apartments on six floors. A building of that age and size is on the post-1968 side of §50-a, so an intercom with talk and release has been required since it opened. Its entry system was chosen by the developer, and the people who manage it now often do not have the paperwork.

The first job in such a building is to find out what is there: the make and model of the panel and the stations, whether it uses a software account or a subscription, who holds the administrator login, and whether replacement parts are still available. Sometimes the answer is a reset and a rebuilt directory. Sometimes it is a new panel compatible with the existing stations. A full replacement is the last resort for a building whose wiring was installed to modern standards.

Two-family brick houses: two buttons, two floors, one door

The two-family brick house is the backbone of Madison's side streets. One household lives on the ground floor and one on the second, and in most houses both use the same front door into a small hall with the stairs going straight up. The usual complaint is that there is one bell, it rings on the ground floor, and the downstairs household ends up answering the door for both. The usual fix is a two-button station at the door, an answering station with a release on each floor, and a strike in the front door frame.

The details depend on the house. If the downstairs apartment has its own inner door off the hall, the release on the front door can be pressed from upstairs without giving anyone access to the ground-floor apartment. If the two households share the hall freely, as relatives often do, a single release is enough. If the house has a separate basement door used by one household, that door can get its own button or stay on a key. We ask who uses which door before we plan the buttons, because the answer changes the job.

Detached brick, stucco and Colonial houses: one family, many floors

Madison's detached houses, from brick and stucco homes with side driveways to the Dutch Colonial on a corner lot that a 2401 Avenue R listing described, need only one button, so owners often think the job is just a doorbell. The real question is where the family answers from. A household that spends the day in a kitchen at the back of the house and sleeps upstairs cannot hear a single chime in the front hall, and walking to the door to see who rang defeats the point.

Our usual plan is a video station at the front door with answering stations in the kitchen and on the bedroom floor, or phone answering for the family with one wall station as a backup. The strike goes in the front door frame so the door can be released from upstairs. Where the family uses a side door off the driveway more than the front door, we discuss which door gets the station, and whether the second door needs one at all.

Shop-and-apartment buildings on the avenues: the residential door beside the store

Along Kings Highway, Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue, the typical building has a store across most of the ground floor and a narrow residential door to the side leading straight to the stairs. The apartments above need a buzzer; the store below may need its own for a back or side door. The two systems must never be able to release each other's doors.

We keep them separate even when their cables share a cellar. The residential panel calls only apartments and its power supply is on the landlord's circuit; the store's station calls the counter or the manager's phone and opens only the store's door. Every line is labeled in the cellar, so the next technician does not connect the bakery's back door to apartment 2.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find Where the Lines Meet, Test Every Apartment, Then Talk About Equipment

These notes describe how our technicians approach a buzzer job in a Madison apartment house or two-family house. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Find where the lines meet. In a prewar apartment house, the technician's first stop is not the lobby panel but the place where all the apartment lines come together, usually a terminal board in the cellar, sometimes a cabinet behind the lobby panel or in a closet near the elevator. That board shows how the system was built, how many times it has been modified, and whether anyone labeled anything. It also shows where the power supply is and which circuit feeds it. A supply that is plugged into an outlet in the super's workshop is a common reason a whole building goes silent at once.

Test every apartment. Next, each line is identified and tested from that board: continuity end to end, no short between the conductors, no short to ground. Where we can, we also ring each apartment from the lobby and note whether the call arrives, whether both sides can hear and whether the release works. The result is a sheet with every apartment on it, which is the document an owner or board should have before choosing anything.

Look at the door. The technician then checks the lobby doors themselves: does the street door close and latch on its own every time, is the closer leaking or adjusted too weakly, is the strike aligned with the latch, and does the inner vestibule door matter? In a house, the same questions apply to the front door, plus which floors the household answers from.

Only then, equipment. With the test sheet and the door notes, the choice between repair, partial rebuild and replacement is usually obvious, and the written price names every line to be reused and every line to be replaced. Nothing is discovered on installation day.

The 1968 Line

Built Before 1968, Bigger Than Eight Apartments: What the Rules Mean for Madison's Prewar Apartment Houses

Because so many of Madison's apartment houses were built between the late 1920s and the early 1940s, the date in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a matters here more than in neighborhoods built later. What follows is how we read the rules for a buzzer job. None of it is legal advice; a tenant attorney, the building's counsel or the city can answer questions about a specific building.

The building size and the date both matter. §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-closing, self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk with a visitor and release the door from their apartments. A building of that size erected before 1968 must provide the same door and intercom once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, and the work then has to begin within 90 days and be finished within six months. Brick Underground's guide adds that in the older buildings this happens at the tenants' expense. For a 47-apartment building on Kings Highway built in 1936, that means a written request from tenants in at least 24 apartments is what turns an intercom from an owner's choice into an obligation.

An existing intercom is not optional. Many of Madison's prewar buildings have had intercoms for decades whether or not tenants ever asked in writing. Habitat's February 2024 article states that the city's Housing Maintenance Code "also requires a functioning intercom," and that courts have treated failing to maintain one as a breach of the warranty of habitability. Andrew Bart of Kagan Lubic Lepper Finkelstein & Gold told the magazine that in a co-op, "the broken intercom could be a defense in court" if the co-op sues a shareholder for nonpayment. In practice, a building that already has an intercom should plan on keeping it working.

The door is part of the system. Under Local Law 71 of 2022, HPD selects 300 class A multiple dwellings a year for proactive inspections of self-closing doors, chosen from complaints, violations and other city data, and its inspectors check "ALL self-closing doors in common areas, public spaces, hallways, corridors, and apartment doors, where accessible." Violations are issued when doors do not close properly, with 14 days to correct a general self-closing door violation and a reinspection within 20 days of that deadline. Violation records for Brooklyn buildings published on Lucid Rents show the wording HPD uses for the street door: "ARRANGE AND MAKE SELF-CLOSING THE DOORS AT BUILDING ENTRANCE AT PUBLIC HALL, 1st STORY." An intercom that releases a door that then drifts open behind each visitor fails on both counts, so the closer is part of every lobby job we price.

Tenants have a route, but it is slow. Brick Underground reports a renter who waited three months for an inspector after a 311 complaint about a broken intercom and a propped-open door, and its advice to tenants is to get a repair or installation estimate and send it to the landlord with the repair request. We can quote a building only for the person or board who controls it, because the work touches the street door, the cellar and every apartment, but a tenant is welcome to pass our number to the owner or managing agent.

Everyone must be able to leave. Any door with a release still has to open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic; Allegion's guide explains that with a strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether it is fail-safe or fail-secure. Magnetic locks hold the whole door, so the building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release on loss of power or fire alarm activation, and hardware listed to UL 294. On a Madison lobby door we use strikes almost without exception.

Reusing the Riser

Four Wires per Line and a Box of Spares: What a 288-Apartment Queens Co-op Teaches a 50-Apartment Madison Building

The best published example of a prewar building keeping its old intercom wire is not in Madison but in Forest Hills. In November 2019, Habitat magazine reported on Aero Owners, a co-op of three six-story brick towers that opened in the 1940s and hold 288 apartments: close cousins, in age and shape, of the apartment houses on Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway. The building had talked about intercoms for years. Thomas Krahn of Vision Enterprises, its property manager, told the magazine, "We've been talking intercoms already for five years." What it finally chose was a video system that ran on the existing copper. "We didn't want to replace all the wires," Krahn said.

The details explain why that worked. The old system used three wires to each apartment plus a ground. The new one needed four wires per line, two for video and two for sound, and each apartment's station recognized its own signal on the shared wires. The contractor, Matthew Arnold of Academy Mail Box, was not worried about the age of the cable: "If I have a problem with a wire, I have a lot of spares." That is the point. In a building wired with more conductors than the new system needs, a failed conductor can often be replaced by a spare in the same cable, without opening a wall. The building paid about $130,000 to buy and install the system, which works out to roughly $450 an apartment, in line with the figure of around $500 per apartment for video in large buildings that Brick Underground attributes to the New York Times.

For a Madison building of thirty-five to fifty-three apartments, the lesson is not the total but the method. First, count the conductors that actually reach each apartment and test them. Second, choose a system whose wiring needs match what is there: some systems run voice, video, power and release on a single pair, others need four conductors, and the riser decides which family of equipment is realistic. Third, plan for the lines that fail, with spares in the same cable where they exist and new cable in a raceway where they do not. A building that follows those steps gets a price that holds. A building that picks the panel first and tests afterward gets a price that changes.

The Aero Owners story also shows something about time. A building that has been "talking intercoms" for five years has usually been paying for repairs throughout, while the riser aged further and the apartments that worked became fewer. In Madison, the most expensive retrofits we see are often in buildings that waited until the system failed completely, when the riser could no longer be tested line by line because nothing on it worked. A building that tests while most of the system still functions keeps more of its options.

How Residents Answer

Wired Handsets, Telephone Entry or Phones: Choosing How a Madison Building Answers the Door

Habitat magazine's May 2017 report "The New Buzzer" sorted building entry systems into three types, and the same three are on the table in almost every Madison apartment house we see. Hard-wired systems let residents talk through an intercom in the apartment and release the door, and some include video. Telephone-based systems call residents over a dedicated phone line, so they can answer on a landline or a cell phone, but they have no video. Smartphone-based systems show residents video and let them answer on their phones, and usually need an internet connection at the door.

Hard-wired stations are the dependable floor. A wired station in each apartment talks to the lobby panel over the building's own cable. It keeps working when the internet is down, when a resident's phone is off, and when the building changes internet providers. For residents who have answered the door from the same spot in the kitchen for thirty years, it is also the least disruptive change. Its weakness is that it only works when someone is home and next to it, and that adding an apartment means a line to that apartment.

Telephone entry suits buildings without spare riser lines. A call box that dials residents' phones needs no cable to the apartments at all, only power and a phone or cellular connection at the door. That is why some older buildings switched to one when their risers failed. The weakness is the dependence on the number on file: if residents change numbers or drop landlines and nobody updates the directory, the door stops reaching them.

Smartphone systems answer from anywhere. Habitat quoted Dan Arnold of Academy Mailbox on the shift: "A lot of our calls now are people who want to abandon the hard-wired intercom units in their apartments." The same article reported a ButterflyMX installation of roughly $5,000 to $7,000 with a monthly charge of $2.50 per apartment paid by residents, and compared it with a virtual doorman service at "about $15,000 a year for a 28-unit building." Swiftlane's cost guide puts software subscriptions more generally at $5 to $15 per unit per month or $30 to $100 per door per month. On a fifty-apartment building, the subscription can outweigh the hardware within a few years, so it belongs in the comparison.

In practice, many Madison buildings end up with a combination: a wired station in every apartment so the door always works, and phone answering for the residents who want it. The board or owner decides on the mix, and the written price shows the hardware cost, any monthly fee and who would pay it.

Installation Day

Fifty Apartments, One Lobby Door: How We Change Over an Occupied Madison Building Without Leaving It Open

Replacing the intercom in a building of thirty-five to fifty-three apartments means visiting every apartment, and residents in a six-story Madison house are at work, at school, at prayer or at home at very different hours. The temptation for any contractor is to ask residents to leave doors unlocked, or to prop the street door open while the lobby panel is changed. We do neither, and the way a building's front door actually fails explains why.

In August 2025, News12 Brooklyn reported from 240 Crown Street in Crown Heights, where security video appeared to show a man using a small child in a stroller to get someone to open the lobby door for him; once inside, he took a package. A resident interviewed afterward said, "I do think it's kind that they let him in." Nothing about the intercom was at fault there. The door was opened by a person being polite. The lesson for an installation day is the same: an entry system protects a building only while its door is closed and its residents are not being asked to open it for strangers, and an installer who props the door or asks residents to buzz in "the intercom people" without checking teaches exactly the wrong habit.

  • The street door keeps locking. The new strike and power supply go in first, and where the door must be off its lock for a few minutes while the strike is fitted, a technician stays at the door.
  • The old system works until each apartment is moved. Where the wiring allows, apartments are cut over to the new system floor by floor, so no one is left without a way to answer the door overnight.
  • No unlocked apartment doors. A technician enters an apartment only when the resident is home, or when the owner, super or managing agent lets them in and stays. Otherwise that apartment moves to a second visit.
  • Names and windows in advance. The owner or managing agent receives the technicians' names and the dates, and each apartment gets a two-hour arrival window, so residents can plan to be home rather than leave a key with a neighbor.
  • A list at the end of each day. The owner gets a list of apartments finished, apartments missed and what remains, so residents know who is still expected and when.

Rules that apply to Madison business entry installations

  • Free exit at all times. Every business door with a buzzer, code or reader must still open from inside without a key or code; an electric strike keeps the inside lever working, so it is our default.
  • Sensor release for magnetic locks. Where a magnetic lock is the only workable choice, the building code calls for an egress-side sensor, a push-to-exit device near the door, release on power loss and fire alarm activation, and UL 294 listed hardware.
  • Fire doors take fail-secure hardware. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes so they stay latched in a fire; stair and corridor doors in Madison's apartment houses are often rated, and we check the label before choosing hardware.
  • Accessible reach. Buttons, keypads and readers on public doors stay within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range, with the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum and the low forward reach at 15 inches minimum.
  • Self-closing doors. A business door that opens onto a building's public hall is a common-area door, and HPD's self-closing door inspections apply to it like any other.
  • Landlord approval in writing. Work on a shared street or lobby door starts only once the landlord or managing agent has approved it.

If a business also wants a camera on its entrance, it can be installed on the same visit and priced with the buzzer; customers who combine the two typically save $200 to $400 in labor.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Madison: Questions Answered

Questions Madison owners, boards, tenants and businesses bring to us, together with ones raised in Habitat magazine, Brick Underground, the ButterflyMX repair guide and the city's own rules, answered for the apartment houses and homes here.

Apartment houses from 1928 to 1941

Our building went up in the 1930s, before the intercom rule, but it has had an intercom for as long as anyone remembers. Can the owner simply let it die?

Not safely. The 1968 line in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a decides when an older building must install an intercom, but once a building has one, Habitat's February 2024 article states that the city's Housing Maintenance Code "also requires a functioning intercom," and that courts have treated failing to maintain it as a breach of the warranty of habitability. In a building of eight or more apartments, tenants occupying a majority can also make the door and intercom mandatory by asking in writing.

For an owner, the practical question is cost, not whether. A building whose intercom still works for most apartments is usually the cheapest kind to fix, because its riser can be tested line by line and only the failures replaced. Waiting until it is dead everywhere removes that option.

In our building some apartments can hear the visitor but the visitor cannot hear them, and two floors get no call at all. Does that mean the whole system has to go?

Not by itself. One-way audio and dead floors are different faults. The ButterflyMX repair guide says "No audio or one-way audio may point to an apartment station, audio path, connection, or shared-equipment fault," so a handful of apartments with one-way audio can be bad handsets or bad connections at the terminal board. Two whole floors with no call usually means the riser has failed or been cut at a junction between those floors and the rest of the building.

We test every apartment line from the cellar and ring every apartment from the lobby, and the results usually separate the two. If the riser can be repaired at one junction and the bad handsets replaced, that is the repair. If lines are failing on every floor in different ways, that is when a new system, often on whatever lines still pass, becomes the better value.

Now and then the lobby buzzer goes off by itself and keeps sounding. What is usually behind that?

Usually the lobby, not the apartments. The ButterflyMX guide notes that continuous buzzing "may indicate a stuck button or another entry-panel issue." In an old panel, a button whose contacts have worn or corroded, or water that has got behind the faceplate, can hold a call open. A short on one apartment's line can cause the same symptom, which is why we test the lines as well as the panel.

If it happens after rain, the panel's seal and mounting are the first suspects. If it happens at random, a failing button or controller is more likely. Either way, the answer often turns out to be one part rather than a new system, and that is what we price first.

Each apartment handset in our building is wired with three conductors and a ground. Is that enough for a video system?

It can be. The Forest Hills co-op Habitat wrote about in 2019 had exactly that, three wires per apartment plus a ground, and its new video system needed four wires per line, two for video and two for sound. Some systems run voice, video, power and release on a single pair, which a three-conductor line can carry with a conductor to spare. Others need more.

The answer for your building comes from testing: how many conductors actually reach each apartment, whether each one is continuous and free of shorts, and how long the runs are. Once we know that, we choose the family of equipment the riser can support, and the written price names any apartment line that needs new cable.

Habitat reported that a Forest Hills co-op paid about $130,000 to put video on its old copper for 288 apartments. What does that tell a Madison building a sixth that size?

Mostly that reusing sound wire keeps a large retrofit affordable, not what your building will cost. That figure works out to roughly $450 per apartment, close to the "around $500 per apartment" for video in large buildings that Brick Underground attributes to the New York Times. A Madison building of forty-five or fifty apartments has the same kind of riser and the same choice.

On our Brooklyn list, a full entry system with a panel and an answering point for every apartment starts at about $2,500 and runs past $10,000 for larger buildings. Where a Madison building lands depends on the test results, whether residents want video, wall stations, phone answering or a mix, and what the lobby door needs.

Tenants, boards and the paperwork

Openigloo lists open HPD violations for our building but does not say what they are. How can we tell whether one concerns the intercom or the entrance door?

HPD publishes the violation records for every building it inspects, and each record carries a description, the class and the dates. Intercom and buzzer violations usually name the intercom, the bell or the return buzzer; entrance-door violations use wording like the one Lucid Rents reproduces for Brooklyn buildings: "ARRANGE AND MAKE SELF-CLOSING THE DOORS AT BUILDING ENTRANCE AT PUBLIC HALL, 1st STORY." Class A violations, the kind Openigloo shows for some Madison buildings, are the least serious category, but they still have to be corrected.

If you find one that concerns the intercom or the street door, send us its description, class and date with your request, because the correction deadline shapes the schedule, and a door-closing violation often means the closer has to be part of the job.

Our managing agent says the call box in the lobby dials each apartment's landline, and most of us have dropped our landlines. What are the options?

Three, and buildings often combine them. The call box's directory can be updated so it dials residents' cell phones instead, which costs least but depends on residents keeping their numbers current. The call box can be replaced with a panel that calls residents through an app over the internet, which adds video but may carry a monthly fee. Or wired stations can be added in the apartments, if the building still has a riser, so the door reaches every apartment regardless of phones.

Habitat's 2017 report describes telephone-based systems as needing a dedicated phone line and offering no video, which is often why buildings that have one start looking for alternatives. We test whatever riser remains before recommending a choice, because a building with good old lines has a cheaper path to wired stations than one without.

Habitat quoted a lawyer saying a broken intercom "could be a defense in court" for a co-op shareholder. Does that mean we can stop paying maintenance until it is fixed?

That is a legal question for a real estate attorney, not for an installer, and Habitat presented it as a possible defense if a co-op sues for nonpayment, not as advice to withhold. What we can say is that the dispute usually ends the same way, with the intercom repaired or replaced, and that the shareholders and the board both benefit from getting there quickly.

If your board is deciding what to do, we can test the system and give the board a written report and price it can circulate to shareholders, so the conversation moves from whether to fix it to how.

Houses on the side streets

Our two-family brick house has two front doors next to each other, one for each apartment. Do we need two separate buzzer systems?

Usually two simple ones, one per door. Each door gets its own one-button station, each apartment its own answering station, and each door its own strike, so a visitor at the upstairs door can only ring and be released into the upstairs apartment, and the same for the ground floor. The two systems can share a power supply in the basement if the owner prefers, on a circuit the owner controls.

If the house has a single shared hall behind one of the doors, or the owner wants to answer both doors from the ground floor, a single system with two door stations can do it. We ask who uses which door before deciding, because a system that lets one household release the other's door is rarely what anyone wants.

Our tenant on the second floor uses the same front door as we do, and the stairs start right inside, beside our own apartment door. Can their button release only the street door?

Yes. The release opens only the door with the strike, which is the street door. Your apartment door, off the hall, stays on its own lock and is not connected to the system at all. The tenant can talk to a visitor and let them into the hall, and the visitor goes straight up the stairs.

If you would rather your ground-floor apartment door also had a buzzer, so you can let in your own visitors to the hall and then to your apartment, it can have one, but it should be a separate station that only you can release. We would not wire your apartment door to the tenant's button.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Madison

Search for buzzer installation and you get confident summaries built from national cost guides and vendor blogs. Some of it holds; some of it does not fit a six-story Madison apartment house or a two-family brick house at all. Below are seven common claims, where they come from and what the field shows. We have not quoted any AI-generated answer word for word; the claims come from the published guides those answers draw on.

"An intercom installation averages about $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's cost guide gives that average, with a range of $1,500 to $15,000, wireless systems at $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired systems at $1,000 to $15,000.

A national average says little about Madison, where the two common jobs sit at opposite ends. Within each, the riser or the old bell pair moves the number more than anything else. Two buildings of the same size on the same avenue can be far apart because one riser tests clean and the other has failed on three floors.

"Old intercoms should be replaced, not repaired"

Summaries often treat an old system as finished because of its age.

The ButterflyMX repair guide says the opposite: "Do not replace a system based on age alone." It lists symptoms that point to specific parts, a stuck button, one apartment's station, the audio path or the wiring, and says the number of apartments affected matters more than the noise itself.

In Madison's prewar buildings, a system with a sound riser and a few bad parts is a repair. We test first and price both paths when both are reasonable.

"Wireless is the do-it-yourself option"

HomeAdvisor says wireless systems are the best choice for DIY, and for a single house that only wants a chime that can be true.

It does not hold for a lobby door. The door still needs a strike, power and a closer, and a building of six floors of brick and plaster is a poor place to rely on radio between the lobby and every apartment.

In a two-family house, a wireless kit can ring the upstairs household but rarely gives it a release, so someone still walks down to open the door.

"Allow one to two hours per station"

HomeAdvisor gives that per-station time for installation.

In a house with an open path for cable, that is close. In a fifty-apartment building, the time is driven less by mounting stations than by reaching every apartment, testing every line and cutting each one over without leaving residents without a way to answer.

The honest schedule comes from the riser test and the access plan, not from a per-station figure, and it goes on the written plan with the price.

"A cellular call box needs no building wiring"

ButterflyMX says its intercom "requires no building wiring," and cellular call boxes are widely sold as needing no data cable at the door.

The panel may need no cable to the apartments, but it still needs power, a strike in a sound frame and a closer that shuts the door, and a lobby deep inside a brick building can have weak cellular signal that has to be tested at the mounting point.

It also moves the building's dependence from its own wire to residents' phone numbers, which have to be kept current for the door to reach anyone.

"Older apartment buildings are exempt from intercom rules"

Summaries of §50-a sometimes stop at the 1968 date.

The same section requires buildings of eight or more apartments built before 1968 to install the door and intercom when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask in writing, and Habitat reports that the Housing Maintenance Code requires an existing intercom to function.

For Madison's 1928 to 1941 apartment houses, "exempt" is the wrong word. Many already have intercoms that must be kept working, and the rest are one written tenant request away from needing one.

"Video is required now"

Vendor material sometimes implies New York's rules call for video or app access.

§50-a describes an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk with a visitor and release the door. It does not mention video, phones or apps.

Video is still worth having in many Madison buildings, because a resident on the sixth floor cannot see the lobby. It is a choice to make on its merits and its cost, including any monthly fee, not a legal requirement.

FAQ

Madison Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

If a visitor rings and nobody answers, can the panel take a message?
+

Some can. Many video and app-based panels can record a short clip or show the resident a missed call with a picture of the visitor, which helps when a courier or a relative came by while no one was home. Wired audio handsets generally cannot. If messages matter to your building, tell us before we price it, because it narrows the equipment choice and may depend on an internet connection at the door.

Can a tenant's new station go in a different room from where the old handset hung?
+

Usually, within reason. The apartment line arrives at the old handset location, so moving the station a few feet along the same wall is simple, and moving it to another room means extending the line inside the apartment, which adds a little labor and is priced in advance. We do not patch or paint, so where a small opening is needed, the resident or the owner's painter finishes it afterward.

Do you need our super on site during the installation days?
+

It helps a great deal, especially on the first day. The super usually knows where the terminal board and the power supply are, which circuit feeds the old system, who holds the keys to the cellar and the roof, and which residents need a reminder about their arrival window. If the super cannot be there, the managing agent or owner names someone who can let us into the cellar and any apartment whose resident is away.

What happens if a tenant will not let your technician in to install their station?
+

We never force the issue. The apartment is marked as not completed on the daily list, and the owner or managing agent arranges another time with the tenant. Until then, the line to that apartment is left ready at the terminal board, so the station can be installed in a short return visit. Arranging access to apartments is the owner's responsibility under the lease, not the installer's.

Over the years some of our handsets were replaced with a different make. Can that mix stay on the new system?
+

Usually not on a new system, and often not even on the old one. A mix of makes on one riser is a common reason some apartments hear poorly or not at all, because handsets from different systems expect different signals. When the lobby panel is replaced, the apartment stations are normally replaced with matching ones so every apartment behaves the same. We count the different handsets during testing so the price reflects how many need changing.

Can the lobby directory list the dental office on our ground floor by name, alongside the apartment numbers?
+

Yes. Button panels can carry a printed label with the practice's name, and directory and code panels can list it as an entry that calls the front desk. The office's button can ring the desk during office hours and be switched off or sent to voicemail afterward. If the practice has its own street door, it is usually better to give that door its own station and keep the lobby panel residential.

Nobody labeled the old intercom wires decades ago. How do you work out which wire goes to which apartment?
+

With a tone generator and a probe. A technician puts a signal on a line at the apartment end and finds it at the terminal board, or the other way round, and labels both ends. In a fifty-apartment building that takes time and access to apartments, which is one reason a riser test is priced as part of the job. At the end, every line is labeled and recorded, so the next technician does not have to start over.

Is a wired intercom harder to break into remotely than an app-based system?
+

A wired system that is not connected to the internet cannot be reached from outside the building at all, which removes that risk entirely. App-based systems are connected by design, so their security depends on the manufacturer's software, the building's network and who holds the administrator login. Both can be secure; with an app system, we set strong credentials, keep the panel on its own network where possible and hand the login to the owner or board in writing.

Our vestibule is too narrow for a panel beside the inner door. Can it go on the outside wall facing the street?
+

Yes, if the outside wall has a flat surface near the door and the panel is rated for outdoor use. Many Madison lobbies put the panel at the outer door so visitors ring from the sidewalk, with the release on the outer door, the inner door, or both. We check the masonry, the cable route into the vestibule and the sun and rain exposure before choosing the spot, and the panel stays within accessible reach height.

Can my apartment station also tell me when someone rings at my own apartment door in the hallway?
+

On many systems, yes. A small button outside the apartment door can be wired to the apartment station so it rings with a different tone from the lobby, which tells the resident whether the visitor is downstairs or already at the door. Video stations can sometimes show a small camera at the apartment door as well. It adds a short run of cable in the hallway, and we include it in the price only when the building or resident asks for it.
