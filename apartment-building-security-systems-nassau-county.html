The County

Why Nassau County Rental Buildings Need an Owner's Security Plan

Nassau County is mostly single-family houses, and that is exactly why its apartment buildings get overlooked. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts table puts the county's owner-occupied housing rate at 81.9 percent for 2020–2024, so the security advice, the products on the shelf and even most installers are pointed at homeowners. The rental buildings sit in pockets: around the train stations, along the main streets of the older villages, in garden complexes built after the war, and in a growing number of new buildings put up near the Long Island Rail Road. An owner of one of those buildings is managing a small multifamily operation inside a county built for houses, and the doorbell-and-camera kit that suits the house next door does not suit thirty apartments with shared halls and a cellar.

The concentration in those pockets is striking. The Village of Great Neck Plaza says that within a third of a square mile it holds 90 multiple-family apartment buildings alongside 148 single-family homes, more than 260 stores and service businesses and a railroad station. In the Village of Hempstead, Census QuickFacts reports an owner-occupied rate of just 47.5 percent, so most households rent, and the village's 2026 Downtown Revitalization Initiative plan notes that between 2013 and 2023 most new housing units there were in properties with more than 20 units, an increase of 1,078 units. The same plan contrasts the village with the Town of Hempstead around it, where 82 percent of occupied units are owner-occupied. Two places a few miles apart can be completely different rental markets, and the security plan has to follow the building, not the county average.

The rules are different from the city, too, and a lot of advice online gets this wrong. New York City's Multiple Dwelling Law, the source of the city's intercom and self-locking-door duties, does not govern a Nassau building. The state's Multiple Residence Law says it applies “to all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population and to all towns and villages,” which takes in every town, village and city in Nassau. On top of that sits the Property Maintenance Code of New York State, part of the statewide Uniform Code, whose building-security section requires that doors, windows and hatchways for rented dwelling units “be provided with devices designed to provide security for the occupants and property within.” Then come local laws: village and town rental permits, inspections, and in some places the state's rent-stabilization program. We are not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice, but an owner should know which set of rules their building answers to before buying hardware.

Our audience on this page is the person or company that owns a Nassau rental building and has to decide what it gets and in which year. If you are a managing agent with several Nassau buildings, our page on property management security in Nassau County is written for you. If cameras are the only question, go to apartment building security camera installation in Nassau County. Co-op and condominium boards have their own pages for Nassau co-op security systems and Nassau condo security systems, and the Long Island apartment building security systems hub compares Nassau with Suffolk.

Nassau numbers and rules that shape an owner's plan

  • Which state law applies. Multiple Residence Law §3: the chapter applies to all cities under 325,000 people and to all towns and villages. Nassau's two cities, three towns and incorporated villages all fall under it rather than under New York City's Multiple Dwelling Law.
  • Building security in rented units. 2025 Property Maintenance Code of New York State §304.18: doors, windows and hatchways of rented units must have devices that secure the occupants and property.
  • Deadbolts. §304.18.1: where a rented unit's door has a deadbolt, it must open from the inside “without the need for keys, special knowledge or effort,” with a throw of at least one inch, and a sliding bolt does not count.
  • Low windows. §304.18.2: operable windows within six feet of the ground or a walking surface that give access to a rented unit need a sash locking device.
  • Hatchways. §304.18.3: basement hatchways that lead into a rented unit need devices that secure it from unauthorized entry.
  • Where the renters are. Great Neck Plaza: 90 multiple-family apartment buildings in a third of a square mile (village website). Village of Hempstead: 47.5 percent owner-occupied (Census QuickFacts), against 81.9 percent for Nassau County as a whole.
  • Rent stabilization. NYS Homes and Community Renewal lists the Nassau localities that adopted the Emergency Tenant Protection Act, among them the cities of Glen Cove and Long Beach, the Town of North Hempstead and villages including Freeport, Hempstead, Mineola, Lynbrook, Rockville Centre and Great Neck Plaza; HCR counted about 6,650 ETPA apartments in Nassau as of April 1, 2021.
  • Good Cause Eviction. The NYS Attorney General's guide says the law applies only to New York City and localities that opt in; its April 2025 list of opted-in cities and villages contains no Nassau locality.

Why one-piece-at-a-time security fails in Nassau buildings

Many Nassau rentals have been owned by the same family for decades, and the security grew the way the building did: a buzzer panel from the 1970s, a keypad someone added on the side door, a camera kit a nephew put up, a padlock on the hatchway, a deadbolt from the hardware store on every apartment door. Each piece made sense on the day it went in. Taken together they leave an owner who cannot say which doors actually lock, who still has keys, whose phone the cameras report to, or whether the apartment deadbolts would pass the maintenance code. Nobody needs to rip it all out. The useful step is to list every opening, decide what each one should do, and then replace pieces in an order the budget can carry.

Package theft shows how small the gap can be. In November 2023 CBS New York reported a run of thefts in Massapequa Park and Wantagh, where Nassau police said mobile devices were stolen six times in 11 days right after FedEx deliveries; one resident said it was “literally four minutes after they delivered.” Those were houses, not apartment buildings, but the lesson carries straight over: anything that sits in an unlocked vestibule or on an outside step is gone within minutes. In a walk-up, the vestibule is often the only place a courier can leave a box, which makes the inner door, not the street door, the one that matters.

The people an owner's system has to work for

The owner wants a single sheet that says what equipment the building has, which logins exist and who holds them, what the building pays each month for any platform, and which part is closest to failing. Because many Nassau owners live in another town and run the building without an agent, everything has to be readable from a phone.

Whoever minds the building, often a part-time handyman or a relative rather than a live-in super in this county, needs to open the closet and understand it: labels on the cable, a short written guide, and the ability to switch off a lost fob or reset a code without waiting for us.

Tenants judge everything by three things: whether the door lets them in and locks behind them, whether they can let a visitor in with or without a smartphone, and whether the lock on their own apartment door is one they can open in the dark without hunting for a key.

Inspectors, insurers and buyers see the building from outside. A village or town code officer looks at locks, doors and detectors during a rental-permit inspection; an insurer asks what is installed; a buyer's engineer wants to know whether the system is documented and in the owner's name.

What We Install

Apartment Building Security System Services in Nassau County, Opening by Opening

Every part below starts with the question a Nassau owner actually asks about one piece of the building, gives the answer we would give at the building, and then points to the Nassau County page on our site that covers that piece in depth. Taken together, they make up what most people mean by apartment building security systems: the hardware on the doors, the entry system, the credentials, the alarms, the cameras and the closet that holds it all.

Entrance doors, vestibules and the hardware that makes them lock

No entry system works on a door that does not close. In Nassau buildings the main entrance is usually in better shape than the back half: the rear stair door onto the parking lot, the side door the tenants use to reach the garbage enclosure, the cellar door at the bottom of an outside stair. Each of those needs a closer that pulls it shut against the wind, a latch or strike that catches every time, and a frame that has not sagged. When we fit an electric strike we set the closer and align the door at the same visit, and our Nassau buzzer repair pages publish $450–$950 for a strike replaced with the door aligned. If the lock body itself is worn out, that is locksmith work, and we will say so before anything electronic goes on the door.

Swing direction matters more than owners expect. The Multiple Residence Law's entrance-door section, §107, says that every door giving access to an entrance hall from outside “shall open outwardly,” and that the door must carry at least five square feet of glazed surface. Which sections reach a particular building depends on its class and when it went up, which is a question for the village or town building department, but the practical point stands: a magnetic lock, a strike and a closer are chosen for the way the door swings, so we never quote hardware for a door we have not looked at.

Vestibules deserve their own decision. A two-door vestibule with the panel at the outer door lets visitors call before entering and gives couriers a sheltered place to leave a box if the outer door is on a timer. The same vestibule with the panel at the inner door keeps the hardware out of the weather but turns the vestibule into an open waiting room. On the windier blocks near the water, and anywhere the street door already takes a beating, we usually put the panel inside and fit a strike on the outer door so it locks overnight.

The intercom: panel, riser and the apartment end

An entry panel is only half of an intercom. The other half is the path from the lobby to each apartment, and in older Nassau buildings that path is a riser of telephone-style pairs run when the building was new. Before we recommend replacing anything, we test the riser: if the pairs are sound, a new panel and new stations can reuse them; if half the pairs are open or shorted, the decision changes completely. Single faults are priced from the list our Nassau County door buzzer repair page publishes: $250–$525 for an apartment station, $250–$475 for a door release relay or button, $325–$650 for a 24-volt transformer, $650–$1,800 for lobby panel work and $850–$2,400 to repair or re-terminate riser pairs. Faults in a panel that is otherwise worth keeping are handled on the intercom repair page for Nassau County, and new systems, with the $1,200–$4,500+ range published for multi-unit buildings, are on our Nassau County intercom installation page.

The apartment end is where long-term tenants judge the job. Some want a handset or a video monitor on the kitchen wall that works whether or not their phone is charged; others want the call to ring their mobile and never think about the wall unit again. Many current panels can do both at once, so a building does not have to choose for every tenant. What we steer owners away from is a panel that only works through a phone app in a building where a large share of tenants are older or do not use smartphones; that is the fastest way to end up with the front door propped open.

Fobs and codes for the doors that are not the lobby

Keys are where most Nassau rentals lose control. A garden complex may have a key for every entrance, the laundry building, the boiler room and the trash enclosure, and after twenty years of turnover nobody knows how many copies exist. A credential system replaces that: each tenant carries one fob, card or phone credential, each door decides who it lets in and when, and a lost fob is cancelled in a minute instead of re-keying a door. Our access control installation page for Nassau County publishes $1,500–$3,500 per door for a networked card reader installed. We set the software up so the owner or the super adds and removes credentials themselves and calls us only for hardware.

One warning that has nothing to do with hardware: a credential system gives an owner the power to switch a tenant off, and New York law treats that seriously. The state Attorney General's guidance on unlawful evictions under RPAPL §768 explains that an owner who changes the locks on an occupied apartment can face separate charges for each unlawful act, and it lists removing, plugging or otherwise disabling the lock on a tenant's entrance door without supplying a key. We do not give legal advice, but we tell every owner the same thing: a fob is a key, and the rules for keys apply to it.

Packages, mail and the vestibule problem

Few Nassau rentals were built with a mailroom. The usual setup is a bank of mailboxes in a small vestibule and packages stacked on the floor beside them, which works until the first theft. The fixes, in order of cost: a strike and closer on the inner vestibule door so only tenants and the carrier can reach the packages, a reader on a small storage room or closet turned into a package room, then lockers or a dedicated package room with a camera over the door. We install the door hardware, readers and cameras; the lockers themselves come from their makers, and we wire their power and network when they need it.

Cellars, hatchways, boiler rooms and roofs

The back of a building is where alarms do more than cameras. The Property Maintenance Code singles out basement hatchways: where one gives access to a rented unit, it must have “devices that secure the units from unauthorized entry.” Beyond the code minimum, a contact on every hatchway, cellar door and roof door, a motion sensor in the boiler room and storage area, and a keypad the super can arm at night will report a forced door the moment it happens instead of on next week's camera review. Monitoring through our central station is $30 a month with a keypad and $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our alarm installation page for Nassau County. The Nassau County Police Department's alarm permit section issues the permit an alarm user needs, and we tell the owner which permit type applies before the system goes live.

Cameras (kept short on purpose)

On a whole-building plan, cameras go where the opening list says they are needed: the entrances, the package area, the rear door, the parking court and the cellar corridor. The details of camera counts, how long footage is kept and recorder versus cloud storage belong on our Nassau County apartment building camera page, and four-camera systems start at $999 on our Nassau County security camera pricing page. In common areas we keep cameras out of anywhere a tenant expects privacy and record video only, never audio.

Elevators

Most Nassau elevator buildings have one or two cars, and a reader in the cab is the simplest way to keep the roof, the cellar or an amenity floor for the people who belong there. Our elevator access control page for Nassau County publishes Level 1 readers from $1,725 per car. The connection inside the elevator controller is made by the licensed elevator company that services the car, and we schedule that visit together so the car is out of service once, not twice.

The equipment closet, the network and power

Everything above ends up in one place: a closet that is dry, locked, ventilated and reachable by the person who looks after the building. In the shore towns that first word is the important one, because a recorder or a power supply on a cellar floor is a recorder that will one day sit in salt water. We raise equipment off the floor or move it upstairs, label every cable at both ends and put the panel, controller and recorder on battery backup so the front door keeps working through a short outage. Cat6 runs, small racks and network switches for the systems we install are covered on our structured cabling page for Nassau County, which lists $150–$300 per network drop. New 120-volt outlets and circuits are the building electrician's work; we do not do electrical work.

Where our work meets the fire alarm

A building's fire alarm is designed, approved and tested under its own rules, and in Nassau that means the County Fire Marshal. The Fire Marshal's annual report describes an office that performs plan review, inspects fire protection systems, “licenses certain businesses and contractors installing fire protection systems, and issues permits for both residential and commercial fire alarm systems.” We do not install or service fire alarm systems in Nassau County, and nothing on this page is fire alarm work. Where our door hardware meets the fire alarm, we work with the fire alarm company of record, because the Building Code of New York State requires electrically locked egress doors to release when the alarm activates or the power fails (§1010.1.9.9 for sensor-released doors and §1010.1.9.10 for magnetic locks).

What we do not sell

Our work is equipment: we put it in, we repair it and we keep it running. We do not run a guard service, a remote lobby attendant or a staffed monitoring room that watches a building's cameras, and we do not sell subscription security packages. If a building already pays a virtual-doorman or remote-monitoring company, we install and service the panels, readers and cameras that company works with, and we can connect new equipment to it where its maker supports that.

Building Specialists

Apartment Building Security Systems for Nassau County Building Types

Nassau's rental stock came in layers: apartments over the stores on the old village main streets, elevator buildings near the stations in the 1920s through the 1960s, garden complexes after the war, converted houses on the side streets, and in the last decade new buildings beside the railroad. The hardware is mostly the same across all of them; where it goes, how the cable gets there and what fails first are not.

Walk-ups above stores

Two or three floors of apartments over the shops on a village main street, reached by a slim door between storefronts. The stores often share the cellar and the rear yard, which is the weak spot.

Station-area elevator buildings

Prewar and postwar elevator buildings within walking distance of a station, the pattern Great Neck Plaza is known for. One or two cars, a lobby panel on an old riser and a service door at the back.

Garden-apartment complexes

Two- and three-story buildings around lawns and a parking court, each with several short entrances and a shared laundry. Credentials have to cover many doors spread over a lot of ground.

New buildings by the railroad

Recent mid-rise rentals beside the LIRR with cloud entry panels, app credentials, garages and amenity rooms, and an integrator who may still hold the administrator logins.

Converted houses

Large older houses split into three or more apartments, with separate doorbells, an outside stair and often a basement entrance through a hatchway.

Shore buildings

Apartment buildings in Long Beach and the south-shore villages, where salt air eats outdoor hardware and cellars have flooded before. Equipment placement matters as much as equipment choice.

Walk-ups above village main-street stores

The residential door between two shopfronts is usually the oldest and least loved door on the street. It takes the most traffic, it often has a mail slot or a mailbox bank right behind it, and in many buildings the stair it opens onto also leads to the cellar the stores use. The first job is separation: the tenants' stair should lead only to the apartments, the cellar door should open only for the owner, the super and the store tenants who need it, and each store's back door should not open into the residential hall. A simple audio or video panel with one button per apartment and a strike on the street door covers the entry. A credential on the cellar door, with the store tenants issued their own fobs that work only during business hours, covers the rest without handing out keys.

Wiring in these buildings follows the stair. There is rarely a spare chase, so cable runs inside the stairwell in a painted raceway or up through a closet that stacks floor to floor. We look for the old buzzer riser first; in many walk-ups it is still usable for a new audio system even when the panel at the door is long dead.

Elevator buildings near the stations

Great Neck Plaza is the clearest example in the county: by the village's own count, 90 multiple-family apartment buildings packed around the Great Neck station, many of them prewar or postwar elevator buildings with a lobby, a single car and a back door to the parking or service area. These buildings usually have a working front entrance and a neglected rear one. The plan we write for them puts a reader on the rear and garage doors, a contact that reports when the rear door is held open, a reader in the elevator if the roof or the cellar should stay off-limits, and a lobby panel sized for the building's tenant count rather than the cheapest one with enough buttons.

Lobbies in these buildings were often finished in stone, terrazzo or wood paneling that the owner wants left alone. A new panel goes into the opening the old one left wherever it fits, readers go on the door frame rather than the stone, and cable comes up from the cellar inside the existing conduit or through the lobby closet. Patching a marble wall is slow and expensive, so we plan around it.

Garden-apartment complexes

A garden complex spreads one building's worth of security across several acres. There may be twenty or thirty short entrances, each opening onto a stair that serves four or six apartments, plus a laundry building, a boiler room, a maintenance shed and a trash enclosure. A separate intercom at every entrance is expensive and hard to maintain; a credential system with readers on the entrances that matter, and one entry panel at each building or cluster, is usually the better design. The cable is the hard part. Where the buildings share a basement or a utility tunnel, we use it; where they do not, the choice is between trenching new conduit, running cable on existing poles or using a point-to-point wireless link between buildings, and each has a cost we write down before anyone commits.

New buildings beside the railroad

Station-area building has picked up across the county. Long Island Business News reported in August 2023 that a transit-oriented development of 119 apartments in an eight-story building was advancing on a 0.64-acre site across from the Mineola station, and the Village of Hempstead's own plan counts more than a thousand new units in larger properties over the decade to 2023. New buildings like these usually open with a cloud entry panel, phone credentials, a garage gate and an amenity floor. The equipment is fine; the problem is control. On a takeover we get the administrator accounts, the panel and controller licenses and the recorder passwords moved into the owning entity's name, and we list every per-door or per-apartment monthly fee so the owner knows what the building is paying for.

Houses converted to three or more apartments

A large older house divided into apartments is still a multiple residence in the eyes of the law once it holds enough families, and the Property Maintenance Code's building-security section applies to every rented unit in it. The checklist is short and practical: a deadbolt on each apartment door that opens from the inside without a key, sash locks on the low windows, a hatchway that cannot be lifted from outside, and an entry system at the front door with a button for each apartment so tenants stop leaving the door unlocked for visitors. Converted houses also tend to have the oddest wiring in the county, with doorbell wire stapled along baseboards and run through window frames, so we usually run new cable rather than reuse it.

Shore buildings in Long Beach and the south-shore villages

Near the ocean and the bays, outdoor hardware is a consumable. Painted steel panels rust at the screws, cheap readers fog, and a strike that is not sealed will stick after a winter of salt spray. We specify stainless or marine-rated entry panels, readers rated for outdoor use and sealed boxes, and we mount the controller and recorder above the cellar floor. In a building that has flooded before, we ask where the water reached and keep everything above that line.

How the method changes from one building to the next

Materials set the method. Frame buildings take surface boxes and need a hood over an outdoor panel; brick and block take anchors and a core drill for the cable; stone lobbies are worked around, not through. Plaster walls in prewar buildings crack if they are fished carelessly, so cable goes in closets and along existing paths. Garden complexes need a plan for crossing between buildings. New buildings need almost no cable but a great deal of paperwork. The survey is where we work this out, which is why we price after seeing the building, not before.

For Owners & Investors

Owning a Nassau Rental Building: Buying, Running and Upgrading Security

Before you close on a Nassau apartment building

A buyer's engineer looks at the roof, the boiler and the structure; almost nobody looks at the security systems, and they are where surprises hide. Before closing, ask the seller for the make and model of the entry panel, the controller and the recorder, who holds the administrator passwords, whether any platform charges a monthly fee per door or per apartment, how many fobs are issued and to whom, and whether the building has an alarm permit. Then walk the building and try every door: does it close on its own, does it latch, does the panel call every apartment? A building whose seller cannot answer these questions is a building whose new owner will be paying for a takeover visit in the first month.

Taking control after closing

The hardware conveys with the building; control often does not. On a takeover we list every device, test it, change every password, move each cloud account into the owning entity's name, cancel the fobs the seller and the old super held, and hand the owner a one-page summary. If a platform account cannot be transferred, we tell the owner what replacing that one component would cost rather than leaving a stranger with the keys to the front door.

Rent-stabilized buildings under the ETPA

In the Nassau localities that adopted the Emergency Tenant Protection Act, New York State Homes and Community Renewal administers rents, and its guidance tells owners of regulated apartments that they are required to keep required services and equipment in good working order. A change to the entry system in a stabilized building should therefore never leave tenants without a working door or intercom. We install the new panel alongside the old one, switch the door release over in a short window, and keep the in-apartment stations for any tenant who relies on one. Whether a particular improvement can be passed on in the rent is a question for HCR and the owner's counsel, not for us.

Rental permits and village inspections

Many Nassau municipalities license rentals. The Village of Freeport's code makes it unlawful to let premises for rent “without first having obtained a permit,” says the permit is valid for two years, and makes owners responsible for repairing or replacing deteriorated windows and doors and for smoke and carbon monoxide detectors. The Town of North Hempstead's rental permit law requires an on-site inspection by a code enforcement officer before a permit is issued or renewed. Those inspections are not security audits, but a door that does not latch or a window lock that is missing is the kind of thing that gets written up, so we suggest owners schedule hardware repairs ahead of the inspection date rather than after it.

When the super or the handyman leaves

The person who looks after a building often holds more of its security than the owner realizes: the master keys, the fob software on a laptop, the recorder password, the alarm code. On the day they leave, change every administrator password, cancel their credentials and phone access, collect their keys, and give the replacement a new set of logins of their own rather than the old ones. We do the password, credential and keypad changes on a service visit; re-keying mechanical locks is a locksmith's job.

Renovations and turnovers

Every renovation is a chance to put cable where it will be needed. If a lobby is being redone, the panel location, the reader on the inner door and the conduit to the closet go in while the walls are open. If apartments are being turned over one at a time, a station or a cable drop can go into each one as it is renovated, so the building is ready for a new entry system long before the owner buys it. A few hours of planning at the start of a renovation saves days of fishing cable through finished walls later.

Compliance callouts for Nassau County owners

  • State law: Multiple Residence Law, which §3 applies to all towns and villages and to cities under 325,000 people. New York City's Multiple Dwelling Law and HPD rules do not apply in Nassau.
  • Apartment doors, windows and hatchways: the state Property Maintenance Code, §304.18 and its three subsections, covering thumb-turn deadbolts with a one-inch throw, sash locks on windows within six feet of grade, and secured hatchways into rented units.
  • Entrance doors: Multiple Residence Law §107 (doors from outside into an entrance hall open outwardly and carry at least five square feet of glazing).
  • Electrically locked egress doors: Building Code of New York State §§1010.1.9.9 and 1010.1.9.10 (release on loss of power and on fire alarm activation; manual release for magnetic locks).
  • Lockouts: RPAPL §768, as explained in the NYS Attorney General's guidance: disabling a tenant's entrance lock without supplying a key is an unlawful eviction.
  • Local permits: village and town rental permits and inspections, such as Freeport's two-year rental permit and North Hempstead's inspection requirement.
  • Alarms: Nassau County Police Department alarm permit; fire alarm work is under the Nassau County Fire Marshal and is outside our scope in Nassau.
Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Security Systems in Nassau County: Questions Answered

Conversions, enforcement and the code on apartment doors

We are turning the empty offices above our stores into apartments. What security should go in during the conversion?

Plan it while the floor is open, because this is the cheapest moment the building will ever have. Decide where the residential entrance will be and keep it separate from the stores; put a panel location and a back box at that door; run cable from the closet to every new apartment for a station or a data jack even if the first system calls tenants' phones; add a reader and a door contact on any door that connects the new apartments to the stores or the cellar; and leave a pull string in every run. Once each apartment's walls are closed, all of that costs several times as much. The conversion itself goes through the village or town building department, and the low-voltage work follows the architect's layout.

Our building is in an incorporated village. Who enforces the building-security rules, the village or the town?

Generally the municipality the building sits in. The state Uniform Code, which includes the Property Maintenance Code, is in effect everywhere in New York outside the city, and it is enforced locally, so a building inside an incorporated village deals with the village building department, and one in an unincorporated hamlet deals with the town. Rental permits follow the same line: Freeport's permit comes from the village superintendent of buildings, North Hempstead's from the town's code enforcement officers for the parts of the town it covers. When you are unsure, call the building department listed for your address before scheduling work, and keep the permit file with the security documents.

The apartment doors have double-cylinder deadbolts that need a key on both sides. Can we keep them?

Not on rented units, as we read the code. Section 304.18.1 of the Property Maintenance Code of New York State says that where a rented unit's entrance door has a deadbolt, it must be openable from the inside “without the need for keys, special knowledge or effort,” with a throw of at least one inch, and that a sliding bolt does not count. A key-inside deadbolt fails the first test and is a real hazard in a fire. Replacing them with thumb-turn deadbolts is locksmith work and usually inexpensive per door; we flag them on the survey so they are fixed before anything electronic goes in.

Tenants in the garden-level apartments say their windows feel exposed. What helps short of bars?

Start with what the Property Maintenance Code already expects: a sash lock on any window within six feet of the ground that opens into a rented unit. Then add what lets the tenant know when something is wrong: contacts on the windows, or a glass-break sensor in the room, connected to the building alarm or to a small panel for that apartment. Better lighting outside those windows and a camera covering the walkway along them deter more than they record. Bars and grilles are a different trade and, on a bedroom window, can block the way out in a fire, so that is a question for the building department before anyone installs them.

Tenants, credentials and the apartment door

A tenant is months behind on rent. Can we deactivate their fob?

Ask your attorney before you do anything like it. The New York Attorney General's guidance on RPAPL §768 describes unlawful eviction as including removing or disabling the lock on an occupant's entrance door without supplying a key, and explains that an owner can face a separate charge for each act. A fob that opens the building door or the apartment door is that tenant's key. The system will let you cancel a credential in seconds, which is exactly why the decision has to follow the legal process rather than the software. We will not cancel a sitting tenant's access at an owner's request; we will cancel a fob the tenant reports lost and issue a new one.

How do we stop tenants from handing out the building code to delivery drivers and friends?

Stop using one code for everyone. On a current panel each apartment gets its own code or its own phone credential, so a code that is shared shows up in the log under one apartment and can be changed for that apartment alone. For deliveries, many panels let a tenant send a time-limited code or open the door for a courier from their phone, which removes most of the reason to share. A plain building-wide code changed every few months is better than nothing in a small building, but it leaks within weeks.

Tenants in our Hempstead village building call us at night every time the buzzer acts up. How do we cut down those calls?

Most late-night calls come from three things, and each has a fix. Visitors who cannot reach a tenant: let the panel ring the tenant's phone as well as the apartment station, so a call is not lost when nobody is home. Lost fobs and forgotten codes: give the super or a family member the right to cancel and issue credentials, with a few spare fobs in a locked drawer, so the owner is not the only one who can help. Equipment that quietly fails: turn on health alerts for the panel, controller and recorder so a dead unit shows up on the owner's phone in the morning, not as a tenant's complaint at midnight. What is left after that is usually a real repair, which starts with the $250 service call.

Doors, hatchways and the back of the building

Our front door swings in. Does it have to be changed to swing out before we add new hardware?

Not necessarily, and do not change it on our say-so. The Multiple Residence Law's entrance-door section says such doors shall open outwardly, but whether that section reaches your building depends on its class and age, and reversing a door is a building-department question. What we need to know is simply which way it swings now, because a magnetic lock, an electric strike and the closer are all chosen for the swing. If the building department or a renovation calls for a reversed door, we time the hardware to go on after the carpenter, not before.

Should the stair doors on each floor be on the access system, or only the lobby and cellar?

Usually only the ground-level doors. The door from each floor into the stairwell is part of the way out of the building in a fire, so it has to open freely from the floor side, and putting a reader on it rarely adds much. What does help is a contact on the stair's exit door at the bottom, so the owner hears when it is left open to the yard or the street, and a reader on any stair door that leads into the cellar or onto the roof. In buildings where tenants use the stair to reach an amenity floor, the elevator reader and a door contact cover it without locking anyone in the stairwell.

For the lobby door of a three-story walk-up, is a magnetic lock or an electric strike better?

Usually an electric strike. A strike works with the door's existing latch and lock, keeps the door locked mechanically if the power fails, and lets the key still work. A magnetic lock holds a door with power alone, so the Building Code of New York State requires it to release on power loss and on fire alarm activation, with a manual release the person leaving can operate; that suits some glass storefront-style doors but adds equipment a small walk-up does not need. We choose after seeing the door, the frame and how the door is used.

Running the building day to day

Couriers tell our tenants they cannot get into the building to deliver. How do we let them in without a shared code?

Give the carriers their own way in and keep it separate from the tenants'. Many current entry panels support a delivery code or a carrier credential that works only on the street door and the vestibule, only during the day, and shows up in the log as a delivery rather than as an apartment. Pair that with a strike on the inner vestibule door, so a courier who gets through the street door reaches the mailboxes and the package shelf but not the halls. In a small walk-up where the panel cannot do that, a tenant answering on their phone and releasing the door is the simpler fix.

A store on our ground floor wants to put its own camera on the front of the building. Should we allow it?

That is the owner's decision and the lease's, but if the answer is yes, set conditions. The camera goes on the store's own frontage, aims at the store's door and the sidewalk in front of it, and does not look into the residential entrance, the vestibule or any apartment window. It records to the store's recorder, not the building's, and the store pays for it and removes it when the lease ends. Cable runs through the store, not through the residential hall. Put those conditions in writing before the bracket goes up, because moving a camera later is always harder than placing it right.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Security Systems in Nassau County

Owners increasingly arrive with an answer from a chatbot, a search summary or a national cost guide on sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Those answers are built mostly from New York City material and national averages, and on the points below they steer a Nassau owner wrong.

1. “A cloud entry panel is a one-time purchase”

Cost answers usually quote the hardware and the installation and stop there. Platform vendors' own cost guides say otherwise: one intercom maker's guide lists software at roughly $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month, on top of the equipment.

For a forty-apartment Nassau building those fees run for as long as the building keeps the platform, they usually rise over time, and they often stay attached to whoever opened the account, which matters when a building is sold or a super leaves.

Cloud panels can be the right choice. The honest comparison is five years of hardware plus fees against a panel with no subscription, written as numbers on the same page, with the account in the owner's name from day one.

2. “Double-cylinder deadbolts make a rental more secure”

Security advice written for houses often recommends a keyed deadbolt on both sides of a door near glass. For rented units in New York outside the city, the Property Maintenance Code says a deadbolt on a unit's entrance door must open from the inside without keys, special knowledge or effort.

A tenant who has to find a key to get out of their apartment in a fire is in danger, and an owner who installed that lock has a code problem on top of a safety problem.

Security on an apartment door comes from a solid door, a one-inch-throw deadbolt with a thumb turn inside, a strike plate fixed into the frame and a peephole or viewer, not from locking people in.

3. “With smart access control, a landlord can lock out a tenant who stops paying”

Some product marketing presents remote deactivation as a collections tool. The New York Attorney General's guidance on RPAPL §768 treats disabling the lock on an occupant's entrance door without providing a key as an unlawful eviction, with a separate charge for each act.

The technology makes the mistake easier, not legal. A credential that opens a tenant's building or apartment door is that tenant's key until a court-ordered process ends the tenancy.

What access control legitimately gives an owner is control over the doors that are not anyone's home: the cellar, the roof, the laundry, the garage, and the fobs of people who no longer live or work in the building.

4. “A passed rental-permit inspection means the building's security is fine”

Village and town rental inspections in Nassau check that a unit is safe and habitable. North Hempstead's law, for example, conditions every permit on an inspection confirming a functioning smoke detector and carbon monoxide alarm, and Freeport's code makes owners responsible for repairing deteriorated windows and doors.

An inspection visits on one day, looks at the apartment being permitted and does not test whether the rear door latches at night, whether the hatchway alarm reports anywhere, or who still holds a key from five tenants ago.

A permit is a floor, not a plan. The owner's own walk-through of every opening, done once a year, catches what an inspection is not designed to look for.

5. “Building cameras can record audio as well as video”

Many cameras ship with a microphone, and product pages advertise two-way audio as a feature. In a hallway, a lobby or a laundry room of an apartment building, recording conversations raises a different legal question from recording pictures, and New York's eavesdropping law in Penal Law article 250 is the starting point for it.

We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice, but the practical rule we follow is simple: in common areas of a rental building, we set cameras to record video only and switch the microphones off.

Two-way talk at the entry panel is different: it is the intercom doing its job, a conversation the visitor starts by pressing the button, and it is not recorded by the systems we install unless the owner asks and has taken advice first.

6. “Any security company can add the fire alarm while it is there”

In Nassau County, fire alarm work runs through the County Fire Marshal, whose office reviews plans, inspects fire protection systems, licenses certain contractors that install them and issues fire alarm permits.

A low-voltage contractor that installs intercoms and cameras is not automatically a fire alarm contractor in Nassau, and we are not one: we do not install or service fire alarms in this county.

What we do is coordinate with the building's fire alarm company so that electrically locked doors release when the alarm activates, as the state Building Code requires, and we keep the two systems separate.

7. “A magnetic lock is the modern upgrade for every front door”

Magnetic locks look clean and hold a door firmly, so they show up in a lot of generic advice. The Building Code of New York State only allows them on egress doors under conditions: the lock must release on loss of power and when the fire alarm activates, and the door needs a manual release the person leaving can operate.

That means more equipment on the door, a coordination job with whoever services the fire alarm, and a door that unlocks whenever the power fails, which in a storm-prone county is not nothing.

For most Nassau walk-ups and elevator lobbies an electric strike working with the existing latch is simpler, keeps the door locked mechanically in an outage and lets the key still work. Magnetic locks earn their place on certain glass and aluminum doors, and we use them there.

Can our existing burglar alarm panel stay when readers are added to the doors?
+

Should the lobby directory show tenants' names or only apartment numbers?
+

Apartment numbers alone are the more private choice and need no updating when a tenant moves, while names help visitors who do not know the apartment. Many owners use numbers on the panel and let tenants give visitors the number. Current panels can show either, so this is a policy choice for the owner, not a hardware one.

Can a tenant who works nights turn off the ring on their apartment station?
+

On many current stations, yes: a do-not-disturb setting silences the call for that apartment for a set time, and the visitor hears that nobody is answering. Older audio handsets usually have no such setting. We show tenants how to use it at handover and tell the owner which models offer it before the panel is chosen.

How do you protect an outdoor entry panel from road salt and snow?
+

We use stainless or outdoor-rated panels, mount them where plowed snow and sidewalk salt spray cannot reach the bottom edge, add a hood where the door faces the weather and seal the cable entry. Owners can help by keeping salt off the step right under the panel, where most corrosion starts.

Will you tidy and label the wiring in our equipment closet even if nothing is being replaced?
+

Yes. A closet cleanup is its own job: we trace and label each cable at both ends, dress loose runs onto the wall, remove dead equipment the owner agrees to remove and leave a written map of what goes where. It is priced after we see the closet.

Can a contractor's code be set to stop working when the job is finished?
+

Yes, on most access-control and entry systems. A credential or code can be limited to certain doors, certain hours and an end date, so a painter's or plumber's access ends on its own. The log shows each time it was used while it was active.

Do you sell extra fobs after the system is installed?
+

Yes. Owners order more fobs or cards from us matched to the building's readers, and we can program them before they are delivered or leave them blank for the owner or super to enroll. Keeping a few spares on hand means a lost fob never leaves a tenant waiting.

Will a new security system lower our building insurance premium?
+

We cannot promise that, because the insurer decides. What we can do is give the owner a written list of what was installed, with models and locations, for the insurer to review. Some carriers ask about entry control, alarms and monitoring; the answer depends on the policy.

Can a camera cover the mailboxes without showing what is in tenants' mail?
+

Yes. We aim the camera at the vestibule and the people in it, from a height and angle that shows faces and hands but not the contents of open boxes, and we can mask part of the image in the recorder if needed. The aim is to see who took a package, not to read anyone's mail.

If we run cable for an elevator reader now, can the reader be added later?
+

Yes. Running cable to the elevator machine room or controller during another project and adding the cab reader later is a sensible way to split the cost. The connection inside the elevator controller still needs the elevator company's mechanic when the reader goes in.
