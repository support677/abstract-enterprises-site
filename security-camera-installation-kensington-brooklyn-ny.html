The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Kensington packs three very different kinds of property into a small area, and each one gets broken into, or simply stolen from, in its own way. The neighborhood sits south of Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, between Windsor Terrace to the north, Borough Park to the west, Prospect Park South and Ditmas Park to the east and Midwood to the south. It was first developed in 1885, after Ocean Parkway was finished, and the parkway still runs through the middle of it. Along the parkway and along Coney Island Avenue, pre-war brick apartment buildings dominate the frontage, and many of them are co-ops. Six-story elevator houses went up on upper Ocean Parkway from the late 1920s until 1941, and taller post-war buildings such as Caton Towers at 135 Ocean Parkway and Americana Towers at 455 Ocean Parkway followed into the mid-1960s. Off the parkway, the side streets hold brick row houses, detached Victorians, American Foursquares and the two-family houses with stacked front porches that Forgotten NY called almost the signature style of the neighborhood. And along Church Avenue, McDonald Avenue, Coney Island Avenue and Ditmas Avenue run the shopping streets.

Those shopping streets carry a lot of Kensington's identity. The corner of Church and McDonald Avenues was co-named Little Bangladesh in October 2022, and the New York Times described it in 2023 as the place where Bangladeshi Brooklyn gathers: Radhuni Sweets and Restaurant, a news site run out of a row house a few steps away, dance classes at the Bangladesh Institute of Performing Arts, men meeting on the corner in the evenings and crowds breaking the fast there every night of Ramadan. On Coney Island Avenue, from about Church Avenue south to Avenue H, Brownstoner describes the Little Pakistan stretch of clothing stores, jewelry shops, groceries, restaurants, alteration shops and offices that help with immigration papers and taxes. Most of these are family businesses in older buildings, with apartments upstairs, a back door onto a yard or alley, and a flat roof shared with the stores on either side. That combination is exactly what the recent break-in reports describe.

In the fall of 2025, police said one man broke into four businesses in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Flatbush and Kensington by coming in through the roof or the ceiling, taking more than $48,000 in cash, gold, jewelry and merchandise. One of those businesses was at 302 Church Avenue, where about $7,500 was taken around September 26. Earlier that year, BoroPark24 reported that a single building on Church Avenue had been broken into three times overnight: on February 4 someone smashed a side window with a rock, on May 15 a burglar forced a side door and took $360 and credit cards, and on May 24 someone climbed a fence, damaged a wall and took $300. Police released video from the third break-in. None of those three entries came through the front door. A camera plan that only watches the storefront would have missed every one of them.

The apartment buildings have their own pattern. In August 2024 a Kensington resident told News 12 that a man had been coming in through her building's back door for months and taking packages from the mailboxes, and that she had caught it on camera herself. The landlord, she said, had told her the back door could not be kept closed because of garbage removal. On the houses, News 12 reported in April 2024 that a man broke into three homes and a commercial building in Midwood and Kensington in under three hours on March 23, all between 5 p.m. and 8 p.m., part of a spree that took close to $200,000 across five locations. Video has also helped police in street cases: in December 2025, investigators reviewed footage after a man slammed a 10-year-old's head against a car windshield at an Ocean Parkway corner, and in March 2026 a home camera on Argyle Road recorded a teenager throwing a tree branch through a first-floor window while a family ate dinner, cutting a 6-year-old with glass. These are the situations we plan Kensington cameras around: the ceiling, the side door, the back door, the early evening, the corner and the window at street level.

One older case shows why the plan does not stop at mounting cameras. In July 2014, Bklyner's 66th Precinct report noted that a pharmacy and optical store at 119 Church Avenue, between McDonald Avenue and East 2nd Street, was entered while it was closed and lost about $55,150 in cash, drugs and eyeglasses. The store's cameras were not working. Every system we install is tested, labeled and handed over with a check you can repeat, so the owner knows the recorder is still recording before the night it is needed.

Ways In

Planning Kensington Cameras Around the Ways In That Local Reports Keep Showing: From Above, From the Side, From the Back and at Street Level

Most camera quotes start with the front door because that is where people picture a burglar. The Kensington reports we read for this page point somewhere else. Below is how each of those entry points changes where a camera goes and what it has to see.

From above: the roof, the bulkhead and the store ceiling

Many Church Avenue and McDonald Avenue stores are one or two stories with a flat roof that runs straight into the roofs next door, and many have a suspended tile ceiling with a gap of several feet between the tiles and the roof deck. Someone who gets onto the row of roofs can cut through the roofing and drop through the tiles without touching a door, a gate or a window, and that is how the 2025 burglar reached 302 Church Avenue. A camera mounted on the storefront sees nothing of that. Inside, we look for the part of the store most worth reaching from above, usually the register, the safe, the jewelry case or the stock room, and mount a camera that views that area from below with the ceiling in the frame, so the recording shows the tiles moving and the person coming down. In a store with a bulkhead or roof hatch, a second camera watches the hatch from the stair below, out of reach from the roof side. On a building with apartments above, the roof door to the top-floor hall gets the same treatment.

From the side: windows, side doors and fences

The Church Avenue building that was hit three times in 2025 shows how a burglar keeps trying different routes. A side window was smashed in February, a side door was forced in May, and then someone came over a fence and through a wall. When a building has a side alley or a yard reached over a fence, we treat that whole side as one zone and plan for it to be covered end to end: one camera looking along the side from the front corner, so anyone entering the alley is seen arriving, and one at the back looking toward the fence and the side door, so the face shows as the person reaches the door. Cameras on a side wall have to be mounted where a ladder or a climbing burglar cannot simply turn them away, and their cable runs inside the wall or in conduit, not loose on the brick.

From the back: the door that stays open for garbage

In apartment buildings, the back door is often the weak point for an ordinary reason. Garbage and recycling go out that way, and in the building News 12 covered in 2024 the landlord said the back door could not be kept closed because of garbage removal. If the door has to be open at certain hours, the camera's job is to make every person who uses it identifiable. We put a face camera just inside the door, mounted lower than a ceiling corner and aimed back at the doorway, plus a second camera that shows the mailboxes or package shelf. The recording then shows who came in, what they did at the mailboxes and which way they went. If the owner can fix the door, that is better; cameras record a theft, they do not prevent a door from being propped.

The early evening and the hours nobody is home

The March 2024 burglaries in Kensington and Midwood all happened between 5 p.m. and 8 p.m., when many people are still at work or on the F train home and it is already dark for much of the year. For houses, that means the cameras need good pictures at dusk, which is the hardest light of the day: bright sky, dark porch and headlights going by. We set exposure for the porch rather than the street, add a small light where a face would otherwise be a silhouette, and set phone alerts so they are most active in the hours the household says the house is empty.

At street level: corners, sidewalks and first-floor windows

Some of the most useful local footage has come from cameras that saw the street, not a door. In the December 2025 Ocean Parkway case, the man who attacked a 10-year-old fled west on Ditmas Avenue toward Cortelyou Road, and police said they were reviewing video. In the March 2026 Argyle Road case, a home camera recorded the teenager who threw a branch through a first-floor window. For first-floor apartments and houses, a camera that covers the front windows from the side, at a shallow angle along the facade, records anyone who reaches them without recording into the rooms across the street.

Brick elevator houses on upper Ocean Parkway and its side streets from the late 1920s to 1941, many now co-ops, with a lobby, a back or service door, a cellar and a roof bulkhead.

Post-War Towers on the Parkway

Taller buildings of the 1950s and 1960s, such as Caton Towers at 135 Ocean Parkway and Americana Towers at 455 Ocean Parkway, often with garages, side entrances and long service corridors.

🏬

Walk-Ups With Stores Below

Three- and four-story brick buildings on Church, McDonald and Coney Island Avenues with shops at street level, apartments above, a separate residential door and a shared roof.

🏡

Duplex-Porch Two-Families

Two-family houses with one porch stacked over the other, one for each household, on the lanes and numbered side streets, where each family usually wants its own door covered.

🏚

Foursquares and Victorians

Boxy American Foursquares on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, and detached Victorians, often with deep porches, side driveways and wood or sided walls.

🧱

Row Houses on the Lanes

Attached brick houses on short streets such as Kermit Place and the boxy two-story houses of Friel Place, where the front is narrow and the back opens onto a run of yards.

Pre-war elevator buildings: cellars, risers and the service door

In a six-story Ocean Parkway building, the lobby camera is the easy part. The harder work is the back or service door, which in many buildings leads from the cellar to the garbage area, and the roof bulkhead six floors up. Cable for those runs usually travels through the cellar and up an existing riser or the stair, and in an occupied co-op the route has to be agreed with the super and the board before we drill anything. We keep the recorder in a locked room or cabinet away from the door it watches, so someone who comes in the back cannot simply pull the drive on the way out.

Post-war towers: garages, long corridors and many accounts

The towers that went up on the parkway between 1960 and 1965 brought garages, longer service corridors and many more apartments sharing each entrance. Cameras here are fewer per apartment but longer per run, and some runs exceed what a single network cable can carry, which is where a switch partway along or a fiber link comes in. Concrete ceilings in garages take anchors, not screws into wood, and conduit is usually the right answer for any cable that a car door, a ladder or a mop could reach.

Walk-ups with stores below: two businesses, one roof

On the avenues, a three-story building can hold a grocery on the ground floor and two apartments above, all sharing one roof with the buildings next door. The store owner, the building owner and the tenants each have different reasons to want cameras, and different rights to see them. When the owner of the building also runs the store, one recorder with separate accounts often works well. When the store is a tenant, we usually recommend two systems: the store's cameras on the store's own recorder and its own internet, and the building's entrance and roof cameras on the owner's.

Two-families, Foursquares and Victorians: wood, siding and porches

Frame houses take cameras differently from brick. A camera fixed straight onto vinyl or aluminum siding will crack it or let water in, so we mount on a block that sits on the sheathing behind the siding, or under the porch ceiling, or on the trim. Porch ceilings are a favorite spot in Kensington because they put the camera close to the door, out of the rain and at a height where a face shows under a hat brim. The attic or the basement is usually the cable route, and on a two-family the owner decides early whether the system is one shared recorder or two separate ones.

Row houses on the lanes: narrow fronts and shared yards

The lanes that Forgotten NY walked in 2012, Kermit Place with its row of attached homes, Friel Place with two-story brick houses holding one apartment per floor, Turner Place and its duplex porches, are short and quiet, and the houses are close together. The front camera has very little wall to work with and very little distance to the sidewalk, so we use a wide lens close to the door. At the back, where the yards meet, the camera is aimed at the back door and the nearest fence rather than across the whole run of yards, so neighbors are not recorded in their own gardens.

Church Avenue shops: the ceiling, the counter and the side door

Church Avenue is where the 2025 reports land, so it is where a camera plan has to be most complete. For a typical store we plan five positions. A face camera inside the front door, set lower than the ceiling so a cap brim does not hide the face. A counter or register camera that shows hands, cash and the customer side of the counter. A ceiling view over the most valuable part of the store, so an entry from above is recorded, as described in the section above. A camera on the back or side door, inside, looking at the door. And an outside camera on the storefront that shows the sidewalk in both directions, for whoever walks up and which way they leave. A small store may start with three of these and add the rest later, and the cable for all five can go in on the first day so the later cameras are a simple add.

Many Church Avenue restaurants and sweet shops stay busy into the evening, and during Ramadan the area around Church and McDonald fills with people breaking the fast. Busy hours are good for business and hard on cameras: the face camera has to deal with a crowd at the counter, and alerts that work on a quiet Tuesday become constant. We set recording to run continuously and keep alerts for after closing, so the busy hours are recorded in full and the phone only buzzes when the store should be empty.

McDonald Avenue: subway stairs, the elevated line and deliveries

The Church Avenue station on the F and G lines opened in 1933 at Church and McDonald Avenues, and its staircases come up at all four corners of that intersection, with an elevator ramp on the west side of Church Avenue and two more staircases at Albemarle Road. Further south, the F runs on an elevated structure over McDonald Avenue, with stations at Ditmas Avenue and 18th Avenue. For a store on McDonald Avenue, that means the storefront camera often has a subway staircase or a steel column in its view, and the sidewalk can be in shadow under the tracks while the street beyond is bright. We pick mounting spots that see around the staircase, and we choose cameras that handle strong contrast so faces under the structure do not turn black. Stores that receive deliveries at the curb get a camera that shows the van, the hand truck and the door at the same time.

Coney Island Avenue: jewelry, clothing and long, narrow stores

On the Little Pakistan stretch, many stores sell clothing, jewelry or both, and many are long, narrow spaces with racks down the middle and a counter near the back. Racks block cameras, so the overview camera usually goes high at the front looking back, and the counter camera goes behind the counter looking at the customer. A jewelry case needs a camera close enough to show the piece and the hands when the case is opened. Alteration and changing areas stay out of every camera's view.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Kensington

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance; ask any installer for both before work starts. Fitting rooms and restrooms. No camera views changing areas, fitting rooms or restrooms, and we will not install one that does. Sound. Cameras we install record video; we leave audio off unless the owner has taken advice on recording conversations in a store. Landmarks. Ocean Parkway itself is a city scenic landmark, and some buildings in Brooklyn sit inside historic districts; where a building is landmarked, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook lets staff approve cameras that are small, well spaced, finished to match and wired with concealed or minimal visible conduit, with no more than two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on a small building and three on a large one. We check a building's status before quoting any facade work. City property. Street trees, light poles, sidewalks and the elevated subway structure are not places for a private camera; everything we mount goes on the owner's building or fence. Sharing with police. In May 2024 the city announced an opt-in, free pilot that lets participating businesses link their cameras to the NYPD in real time with access times they choose; it began in Queens with funding to expand to nine more precincts. Whether or not a store joins, we set up exports so the owner can hand over a clip quickly when asked.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Kensington Building, Starting With Every Way In

This describes how we approach buildings here, not one particular job. Before we talk about camera models, we ask the owner to walk us around the outside of the building and show us every way a person could get in: the front, the side alley, the back door, the yard fence, the cellar door, the roof. On a Church Avenue store we ask to see the roof, or at least to lift a ceiling tile and look up, because the 2025 break-ins showed what a shared row of flat roofs allows. In an Ocean Parkway building we ask the super which door stays open and why, and we stand where the mailboxes are and look for where a face would show to someone walking in from the back. On a two-family we ask who lives on which floor, which door each household uses and who should be able to see which camera.

Then we come back at the hour that matters. For a house, that is often dusk, when the porch is darkest against the sky. For a store, it is after closing, with the gate down and only the security light on. Only then do we write the quote, one line per camera, each with its job written next to it: back door face, mailbox view, ceiling over register, corner looking west.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Kensington: Questions Answered

Our Church Avenue shop has a tile ceiling under a flat roof, like the store at 302 Church Avenue that a burglar entered from above in 2025. Can a camera inside actually record someone coming through the ceiling?

Yes, if it is planned for that job. A camera mounted in a ceiling corner looking down at the floor will often show only feet landing. We mount the camera lower on a wall, or on a short drop from the ceiling, aimed across the room so that both the ceiling tiles above the register or stock room and the floor below are in the frame. When someone cuts through the roof and pushes a tile aside, the recording shows the tile moving, the legs and then the person, and it keeps showing them as they reach the register. We also check what the roof side looks like. If your store has a roof hatch or a bulkhead door, a second camera on the hatch from below is worth more than another camera on the sidewalk. If the store's ceiling space is open to the stores next door, which happens in older rows, we tell you, because a camera records the entry but cannot close that gap. Fixing a shared ceiling space is a job for the landlord and a contractor, and it is worth raising with them alongside the cameras.

One building on Church Avenue was broken into three times in 2025, through a side window, a side door and then over a fence. If we cannot afford to cover every route at once, how do we decide what comes first?

Start with the route that leads to the thing a burglar wants, and with the spot where a face will show. In that building, all three entries were on the side or the back, so the first camera would go inside, facing the side door, because every route ends up passing a door or a window on that side and a person reaching a door is close enough to identify. The second camera would look along the side from the front corner, recording anyone entering the alley or yard. The third would cover the fence line from the back. We run the cable for all three on the first visit even if only one camera goes up, so adding the others later is an hour's work, not another day of drilling. Finally, we would ask the owner to walk us around the outside once a month: three entries by three routes in four months means someone is testing the building, and the cameras are the record, not the lock.

In 2014 a pharmacy at 119 Church Avenue was emptied while its cameras were not working. Our store came with a camera system when we took over the lease, and nobody knows whether it records. Who owns it, and what does a checkup involve?

Ownership first: your lease decides whether the equipment belongs to the landlord, to you or to the previous tenant, and it is worth asking the landlord in writing before anyone changes passwords or removes a recorder. Once that is settled, a checkup on our setup-only rate covers the basics. We find the recorder, reset access so the person who owns it has the main password, check that every camera is actually recording and how many days the drive holds, sync the clock so times on the footage are right, trace any cable that goes nowhere, and practice an export with you. Often a system like this is half working: two cameras record and two show a picture that never reaches the drive. We write down what we found and what it would take to fix, and you decide whether to keep it, repair it or replace it. We also show you a quick weekly check, so you are not the next store whose cameras turn out to have stopped months ago.

Our building's back door is left open for garbage removal, like the Kensington building in the 2024 News 12 story where a man kept coming in and taking packages from the mailboxes. If the door cannot be kept shut, where should cameras go?

Two cameras do most of the work. The first goes just inside the back door, mounted lower than the ceiling and aimed back toward the doorway, so anyone walking in from the yard is facing it. The second shows the mailboxes or the package shelf from the side, so the recording shows what the person did there. If the path from the back door to the mailboxes turns a corner, a third camera covers the turn. We also check the light at the door, because a bright yard behind a dark hallway turns a face into a silhouette, and a small light inside the door fixes that. Then we recommend the owner keep the door closed outside garbage hours, because the cameras will record every theft but cannot stop a propped door from inviting the next one. The building's managing agent or super should decide who can see this footage and how tenants ask for a clip, before the first theft happens rather than after.

After the December 2025 attack on a child at an Ocean Parkway corner, police said they were reviewing video. Our building is on a corner of the parkway. Should our cameras cover the corner itself and not just our entrance?

If the board is willing, yes, and the cost is usually one or two extra cameras. In that case the man fled west on Ditmas Avenue toward Cortelyou Road, which is exactly the kind of detail a corner camera records: which way someone went after leaving. We set one camera on each street face of the building, aimed along the sidewalk and the near edge of the street, and set the image so a walking person is large enough to describe. These cameras are not meant to read every license plate on Ocean Parkway's main road, which is wide and fast, or to look into the windows across the street. The entrance cameras stay as they are, close to the door for faces. Keep in mind that footage from a corner camera is most useful when the building can find and export it quickly, so we make sure the super or a board member knows how.

Our duplex-porch two-family has the upstairs family's porch built right above our front door. Can one camera under the upper porch cover the downstairs door, or does each porch need its own?

Each porch normally needs its own camera, and the stacked design actually makes that easy. The underside of the upper porch is the ceiling of the lower one, which is a good place for the downstairs door camera: close to the door, out of the rain, at a height where a face shows. The upstairs porch needs a separate camera under its own roof, because a camera downstairs cannot see who climbs the stairs and stands at the upper door. If the house has one owner and a tenant, decide early whether the two cameras go on one recorder with separate accounts or two separate systems, and whether the tenant can see the downstairs camera at all. We also look at the stairs between the porches, since that is the path anyone takes to the upper door, and often one of the two cameras can be aimed to include it.

Our Foursquare off Beverley Road has a wide front porch with thick square columns. Where can a camera see the steps without a column in the way?

Untapped Cities notes that the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue still have many of these boxy houses, and their porches are both a help and a challenge. The columns block side views, so we rarely put a camera at the corner of the porch. Instead, the front-door camera goes on the porch ceiling or high on the wall beside the door, looking straight out at the top of the steps, where the gap between the columns is widest. A second camera at the corner of the house, under the eave, can look along the side driveway that many of these houses have. If packages are left on the porch, we make sure the drop spot is inside the door camera's view. The porch ceiling is usually wood with an attic or a floor joist space behind it, which makes the cable route simple.

Our house in Parkville sits on one of the slanted blocks, so the sidewalk does not run square to the front of the house. How do you aim a camera along a street that runs at an angle?

Parkville, between 18th Avenue and Foster Avenue, has a slightly diagonal grid, laid out after the Freeman's Association bought the land in 1852. On these blocks the house front and the sidewalk meet at an angle, so a camera mounted flat on the wall looks across the street on one side and into the neighbor's yard on the other. We mount the camera on an angled bracket or at the corner of the house, turned to follow the sidewalk, so people walking toward your door are seen coming and going. Where the angle puts a neighbor's front windows in the frame, we use the camera's privacy masking to black out that part of the picture before it is recorded. The front-door camera itself is aimed at the door and steps, which are square to the house and do not have this problem.

Who Sees the Footage

Co-op Boards, Landlords, Tenants and Neighbors: Who Decides and Who Watches a Kensington Camera

Cameras in Kensington almost always involve more than one person's interests. A co-op building has shareholders and a board; a rental has a landlord and tenants; a store has a landlord and maybe apartments upstairs; a two-family has two households; and every outside camera has neighbors. Getting this right before the cameras go up saves arguments later.

Co-ops on Ocean Parkway and Coney Island Avenue

In a co-op, the lobby, hallways, back door, garage and roof are common areas, and cameras there are a board decision. A shareholder who wants a camera at their own apartment door usually needs board permission too, because the door frame and hallway are not theirs alone. We put together what a board typically needs to decide: a list of camera locations with a sentence on each one's purpose, sample pictures from those spots, where the recorder will sit, who will have viewing access, how long footage is kept, how a shareholder asks for a clip, and a written quote.

Rentals: landlords, supers and tenants

In a rental building, the landlord owns the common-area cameras and decides who sees them. Brick Underground, asking two New York attorneys about a tenant whose landlord would only review lobby footage for the police, reported that a landlord is not obliged to share footage with tenants outside a legal process, but must hand it over when police request it or a court orders it, and that New York City buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. Tenants who want footage reviewed after repeated thefts have the most leverage acting together, for example through a tenants' association. When we install for a landlord, we recommend writing down a simple process for tenant requests before the first one arrives.

Neighbors and the backyard

Kensington houses are close together, and a back camera can easily take in three yards. CBS New York reported in 2017 that a state law sponsored by Assemblyman Edward Braunstein lets a homeowner or renter sue over a neighbor's camera aimed at their backyard if it annoys, alarms, harasses or threatens them, with the remedy being a court order to remove it. We aim back cameras at your own door, windows and fence, and use privacy masking to black out neighbors' yards and windows where they would otherwise fall in the frame. If a neighbor asks about your camera, showing them what it actually records usually ends the conversation.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Kensington

The claims below are the ones that come up again and again in AI-written answers, cost guides and installer directories when people ask about cameras for a Brooklyn home or store. Where a claim comes from a named source, we say so. Each is followed by what we see on Kensington buildings.

"Camera installation runs from about $100 for one camera to $1,000 to $2,200 for a whole system."

That range is from Thumbtack's Brooklyn camera installer page, and Thumbtack itself says the final price depends on the number and type of cameras and other factors. It is a reasonable picture of a simple job on a house with easy access. It says nothing about the jobs that make up much of Kensington's work.

A camera on the ceiling of a Church Avenue store that has to be reached through tiles under a shared roof, a back-door camera in a six-story Ocean Parkway building whose cable must travel through a cellar and up a riser, or a garage camera under a 1960s tower that needs conduit on concrete, each takes far more labor than a doorbell on a frame house. The number of accounts, the days of storage and whether a board has to approve the plan all change the price as well.

That is why we do not publish a per-camera price. We quote each building in writing after photos or a walk-through, with every camera and every cable run on its own line, so you can see exactly what you are paying for and compare it fairly with any other quote.

"Put your first camera on the front door."

For many houses, the front door is the right first camera. For Kensington stores and apartment buildings, the local record points elsewhere. The 2025 serial burglar came through roofs and ceilings. The Church Avenue building hit three times in 2025 was entered by a side window, a side door and over a fence. The package thief in the 2024 News 12 story came in through a back door.

A plan that spends the whole budget on the storefront or the lobby can leave every one of those routes unrecorded. The first question we ask is not where the front door is, but which ways in the building actually has and which of them leads to what a thief would want.

The front door still gets covered, of course. But on many Kensington jobs it is the second or third camera, after the back door, the side door or the ceiling over the register.

"Wireless cameras are the simplest choice for apartments and older buildings."

For a single camera inside one apartment, a Wi-Fi camera can be fine. For the common areas of a pre-war brick building, Wi-Fi struggles. The back door is often at the far end of a cellar, two or three masonry walls from the nearest router, and the roof bulkhead is six floors up. Battery cameras also need recharging, which nobody remembers to do in a building's back hallway.

Wired cameras on network cable get their power and their connection from the same cable, and they keep recording when the Wi-Fi is down. In a building, the extra work of running cable once is repaid every day the cameras do not drop out.

Where cable truly cannot be run, for example to a detached garage across a yard on a frame house, we say so and suggest the best wireless option for that one spot, rather than pretending a whole building can run on Wi-Fi.

"Kensington is a quiet residential area, so a doorbell camera is enough."

Kensington's side streets can be quiet, but the neighborhood is not only side streets. It has busy shopping corridors on Church, McDonald, Coney Island and Ditmas Avenues, a subway station at Church and McDonald with staircases on all four corners, and apartment buildings along Ocean Parkway holding many households behind each entrance.

The local reports cover all of it: store break-ins on Church Avenue, package thefts through an apartment building's back door, early-evening house burglaries in March 2024 and street incidents at an Ocean Parkway corner and on Argyle Road. A doorbell covers one door of one house.

For a house on a quiet block, a doorbell plus a back-door camera may well be enough, and we will tell you so. For a store or a building, it almost never is.

"Night vision means darkness is not a problem."

Most cameras switch to infrared at night and can see in the dark. The harder problem is dusk and mixed light. The early-evening hours when the March 2024 burglaries took place, 5 p.m. to 8 p.m., are when a porch is darkest against a still-bright sky, and a camera exposed for the sky turns a face on the porch into a black shape.

The same thing happens under the elevated F structure on McDonald Avenue, where the sidewalk is in shadow and the street beyond is bright, and at a back door that opens onto a lit yard. Infrared also strips color, so a red jacket and a blue jacket can look the same.

We test cameras at dusk and at night before handover, choose cameras that handle strong contrast where the view needs it, and add a small light where a face would otherwise be a silhouette.

"Visible cameras stop burglars by themselves."

Cameras can make a building a less attractive target, but they do not lock anything. The 2014 burglary at the 119 Church Avenue pharmacy and optical store happened in a store that had cameras, which were not working. The 2025 Church Avenue building that was broken into three times had video of the third break-in, and police released it, but that came after three entries.

What cameras do reliably is record, and only if they are working, the clock is right and someone can export the clip. That is why our handover includes a recording check, a clock check and an export practice, and why we show owners a short weekly check.

When the problem is a propped back door, a shared ceiling space or a broken lock, we say so plainly. Fixing those is often cheaper than another camera, and the cameras then record a building that is harder to get into.

AI answers drawn from that mix can attach another city's reputation to Brooklyn's Kensington.

FAQ

Kensington Security Camera FAQ

Does Ocean Parkway's landmark status mean a camera on our building needs Landmarks approval?
+

Not automatically. Ocean Parkway is designated as a scenic landmark, which concerns the parkway itself. Whether your building needs a Landmarks permit depends on whether the building is individually landmarked or inside a historic district, and we check that before quoting any work on a facade. Where a permit is needed, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook lets staff approve cameras that are small, well spaced, finished to match the wall and wired with concealed or minimal visible conduit.

Can a camera be fixed to the street tree, the light pole or the elevated subway structure outside our building?
+

No. Street trees, light poles, sidewalks and the elevated F structure over McDonald Avenue belong to the city or the transit system, and we do not mount private cameras on them. Everything we install goes on your own building, porch, fence or post on your own property. If the best view of your door seems to need the pole across the sidewalk, we find a spot on your building that gets close to it, often a second camera at the other end of the storefront.

Our block is near the line between the 66th and 70th Precinct areas. Which precinct gets our video if something happens?
+

Give it to the detective or officer handling the case, whichever precinct that is, and ask for the complaint number so the clip is attached to the right report. The 66th Precinct's Sector D covers Kensington up to Coney Island Avenue, while News 12 asked tipsters in the 2026 Argyle Road window case, east of Coney Island Avenue, to call the 70th Precinct. Either way, we set up your system so you can export a clip to a USB stick or your phone without handing over the recorder.

We park on the street and the spot changes every night. Can a camera on our house still help with the car?
+

Partly. A camera on the front of a house can cover the curb in front of it and a little either way, so if you park there it records anyone at the car. It cannot follow the car down the block. We usually aim one camera along the curb at a shallow angle and set it so a person beside a car is large enough to describe. Reading plates on passing cars is a different job that needs a dedicated, zoomed camera, and on most side streets it is not worth the cost.

Will you label every camera cable and leave a map of where each one runs?
+

Yes. Every cable is labeled at both ends with the name of the camera it serves, and we leave a written map showing each camera, its job and the route its cable takes through the cellar, ceiling, riser or attic. In older Kensington buildings, where cable often passes through spaces nobody opens for years, that map is what lets the next electrician, super or installer find a camera's cable without opening walls.

We live above our store on Church Avenue. Can the store's recorder sit upstairs in the apartment instead of in the shop?
+

Often yes, and it can be a good idea, because a burglar who gets into the store cannot reach a recorder upstairs. The cable runs up through the building from the store ceiling, and we check the route during the walk-through. The apartment needs a spot with power, some ventilation and the internet connection nearby. If the building belongs to someone else, the landlord has to agree to the cable passing between the floors.

Can we watch our Church Avenue store and our house a few blocks away in the same phone app?
+

Yes. Each place gets its own recorder, on its own internet connection, so a problem at one never affects the other, and both are added to the same app on your phone. You switch between the store and the house with a tap. Other people can be given access to one location only, so store staff see the store cameras and never the house.

Our co-op's pre-war lobby has stone or tiled walls. Can lobby cameras go up without drilling the stone?
+

Usually. We look first for plaster above the stone or tile, a ceiling, a door frame or a soffit, and mount there with the cable hidden in the ceiling.
