The Neighborhood

Why Bensonhurst Properties Need TV Installation

Ask ten people where Bensonhurst ends and you will get ten maps. Wikipedia, which notes that New York has no official neighborhood boundaries, draws it roughly between 14th Avenue and 60th Street on the north side, Avenue P, 22nd Avenue and Bay Parkway on the south and east, and 86th Street on the side facing Bath Beach, with Dyker Heights, Borough Park, Mapleton, Bath Beach and Gravesend all around it. The post office splits it too: ZIP 11204 north of Bay Ridge Parkway and 11214 south of it. When the Department of City Planning rezoned the area in 2005, its study area ran about 120 blocks, from Bay Parkway and 61st Street down to Avenue U, between Stillwell Avenue and McDonald Avenue. We work every one of those blocks at the same Brooklyn rate, so whether your lease says Bensonhurst, Mapleton or Bath Beach does not move the price. What moves the price, and the plan, is the building, and Bensonhurst's buildings are unusually consistent about one thing: there is a good chance the wall behind the TV is brick.

That comes from when and how the neighborhood was built. City Planning's summary says Bensonhurst was settled mainly in the 1920s, after the BMT took over the old Sea Beach and West End railways, and that it was largely built up by the Second World War. The builders were answering demand from families leaving the crowded Lower East Side, and the houses they put up were often multi-family. Bklyner's architecture writer describes the classic type as a semi-detached brick house, probably built for four families, separated from its neighbor by a side driveway that runs back to a car port, with eight- to nine-foot ceilings and "thick walls of layered brick." Those walls are why so many of the houses are still standing a century later. They are also why a stud finder is close to useless on the outside walls of a Bensonhurst living room, why the wrong anchor can spin in a mortar joint, and why the brick add-on on our list is the one we quote most often here.

Not every wall is a hundred years old. Small builders kept working through the 1980s, City Planning notes, mostly putting up groups of three- and four-story rowhouses with a garage at street level, and those have their own mix of masonry and framed partitions. Then the 2000s brought a building wave that the neighborhood still argues about. Wikipedia records that, after earlier zoning allowed it, many single-family homes were replaced by three-story brick apartment buildings and condominiums, and that 1,200 new housing units were approved between 2002 and 2005 alone; the 2005 rezoning was the city's response, limiting large stretches to detached and semi-detached houses. Newer still are small elevator condos such as Tower 85, an 11-unit building finished in 2022 at 2066 85th Street, and The Bealin, a six-story, 10-unit condominium completed in 2024 at 7616 Bay Parkway. Inside those buildings, the TV wall is far more likely to be drywall on light-gauge steel or wood framing, sometimes with a concrete or masonry wall at the back, and there is usually a board or a managing agent who wants to know what is going into it.

The households in those buildings shape the job as much as the walls do. Census Reporter's 2024 American Community Survey tables for Community District 11, which takes in Bensonhurst and Bath Beach, count 183,378 residents in 64,911 homes, about three people per household, with 58.5 percent born outside the United States and 74.1 percent speaking a language other than English at home. Wikipedia describes a large, mostly Cantonese-speaking Chinese community alongside Fuzhou and Mandarin speakers, a long-established Italian-American community whose shops still line 18th Avenue, and Russian, Ukrainian, Uzbek, Georgian, Albanian, Arab, Pakistani, Mexican and Guatemalan neighbors. In a district that averages three people per home, one TV often has to do more than one thing: show the game, run the kids' streaming apps, and play programming from overseas through an internet box that someone's relative set up. When we book, we ask who watches the TV and on what, because a setup that makes sense to one person in the house can be a puzzle to another.

Then there are the streets. Bensonhurst's commercial life runs along five corridors that the city's small-business department studied in 2022 and 2023: Bath Avenue, Bay Parkway, 18th Avenue, 20th Avenue and 86th Street, with 1,265 storefronts between them. 86th Street sits under the elevated West End line, where the D stops at 18th Avenue, 20th Avenue, Bay Parkway and 25th Avenue, and businesses along the curve near Stillwell Avenue have complained for years about the shaking; a tire shop owner there told the Brooklyn Reporter in 2014, "Every time the train comes by, my entire building and everything inside of it shakes." For ten days every August, 18th Avenue between 68th Street and Bay Ridge Parkway closes for the Feast of Santa Rosalia; in 2026 it ran from August 20 to August 30, with the street closed from 4 p.m. on weekdays and from noon on weekends. A technician carrying a 65-inch box needs somewhere to stop the van and a clear path to your door, so the calendar and the block decide when we come almost as much as the wall decides how.

Six Bensonhurst facts that shape a TV job before we pick a bracket

  • Brick is the default exterior wall. The 1920s semi-detached houses were built with thick layered brick, per Bklyner. A TV on an outside wall usually means a masonry anchor and the +$65 brick add-on on our list, and the side wall facing a shared driveway is often brick too.
  • Most side-street homes hold one or two households. City Planning describes the midblocks as two- and three-story one- and two-family houses.   • The newer buildings are a different job. The three-story brick apartment buildings and condos of the 2000s, and boutique condos like The Bealin (2024) and Tower 85 (2022), usually have framed interior walls and a board, sponsor or managing agent with rules about contractors and insurance.
  • Ceilings are lower than in a brownstone. Bklyner puts the typical 1920s ceiling at eight to nine feet. That keeps most TVs at a comfortable height, but it also means a screen above a mantel can end up higher than people expect, which is why we tape the outline before drilling.
  • Households are big and multilingual. About three people per home and 74.1 percent speaking a language other than English at home, per the 2024 Census tables for the district. One TV often serves three generations and several kinds of programming.
  • The streets keep their own calendar. The feast closes 18th Avenue for ten days in late August, the D runs overhead on 86th Street, and Bay Parkway carried about 17,600 vehicles a day between 86th and 67th Streets in the city's 2019 count. Same-Day Repair in Bensonhurst

Need Repair in Bensonhurst? A Bracket Loose in Brick, a Screen Sagging Off Plaster, or a Wall Left Full of Holes

The urgent TV calls from older Brooklyn houses tend to look alike, and Bensonhurst's brick makes one kind especially likely. A bracket on a masonry wall that was fixed with plastic plugs meant for drywall, or screwed into the soft mortar between bricks instead of the brick itself, can feel solid for months and then start to rock. Each time someone pulls a full-motion arm out to watch from the kitchen, the plug grinds a little more of the joint into powder. The second kind is a screen sagging on one side on a plaster wall, where the fixings held the plaster skin but nothing behind it. The third is the wall left behind by a previous tenant or owner: an empty bracket, a ring of oversized holes, a power cord pushed into a hole in the plaster and out again near the floor.

If a TV is tipping or one corner has dropped, do not try to push it back level. If two people can lift it safely, take the weight off, unplug it, and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, that part is priced as a new mount on the wall it is going onto, with the take-down added, so a 55-inch TV rehung on brick comes to $179.99 plus $65 plus $85. When the old holes sit exactly where the new anchors need to go, the technician will move the bracket a little sideways or up rather than reuse damaged brick, and will show you why before drilling. If we find the problem is the wall itself, a cracked face brick, a bulge in the plaster or a joint that crumbles under a finger, we say so plainly, because that is a job for the owner or a mason and not something a bigger anchor should hide.

A cord that has been run inside the wall without an in-wall kit is something we fix rather than reuse. Flexible power cords are not allowed concealed inside walls under the National Electrical Code, so on a repair visit we pull the cord back out and either fit a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit ($169) or leave the cord on the surface in a paintable cover ($59), whichever you choose.

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Bensonhurst TV call. They differ in three ways that matter more than any brand: what the wall is made of, how far and how high the TV has to be carried, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

The semi-detached brick house

The 1920s type with a side driveway, layered brick outside walls and framed or plaster partitions inside, often split between two or more households.

The detached house toward Dyker Heights

Freestanding one- and two-family homes on the western blocks, with four exterior walls to choose from and more chances of a fireplace.

The garage-front rowhouse

Three- and four-story groups built into the 1980s with a garage at street level and the living floors above, where the carry and the stairs set the pace.

The six-story apartment house

The older elevator buildings on wider streets such as Quentin Road, Avenue P, Kings Highway and 65th Street, with plaster partitions, masonry around the shafts and a super with opinions.

The post-2000 brick apartment building

The boutique elevator condo

The semi-detached brick house: thick outside walls, a shared driveway and an owner close by

This is the house most people picture when they say Bensonhurst, and it is the one we plan around first. The outside walls are brick, often more than one layer thick, and in the front room the long wall facing the driveway or the street is usually one of them. That is good news for holding a TV: a well-set masonry anchor in sound brick holds far more than any bracket needs. The work is in setting it properly. The technician finds the face of a whole brick rather than a joint, drills with a hammer drill and the right masonry bit, clears the dust from the hole, and sets an anchor sized for brick. If the plaster on the inside of that wall was laid straight onto the brick, there is no gap to hide cables in, so the cable plan for an outside wall is usually a paintable cord cover or a short drop to a console, not an in-wall kit.

Inside, the walls between rooms are often framed and plastered, and the wall between two apartments may be a heavier partition or a fire separation. Those interior walls are where an in-wall power and cable kit usually becomes possible, if the TV can go on one. Many of these houses were built for two to four families and still hold more than one household, so the person who decides about holes is often the owner living one floor away. We are happy to talk to them directly, show them the anchor we will use and explain how the holes can be filled when the bracket comes off one day.

The detached house: more walls to choose from, and the chimney question

Toward the Dyker Heights side the houses stand on their own, and a living room may have three exterior walls and only one interior one. That gives you choice, which is useful when the best viewing wall turns out to be the one with the radiator under the window. Detached houses are also where we meet more fireplaces, working or long since sealed. A TV above a mantel is $130 on our list on top of the wall add-on, and before quoting it we ask two things: whether the fireplace is ever used, because heat rising off a working firebox matters to a TV, and how high the mantel shelf sits, because with an eight- or nine-foot ceiling there is not much room between the shelf and the crown molding. Taping out the screen with you sitting on the sofa settles more of these jobs than any rule of thumb.

The garage-front rowhouse: the stairs set the schedule

The groups of three- and four-story rowhouses that small builders kept putting up into the 1980s put the garage at street level and the living room a flight or two above it. The front wall is usually masonry, the party walls are shared with the houses on either side, and the interior partitions are framed. For a TV job, the main thing is the carry: a large screen in its box has to go up a narrow staircase with a turn or two, so we ask for the TV size and the staircase layout when you book and bring a second person for the biggest screens. We avoid drilling party walls when another wall will do, because a party wall is shared with your neighbor, and if it is the only option we keep anchors well inside the brick and away from where your neighbor's TV or kitchen cabinets probably sit on the other side.

The six-story apartment house: plaster, a super and an elevator to book

City Planning describes six-story and taller apartment houses on the wider streets, including Quentin Road, Avenue P and parts of Kings Highway and 65th Street. Inside, they usually have plaster partitions, masonry around the outside and around the stairs and elevator shaft, and steel or concrete in places a scanner will flag. The super or managing agent may set working hours, want the elevator protected with pads, or ask for a certificate of insurance naming the building before we come in, and we arrange all of that before the day. Walls that back onto the stair hall or the elevator are often masonry fire walls that the building would rather nobody drilled; a short conversation with the super about which walls are off limits saves a wasted trip.

The post-2000 brick apartment building: brick outside, anything inside

The three-story brick buildings that replaced frame houses in the early 2000s look solid from the street, and the outside walls are. Inside, though, they are mostly framed with wood or light-gauge steel studs and finished with drywall, so the TV wall in the living room is often the easiest wall in the neighborhood to mount on and the easiest to hide cables in. The surprise is the steel stud. A screw that would bite into wood just spins in thin steel, so the technician checks with a magnet or a pilot hole before choosing a fastener made for metal framing, or spreads the load across two studs. Many of these buildings are owned by small landlords who live nearby, and they usually want to know two things: that the bracket will not damage the wall, and that the holes can be filled cleanly later.

The boutique elevator condo: a board, a handbook and drywall that hides things

The newest buildings, such as the 10-unit Bealin on Bay Parkway and the 11-unit Tower 85 on 85th Street, are small elevator condos with framed drywall walls and, usually, a board, a sponsor or a managing agent who has written down how contractors work in the building. Typical requests are a certificate of insurance naming the condominium and its managing agent, work only on weekdays between set hours, and the elevator booked and padded. Behind new drywall there can also be things you do not want to drill: plumbing risers, sprinkler lines, the building's intercom and data wiring. The technician scans for metal and moisture, checks the floor plan if you have one, and drills a pilot hole before committing to the bracket position.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks on a Bensonhurst Brick Wall Before the First Hole

These notes describe how our technicians work on walls like the ones in Bensonhurst's older houses. They are our standard practice, not a story about any particular address.

Find out which side of the house you are on. Before any tool comes out, the technician works out whether the TV wall faces the street, the driveway, the yard or the house next door. In a semi-detached house, one long wall is shared with the neighbor and the other faces the driveway, and they are built differently even though they look the same from inside.

Knock, then scan, then drill once. Knocking at screen height tells the technician a lot: a sharp, solid sound on an outside wall points to plaster on brick, a slightly hollow one to plaster on lath or drywall on framing. A scanner in deep mode helps on framed walls but often reads nothing useful on brick. So the decision is made with one small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it. Red or orange dust means brick, grey grit can mean block or mortar, white powder followed by wood means lath, and a sudden drop means a hollow wall.

Aim for the brick, not the joint. Mortar in a century-old wall can be soft. The technician marks the brick courses, sets the bracket so its holes land on whole bricks, and, if the bracket's slots will not line up, moves the bracket a little rather than drilling into a joint. Each anchor is set, tightened to the right torque and pulled on before the next one goes in.

Tell you before anything changes. If the pilot hole shows a different wall from the one quoted, the technician stops, shows you, and explains what that does to the price before drilling again. Nothing changes on the bill without your say-so.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Plaster, Steel or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Bensonhurst Wall and What It Adds to the Bill

The base price on our Brooklyn list assumes the easiest wall there is: drywall over wood studs, the kind found in most newer houses across the country. In Bensonhurst, that is the wall we see least often in the older houses and most often in the newer buildings. Here is how each wall we meet behaves, what it takes to hold a TV safely in it, and what it adds.

Solid or layered brick, +$65. The outside walls of the 1920s houses, many party walls, and often the chimney breast. Brick holds a bracket extremely well once the anchor is in whole brick and sized for it. Old mortar does not, which is why the technician lays out the bracket against the brick courses before marking a single hole. Brick drilling makes fine red dust; we vacuum at the bit as we drill and lay drop cloths below. On a wall where the plaster sits directly on the brick, there is no cavity for cables, so the cable plan is a cord cover, a drop into the console, or moving the TV to a framed wall if you want everything hidden.

Plaster, +$45. The interior partitions of the older houses and the apartment houses, usually plaster over wood lath, sometimes over metal lath or clay tile. Plaster is hard on the surface and crumbly behind, so it will not hold a TV on its own; the fixings have to reach a stud or the masonry behind it. Our technician drills plaster slowly with a sharp bit to avoid cracking the surface, and on old plaster that already has hairline cracks around the spot, we point them out before starting so nobody is surprised later. If the pilot hole shows brick behind the plaster, the wall is priced as brick instead, and you hear that before we continue.

Drywall on steel studs. Common in the post-2000 brick apartment buildings and in the new condos. A screw for wood will not hold in thin steel, so we use fasteners made for metal framing or spread the load with a toggle-type fastener across the stud face, depending on the weight and the bracket. The steel stud situation is one of the reasons we ask for the TV's model number in advance: a light 50-inch screen on a fixed bracket and a heavy screen on a long full-motion arm need very different fixings in the same wall.

Drywall on wood studs, base price. Found in newer framed partitions, some renovated houses and some condos. The technician finds the studs, confirms them with a small pilot hole, and lags the bracket into two of them. This is the wall our base price covers, and it is also the easiest one in which to hide cables inside the wall.

Concrete block. Found in some basements, garages under rowhouses and around shafts in larger buildings. Hollow block needs anchors that expand inside the hollow or set into the solid web of the block, never into the joint. We quote block from your photos, and if the pilot hole shows it on a wall we expected to be brick, we stop and talk it through before going further.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common shortcut we undo in Brooklyn living rooms is a TV power cord pushed through a hole behind the screen and out again near the floor. It looks tidy, and it is against the rules. As Gizmodo summarized, the National Electrical Code's article on flexible cords, 400.8, lists among the uses not permitted cords "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings", and the article warned that a setup like that "could void your insurance coverage." A TV's power cord is a flexible cord, so it cannot simply be buried in the wall.

There are two proper ways around that. The first is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list: a recessed inlet near the floor is connected to a recessed outlet behind the screen through a short length of cable rated for use in walls, the TV plugs into the outlet behind it, and the signal cables pass through the same pair of plates. Nothing but a short cord shows, and nothing illegal sits inside the wall. It needs a hollow wall, so it suits framed partitions and drywall in the newer buildings, and it does not suit brick with plaster laid directly on it. The second is a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list: a slim channel fixed to the wall surface from the TV down to the outlet or console, which you or your painter can paint to match the wall. On an outside brick wall in an older house, the cord cover is usually the right answer.

Signal cables are a different matter. HDMI, coax and network cables are low-voltage, and cables rated for in-wall use can be run inside a hollow wall. That is how a cable box or a game console can sit on a shelf across the room while only the screen is on the wall. In houses where the internet arrives in the cellar or the garage, a wired network connection to the TV or to an internet TV box often streams more reliably than Wi-Fi forced through a couple of layers of brick, and we can tell you from photos whether that is worth doing in your house.

Renters and Owners

Two-Family Landlords, Leases and Condo Boards: Mounting a TV When the Wall Belongs to Someone Else

In Bensonhurst, the person who owns your wall is often not a faraway management company. In a two-family or a semi-detached house split into apartments, it may be the owner on the ground floor; in a small post-2000 building, a landlord who lives a few blocks away; in a condo, a board with a handbook. Each of them cares about the same two things: that the wall is not damaged, and that whatever goes up can come down cleanly.

The rules on what you can be charged for are less clear than many people think. Brick Underground put the question of wall holes to people on both sides of New York City rentals. A property company CEO, Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group, told them that "a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame or shelving system would fall within wear and tear on the apartment." The attorney Justin C. Brasch warned that "careless drilling of the wall or drilling to hold heavy hangings" is something a landlord can charge for, and noted that wear and tear "is not defined in any of the statutes," so it ends up being decided case by case. A TV bracket is a heavy hanging. The same article lays out the deposit rules: a landlord has 14 days to return a security deposit, and any deductions must come with an itemized list.

So our advice is simple. If you rent, ask first, in writing, and keep the answer. A text from the owner downstairs saying "fine, go ahead" is worth more than an argument a year from now. If the answer is no, or you are planning to move soon, a TV on its own stand ($119.99) with a soundbar placed in front of it ($79) avoids the question entirely. If the answer is yes, send the owner what we will be doing: the wall, the number of fixings, the anchor type and where the cables will go. We are happy to put that in writing for you before the visit. We do not patch or paint walls, so when the bracket eventually comes down, the filling and painting is a job for the owner's painter or for you, and it is easier the fewer and neater the holes are, which is one more reason we plan the anchor positions carefully.

Condo and apartment-house boards generally want three things in advance: a certificate of insurance naming the building and its managing agent, the date and the hours of the work, and the elevator booked if there is one. We can send the certificate of insurance and schedule within the building's working hours. If the board's rules say walls cannot be opened, we plan around a cord cover and a console rather than an in-wall kit, and we tell you that before you book so the price you see is the price for what the building actually allows.

Compliance notes for Bensonhurst businesses and buildings

Protruding objects. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, section 307.2, an object fixed to a wall with its leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor should not stick out more than 4 inches into a walkway, because a person using a cane may not detect it. A TV on a full-motion arm in a narrow shop aisle or a clinic corridor can break that rule easily. We mount screens in walkways either above 80 inches, on a low-profile bracket that stays within the limit, or out of the path entirely.

Power. The same electrical code rule applies to businesses as to homes: no flexible cords concealed in walls or above a drop ceiling. A screen in a commercial space gets an in-wall kit, a surface cover or a properly installed outlet behind it. We do not do electrical work ourselves, so where a new circuit or receptacle is needed, that is for your electrician, and we will tell you exactly where we need it.

Life-safety devices. As a matter of practice, we keep screens and brackets clear of exit signs, sprinkler heads, smoke detectors and fire alarm strobes, and we never fix anything to sprinkler piping or hang a screen from a ceiling grid.

Brick, party walls and old finishes

The long wall in our front room runs along the shared driveway of our semi-detached house. Is that wall solid brick, and does a 55-inch TV on it need anything special?

In most of the 1920s semi-detached houses, yes: the wall facing the driveway is an outside wall, and outside walls in these houses are usually brick, often more than one layer thick, with plaster on the inside face. That makes it a strong wall for a TV, and on our list a 55-inch TV on brick is $179.99 plus the $65 brick add-on, with the bracket extra unless you have one. What it needs is the right anchor in whole brick, not in the mortar, and a cable plan that accepts there is no hollow space behind the plaster. That usually means a paintable cord cover ($59) running down to the outlet or console, or a console directly below the screen so only a short cord shows. If you want every wire hidden inside the wall, the answer is usually a different wall: an interior framed partition where an in-wall kit can go. We can tell from a photo of the room which walls are likely to be which.

Our neighbors in the other half of the semi-detached house have their TV on the wall we share. Can we mount ours on the same wall without drilling into theirs?

Usually, yes, because the wall between two halves of a semi-detached house is normally thick masonry, and a TV anchor only goes a couple of inches into it. Anchors for a typical bracket sit well inside the brick and nowhere near the other side. What we avoid is putting our anchors exactly opposite theirs if we can help it, and we stay clear of the chimney flues that sometimes run in shared walls in older houses. If you know roughly where your neighbor's TV hangs, tell us; the technician will offset your bracket slightly. The bigger issue with a shared wall is sound rather than structure: a soundbar bolted to a party wall can carry bass into the house next door. If that is a concern, we suggest placing the soundbar on the console ($79) instead of fixing it to the wall ($129), which decouples it from the masonry.

A previous owner put wood paneling over the brick in our living room decades ago. Can a TV still be anchored through it?

Yes, but the paneling itself cannot hold anything, and it hides what is behind it. Paneling of that era was usually nailed to thin wooden strips fixed to the brick, which leaves a gap between the panel and the masonry. The technician drills a pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it to see how deep that gap is and whether the strips are sound, then uses anchors long enough to pass through the paneling and the gap and set fully into the brick. Sometimes a spacer is needed so the bracket does not crush the paneling when it is tightened. The wall is priced as brick, $65 on top of the base. The gap behind paneling can occasionally carry low-voltage cable down to the floor, but a power cord still cannot be hidden there, so the power plan is the same as for any brick wall.

Newer buildings and the walls inside them

Our three-story brick building went up in the early 2000s where a frame house used to stand. Why did someone tell us our interior walls might have steel studs?

Because a building can be brick outside and steel-framed inside, and nothing on the surface tells you which. Wikipedia records that, before the 2005 rezoning, many single-family homes in Bensonhurst were replaced by three-story brick apartment buildings and condominiums, and some buildings of that kind are framed inside with light-gauge steel; the only way to be sure is to check. A steel stud behaves very differently from a wooden one: an ordinary wood screw will spin in it and hold almost nothing. Our technician confirms the framing with a magnet and a pilot hole and then uses fasteners designed for metal framing, spread across the stud, or across two studs for a heavier screen. The good news is that a framed partition is hollow, which makes it the best kind of wall for an in-wall power and cable kit ($169), so the TV in a building like yours can usually have every wire hidden.

The TV wall in our new condo already has the thermostat and the building intercom screen on it. Can the TV fit between them?

Often it can, but those two devices tell us something about the wall: there is low-voltage wiring running to each of them inside it, usually straight up from the floor or down from the ceiling. The technician scans the wall, plans the bracket so its fixings stay clear of the line directly above and below each device, and keeps a few inches of space around both so you can still read the thermostat and answer the intercom without moving the TV. Where the screen would cover either device, we say so before drilling and suggest moving the TV left, right or slightly higher. In a new building we also ask whether the board or sponsor has rules about the wall, because some condos prefer residents not to open walls at all, which changes the cable plan from an in-wall kit to a paintable cord cover.

We are swapping our 50-inch TV for a 55-inch one. Can the bracket that is already set into our brick wall stay, or does it all have to come out?

If the bracket is rated for the new TV's weight and its arms fit the new TV's bolt pattern, it can often stay, and that saves drilling new holes in the brick. The model number on the back of the new TV tells us its weight and its bolt pattern, and the bracket's own label or box tells us its limits, so send us both before booking. The technician also checks the existing anchors: whether they are in whole brick or in mortar, and whether any have started to work loose. If the bracket is suitable and the anchors are sound, hanging the new TV on it is priced as a mount on that wall, and taking the old TV down is $85. If the bracket is too small or the anchors are in poor shape, we replace it and set new anchors a little away from the old holes.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Bensonhurst

These are claims that turn up in generic online answers and national cost guides, paraphrased, each followed by what Bensonhurst's houses, apartment buildings and streets actually show.

"A stud finder will show you where to drill"

Generic guides start with the same instruction: run a stud finder along the wall, mark two studs, and screw the bracket into them.

The living room walls in Bensonhurst's 1920s semi-detached houses are often brick, built in thick layers, with plaster on the inside face. There are no studs in that wall to find, and a stud finder either reads nothing or reads the whole wall as solid. Interior walls in the same houses may be plaster on wood lath, which confuses scanners in a different way.

The reliable test in these houses is a single small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it. The dust and the feel of the drill tell the technician whether the wall is brick, plaster on lath, block or a hollow framed wall, and that decides the anchor.

"Mounting a TV costs about the same everywhere"

Cost guides give a single number for a city. Angi puts the Brooklyn average at $280, with most jobs between $172 and $376, and HomeAdvisor gives $155 to $350.

Those figures do not say which wall they assume, and in Bensonhurst the wall is the biggest variable. On our Brooklyn list, a 55-inch TV on drywall over wood studs is $179.99, the same TV on brick is $244.99, and on plaster $224.99. Add a fireplace and the job changes again.

The useful question is not "what does TV mounting cost" but "what does it cost on my wall." A photo of the wall and the TV's model number answer that before the visit.

"You can run the TV's cord through the wall to hide it"

Plenty of how-to answers suggest cutting a hole behind the TV and another near the floor and feeding the power cord between them.

The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls; as Gizmodo summarized, article 400.8 lists cords "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings" among the uses not permitted, and a setup like that "could void your insurance coverage." In a house where the plaster sits directly on brick, there is not even a cavity to try it in.

The legal options are an in-wall power and cable kit on a hollow wall, $169 on our list, or a paintable cord cover on the surface, $59. On an outside brick wall the cord cover is usually the right answer.

"Renters can mount a TV and fill the holes when they leave"

Online advice often treats a few holes as harmless wear and tear that a tenant can fix with filler at move-out.

Brick Underground found the picture is less clear in New York City. A property manager told them a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame falls within wear and tear, while an attorney warned that "drilling to hold heavy hangings" can be charged to the tenant, and pointed out that wear and tear "is not defined in any of the statutes." A TV bracket in brick is a heavy hanging, and holes in masonry are harder to hide than holes in drywall.

In a two-family where the owner lives downstairs, the simplest protection is asking first and keeping the answer in writing. If the answer is no, a TV on its stand is $119.99 on our list and leaves the wall untouched.

"Wi-Fi will reach the TV anywhere in the house"

Generic setup guides assume the router is a room away and that every smart TV will stream happily over Wi-Fi.

In a house where the internet arrives at the cellar or a ground-floor garage and the living room is one or two floors up, there may be walls of layered brick in between. Brick is hard on Wi-Fi signals, and an internet TV box streaming from overseas is often the first device to show it.

Where the house allows it, a tested network cable to the TV or the streaming box is the more dependable answer, and we can tell from photos and a description of the house whether it is worth doing.

"Book any day that suits you"

National booking pages show a calendar and treat every day as the same.

In Bensonhurst, 18th Avenue between 68th Street and Bay Ridge Parkway closes for ten days every August for the Feast of Santa Rosalia, from 4 p.m. on weekdays and from noon on weekends in 2026, and 86th Street runs under the elevated D, where curb space is tight. "Any handyman can do it"

Some answers suggest the cheapest available handyperson for a job that "only takes an hour."
