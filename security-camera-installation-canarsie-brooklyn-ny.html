The Neighborhood

Why Canarsie Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Canarsie is a neighborhood of houses built on what used to be marsh and summer resort, and that history decides where a camera can go and where its recorder should not. The fisheries and farmland at the edge of Jamaica Bay were taken over by Dutch settlers, who founded a town called Achtervelt, according to Urban Archive. The Brooklyn and Rockaway Beach Railroad opened in 1865 and turned Canarsie into a summer resort, home to the Golden City amusement park until 1939, the city's Avenue NYC report on the neighborhood notes. In 1896 the still largely rural town of Flatlands, Canarsie included, was annexed into the City of Brooklyn, the Brooklyn Public Library records. After the Second World War, Robert Moses persuaded the city to buy 500 surplus Quonset huts in 1946 to house returning families, and building on former wetlands carried on into the 1950s, producing Seaview Village and NYCHA's Breukelen and Bay View Houses. Most of the houses standing today went up between the postwar years and the 1970s: detached one- and two-family Waxman split-levels from the 1960s in the stretch from Rockaway Parkway to East 108th Street, ranch and split-level houses and attached rows across the forty blocks of Seaview Village, and brick two-, three- and four-family houses in Paerdegat. Brick Underground adds that the neighborhood has single- and multi-family houses and condos but no co-ops.

For a camera job, that mix means the person who says yes is usually the person who lives there. Homeownership in Canarsie has stayed high compared with much of Brooklyn, and most camera calls come from an owner standing in their own driveway. The complications are inside the house rather than on a board's agenda. Urban Omnibus reports that more than 70 percent of Canarsie's homes are connected to a neighbor on one or both sides, so a camera on the front or the back can easily look into the yard or windows next door. Homes.com notes that most houses have a driveway or a backyard garage, which puts a parked car a few feet from the front wall. And many owners rent out a basement or an upper floor; City Limits reported that before Sandy, many immigrant families lived in the basements of houses owned by middle-class residents. A tenant who comes and goes through a side door has a right not to be watched like a suspect, and an owner has a right to know who is at the basement window at 3 a.m. A good Canarsie camera plan has to respect both.

News 12 also reported a home invasion at East 92nd Street and Avenue J in which the suspect posed as a delivery driver, rang the doorbell and forced his way inside, and neighbors told the station it made them more anxious about accepting deliveries; a 23-year-old man was later arrested. The Canarsie Courier reported a home invasion just after midnight on Saturday, October 24, at 108-31 Avenue N, within the 69th Precinct. At an earlier community meeting, the paper reported, the precinct captain said residential burglaries, including copper pipe theft, had risen over a 28-day period, that thieves were getting in through basement windows, and that homeowners should put grilles on those windows and install camera systems. And on October 30, 2026, ABC7 New York reported that surveillance video near East 93rd Street and Avenue L showed two men in a sedan around 1:30 a.m. in a case police were investigating; a resident told amNY that it was "normally a really quiet area." Overnight video from private cameras was the starting point in each of those reports. We found no source that ranks Canarsie blocks by crime, and we do not offer one.

Canarsie's stores face the same pressure from the street side. When the Canarsie Courier looked at the neighborhood's three main commercial corridors, Avenue L, Flatlands Avenue and Rockaway Parkway, merchant surveys put safety and crime first among owners' concerns, ahead of sanitation and commercial rent. The paper's later piece on Rockaway Parkway described storefronts that served generations of families now sitting dark behind metal gates, which leaves the businesses that remain with fewer lit windows around them at night. The district's Council Member, Mercedes Narcisse, is listed as sponsor of enacted legislation establishing a program of financial help for small retail businesses buying security system technology, and another reimbursing nonpublic schools for video surveillance cameras. None of that changes how a camera should be aimed. Every system we install, house or shop, is framed on the owner's own driveway, doors, windows, yard and floor space, with neighboring yards and windows masked out in the settings, and the recordings stay on a recorder the owner controls.

Three Moments

The Driveway at 1:30 a.m., the Ring at the Door, the Window Below the Sidewalk: Planning a Canarsie Camera Around Three Moments

Most camera systems are planned by walking around a house and asking where a camera could physically go. We plan Canarsie houses the other way round, starting from the moments the local reports describe and asking what picture would have helped. Three keep coming back: a stranger walking up a driveway to a parked car in the early hours, a person at the front door who is not who they say they are, and someone working at a basement window that sits below the eye line of anyone on the sidewalk. Get those three right and most of what a private camera can do for a Canarsie house is done. Everything else, the back yard, the side door, the garage, is added on top in order of how the house is used.

The driveway: frame the driver's door, not the street

The October 19 video showed the approach clearly: a man getting out of a red car and walking toward a parked vehicle at about 1:30 a.m. What makes a clip like that useful is the few seconds when the person is close to your car, facing it, under whatever light there is. A camera mounted high on the front wall and set wide to take in the whole street will show a small figure and a blur of headlights. A camera set to fill most of its frame with your own car, from about eight feet up on the house or the garage, will show a body, clothing and, if the angle allows, a face at the driver's door. On a split-level where the driveway runs down to a garage under the house, that camera often goes under the eave above the garage door, looking back up the drive, so anyone walking down toward the car walks toward the lens. On a short pad in front of an attached row, it goes on the front wall beside the door, angled across the pad. We set a fast shutter for night so a moving person does not smear, and we record continuously rather than on motion only, because a slow, careful walk up a driveway can slip under a motion threshold and the moments before someone touches the door handle are the ones worth having.

A second, wider camera can show the car that drops someone off, the way the red car appears in the October footage. That one is aimed along your own frontage to the curb cut, not across the road. Reading a license plate at night from a moving or stopped car in the street is a specialist job; it needs a dedicated camera at a narrow point, tuned for plates, and on an open Canarsie block it usually does not earn its cost. We say that plainly at the walk-through rather than sell a camera that will not do what the owner expects.

The front door: a face before you open it, and a face as they leave

The East 92nd Street and Avenue J case began with a doorbell. A video doorbell lets you see and speak to someone without opening the door, which is the single most useful thing a camera can do in that situation, but a doorbell sees from chest height straight out, and a person who keeps their head down or a box in front of their face can still hide. A second camera at the top of the front steps or the corner of the porch, looking back at the door from the side, records the face as the person turns to go and catches anyone who stands to one side of the bell. Many Canarsie houses have a front-yard gate or a few steps up from the walk, and that pairing covers both. We also help owners set a simple household rule: nobody opens for an unexpected delivery, the courier is asked to leave the box, and the camera records where it went. That rule works better with a camera that can actually see the step.

The basement window: watch the well, not the sky

The precinct's advice was specific: grilles on basement windows and camera systems. Basement windows on Canarsie houses sit low, often in a small well beside the driveway or at the back, below the line of sight of anyone passing. A camera mounted at the eave, looking straight out, will see the yard and miss the window entirely. We mount the basement-window camera on the wall a short way above and to one side of the window, angled down into the well, so a person kneeling or crouching to work at the glass or grille is in the middle of the frame. On the back of attached rows, one rear camera aimed down the wall can often cover a pair of basement windows and the back door at once. Inside, a small camera at the bottom of the basement stair, or facing the boiler and water heater where copper pipe runs, records anyone who does get in. That indoor camera stays out of any rented living space; it covers the utility area only.

The back yard below the house

The Department of City Planning's 2017 report on Canarsie describes many backyards sitting several feet below the street, reached by steps down from the kitchen door, and front yards that slope down one to four feet toward a garage. A rear camera mounted at the top of the house looks down steeply into a yard like that and sees the tops of heads. We usually mount it lower, at the level of the kitchen door or just above, looking across and down the yard toward the back fence and any rear gate, and stop the frame at the fence so the neighbors' yards on either side stay out of it. Where the back steps are the only way up from the yard to the house, a camera at the top of the steps looking down catches anyone climbing toward the door full face.

Keeping the Recording

Where the Recorder, Switch and Battery Belong in a Canarsie House, and Why the Basement Floor Is the Wrong Answer

A camera system is only as good as the box that keeps the recordings, and in most Brooklyn houses that box ends up in the basement beside the internet modem, because that is where the cable comes in and where nobody minds the hum. In Canarsie that habit is a risk. The Department of City Planning's Resilient Neighborhoods work found extensive flooding of basements and ground floors throughout Canarsie during Hurricane Sandy, with water entering through Fresh Creek and Paerdegat Basin at five to seven feet above grade in places, and noted that even smaller, ordinary storms bring street flooding and sewer backups. In March 2020 a water main break sent water into basements on East 78th and East 79th Streets; one resident told CBS New York that all his electrical equipment had been on the floor and he could not save any of it. A recorder on a basement shelf can be lost in an hour, along with every recording of whatever happened before the water came in.

So on Canarsie jobs the basement carries cable, not equipment. The recorder, the network switch that powers the cameras and the battery backup unit go up to a closet, a cabinet or a high shelf on a living floor, ideally in the owner's part of the house and near where the internet line can reach. Where the modem has to stay in the basement because that is where the provider brought the line in, we mount it high on the wall with a battery unit beside it and run a single network cable up to the recorder, so a flood may cost the internet connection for a few days but not the recordings. Cable through the basement is fixed to the joists along the ceiling rather than run along the floor or low on the walls, with drip loops where it comes in from outside so water follows the loop down rather than the cable in. Every opening through the foundation or rim joist is sealed.

Owners sometimes ask whether this means they should keep everything in the cloud instead. For most houses, no: a recorder on a living floor keeps weeks of continuous recording with no monthly fee from us, which no affordable cloud plan matches. But a small off-site copy can make sense, especially for a store or for a house in the lowest-lying blocks near the water. We can send only the alert clips from one or two important cameras, such as the driveway and the basement window, to a cloud service, so if the house ever does take water or the recorder is stolen, the key moments survive somewhere else. The quote names the service and says plainly whether it carries a subscription.

A short storm-season routine for Canarsie owners

  • Check that the recorder's clock and every camera's picture are right once a month, so nothing has quietly failed before a storm.
  • Before a forecast storm, export any clip you might need from the last few weeks to a USB stick or your phone.
  • Make sure the battery unit under the recorder is not showing a fault light; batteries in those units wear out over a few years.
  • After water gets into the basement, do not power up anything that sat in it; call us to check the cable and connections before the system goes back on.

Split-levels: two floors of entrances on one front

A Waxman split-level puts two entrances on one short front: the garage door at the bottom of the driveway and the main door up half a flight of steps beside it. That arrangement is good news for cameras, because the eave over the garage looks back up the driveway at anyone approaching the car, and a camera beside the raised door looks down the steps at anyone climbing them. Cable is usually easy too: the garage ceiling is often open or finished in plain board, and the short stair inside means the run up to a closet on the main level is only a few feet. The trap is light. A raised front door with a light above it and a garage below it in shadow gives a camera a bright top half and a black bottom half at night, so we set the driveway camera's exposure for the garage end and let the doorbell handle the lit door.

Seaview Village rows: the basement is the highway

On the attached rows of Seaview Village there is no side passage, so the only way from the front of the house to the back is through it. Fortunately most of these houses have a basement running front to back, and that becomes the cable route: runs for the driveway, front door, basement-window and back-yard cameras travel along the joists and come up through small sealed holes at the front and rear walls, so nothing is stapled across the brick. Mounts go into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, which drills cleanly and is easy to repoint if a camera ever moves. The shared wall with the next house is never used for a mount. Because these basements sit on former marsh, the recorder does not stay down there; it goes up to a closet on the main floor, as described above.

Paerdegat multi-family houses: separate views on one recorder

The brick two-, three- and four-family houses built in Paerdegat in the 1960s usually have an owner on one floor and tenants on the others, sharing either a front hall or a set of separate doors. All the outside cameras, the shared hall and the basement utility area go on one recorder in the owner's unit. Each tenant household can be given an account that shows its own entrance and nothing else, and the owner keeps the full view and the playback. When a tenant moves out, their account is switched off the same day without touching the recordings.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Night Test on a Canarsie Driveway

This describes how we finish a Canarsie house job, not one particular customer. A driveway camera looks perfect in daylight and can be useless at 1:30 a.m., so we do not hand a system over until we have seen it after dark. Once the cameras are mounted and the recorder is set up, one technician turns off the porch light and any motion light over the garage, because those may be off or burned out on the night that matters. Then one of us walks the route a stranger would take: from the curb cut, up or down the driveway, to the driver's door of the owner's car, and around the back of the car. The other watches live on the recorder and then plays the clip back, checking three things. Is the person's body and clothing clear at the car door, not just a moving shape? Does the face show at any point on the walk in? Does the alert fire when the person reaches the car, and stay quiet when someone walks along the sidewalk? If any answer is no, we change the angle, the zoom, the shutter speed or the alert zone and walk it again. We then do the same at the basement window, kneeling where someone would kneel, and on the back steps. Last, we cut the power to the recorder at the battery unit and watch it come back on its own with the right time on the clock. The owner sees each clip before we leave, so they know what the camera actually shows in the dark, not what the box promised.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Canarsie: Questions Answered

Driveways and the front of the house

In the October driveway theft near Avenue J, the video picked up the man as he left the red car. For our own driveway, should the close camera favor the driver's door or the back of the car where the plate is?

The driver's door, in almost every Canarsie driveway. Your own plate is already known to you and to the police; what matters after a theft is the person who walked up to the car, and they nearly always go to the driver's side first. We set the close camera so the driver's door and the space beside it fill much of the frame, from high enough on the house that it sees the person's body and, as they approach, their face. A second, wider camera aimed along your frontage to the curb picks up the arrival car the way the October footage did. If the car is parked nose-in on one visit and tail-in on the next, we frame for the spot most often used and show you on the recorder what the other position looks like.

Our split-level has the garage half a flight below the front door. Is that one camera's job or two?

Usually two, and the shape of the house makes both easy. One camera under the eave above the garage looks back up the driveway, covering the car and anyone walking down to the garage door. A doorbell or a small camera beside the raised front door looks down the steps at whoever is climbing them. A single camera trying to cover both levels ends up too wide for either, and at night it struggles with a lit door above and a dark garage below. Cable for both usually runs through the garage ceiling and up the short interior stair to a closet on the main level, which is one of the shortest runs we see in the neighborhood.

Since the East 92nd Street and Avenue J case, my mother refuses to open the door for any delivery. Can the cameras let her keep the door shut and still know her package arrived?

Yes, and that is a sensible habit. A video doorbell lets her see and talk to the courier from her phone or a small indoor screen without opening the door, and she can ask them to leave the box on the step. A second camera at the top of the steps or by the gate shows exactly where the box was left and who, if anyone, picked it up afterwards. We can set the system so her phone or a screen in the kitchen shows the door as soon as someone rings, with large, simple controls. News 12 reported that the suspect in that case posed as a delivery driver and forced his way in after ringing the bell; keeping the door closed and using the camera to talk removes the opening he relied on.

Basements, tenants and the back of the house

The 69th Precinct said burglars were coming in through basement windows to take copper pipe. Our basement windows sit in wells below the sidewalk line. Where can a camera even see them?

From the wall just above and to one side of each window, angled down into the well. A camera at the roof edge looks out over the yard and misses a window that sits below ground level, which is exactly why basement windows are attractive to burglars. Mounted lower and angled down, the camera puts anyone kneeling or crouching at the glass or grille in the middle of the picture. On the back of attached rows, one camera on the rear wall can often cover a pair of basement windows and the back door together. Inside, a small camera at the foot of the basement stair or facing the boiler and water heater records anyone who does get in, where the copper is. The precinct also recommended grilles; a camera does not replace them, it records whoever tries them.

Our basement tenant's side door is the entrance most likely to be tried at night, but she does not want us tracking her every coming and going. How do we cover that door fairly?

Give her the camera, not you. The camera that covers her side door can be put on her own account in the app, so she sees who comes to her door and gets the alerts, and set so that on the owner's account it records but does not notify. The owner can still play it back if something happens, such as a pried window or a break-in, and that is agreed with the tenant in writing before it goes up. Nothing inside her apartment is ever recorded, and the camera is framed on the door and the path to it, not on her windows. Most tenants we talk to welcome a camera on their own door once they know they control the alerts and the owner is not watching them live.

Our back yard is several feet below the kitchen door, down a run of steps. A camera at the roof edge just shows the tops of heads. What works better?

Bring the camera down. The Department of City Planning describes many Canarsie back yards sitting several feet below grade, and in a yard like that a camera at the roofline is looking almost straight down. We mount the rear camera at about the height of the kitchen door, looking across and slightly down toward the back fence and gate, so someone crossing the yard is seen from the side and from the front as they come toward the steps. A second camera at the top of the steps looking down catches anyone climbing toward the door full face. Both stop at your fence line so the yards next door stay out of the picture.

The Avenue N home invasion happened just after midnight with people in the house. Should our indoor cameras keep recording while we are asleep?

One should, and it should be pointed at the way in, not at the family. The Canarsie Courier reported that the October 24 home invasion at 108-31 Avenue N happened around 12:10 a.m. For a house where people sleep upstairs, an indoor camera at the inside of the front door or at the top of the basement stair, recording continuously, covers the route someone would take into the living space. It never covers bedrooms or bathrooms. Alerts from that camera can be scheduled to sound only between, say, midnight and six, so the household is not pinged every time someone gets a glass of water, and a household member can check the picture from their phone before going downstairs.

After the 2020 water main break on East 78th Street, a resident said everything plugged in on his basement floor was lost. If we hang our camera power supply high on the basement wall, is that enough?

It is better than the floor, but for a Canarsie house we would still move the recorder itself upstairs. A camera power supply or switch mounted high on the wall may survive a few inches of water, but Sandy put water five to seven feet above grade in parts of Canarsie, and sewer backups can fill a basement faster than anyone can reach it. The recorder holds the recordings, which are the one part of the system you cannot replace, so it belongs on a living floor on battery backup. The basement keeps only cable fixed to the joists, with drip loops where it enters, and any junction that has to stay down there goes in a sealed box as high as practical.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Tenant, Condo Board, NYCHA or Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Canarsie Camera Job

Canarsie has no landmark district that we could find, so the rules that shape camera jobs here are about who owns what, who is being recorded and who is allowed to do the installing. This is how they play out on the houses, condos and stores in the neighborhood. It is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

Who may install. New York General Business Law §69-m says no person may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems, or present themselves as able to, without a license. The Department of State's installer application adds two points: owners installing a system on their own property are not "in the business" and need no license, and installation of "passive," nondetecting security devices does not require licensure. Where an ordinary camera system falls under those words is not spelled out in the sources we found, so we treat any paid installation as licensed work. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to anyone who asks.

Owners and tenants. In a house the owner decides about the outside, the driveway, the yard, any shared hall and the basement utility area. A tenant, whether in a basement unit or upstairs, decides about cameras inside their own apartment. In a privately owned multi-family house, a tenant needs the owner's permission for anything on the outside or in shared spaces. At Breukelen and Bay View, NYCHA and its partners decide everything outside the apartment.

Condos and gated developments. Canarsie has condominiums but, Brick Underground notes, no co-ops. In a condo or a gated development such as Seaview Estates, the association usually controls the exterior walls, common walks and gates, so a camera on the outside of a unit needs the board's written approval first. Inside the unit, the owner decides.

Neighbors. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a, as Gavel Insight explains, lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard. With more than 70 percent of Canarsie homes attached on one or both sides and back yards side by side, every outdoor camera we install is framed on the owner's own driveway, doors, windows and yard, and anything else that falls in the frame is masked out in the camera's settings.

Public property. Cameras go on your house, your garage, your fence or a post on your own lot, never on a lamppost, street tree, sign pole or anything else that belongs to the city.

Walls and water. Every opening through brick, siding or the foundation is sealed against water, and cable entering from outside is given a drip loop. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a mixed-use or multi-family building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

"Canarsie is a quiet residential area, so cameras are mostly for peace of mind."

Our Installation Process

Every Canarsie camera job, from one indoor camera in a Breukelen apartment to a split-level, a Paerdegat three-family or a corridor store, follows the same eight steps. The driveway, front door, basement windows and doors, back yard and any storefront, plus who owns the house, who rents part of it and who will watch the footage. Every request is booked within 2 hours.

Each cable certified, the clock synced, every view walked at night with the lights off, alert zones drawn around the car and windows, and a power cut to prove the system restarts on its own.

8. Hand over logins, a camera map and an export test

Each household gets only its own views, the owner exports a test clip while we watch, and we leave a written map naming every camera and cable.

FAQ

Canarsie Security Camera FAQ

Does a Canarsie house with a finished basement ceiling cost more to wire for cameras than one where the joists are still open?
+

Usually yes, because the basement is the main cable route through most Canarsie houses. With open joists, runs for the driveway, door, basement-window and back-yard cameras are fixed along the ceiling quickly and come up through small sealed holes. With a finished drywall or tile ceiling, we either fish cable above it through a few small access holes that are patched afterwards, run it in a neat surface channel along a wall, or find another route through a closet or the garage. Each choice is priced as its own line on the written quote, on our Brooklyn rate, so you can see what the finished ceiling adds and decide which route you prefer. Estimates are free and requests are booked within 2 hours.

We heard Council Member Narcisse sponsored a city program to help small stores pay for security systems. How does that affect a camera quote for our shop on Avenue L?
+

It does not change the camera plan, but it may change what paperwork you need. The city legislation database lists an enacted bill sponsored by Council Member Mercedes Narcisse establishing a program of financial assistance for small retail businesses buying security system technology. We do not run that program and the sources we found do not give its application rules, so check the current details with the city before you buy. What we can provide is a written, line-by-line quote naming every camera, recorder, cable run and labor item, and an itemized invoice afterwards, which is the kind of record a reimbursement or grant program usually asks for.

An installer bidding on our Flatlands Avenue store says cameras are passive devices, so he needs no state license. Is that right?
+

It is not settled, and we would not rely on it. New York General Business Law section 69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems without a license. The Department of State's installer application does say that installation of passive, nondetecting devices does not require licensure, but it does not define where a camera system with motion detection and alerts falls. Many modern cameras detect people and send alerts, which is hard to call nondetecting. The safe course for a store owner is to hire a licensed installer and ask for the license number in writing. AESS holds NYS license number 12000287431.

The neighbor in our attached row asked us to angle our driveway camera so it also covers his car. Should we?
+

It can work if both of you agree in writing, but it usually weakens your own coverage. A camera angled to take in two driveways shows both cars smaller, and the driver's door of your car may end up at the edge of the frame. A better answer is for each house to have its own close driveway camera, which also means each owner controls their own footage. If he still wants your camera to include his car, write down that he asked for it, frame it so his windows and yard stay out, and agree how a clip will be shared if something happens to his car. That keeps two attached neighbors on good terms.

Our block is inside the flood zone that grew from 26 buildings to about 5,000 on the post-Sandy maps. Does that change which equipment you choose?
+

It changes where the equipment goes more than which brand. Outdoor cameras we install are weather rated, which suits rain and spray on any Canarsie house. The flood zone matters for the recorder, switch, modem and battery unit, which go on a living floor rather than in the basement, and for any connection that has to stay below, which goes in a sealed box mounted as high as practical. Cable entering from outside gets a drip loop, and runs through the basement are fixed to the joists. For the lowest-lying blocks we also suggest sending key alert clips to a cloud service, with any subscription named on the quote.

Our parish school in Canarsie may qualify for the city program that reimburses nonpublic schools for surveillance cameras. Can AESS quote to fit it?
+

We can quote in a way that is easy to submit. The city legislation database lists an enacted bill, sponsored by Council Member Mercedes Narcisse among others, reimbursing nonpublic schools for the cost of video surveillance cameras. We do not know the current reimbursement limits or approved-vendor rules, so the school should get those from the city first. Once you have them, we write a quote that separates cameras, recorders, cabling and labor on their own lines, follow any placement limits the program sets, and provide an itemized invoice and proof of our state license and insurance at the end.

Our Canarsie house sits on a corner with a long side wall facing the cross street. Do we need cameras on both street sides?
+

Usually one more camera than a mid-block house, not double. A corner house has a front, a long side open to the street and often a driveway or side gate on the cross street, so the side wall becomes a way in that a mid-block house does not have. We typically add one camera at the back corner looking along the side wall toward the front, which covers any side windows, a side gate and anyone walking from the street to the back yard. If the driveway opens onto the cross street, the driveway camera moves to that side. The front and back cameras stay as they would on any house.

Nobody is home on weekdays between morning and mid-afternoon, the hours an old precinct report said daytime burglars favored. What should the cameras do during those hours?
+

Record as always, and alert more. An undated Brooklyn Paper report quoted the 69th Precinct describing daytime burglaries clustered roughly between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m., often after a knock to check that no one was home, with entry through a back window or door. We can schedule alerts so the back yard, basement windows and back door notify your phone during the hours the house is empty, while the front door alerts on anyone who rings or knocks. A doorbell lets you answer that knock from work, so the house does not look empty. In the evening the alerts drop back to the driveway and overnight schedule.

Our block association wants to keep a list of which houses have cameras, so neighbors know whom to ask after an incident. How should that be organized?
+

Keep it simple and voluntary. A shared list with each participating address, a contact person and roughly which way each camera faces, such as driveway or street corner, is enough for neighbors to ask the right house for a clip after something like the October driveway theft. Nobody should be given access to anyone else's system; each owner exports and shares their own clip if they choose. We set each recorder's clock to an internet time server, which makes clips from different houses line up, and we can show each owner how to export a short clip quickly. Owners who list their cameras keep full control of their recordings.

Our front steps have an aluminum awning over them. Can the door camera mount under the awning instead of on the brick?
+

Often it can, and an awning keeps rain and sun off the lens. A thin aluminum awning is too flimsy to hold a camera on its own, so we fix the camera to the house wall or to the solid frame of the awning where it meets the wall, never to the thin panel. Under an awning the camera sits in shade, which helps with daytime glare but can make the picture dark at night, so we check it after dark and adjust. Cable runs along the awning frame into the wall behind it and is sealed where it enters.
