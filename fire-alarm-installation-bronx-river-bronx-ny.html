The NYCHA towers and senior buildings: whose system it is, and what residents can do

The Bronx River Houses dominate the neighborhood physically, and they shape how people think about fire safety here, so it is worth being plain about where we fit. NYCHA's design guidelines call for hard-wired smoke and carbon monoxide detectors with battery back-up in its apartments, citing Section 907.2.11.2 of the NYC Building Code, and NYCHA's procedure manual says its fire safety vendor inspects alarms monthly and tests them semi-annually. New fire alarm work in NYCHA buildings goes through NYCHA's own capital program and vendors. We are not NYCHA's vendor, we do not describe the Bronx River Houses, the Addition or any other development as a customer, and nothing on this page should be read as a description of the systems inside them. For a resident, the practical point is that a hard-wired detector in a NYCHA apartment is NYCHA's equipment: a chirp, a missing cover or a detector that does not sound when tested should be reported through NYCHA's own repair channels, and the report kept, rather than the device being taken down. The senior buildings of the Addition, with 225 apartments for older residents across a 6-story and a 14-story building, are the kind of occupancy where fast, audible warning and help getting out matter most, and families who visit can do the simplest useful thing: press the test button with the resident, watch for an expired date and keep the path from the bed to the door clear. Our own work in this part of the neighborhood is in the private buildings around the developments, which is where the rest of this section turns.

The private high-rise: one building system, hundreds of households, a designer in charge

A privately owned building of 14 to 19 stories is a different category of job from anything on the house blocks. It almost certainly has a building fire alarm system with a panel, devices on every floor, connections to sprinkler flow and supervisory switches, elevator controls and doors, and a central station that receives its signals. Any change beyond like-for-like repair belongs to a registered design professional and the FDNY. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules draws the line clearly: replacing detectors, pull stations or a control panel with identical parts and no change in design is treated as a repair, while moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY. Height adds another layer. The rules summarized by SPARC require one-way voice communication in buildings more than 125 feet tall, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor; a 19-story building may well be over that line, and the design professional takes the measurement, not us. The January 2026 fire at 825 Morrison Avenue began on the fourth floor of a 19-story building, which is the kind of event a building system exists for: early detection on the fire floor, notification to the floors that need it, and a signal that reaches the people responsible for the building. Our part in a high-rise is installation, labeling and pre-testing to the approved drawings, coordination with the building's sprinkler and elevator contractors, and testing support. We say that at the first meeting so no one expects us to be the engineer of record.

The older walk-up: a panel in the cellar, a shared stair, and a void over the top floor

The apartment buildings on the street grid between the towers and Westchester Avenue are where the Watson Avenue fire belongs, and they are where we spend the most survey time. A building like this usually has a panel in the cellar or by the entrance, detection in the halls and the stair, smoke and CO alarms inside each apartment, and a roof reached by a bulkhead or hatch. Its weak spots are predictable. The top floor sits under a cockloft that may connect along the row to the next building. Detectors inside apartments go missing when tenants change, get painted over during turnovers, or are taken down because they sound when someone cooks. The panel may be decades old, with batteries nobody has dated and zones nobody can name. Our survey begins at the roof hatch and the top-floor hall, because that is where the evidence of this neighborhood says to begin, then works down through every apartment we can get into, the stair, the cellar and the boiler room, matching each device to the panel. The written result tells the owner three things: what works, what does not, and whether fixing it is a repair or a change that has to be designed and filed.

The two- to four-family house: owner upstairs, tenants downstairs, one fire for everyone

Many of the houses on Bronx River Avenue and Manor Avenue were built between about 1915 and 1930 for more than one family, and most still hold two, three or four households. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer when each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has its own direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard, which is why most two-story houses are handled with smoke and carbon monoxide alarms rather than a panel. That does not make the job small. In a house where one family cooks on the first floor and another sleeps on the second, the useful system is one where every alarm talks to every other alarm, so a fire on either floor wakes both households. We place alarms in each sleeping area, outside each sleeping area, on every level including the cellar, and near the boiler and any gas appliance for carbon monoxide, then link them, hardwired where the walls and ceilings allow it and wireless-linked where cutting plaster in an occupied apartment would do more harm than good. Three- and four-family houses add a shared stair and hall, which become the route every household depends on to get out. We cover that route on its own and make sure the alarm in it is heard inside each apartment with the doors closed.

The garage, repair bay and storage building: empty at night, full of fuel by day

The working edge of the neighborhood along the river is a fire alarm problem of its own. A repair bay has engines running, exhaust, welding or grinding at times, and oil, tires and parts in quantity. A storage or warehousing premises has racks, cartons and locked units and may sit dark from Saturday afternoon to Monday morning. Some of these buildings have apartments or offices above or beside them. Ordinary smoke detection in a bay is a false-alarm machine, so we lean on heat detection in the work areas and smoke detection in offices, stairs and any residential part of the building, chosen and spaced for the actual ceiling height. The more important questions are about the hours when nobody is there. Who receives a signal at 2 a.m.? Who can open the roll-down gate for the fire department? Is there anyone upstairs who needs to hear the alarm before smoke reaches them? We put those answers in writing before we design anything, and where an owner wants a professional response when the building is empty, central station monitoring through our partners is listed at $19.99 a month.

The new affordable and senior building: fire protection designed in from the first drawing

The neighborhood is still building. Soundview Housing is a $95 million project in three phases, awarded to Lemle & Wolff, that creates 222 affordable apartments on NYCHA land, and Casa Celina, a 16-story building with 204 apartments for seniors, opened in 2024. New buildings like these are designed with their fire alarm systems from the beginning. The NYC Business portal's page on the FDNY fire alarm application lists what such a filing needs: fire alarm plans including a riser diagram, floor plans and an input and output matrix, sealed and signed by the registered design professional, compliance items for landmark buildings or flood hazard areas where those apply, and a fire alarm plan involving a Licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder. On a project of this kind, our role is installation to the approved drawings, labeling, pre-testing and coordination with the general contractor and the electrician. We do not take on design or filing responsibility, and we say so up front.

A note for owners who want more than fire protection. Owners of side-street houses and small buildings often ask about cameras at the same visit, and business owners near the river often want both. Adding cameras to a business alarm runs $1,000 to $2,500 on our list, and planning both together means ceilings and closets are opened once instead of twice.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Survey a Bronx River Building, and Why the Roof Comes First

Start at the top. In most neighborhoods a technician starts at the panel. Here we start at the roof hatch or bulkhead and the top-floor hall, because the Watson Avenue fire was found in the space above the top floor. We look for access into the cockloft, signs of old roof leaks on the top-floor ceilings, the condition of the top-floor detectors, and whether the roof line continues across the neighboring building without a break.

Listen from the worst bedroom. On every floor, one person triggers a test while another stands in the farthest bedroom with the door shut and the window unit running. If the alarm cannot be heard there, the floor needs another device or a linked one, whatever the drawings say.

Note where the batteries live. We ask, politely, where e-bikes, scooters and power tool batteries are charged in each apartment and in any ground-floor business. After the 1440 Bronx River Avenue fire, that is a question we put on the sheet in every building, not just the ones that look like they have a problem.

Find the fuel and the quiet hours. On the ground floor we note the boiler, gas appliances, any repair bay or storage room, and the hours each space is empty. A closed shop under occupied apartments is the arrangement where early detection buys the most time.

Panel last, records after. Only once we have seen the building do we open the panel, read its history and compare it with the owner's paperwork, because the building tells us what the paperwork should say.

Rules Bronx River owners and businesses run into

  • Plans come before installation. The FDNY's Fire Code chapter on fire protection systems requires design and installation documents for fire alarm systems to be submitted to the Fire Department for review and approval before installation starts.
  • Repair and redesign are different jobs. Identical replacement parts with no change in design count as a repair; moving or replacing a control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Who signs the plans. The NYC Business portal says fire alarm plans are sealed and signed by the registered design professional, and the fire alarm plan requires a Licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder.
  • Flood and landmark compliance where applicable. The same application lists landmark building and flood hazard area compliance items if they apply. We found no landmark district for this area, and whether a given building near the river falls in a flood hazard area is something the design professional checks address by address.
  • Small houses have a code exception. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer when each dwelling unit has one-hour separation and its own direct exit.
  • Tall buildings need voice. One-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor.
  • Outages are limited. The 2022 NYC Fire Code sets thresholds for systems that are out of service for 8 or more hours in 24, or 30 or more hours in a month, and those limits govern every upgrade in an occupied building.
  • NYCHA follows its own standard. NYCHA's guidelines call for hard-wired smoke and CO detectors with battery back-up under Building Code 907.2.11.2, inspected monthly and tested semi-annually by its fire safety vendor.
The Roof Space

The Watson Avenue Fire and the Space Between the Top Ceiling and the Roof

In July 2019 firefighters were called to an apartment building on Watson Avenue and found fire on the top floor, in the cockloft space between the ceiling and the roof. It became a four-alarm fire. NY1 reported that it affected at least two buildings after spreading through the cockloft. ABC7 reported that the FDNY was looking into whether there were working smoke detectors in the building, and that a resident said the alarm did not sound. We have not seen the outcome of that inquiry and make no claim about what was or was not installed. We start with this fire because it describes, in one event, the way older walk-ups in this neighborhood can fail: a fire that grows in a hidden space, above the last ceiling anyone looks at, and reaches the building next door before most people below know it is there.

Why a cockloft is hard to protect. A cockloft is usually too low to stand in, reached through a small hatch if at all, hot in summer and cold in winter, dusty, and in some rows continuous with the cocklofts of neighboring buildings. Smoke detectors do poorly in spaces like that: dust and temperature swings cause false alarms, and a device nobody can reach is a device nobody will ever test. Fires can also start or spread there unseen, from old wiring, roof work or a fire that broke through a top-floor ceiling. That combination is why the top floor and the roof space get their own attention on every survey we do.

What we do on the top floor. Inside the top-floor apartments and hall, we confirm there is working smoke detection close to the ceiling, check that it is not shielded by soffits or old repairs, and test it from the farthest bedroom. In a building with a panel, we make sure the top-floor devices are on the system, identified by floor and location, and not left on a forgotten zone from an earlier renovation. Where tenants have changed the layout of a top-floor unit, we check that the alarms followed the walls.

What can go in the void itself. Where the design permits and there is safe access, heat detection suited to an unconditioned space can be placed in the cockloft, so a fire there reaches the panel before it breaks through. Whether that is required, allowed or sensible in a given building is a design question, and in a filed system it belongs to the registered design professional. Where nothing goes in the void, the top-floor coverage has to be strong enough to catch the first smoke that comes down.

Openings, hatches and the neighbor. Every hole our cable makes through a top-floor ceiling or a wall is sealed with fire-rated material, so the fire alarm never becomes the path a fire uses. We note the condition of the roof hatch and whether the roof line continues over the next building, and we tell the owner in writing about any open penetrations we see in the void, even ones that have nothing to do with our work. We do not repair roofs or fire walls, but we will not leave without pointing out an opening that a fire could use.

Lithium-Ion Risk

E-Bikes, Chargers and a 10th-Floor Fire on Bronx River Avenue

Gothamist and Fox 5 reported a fatal fire at 1440 Bronx River Avenue, the block between East 174th Street and the Cross Bronx Expressway. It broke out in a 10th-floor apartment, firefighters found an e-bike inside, and the FDNY said a lithium-ion battery caused it. It took 78 firefighters from 20 units to bring under control, one person died and several others were hurt. We could not confirm the year of that fire from the pages we read, so we do not date it here. The citywide trend is clearer: Hoodline's report on the September 14, 2026 fifth-floor fire in Soundview noted that FDNY fire marshals had investigated 282 lithium-ion battery fires through August 2026, compared with 277 in all of 2024. That report did not establish that a battery caused the September fire, and we do not suggest it did.

What a fire alarm can and cannot do. A detector does not prevent a battery fire, slow it down or put it out. Battery fires are known for growing quickly and producing heavy smoke, so the value of detection is time: the seconds between the first smoke and the moment everyone in the apartment and the building knows. That makes placement matter. If a household charges an e-bike in the entry hall, the detector nearest that spot and the one between the charger and the bedrooms are the two that buy the most time, and they need to be linked to the rest of the apartment so the warning reaches a closed bedroom.

Where the charger sits changes the plan. The worst arrangement we see is a bike charging in the only path from the bedrooms to the front door. When a fire starts there, the people furthest inside have to pass it to get out. We cannot tell tenants how to live, but we can tell an owner where detection should go given where people actually charge, and we can tell everyone what the plan assumes. In buildings with ground-floor businesses that charge or store batteries, detection in that space and a signal that reaches the apartments above become part of the same conversation.

The door matters as much as the detector. The September report also left open whether self-closing doors worked in that building. In an apartment building, an apartment door that closes behind the people leaving keeps smoke in the unit and out of the stair everyone else depends on. When we survey a building, we note apartment and stair doors that are propped, broken or missing closers, because the alarm and the door work as a pair. Fixing doors is the owner's responsibility and not our trade, but it goes in the report.

Proving the alarm works, from the top floor down

After the Watson Avenue fire, a neighbor told reporters the alarm never sounded. How can the owner of a similar building find out whether ours would?

By testing it the way a fire would use it, end to end and floor by floor, and writing down what happens. Pressing one detector's test button proves very little. A real check starts at the panel, if the building has one, by reading its condition, its history and the date on its standby batteries. Then each detector is triggered with test aerosol or, for heat devices, the manufacturer's test method, and the technician confirms three things: the panel registers the right location, the notification sounds where it should, and the sound can be heard in the farthest bedroom of each apartment with the door shut. In a building without a panel, the same check is done unit by unit: each smoke and carbon monoxide alarm is tested, its date read, and its link to the other alarms in the unit confirmed. The top floor gets extra time, because that is where the Watson Avenue fire was found, and we look at the roof access and any devices near the cockloft. At the end the owner receives a device-by-device list of what passed, what failed and what was missing, with a written price for anything that needs fixing. That list is the honest answer to the question, and it is the record an owner wants to have before anything happens, not after.

The Lafayette Avenue fire climbed from window to window across three floors. Can detection do anything about fire that travels up the outside of a building?

Detection cannot stop fire from lapping up a facade, but it can make sure the apartments above learn about it early. The December 2025 fire at 1965 Lafayette Avenue was seen on video with flames shooting from upper-floor windows, and it spread to three floors; we do not know how it travelled inside or out. In general, when a fire vents out of a window, heat and smoke can enter the apartment above through its own windows, often before anyone inside smells it. The first useful step is ordinary but often neglected: working smoke detection in every room people sleep in and in the living areas near windows, linked so a detector in the front room wakes someone in the back bedroom. In a building with a fire alarm system, the design decides which floors are notified and how; in a high-rise, that is the registered design professional's work, and we install to it. What owners can add is maintenance discipline on the upper floors, where tenants are least likely to report a missing detector, and a clear instruction to residents to close windows and the apartment door when they leave during a fire. Construction details such as window spacing and fire-rated assemblies are outside a fire alarm installer's scope, and we would not pretend otherwise.

Houses with more than one household

Our four-family house has one stair that every tenant uses to get out. Should that stair have detection of its own, separate from the alarms inside the apartments?

Yes, and the stair should be planned as the route every family's life depends on. In a four-family house, each apartment has its own smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, but the shared stair and halls are the one space nobody thinks of as theirs. A fire that starts there, from a stored stroller, a bike, a pile of mail or an electrical fault in the hall light, can cut off every household at once. We place smoke detection at the top of the stair and on each landing as the layout requires, and we make the stair alarms loud enough to be heard inside each apartment with its door closed, or link them so they sound inside the units. The reverse matters too: a fire inside one apartment should warn the stair and the other households before the smoke fills it. In a four-family house this is also where the building begins to feel like a small apartment house, and whether it needs a building fire alarm system depends on its height, layout and separations under the Building Code. We tell the owner which side of that line the building sits on before quoting. Finally, keep the stair clear: detection buys time, but only an open stair turns that time into a way out.

We want to turn the empty store at the bottom of our building into an apartment. What changes for the detection?

A change of use like that is a construction project first and a fire alarm job second, so the order of steps matters. Converting commercial space to a dwelling normally involves an architect or engineer, the Department of Buildings and, where the building has a fire alarm system, the FDNY, because adding a dwelling unit can change what the building's system has to do. Before any plans are drawn, we can survey what the store has now, such as a separate commercial alarm, a sprinkler connection or devices tied to the building panel, and give the design professional the as-found conditions. Once the conversion is designed, the new apartment gets what every apartment needs: smoke alarms in and outside the sleeping areas, carbon monoxide detection near fuel-burning equipment, and a connection to the building system or to linked alarms, as the design calls for. Any commercial devices that no longer serve a purpose are disconnected and removed properly rather than left on the panel as orphans that will cause trouble later. A ground-floor apartment converted from a store also tends to sit close to the boiler room or cellar stair, so we look carefully at carbon monoxide and at what is directly underneath before we place anything.

Half the tenants in our building work nights. How do you test every apartment's detectors without skipping the units where people are asleep?

By scheduling the building in blocks and telling tenants exactly when their unit will be done. Skipped units are the most common gap we find in building records, and they are usually the apartments of people who work nights or long shifts. Before a full check, the owner or super posts a notice and we offer two or three windows across the day, including an early slot for people coming off night shifts and a late afternoon slot for people heading out. Tenants who cannot be home can give the super permission to let us in, and we test quickly and quietly: an alarm test is loud for a few seconds, so we tell the occupant before pressing anything and keep the time inside each unit short. Any apartment we still cannot reach is listed by number in the report, with the reason, and we come back for it rather than marking it as passed. For buildings with a panel, the common-area devices and the panel itself can be tested on the main visit, so the return trips only cover the apartments. The owner ends up with a complete list, not one with a blank line for every tenant who was asleep.

Repair bays and storage along the river

Engines idle inside our repair bay every morning, and there is an apartment over the shop. How do we detect a fire in the bay without the alarm going off every time a car warms up?

Use the right kind of detector for each part of the building, and link the parts so the apartment is warned. Exhaust, road dust and the haze from welding or grinding will set off ordinary smoke detectors in a bay, and an alarm that sounds every morning gets disconnected sooner or later, which is the worst outcome. In the work area itself we normally use heat detection, rate-of-rise or fixed temperature depending on the ceiling height and the work done, because heat detectors ignore exhaust while still responding to a real fire. The office, the parts room and any stair or hall connected to the apartment get smoke detection, placed away from the bay doors. The apartment upstairs gets its own smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and carbon monoxide deserves attention here, since a repair bay below a home is exactly where fumes can rise through the floor or the stair. The key is that a fire in the closed bay at night has to reach the family upstairs, either through the building's panel or through linked devices, and, if the owner wants it, a monitoring center through our partners. We put the device choices and the reasons in writing so the next mechanic does not swap a heat detector for whatever is cheapest.

Our storage business rents units we are not allowed to open. How can detection cover a fire inside a locked unit we cannot see into?

Detection is planned for the building around the units rather than inside each one. In a storage premises, the operator usually controls the corridors, ceilings and structure, while the customer controls what is inside the unit. A fire in a locked unit will show itself as heat and smoke rising out of it, so devices are placed on the ceiling above the corridors and, where units have mesh or open tops, above the units themselves, spaced for the ceiling height and the layout of the aisles. Where the units are fully enclosed, the design has to account for that, and for a large or multi-story storage building it is a job for a registered design professional and a filing. The operator's part is just as important as the devices: rental rules about what can be stored, a list of who to call when the panel signals, and a way for the fire department to get in when the office is closed. Because these premises are empty for long stretches, monitoring matters more than usual; partner monitoring is listed at $19.99 a month, and where the code requires a system, FDNY-approved central station monitoring is mandatory. We also check that racking and stored goods are not blocking existing detectors, which is the problem we see most often.

Filing, flood and systems nobody looks after

The FDNY application asks about flood hazard compliance. Does that apply to our building near the river?

It might, and the answer comes from the building's address and the flood maps, not from how close the river looks. The NYC Business portal's page on the FDNY fire alarm application lists landmark building and flood hazard area compliance items "if applicable," which means a design professional has to check whether the property falls in a mapped flood hazard area before the plans are filed. We did not find flood-zone mapping for these blocks in our research, so we will not tell you whether your building is in or out; the registered engineer or architect preparing the filing confirms it. What we can tell you is how flood risk changes our installation choices regardless of the filing: panels, power supplies and standby batteries go above the level where water has come in before and off the cellar floor, cable on low routes goes into conduit, and devices in the cellar are chosen for damp conditions. If your cellar has taken water in storms, tell us before the survey and show us the high-water marks, because the location of the panel is far easier to decide before the drawings are done than after it is installed in the wrong place.

The company that installed our building's fire alarm has closed, and nobody services it anymore. Will you take over a system you did not put in?

Usually, yes, after a survey to find out what we are taking over. Orphaned systems are common in older buildings: the installer retires or closes, the owner changes, and the panel keeps running with nobody responsible for it until a trouble light comes on. We start by documenting the system as it is: the panel's make, model and condition, every device and where it is, the zones or addresses and what they actually cover, the standby batteries and their dates, and the monitoring account if there is one. We compare that with any FDNY paperwork or drawings the owner can find. Then we tell the owner honestly whether the system can be maintained and repaired like-for-like, whether parts are still available, and whether a change, such as replacing or moving the panel, would require new plans filed with the FDNY. If we can maintain it, we set a testing calendar and become the call when it signals trouble. Service visits outside warranty are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, no matter who installed the equipment. If the system has been altered by others in ways that make it unreliable, the report says so plainly, so the owner can decide what to do next.

Our landlord pulled out the old hallway alarm system after it kept failing and stuck battery smoke alarms in its place. Is that allowed?

If the building was required to have that system, swapping it for battery alarms is not a substitute, and it should not have been removed without the proper process. Whether a building needs a fire alarm system depends on its size, height and occupancy under the Building Code, and many small multi-family buildings legitimately rely on smoke and carbon monoxide alarms rather than a panel. But where a system was required and installed, it cannot simply be taken out because it was troublesome; changes to a required system go through the FDNY, and removing one is a change. Battery alarms in the halls may sound locally, but they do not report to anyone, do not link to the apartments and do not supervise themselves the way a panel does. For a tenant, the practical steps are to write down what was removed and when, keep any photos, and raise it with the landlord in writing. For an owner who did this because the old system kept failing, the better path is a survey: we can tell you whether the old system was required, what repairing or replacing it would involve, and when a design professional and a filing are needed, so the building ends up with protection that works and is legal rather than a set of battery units on the ceiling.

FAQ

Bronx River Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

The front door of our building uses a magnetic lock. Should the fire alarm release it when it goes off?
+

In many buildings, electrically locked doors on the way out must unlock when the fire alarm activates, so nobody is trapped and firefighters can get in. Whether your door has to be tied to the alarm, and how, depends on the building and the code, and in a building with a filed system it is part of the design. We check how your lock is powered and controlled, coordinate with whoever maintains it, and test that the door releases when the alarm sounds.

Our phone company is replacing the copper lines in the building. Will that affect our monitored fire alarm?
+

It can. Many older panels send signals to the monitoring center over copper phone lines, and when those lines are cut over, the panel can lose its path without anyone noticing. Tell us the cutover date before it happens. We check how your panel communicates, arrange with the monitoring company for a cellular or other approved path where needed, and send live test signals afterward to prove the center still receives them.

Our sprinkler contractor is adding waterflow and valve tamper switches. Who connects them to the fire alarm panel?
+

Usually the sprinkler contractor installs the switches on the piping, and the fire alarm side wires them to the panel, programs them and tests that a waterflow produces an alarm and a closed valve produces a supervisory signal. We agree with the sprinkler contractor in writing who does what before the work starts, so no switch is left mounted but unconnected, and we test both signals with them present.

Our panel is in the cellar. Can a second display go by the entrance so firefighters do not have to go downstairs to read it?
+

Many panels support a remote annunciator, a small display that shows alarm and trouble information at a more useful location such as the main entrance. Whether one is required, and exactly where it goes, can be part of a filed design, and in that case the design professional decides. Where it is an owner's choice, we confirm the panel supports it, run the cable indoors and test that the display matches the panel.

How do you prove a carbon monoxide detector works without putting the room in danger?
+

We use the method the manufacturer specifies, which for many models is a test button that checks the electronics and sounder, and for others a small amount of canned test gas applied directly at the sensor. Neither fills the room with carbon monoxide. We also read the end-of-life date printed on each unit, because a detector past that date should be replaced even if its test passes.

Who patches the small holes left when cable is fished through an apartment wall or ceiling?
+

We keep openings as small and as few as the route allows, and the written quote states whether basic patching, and any painting, are included before the job begins. Openings through fire-rated walls and ceilings are always sealed with fire-rated material as part of our work. If a tenant's apartment needs finish painting afterward, we say so in advance so the owner can plan for it.

When you replace an old system, what happens to the old wiring left in the walls?
+

Where old cable can be pulled out without damaging the building, we remove it. Where it is buried in plaster or masonry, we disconnect it at both ends and mark it clearly as abandoned, so a future technician does not mistake it for a live circuit. The handover records note what was removed and what was left, and old detectors and panels come out with us.

Besides the FDNY, does fire alarm work in our building need anything from the Department of Buildings?
+

It can. Fire alarm plans go to the FDNY, but electrical work connected to a fire alarm, such as the dedicated power circuit for a panel, is electrical work that involves a Licensed Master Electrician, and some projects also involve DOB filings for the construction around them. On filed jobs, the design professional sets out which approvals apply. On household installations, we tell you plainly when an electrician needs to be part of the job.

Does the refuse or compactor room in an apartment building need detection?
+

Refuse rooms and chutes collect exactly the kind of material that burns, and in many buildings they are covered by sprinklers and by the building's fire alarm design. Because those rooms are dusty and dirty, heat detection is often more reliable there than smoke detection. In a building with a filed system the design decides; in a smaller building we look at the room on the survey and include it in the plan if it is not already covered.

Our windows face heavy traffic and road dust gets in. How often should smoke detectors be cleaned?
+

Dust that builds up inside a smoke detector's chamber can cause false alarms or make it less sensitive. Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions, which for most household units means gently vacuuming the vents on a regular schedule, and pay closer attention to detectors near windows that open onto busy roads. If a unit keeps false alarming after cleaning, or is past its replacement date, replace it rather than taking it down.

+

Any swap should be recorded on the device list so the records stay accurate.
