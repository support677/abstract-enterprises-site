The Neighborhood

Why Chelsea Homes, Lofts and Galleries Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Door by Door

In Chelsea the kind of front door can change from one block to the next. The neighbourhood runs roughly from 14th Street up to the high 20s or 34th Street, depending on who is drawing the line, and from Sixth Avenue west to the Hudson. Its Wikipedia entry describes it as primarily residential, with a mix of tenements, apartment blocks, two city housing projects, townhouses and renovated rowhouses — and, west of Tenth Avenue, one of the centres of the city's art world. A smart lock that is right for one of those doors is often wrong for the next one along the street, which is why we start every Chelsea job by naming the building type before we talk about brands.

The oldest doors are on the side streets. A historical marker on West 23rd Street records that Chelsea was developed from the 1830s to the 1850s, largely on land owned by Clement Clarke Moore, and that the result is “an unusually uniform and elegant residential neighborhood in Greek Revival and Italianate styles.” The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Chelsea Historic District in 1970 and extended it in 1981. On Cushman Row, 406–418 West 20th Street, Village Preservation describes houses set ten feet back behind iron fences, stoops with candelabrum newels and “deep brownstone temple-like entryway enframements.” Those entrances are beautiful and deep, and they are regulated: the LPC's own rules say historic doors may be replaced only if they are deteriorated beyond reasonable repair. A smart lock on one of these doors has to be fitted to the door that is there, not to a new one.

The biggest buildings are mid-century and earlier. London Terrace, which opened in May 1930 on the block between Ninth and Tenth Avenues and West 23rd and 24th Streets, has about 1,700 apartments in 14 joined buildings of 17 to 19 stories — run on the inside as two separate organisations, London Terrace Towers, a co-op, and London Terrace Gardens, a rental. A few blocks north and east, Penn South, officially Mutual Redevelopment Houses, is a limited-equity co-op of 2,820 apartments and close to 5,000 residents between Eighth and Ninth Avenues from 23rd to 29th Streets, supervised by the city's Department of Housing Preservation and Development. Nearby, NYCHA's Fulton and Elliott-Chelsea Houses are in the middle of a demolish-and-rebuild plan in which, according to the city, all 2,056 existing public housing apartments will be replaced. Each of these complexes has its own rules for what a resident may do to a unit door, and in the NYCHA buildings the answer belongs to the Housing Authority and its development partners, not to a contractor.

West of Tenth Avenue the doors are industrial. In 2008 the LPC designated the West Chelsea Historic District, a seven-block band between West 25th and 28th Streets from Tenth Avenue to the West Side Highway, to protect brick industrial buildings dating back to 1885, including the New York Terminal Warehouse Company's Central Stores — 25 interconnected brick buildings filling the block between West 27th and 28th Streets. Around them, former industrial buildings hold the galleries that made the area famous: MTA Away puts the number of contemporary art galleries in West Chelsea at more than 300, open for most of the year Tuesday to Saturday from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., with exhibition openings on Thursday or Friday evenings at 6. Converted industrial buildings are also where lofts of the type Brick Underground describes are found, with a key-locked elevator that may open directly into the apartment. Those are doors with glass, steel, freight landings and crowds — not the doors a consumer smart lock box was designed for.

Two more facts shape the work. Most Chelsea households rent: the NYU Furman Center puts the 2024 homeownership rate for Clinton/Chelsea, the community district that also takes in Hell's Kitchen, at 19.2 percent, well below the citywide 32.7 percent. And the western blocks sit close to the river. When Hurricane Sandy struck in October 2012, Artlyst reported three to five feet of flooding inside Chelsea galleries, and one gallery posting that “Chelsea is flooded up to tenth avenue.” A lock controller, power supply or battery pack in a ground-floor space west of Tenth Avenue belongs high on the wall, not under the reception desk.

Put together, that is a neighbourhood where the same request — “we want a keypad on the front door” — can mean a careful retrofit on a landmarked door, a conversation with a co-op's management office, a steel loft door off an elevator landing, or an electrified strike and reader on a gallery's glass entrance that must release cleanly for a Thursday crowd. We plan each one on its own terms.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Chelsea's Architecture

Chelsea's buildings fall into six families, and each family decides the lock before the catalogue does. These are the ones we plan for, oldest first.

Greek Revival and Italianate Rowhouses, 1830s–1850s

The landmarked side streets around the General Theological Seminary block and Cushman Row: wood doors in deep brownstone enframements at the top of a stoop, often a second entrance under the stoop, and LPC rules that protect the historic door itself. Bores are reused wherever possible and the street face is kept plain.

Tenement Walk-Ups on the Avenues

Walk-up buildings on Eighth, Ninth and Tenth Avenues with a buzzer at the street, a narrow stair and apartment doors that have carried a succession of deadbolts. The work is in the door and the frame: filling old holes, squaring the strike, and choosing a lock that tolerates a door that is no longer flat.

Pre-War Complexes Like London Terrace, 1930

Hundreds of near-identical unit doors on long corridors, a staffed lobby, and — at London Terrace — a co-op and a rental sharing one block. The building decides the hardware standard; we work inside it.

Mid-Century Towers Like Penn South, 1962

Towers of 21 residential floors with two elevators each and a co-op management office that handles maintenance for thousands of apartments. Unit locks need its consent, and the inside of the door is where the electronics live.

Warehouses, Garages and Factories, 1885 Onward

West Chelsea's brick and early reinforced-concrete industrial buildings, now galleries, studios, offices and lofts. Expect steel doors, steel frames and freight elevators, and very little that is standard.

New Condos Along the High Line

Recent towers on the blocks beside the elevated park, which opened in phases from 2009: buildings that may come with a developer-chosen entry platform and a unit lock already fitted. The choice is whether to work with what the building installed or replace it with a lock you control.

How the method changes by building family

Landmarked rowhouses. We start with a door survey rather than a lock. The door is photographed both sides, the existing bores and backset measured, the thickness and condition of the stiles checked, and any cracks or old repairs noted. Because the LPC's rules allow a historic door to be replaced only when it is deteriorated beyond reasonable repair, the safest smart lock is the one that needs the least new wood removed — often an interior retrofit over an existing deadbolt, or a smart deadbolt that drops into the existing bore. Where a keypad would show on the street face, the owner decides with us, and with an architect if there is one, whether that is acceptable or whether an inside-only unit is the better answer.

Walk-ups. These doors have usually been drilled more than once. We plug and dress old holes so the new lock sits on sound wood or steel, adjust or replace the strike so the bolt throws without the motor straining, and test the radio at the door — in a narrow brick building the strongest signal at your door may be a neighbour's. Renters keep the right, under the state's Multiple Dwelling Law, to add their own lock as long as the landlord receives a duplicate key on request.

Large co-op and rental complexes. The management office comes first. We send the specification of the lock and a short description of the door work, confirm the building's rule on emergency entry, and match the corridor-side finish to the building's standard or leave the corridor side untouched. In a complex where one side is a co-op and the other a rental, we confirm which side of the line the apartment is on before anything else.

Warehouse lofts and studios. Every door is treated as a possible fire-rated assembly until we have found and read its label. Electric strikes in labelled frames are fail-secure and listed; readers and power supplies use existing pathways; the elevator's key switch is not ours to touch. Where the loft's elevator opens straight into the apartment, we look first at the second door, if there is one, and at the building's video intercom.

Galleries and showrooms. Gallery entrances are commonly glass, in an aluminium frame or with no frame at all, and the ground floors west of Tenth Avenue are the ones that flooded in 2012. We pick hardware that suits the glass — a strike in an aluminium frame, an electrified lock in a stile, or a glass-door bolt or bracketed magnetic lock where there is no frame at all — and we mount the controller and battery backup where water will not reach them. Most Chelsea galleries that add keyless entry also want the door on camera — our commercial security systems for Manhattan businesses cover both.

High Line condos. We ask the managing agent for the building's integration list before anyone buys a lock, and then either enrol a supported model in the building platform or keep a separate lock that the owner controls, with the desk's emergency access still working.

Business Smart Lock Installation in Chelsea

Chelsea's business doors cluster in a few places, and each cluster has its own door and its own rhythm. We work in all of them; these are the patterns we plan around.

The West 20s Gallery Blocks

The streets from about West 18th to West 28th between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, where MTA Away counts more than 300 contemporary galleries. Glass street fronts, back-of-house storage and viewing rooms, art deliveries, and a week that runs Tuesday to Saturday with openings on Thursday or Friday evenings.

Chelsea Market and Ninth Avenue

The former factory building between Ninth and Tenth Avenues that is now a food hall, shopping arcade, office building and television production facility. The New York Times reported Google's $2.4 billion purchase of it in 2018. Stall shutters, back-of-house doors and office floors, each with a different owner of the key.

Starrett–Lehigh and West 26th–27th Streets

The 1931 freight terminal that linked rail, trucks, manufacturing and storage in one structure, with entrances on 26th and 27th Streets, now a campus of fashion, design and architecture tenants. Suite doors off long corridors, inside a landlord-run lobby.

The Terminal Warehouse Block

The Central Stores of the New York Terminal Warehouse Company, 25 interconnected brick buildings between West 27th and 28th Streets inside the West Chelsea Historic District, converted from storage into work and retail space. Industrial openings, new tenant doors and landmark oversight of the exterior.

Shops Under the Walk-Ups on Eighth and Ninth

Ground-floor restaurants, bars, salons and shops below tenement apartments along the avenues. A storefront door, a back door to a yard or alley, and often a stair door the shop must not control, because it belongs to the tenants upstairs.

Studios and Small Offices on the Side Streets

Design studios and small firms on upper floors of converted loft and rowhouse buildings across the neighbourhood. They want phone entry and a log for a handful of people, not a full access-control build.

Compliance callouts for Chelsea business doors

  • Locking the main door during business hours: model building codes — the International Building Code and the 2020 Building Code of New York State — allow the main door of business and mercantile spaces to have a key-operated lock on the egress side only under conditions that include a durable sign reading “THIS DOOR TO REMAIN UNLOCKED WHEN THIS SPACE IS OCCUPIED.” New York City has its own Building Code; we set galleries up so the door never relies on a key from the inside.
  • Electric strikes on rated doors: door-hardware specialists at iDigHardware and Allegion explain that electric strikes on fire doors must be fail-secure, so the door stays positively latched in a fire, and that NFPA 80 listings require it. Job-site preparation of rated doors and frames is limited to what the listings allow.
  • Magnetic locks: electromagnetic locks are only available fail-safe, and NYC Building Code section 1010.1.9.9 permits them on egress doors and tenant-space doors only with door-mounted release hardware that cuts power directly with one hand and unlocks on power loss.
  • Landmarks: in the Chelsea, Chelsea Extension and West Chelsea historic districts, exterior changes need the LPC's permission; the Commission says 95 percent of its approvals are issued at staff level without a public hearing. What is visible from the street is what draws attention.
  • Elevators: under the city's Administrative Code it is unlawful to perform elevator work unless it is performed by or under the direction of a licensed elevator agency director. Elevator key switches and car controls are outside a lock installer's scope.
  • Low-voltage wiring: NYC Administrative Code § 28-105.4.7 exempts from electrical permits the under-50-volt circuits used for signalling, communication, alarm and data, provided the installer is a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person under the city electrical code. The 120-volt feed to a power supply is separate electrician's work.
  • Reach and operation: on a public entrance we mount keypads, readers and exit hardware at heights a seated visitor can reach, and choose parts that work with a closed fist rather than a pinch or a twist.

We cite these rules so that an owner, a landlord or an architect can look them up; treat them as pointers, not as legal advice. A gallery that goes keyless usually wants a camera on the same door — see our commercial security camera installation in Manhattan.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Chelsea: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from Chelsea's own doors, grouped by building: landmarked rowhouses, lofts with freight landings and keyed elevators, the big complexes and the city's housing, gallery entrances on the West 20s, and buildings under repair. Renter and co-op rules for the whole city are on our New York City smart lock page, and doorman-building, tower-suite and short-stay questions are on the Manhattan borough page. Cost, scheduling and coverage are in the FAQ further down. None of this is legal advice.

Landmarked rowhouse doors

If drilling for a smart lock splits our original 1840s front door, can we simply order a new one?

Not simply, and that is why the drilling plan matters more on a landmarked Chelsea door than anywhere else. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules for windows and doors say that new doors may be approved if they match the original or historic ones in details, materials, operation, configuration and finish, and that historic doors may be replaced only if they are deteriorated beyond reasonable repair, as described in a condition assessment. A door you damaged last Tuesday is not “deteriorated beyond reasonable repair”; it is a repair job, and probably a specialist joiner's one.

So we design the installation around the door's weakest point. Old stiles may have been bored for three or four different locks over a century and a half, and the wood between those bores can be thin. We measure the existing bores and backset, look for old patches and shrinkage cracks, and choose a lock that reuses what is there: an interior retrofit that mounts over an existing single-cylinder deadbolt and leaves the outside untouched, or a smart deadbolt that fits the existing bore without enlarging it. If the only lock you like needs new holes in a fragile stile, we say so, and we show you the alternative.

If a door is already failing — split rails, loose joints, a stile that will not hold a screw — we stop and photograph it before anyone installs anything, so the condition is on record and the owner can decide with a restorer or architect what happens next.

Who files with the Landmarks Preservation Commission for a keypad — the owner, the architect or the installer?

The application belongs to the property owner, or to someone the owner authorises, such as an architect or expediter; a lock installer is not the applicant. LPC lists three kinds of permit — the Permit for Minor Work, the Certificate of No Effect and the Certificate of Appropriateness — and says that 95 percent of its approvals are issued by staff without a public hearing. Whether a particular keypad needs a permit at all depends on what changes on the exterior; the NoHo business improvement district's guidance, for example, notes that repairing or replacing hardware such as knobs, hinges and handles does not need LPC approval, while most other work on doors does.

What we do is make the owner's file easy. Before a landmark-district job we supply photographs of the door and entrance as it is, the manufacturer's specification sheet and finish options for the proposed lock, a marked-up photo showing exactly where anything would be visible, and a description of every hole: whether it reuses an existing bore or is new, and its size. That is the material staff ask to see.

Many owners avoid the question entirely by choosing an inside-only retrofit, which leaves the street face and the key exactly as they were. Others decide that a small keypad is worth an application. Either way, nothing is drilled on a landmarked facade until the owner has told us, in writing, which path they are on.

Our rowhouse used to be four apartments and is one home again — what should happen to the old apartment doors and their locks?

Reconverted rowhouses often keep the doors from their apartment years: a deadbolt on every landing, a peephole at each floor, keys that nobody can match to a door any more. The first job is to decide which of those doors still needs to lock. Usually it is the street door, perhaps the garden-level door under the stoop, and one or two interior doors — a home office, a room for a nanny or a lodger — and nothing else.

The rest can have their deadbolts removed and the holes filled, or the deadbolt kept but re-set to work as a simple privacy latch, so nobody is ever locked into or out of a room in their own house by a forgotten key. Where the house is sometimes let in parts, we can put smart locks on the doors that matter and plain passage hardware on the others, with all the codes in one app.

We also check that every door on the way out of the house opens from inside without a key. An old apartment door with a double-cylinder deadbolt, left over from the building's multi-family years, is the sort of thing that is easy to forget and dangerous in a fire.

Lofts, freight landings and keyed elevators

Our loft's elevator opens straight into the apartment on a key — where does a smart lock go?

Brick Underground's guide to these apartments explains that a key-locked elevator will not move without a key in the car's lock or a resident ringing the visitor up, and that the elevator's front door is locked and opens only by key or when someone provides access. In that arrangement the elevator itself is the front door, and the lock that governs it is the building's, not yours. The same guide notes that some lofts also have a separate entrance door between the elevator and the apartment, and that some buildings add steel gates outside the elevator.

If your loft has that second door, it is the natural place for a smart lock: it is yours, it is a normal door, and it gives you a log and personal codes for the people who reach the landing. If there is no second door, a smart lock usually goes on the stair or fire-exit door that also reaches the apartment — always with free exit from inside — and the building's video intercom handles visitors arriving by elevator.

What we do not do is fix anything to the elevator's doors, gate or car. That equipment is inspected and maintained by a licensed elevator company, and the building will want it left alone.

Can the key switch inside our loft elevator be swapped for a keypad or a phone?

It can be, in many buildings, but not by a lock installer. Brick Underground notes that in newer construction the elevator may be controlled by magnetic cards, keypads, codes or fingerprint readers instead of a physical key, so the technology exists. The obstacle is the law: the city's Administrative Code makes it unlawful to perform elevator work unless it is done by or under the direction of a licensed elevator agency director, and New York State requires anyone working on an elevator to hold an elevator mechanic licence.

The right route is through the building. The board or managing agent asks the elevator company that services the car what credential readers its controller can accept. If the building wants the reader to share credentials with apartment doors and the lobby, we can supply and configure the access platform and the reader on the landing side, while the elevator company makes the connection inside the car and controller.

Until then, the practical step is to treat elevator keys like apartment keys: know how many exist, collect them when people leave, and use the building's video intercom to let visitors up instead of handing out keys.

Big complexes and the city's housing

Half our block is a co-op and half is a rental under one roofline — whose rules decide what goes on my door?

The organisation you hold your apartment from decides, not the building you can see. London Terrace is the clearest example: the 14 joined buildings look like one complex from the street, but Wikipedia records that they are run on the inside as two organisations — London Terrace Towers, a co-op managed by Douglas Elliman, and London Terrace Gardens, a rental managed by Rose Associates — with many internal connections closed off.

If you own shares, your proprietary lease and the co-op's house rules govern the door, and the board's managing agent is the one to ask. If you rent, your lease and the state's Multiple Dwelling Law govern it, and the landlord's management company is the one to tell. The neighbour across the courtyard may be living under completely different rules on an identical door.

So the first line of any request we write in a split complex names which side of the line the apartment is on, which management company runs it, and which document — proprietary lease or lease — we are working from.

My old apartment key turned out to open a neighbour's door — will a smart deadbolt fix that?

It fixes your door, and it is a good reason to tell the landlord at once. Older walk-up and mid-century buildings sometimes have unit cylinders keyed alike, or master-keyed in ways that have drifted over the decades; New Yorkers on the r/AskNYC forum have described buildings where one key opens several units. Until the landlord re-keys, your existing lock should not be treated as private.

Adding a smart deadbolt gives your door a lock with its own keyway and its own codes. In a building of three or more apartments, the state's Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant install an additional lock of up to three inches in circumference, provided a duplicate key is given to the owner on request. We fit the new deadbolt, leave the landlord's lock in place, and give you the duplicate key to hand over.

The landlord's own cylinder still needs fixing for the neighbours' sake. We put what you found into a short written note you can send to management, so the building re-keys the affected units.

Our household is moving into one of the new Fulton or Elliott-Chelsea towers — is it worth fitting a smart lock before the move?

No; wait until you are in the new apartment and know its rules. The city's PACT page for the project says all 2,056 existing NYCHA apartments at Fulton and Elliott-Chelsea will be replaced with new, permanently affordable homes, and The City Reporter reported in September 2026 that NYCHA, working with Related Companies and Essence Development, plans to take down 18 buildings in three developments and replace them with six towers. The new buildings will come with their own doors, hardware and entry systems, chosen by the developers and the managing agent.

Whatever you add to a public housing door now belongs to a door that is due to come down, and NYCHA, not a contractor, decides what may be fitted to its doors. In the new towers the managing agent will set its own rules for unit doors, and those are the rules we would follow.

When you have moved and have the building's rules in hand, call us with them. If an additional lock is allowed, we will fit one that suits the new door and keep a copy of the paperwork on file.

Gallery doors on the West 20s

Hundreds of people come through on an opening night — should the gallery door be locked, unlocked or on a schedule?

Unlocked for entry while the gallery is open, always free to leave, and on a schedule the rest of the time. MTA Away's guide to the district notes that openings are generally on Thursday or Friday evenings from 6 p.m.; at that hour the door is a public entrance, and staff cannot be expected to buzz in a crowd. We set the lock or strike to a “passage” mode for the opening, triggered by the schedule or by a staff member's phone, and back to locked when the last visitor leaves.

Exit never depends on the schedule. Electric strikes leave the lever or push bar working at all times, so people inside can leave whether the strike is locked or not, and a magnetic lock must release from door-mounted hardware and on power loss under the city code. The model codes go further for main doors locked with a key from inside, requiring a sign that the door must stay unlocked while the space is occupied; we avoid that arrangement altogether.

For ordinary days the schedule mirrors the gallery's hours — for most galleries Tuesday to Saturday, with Saturdays off in summer — and the front desk can lock the door instantly from a phone if something in the room needs attention.

Our gallery door is frameless glass with nothing to cut a strike into — what can lock it electronically?

Several things, none of which require replacing the glass. Electric bolt locks for glass doors are made to sit in a top rail, in a floor box, or in a surface patch that clamps to the glass, and magnetic locks can be mounted with a U-shaped bracket that wraps the top edge of an outward-swinging glass door, as access-control makers such as Ubiquiti show in their installation guides. The right choice depends on whether the door swings in or out, whether it has patch fittings at the top and bottom already, and where cable can run without showing.

Each option has trade-offs. A magnetic lock is fail-safe by nature and needs door-mounted release hardware under the city's code; an electric bolt can be fail-secure but needs careful alignment on a heavy pivoting door; a surface patch is visible, so it has to look right on a storefront that may be in a historic district.

We assess the door, the pivot and the floor, show you the options with photographs of comparable hardware, and price the one that suits the gallery — reader inside the glass where the glass allows it, so nothing new needs to be fixed to the facade.

Should the electric strike on our gallery entrance be fail-safe or fail-secure?

For nearly every gallery front door, fail-secure. As the door-hardware writers at iDigHardware put it, fail-safe products are unlocked on the access side when power is removed, and fail-secure products stay locked. A fail-safe strike would leave the gallery open to the street every time the power drops; a fail-secure one keeps the door latched and still lets everyone inside leave, because the lever or push bar works independently of the strike.

There are also places where the choice is made for you. Electric strikes on fire-rated doors must be fail-secure so the door stays positively latched in a fire, and magnetic locks are only made fail-safe. If your door is rated, or uses a magnet, the decision is already in the hardware.

Staff still need a way in during an outage, so we keep a key override on the lockset and put the controller on a battery backup that carries the strike through a short cut. You decide, with us, how long that backup should last.

Where should the lock controller and battery backup go in a ground-floor space that took water in 2012?

High on the wall, on the dry side of the room, and never on the floor or inside a low cabinet. Artlyst reported three to five feet of water inside Chelsea galleries after Sandy, with water reaching five feet inside one of them. A controller, power supply or battery under the reception desk at that height would have been underwater, and an access controller that has been submerged is a replacement, not a repair.

We mount the controller and its battery at or above head height in a labelled enclosure, run the cable down to the door from above, and keep any connection that has to be low — a door contact, a request-to-exit button — simple, sealed and cheap to replace. The lock on the door itself is chosen to fail in a predictable way: locked, with a key override.

We also leave a one-page sheet in the enclosure: what to switch off when a storm warning goes out, what to check before power is restored, and the number to call. A gallery that already had water once tends to want that written down.

While the building is being worked on

Scaffolding is going up on our building for facade work — which doors need attention while it is there?

Any door or window that a person on the scaffold can reach, which is usually more than people expect. The city's Facade Inspection and Safety Program requires owners of buildings taller than six stories to have exterior walls inspected every five years, so sidewalk sheds and pipe scaffolds come and go across Chelsea. In December 2021, Patch reported that a burglar scaled scaffolding outside an artists' studio on West 39th Street in Midtown and got in through an unlocked second-storey window.

Before the scaffold goes up, list every opening on the scaffolded faces: terrace and balcony doors, roof bulkhead doors, back doors onto setbacks, and windows. Doors in that list are worth a lock that can tell you when it opens and that locks itself after a set time; windows need their own latches or locks and are outside a door lock's reach.

When the work ends and the scaffold comes down, we can switch the extra alerts off again. If the building's own doors are part of the problem, the board or landlord should hear about it in writing.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Chelsea

A note on method: Google's AI Overview could not be retrieved while this page was researched, so none of the seven headings below is a quotation from it. Each one paraphrases a general claim found in cost guides, product listings and how-to articles — the material answer engines tend to summarise. Under each is what changes once the door is a landmarked rowhouse door, a loft landing or a gallery entrance in Chelsea.

“Any smart deadbolt drops into any front door in a few minutes”

Retail smart deadbolts are designed around the standard modern door: a bore of a common size at a common backset, in a door of a common thickness. On a new door in a new building, the box's claim is fair.

A Chelsea rowhouse door may be a century and a half old, bored three times already, and protected by rules that allow replacement only when the door is beyond reasonable repair. A walk-up door may be steel-skinned with a frame that is no longer square. A loft door may be twice the weight of a house door. The minutes the box promises are the minutes after somebody has worked out what the door can take.

Measure first, then buy. If you have already bought, we will check the lock against the door before anything is drilled.

“Historic-district rules only matter if you are renovating”

It is easy to assume that landmark rules are for facades, windows and additions, and that a lock is too small to notice. For ordinary hardware repair that is broadly right: the NoHo BID's guidance notes that repairing or replacing hardware such as knobs, hinges and handles does not need LPC approval.

But the Chelsea, Chelsea Extension and West Chelsea historic districts protect the exterior of every building in them, and something new and visible on a historic door is exactly what the rules exist to review. Damage to the door itself is worse: replacing a historic door requires showing it was beyond repair.

An inside-only lock keeps the question small. A visible keypad may be approvable at staff level; it should be asked about first, not after.

“Smart locks are a home product”

Smart lock marketing shows suburban front doors and family phones, and most reviews test locks on houses. The impression is that businesses need a separate, much larger “access control system.”

In Chelsea, a large share of the doors that need keyless entry are galleries, studios and small offices with a handful of staff. Some of them suit a commercial-grade smart lock with an app and a log; others need an electric strike and a reader on a glass door. The line between “smart lock” and “access control” is the door and the number of people, not the brand.

We price both from the same published figures and recommend the smallest system that does the job.

“When the power goes out, an electric lock lets everyone in”

This one is half true, which is why it spreads. Magnetic locks really are fail-safe only — with no power there is no magnetic bond — and some electrified locks are ordered fail-safe on purpose.

Electric strikes and most electrified locksets can be fail-secure: locked on the outside when the power is gone, while the lever inside still lets people out. iDigHardware notes that fail-secure products are more common than fail-safe for exactly this reason, and that strikes on fire doors must be fail-secure.

A gallery that loses power during a storm should stay locked to the street and open to the people inside. That is a hardware choice made on the day of installation.

“Glass doors cannot take keyless entry without being replaced”

Frameless glass looks as if it has nowhere to put a lock, and some advice suggests replacing the entrance with a framed door. For a storefront in a historic district that would be a much bigger job than anyone wants.

Electric bolt locks for glass are made to hide in a top rail or a floor box or to clamp to the glass in a patch, and magnetic locks are made with brackets that wrap the top of a glass door. Readers can often sit behind the glass and read through it.

The glass stays; the question is which hardware suits its swing, its fittings and the street.

“A keyed elevator is all the security a loft needs”

Brick Underground quotes an agent who grew up in a loft with a keyed elevator saying that a key-locked apartment is as safe as, or even safer than, having a doorman. In a well-run small building with careful key control, that can be true.

The same article notes that an apartment's overall security depends on the building's entire system — entrance, intercom, elevator controls, locks and cameras. Keys are lent to cleaners, contractors and guests; some buildings rely on residents turning the key back to the locked position.

A second door with a smart lock between the landing and the apartment, where there is one, closes the gap without touching the elevator.

“Indoor electronics are safe anywhere inside the building”

Installation guides tell you to keep controllers and power supplies indoors, out of the weather, and leave it there. Indoors is the usual answer, and under a desk or in a low cupboard is the usual place.

West of Tenth Avenue, indoors is not enough. After Sandy, Artlyst reported three to five feet of water inside Chelsea galleries, with one gallery posting that the neighbourhood was flooded up to Tenth Avenue.

On those blocks the controller, power supply and battery go high on the wall, and the door hardware is chosen to fail locked and be easy to replace.

Flood-Aware Installs

Above the Waterline: Lock Electronics on West Chelsea's Low Blocks

Sandy put three to five feet of water into Chelsea galleries in 2012, according to Artlyst's report at the time. Nobody can promise a door will stay dry, but where the lock's electronics live decides whether a flood costs a new door contact or a whole new system. These are the rules we follow on the ground floors west of Tenth Avenue.

  • Controllers and power supplies high. At or above head height on an interior wall, in a labelled enclosure, never on the floor, under a desk or inside a low cabinet.
  • Batteries with them. The backup battery sits in the same high enclosure, not on the floor beside it.
  • Cable from above. Runs come down to the door from the ceiling, so the only low parts are the ones that must be at the door.
  • Cheap, sealed parts at the bottom. Door contacts and exit buttons are simple, sealed and inexpensive to replace if water reaches them.
  • Locks that fail locked. Fail-secure strikes and a key override, so a power cut during a storm leaves the door latched and the gallery closed to the street.
  • A storm sheet in the enclosure. What to switch off when a warning is issued, what to check before power returns, and the number to call.
  • Admin access off site. At least two administrators on two phones, so the door can be managed from wherever the staff are if the building is closed.

The same thinking applies to a loft's bridge or a rowhouse's border router on a lower ground floor: keep it off the floor, on a permanent outlet, and somewhere a person can reach without wading.

Glossary

Chelsea Door and Building Terms You Will Hear on a Lock Job

  • Historic district: an area designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission; in Chelsea, the Chelsea Historic District (1970), its extension (1981) and the West Chelsea Historic District (2008). Exterior work generally needs LPC permission.
  • Permit for Minor Work, Certificate of No Effect, Certificate of Appropriateness: the three kinds of LPC permit. The first two are issued by staff; the third goes to the full Commission.
  • Condition assessment: the written evaluation the LPC's rules call for before a historic door may be replaced as deteriorated beyond reasonable repair.
  • Entrance enframement: the stone or brownstone surround of a rowhouse doorway — on Cushman Row, a deep, temple-like frame that puts the door well back from the stoop.
  • Stile and bore: the vertical edge member of a door that holds the lock, and the round hole drilled through it for a deadbolt or knob.
  • Proprietary lease: the document that governs a co-op shareholder's apartment, including what may be changed on its door.
  • Limited-equity co-op: a co-op, like Penn South, where shares are resold without profit to keep apartments affordable, under city supervision.
  • Keyed or key-locked elevator: an elevator that will not move to a floor, or open into an apartment, without a key or a resident's release.
  • Freight landing: the lobby or corridor outside a freight elevator in a converted industrial building, often the only way into a loft or studio.
  • Fail-safe and fail-secure: whether electrified hardware is unlocked (fail-safe) or locked (fail-secure) on the outside when power is removed.
  • Passage mode: a lock or strike setting that leaves the door free to open from outside for a set period, such as a gallery opening.
  • Request-to-exit (REX): the push button, motion sensor or switch built into a lever or bar that signals a powered lock to release for a person walking out.
  • FISP: the city's Facade Inspection and Safety Program, which brings scaffolds and sidewalk sheds to taller buildings every five years.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Chelsea Door Before Choosing a Lock

The habits our technicians bring to Chelsea doors in general; this describes method, not a particular client's door

We look at the door before we look at the lock. On a rowhouse, that means standing at the bottom of the stoop first, because that is where anyone judging the facade will stand. Then up close: the stile, the old bores, the filler from locks long gone, the way the door meets the frame. Old doors move with the seasons, and a deadbolt that throws easily in October can bind in a humid August.

On a loft landing, the question is what the landing is. If the elevator is the front door, we talk about the second door, the stair door and the intercom, and we leave the elevator to the elevator company. Steel doors get a magnet test for the frame and a look at the hinge edge for a label before any hardware is chosen.

At a gallery, we ask for the calendar before the floor plan: when it is closed, when the next opening is, when the art moves. Then we look at the glass — framed or frameless, which way it swings, whether there are patch fittings — and at where the electronics can live above the floor.

Everywhere, we test the radio at the door with a phone before choosing how the lock connects, and we leave a one-page sheet that says who holds which credential. The lock is the easy part; the door and the people are what take the thought.

FAQ

Chelsea Smart Lock Installation FAQ

What does a keyless gallery or studio door with an electric strike cost in Chelsea?
+

A gallery or studio entrance with a keypad or fob reader and an electric strike is $1,875 to $3,125 installed, the same published figure we use across Manhattan. Swapping in a strike alone runs $313 to $563, and fixing a strike that has failed runs $369 to $606. Rowhouse and apartment doors have no set figure; they are priced flat once the free door survey is done.

Do you cover all of Chelsea, from 14th Street to the edge of Hudson Yards and from Sixth Avenue to the river?
+

Yes. We work the whole neighbourhood: the historic-district side streets, the avenues, London Terrace and Penn South, the West Chelsea gallery blocks and the condos beside the High Line, as well as the Meatpacking District, Flatiron, NoMad and Hell’s Kitchen next door. Prices are the same as elsewhere in Manhattan.

Can the installation happen on a Monday, when most Chelsea galleries are closed?
+

Yes. Galleries usually open Tuesday to Saturday, so Monday is often the best day to fit a gallery door, and we can also start early on an open day and finish before the doors open. We confirm the date with the gallery manager and with the building.

How long does the door survey on a landmarked rowhouse take?
+

Usually under an hour at the door. We photograph both faces and the entrance from the street, measure the bores, thickness and backset, check how the door hangs and test the signal; you receive the photographs and a one-page summary afterwards, whether or not you go ahead.

Can the gallery be alerted if the loading door is left propped open after a delivery?
+

Yes, if the door has a door-position contact. The controller or lock can send an alert when the door stays open longer than a set time and record who opened it last. We fit the contact where deliveries will not knock it off and set the delay with you.

Can one visit cover both our Chelsea apartment and our studio a few blocks away?
+

Often, yes. If both doors are ready and both buildings allow it, we book them back to back on the same day. Each door is still priced on its own, and the two sets of credentials can sit in one app or in separate accounts, as you prefer.

Can the wiring left from an old buzzer or strike on our loft door be reused for the new lock?
+

Sometimes. We test the old cable and trace where it runs; if it is sound and suits the voltage and current of the new hardware, we reuse it, and if not, we replace it along the same route where possible. You know which before the price is final.

Can you work in the gallery while a show is on the walls without putting the art at risk?
+

Yes, with the gallery’s agreement on the details. We work from the door side, keep ladders and cable runs away from the works, use dust extraction at the drill and cover the floor, and the gallery decides whether anything near the entrance should be moved or covered first.
