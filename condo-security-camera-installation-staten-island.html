The Borough

Why a Staten Island Condo Camera Plan Reads Like a Site Plan

Ask a Manhattan board where its cameras go and the answer is a list of rooms: lobby, mailroom, elevator, garage. Ask the same question at a Staten Island condominium and the answer is usually a walk outdoors. The front entrance off the main road, the private lane that loops past forty or sixty front doors, the parking courts and carports behind them, a row of detached garages, the mailbox pad, the pool and the clubhouse, and a stretch of woods or wetland along the back line. That geography is the first thing that sets condo security camera installation on Staten Island apart from the other four boroughs, and it decides almost every choice that follows.

The city's own tax-lot data shows why. In MapPLUTO, the Department of City Planning's lot file published on NYC Open Data, Staten Island's residential condominium lots hold about 11,460 homes, and roughly 63 percent of them sit in lots the Department of Finance classes R3, defined as a "Condo; Residential Unit In 1-3 Story Bldg." Elevator condominiums (class R4) and walk-up condominiums (class R2) together account for about 2,400 units. Nacmias Law, a Staten Island real estate firm, describes the pattern plainly: the borough holds more low-rise condominium units than any other, nearly all of them in planned attached townhouse communities, with one declaration covering an entire community and the association often owning the roads, parking, drainage and grounds.

Age matters as much as shape. Of the borough's condominium lots that carry a construction year in PLUTO, the largest group, 63 lots, dates from the 1980s, with only a dozen from the 1970s and seven from the 1990s, before a second wave of mostly small two- to six-unit buildings from 2004 on and a handful of larger elevator buildings since 2014. So a typical Staten Island condo board is not dealing with a sponsor's camera system installed last year. It is dealing with a forty-year-old community whose original low-voltage wiring, if any, was never designed for cameras, and whose first cameras were often added piecemeal by a past board, a single director or individual owners.

Ownership is the other constant. New York's Condominium Act treats yards, gardens, parking areas, recreational and community facilities, storage spaces and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" as common elements (Real Property Law §339-e), and many Staten Island communities add a homeowners association layer above the condominium; the Attorney General's offering-plan rules for HOAs, 13 NYCRR §22.3(f), require a plan to describe common areas including land, parking facilities and recreational facilities. Before we can draw a camera plan we need to know which body owns which ground, because a camera on the clubhouse may belong to the HOA while a camera on a building corner belongs to the condominium. Rental buildings with a single owner follow different rules; they are covered on our page about apartment building security camera installation on Staten Island.

Staten Island condo facts that shape the camera plan

  • About 11,460 residential condominium units sit on 216 residential condo lots in the borough (MapPLUTO, NYC Open Data).
  • About 7,230 of them, close to two in three, sit on lots the city classes R3: condo homes in buildings of one to three stories.
  • ZIP code 10314 (Bulls Head, New Springville, Willowbrook, Travis and nearby) holds about 4,950 of them, around 43 percent of the borough total (MapPLUTO).
  • The 1980s produced more of today's condominium lots than any other decade (63 dated lots in PLUTO).
  • With 495,747 people counted in 2020 across roughly 58.5 square miles, Staten Island has the lowest population density of the five boroughs (Wikipedia, citing the Census Bureau).
  • Hurricane Sandy left more than 11,700 buildings in inundated areas of the East and South Shores, and 23 storm-related deaths on the Island (NYC Special Initiative for Rebuilding and Resiliency).
Building Types

Five Staten Island Condo Layouts and the Cameras Each One Needs

We group Staten Island condominiums into five layouts when we plan cameras. The labels are ours, drawn from our own walk-throughs and the PLUTO data above, not official categories, and plenty of communities blend two of them.

Townhouse community with a private road

The classic South Shore and Mid-Island condo: rows of two- and three-story attached homes along a looped private lane, each with its own front door and often a garage, sometimes behind a gate. The camera plan covers the entrance, the lane intersections, guest parking and any shared amenities rather than individual homes.

Garden complex with parking courts

Two- and three-story buildings with shared entry halls set around open lots, common around Bulls Head, New Springville and Willowbrook. Cameras watch building entries, each parking court and the dumpster corral, and the recorder usually lives in a utility room in one building with links out to the others.

North Shore elevator condo

Newer and older elevator buildings around St. George, Tompkinsville and Stapleton, a few on steep streets with the garage entrance a level or two below the lobby. Coverage looks more like a city building: lobby, mail area, elevator cab, garage and stair doors.

Small newer condo, two to six units

Most condominium lots built since 2004 are small: a two-family-style building or a short row divided into condo units on a former house lot. A compact four- to six-camera system with a small recorder often covers the whole property, which is where the $999 starting system becomes most of the project.

Mixed-use condo on a commercial street

Shops at street level with condo homes above, along corridors such as Bay Street, Victory Boulevard, Forest Avenue and Richmond Avenue. The declaration's line between commercial and residential common elements decides which entrances and which cameras the residential board controls.

Commercial condo

Office, medical and light-industrial condo buildings, many along the arterial roads and the West Shore. Each unit owner usually runs its own interior cameras; the condominium's system covers the shared lot, loading area, lobby and building perimeter.

The difference between these layouts shows up in three places on the proposal. First, distance: a garden complex may need cable runs of several hundred feet between buildings, which changes whether a camera connects by copper network cable, by fiber or by a wireless bridge. Second, power: a townhouse community's entrance sign or mailbox pad may sit far from any building with an outlet. Third, who watches: an elevator building with a superintendent has someone on site, while a 1980s townhouse community with a volunteer board may have nobody looking at footage until an owner reports a problem.

That last point is why we design Staten Island systems to be reviewed quickly after the fact, not monitored live. A board treasurer who gets a call about a damaged car on a Saturday morning should be able to open the right camera, jump to the right hour and save a clip in a few minutes from a phone, without knowing anything about recorders. Camera names that match the community's own map ("Lane B at Court 3" rather than "Camera 14"), a clean list of accounts and written export steps matter more here than another megapixel.

Entrances

The Entrance Road, the Gate and the Community Sign

In a townhouse community the entrance is the one place every car and most people pass. If a board can afford only a handful of cameras, at least two of them usually belong there. What those two cameras should do is different from what most people expect, so it is worth separating the jobs.

Overview and detail are separate cameras

An overview camera shows the whole entrance: both lanes, the sign, the sidewalk and the first stretch of road. It answers "what happened and when," such as a car clipping the sign or someone walking in on foot at two in the morning. A detail camera is aimed tight on the inbound lane at a point where cars slow down, framed to show a vehicle's front and, under good light, its plate and the driver's side window. It answers "which car." One camera rarely does both well, because a wide lens spreads its pixels across the scene and a tight lens sees only a slice of it. Pairing them is cheaper than buying one expensive camera and hoping.

Where the cameras mount

Entrances on Staten Island rarely have a building close enough to hold a camera at the right height and angle. The usual answers are the gatehouse or guard booth if there is one, the entrance sign structure if it is solid masonry, a light pole the association owns, or a new pole set on association land. Each has trade-offs: a light pole may already carry power but can sway in wind, a sign may be too low and too easy to reach, and a new pole needs a footing and a buried conduit back to the nearest building. We mark the options on the site plan with heights and distances so the board chooses with the costs in front of it.

Gates and gate arms

Communities with a vehicle gate get the most out of a camera aimed at the gate from the inside, looking out, so it records every car that enters behind another one and every arm that gets pushed up by hand. Gate operators, keypads, call boxes and fob readers are a separate system; we cover how they work for condominiums on our Staten Island condo security systems page, and the camera plan simply makes sure the cameras see what those devices do.

Two practical notes apply to every Staten Island entrance. Snowplows and salt are hard on anything low and close to the curb, so camera poles stand back from the plow line and hardware is stainless or coated. And because the entrance is often the farthest point from the recorder, the link back to it, whether buried cable or a wireless bridge, is planned first, before anyone picks camera models.

Parking

Parking Courts, Carports and Rows of Detached Garages

Cars are what Staten Island condo boards call us about most often after packages. Staten Island is the only borough the subway does not reach, and townhouse and garden communities were built around parking: courts behind each row, carports along the lanes, guest spaces at the ends and, in many communities, separate garage buildings. Local reporting gives a sense of the problem. CBS New York reported in July 2022 that Staten Island had seen more than 250 catalytic converter thefts that year to date, at a police etching event in Graniteville that drew about 200 people. In September 2022, SILive reported that for more than two years police had been urging residents to lock their cars amid a surge in car break-ins and grand larceny. Those figures cover the whole borough, not condominiums alone, but parking courts are where a condo board feels them.

What a parking camera can realistically show

A camera mounted high on a building corner and aimed down a long parking court records movement across thirty or forty spaces. At night, at that distance, it will show a figure walking between cars and a car arriving or leaving, but not a face and rarely a plate. That is still useful: it fixes the time of an incident, shows which direction someone came from and which car they left in, and points the reviewer to the camera that might have a closer view. Boards get better results by adding a second, tighter camera at the court's only drive-in point, where every car must pass within a few yards of the lens, than by buying a higher-resolution camera for the overview.

Under-car theft and low angles

Catalytic converter theft happens low and fast, often under a car parked nose-in against a building. A camera on the second-floor eave looking straight down sees roofs. For courts that have had this problem we angle at least one camera lower and along the row, from the end of the court, so the sides of the cars and the space beneath them fall in frame. Lighting decides the rest; a court lit only by porch lights will record mostly darkness, and full-color cameras with their own low light, or the association adding a fixture, make a larger difference than any setting.

Carports and detached garage rows

Carport roofs give good, protected mounting points and short cable runs, and domes under a carport ceiling are hard to reach or knock out of line. Detached garage rows are a different case. Each garage interior belongs to an owner, or is a limited common element assigned to one, so the camera covers the apron and the lane in front of the doors, never inside an open garage. The garage building usually has no network connection of its own, which means the link back to the recorder is the main design question: a buried conduit if the association will allow digging, a fiber run along an existing utility path, or a point-to-point wireless bridge from the garage roofline to the building that houses the recorder.

Mail & Packages

Mailbox Pads, Stoops and Parcel Lockers in Townhouse Communities

A city condo has a package room. Most Staten Island townhouse communities do not. Parcels land on dozens of separate stoops and porches, and letter mail goes to a cluster mailbox unit on a concrete pad near the entrance or at a lane intersection. That changes what a condo camera system can and cannot do about package theft.

SILive's August 2025 report from Elm Park is a useful reminder. A homeowner there had a porch theft recorded on her own camera, and she found out two days later because of a notification problem; the same report noted that more Staten Islanders are turning to locked options for receiving packages, and quoted another household member: "It's unfortunate that people are having to set up lock boxes because of people (who steal)." That was a private house, not a condominium, but it captures the two lessons we apply in condo communities. A camera that records a theft nobody reviews in time is only half a system. And the most effective fix for porch theft is often a place to put packages, with cameras watching that place.

Why the association's cameras do not watch every stoop

A community system that tried to cover sixty front doors would need sixty cameras, or a few wide views that show every doorway too small to identify anyone. It would also put the association's lenses in front of owners' homes, which is where privacy objections start. Front doors and stoops are usually part of the unit or a limited common element serving it, and most boards leave the doorstep to the owner's own doorbell camera. The community system watches the shared ground that a thief has to cross: the lane, the entrance and the parking court. A van that stops at four stoops in ten minutes is visible on a lane camera even if no single doorway is.

The mailbox pad

The cluster mailbox is the one shared object every owner uses daily, and forced or pried mailbox doors are a recurring complaint. A single camera on a nearby pole or building wall, aimed so it sees the front face of the unit and the person standing at it, covers it well. We frame the camera so it shows who is at the boxes and when, without being able to read the contents of an open mailbox or an envelope in someone's hand. If the pad is too far from power and network, a powered pole with a short buried run, or a solar-assisted camera where wiring is impractical, can work, and we price both.

Parcel lockers and package sheds

Some communities add a parcel locker bank or a small package shed by the clubhouse or the mail pad. If yours is planning one, tell us early: a camera aimed at the locker doors from the side, plus one on the shed entrance, is cheaper to include when the locker goes in than to add after. The camera does not need to see a locker's screen or code entry, only who opened which door and when.

Amenities

Pools, Clubhouses, Courts and Playgrounds

Many Staten Island townhouse communities share a pool, a clubhouse or party room, tennis or basketball courts and a playground. These are the places owners care about most and the places where cameras need the most restraint, because people use them in swimsuits, with children and at private events.

The pool

Pool cameras earn their keep on the gate, the fence line and the equipment room door. Most pool trouble in a community happens after hours: people climbing the fence at night, a gate propped open, chairs thrown in the water, a break-in at the pump room. Cameras aimed at the entry gate and along the fence, plus one covering the deck from a high corner, record those events without zooming in on bathers. We do not put cameras in changing rooms, showers or restrooms at any pool house; New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law §250.45, makes recording in a bathroom, changing room or shower without consent a felony, and no condo purpose changes that.

The clubhouse

A clubhouse usually has an entrance, a main room, a kitchen and restrooms, and sometimes a fitness room or office. The entrance camera is uncontroversial; whether the main room records during private rentals is a board choice we answer in the questions below. Restrooms are never on camera.

Courts and playgrounds

Tennis courts, basketball courts and playgrounds get cameras for vandalism and after-hours use, typically one camera from a building or light pole that takes in the whole space at moderate resolution. Playgrounds are a place to be deliberate. Recording children at play is lawful in a common area, but footage of minors should be locked down in the policy: reviewed only when there is an incident, never exported for anything except that incident, and never posted. We set the playground camera to a wider, lower-detail view and recommend the board say so in its owner notice.

Walking paths and back lines

Communities that border woods, wetlands or parkland, which on Staten Island means a lot of them, often have informal paths in from the back line. A camera at the point where a path meets the community's lane shows foot traffic in and out. Cameras pointing into the woods themselves mostly record branches moving and animals, and the false alerts that come with them, which we address under conditions below.

Elevator Buildings

Lobbies, Elevators and Garages in North Shore Condo Buildings

The North Shore is where Staten Island condominiums look most like the rest of the city. St. George, Tompkinsville and Stapleton have older elevator buildings from the 1930s and 1960s, a few newer towers, and the waterfront development that followed the Bay Street Corridor rezoning, which the City Council approved on June 26, 2019, covering about 20 blocks and roughly 1,800 planned homes according to City Limits. Commercial Observer's January 2026 survey of Staten Island multifamily development described the borough's zoning as a brake on apartment construction for decades; one attorney it quoted put it bluntly, saying "the zoning on Staten Island makes it virtually impossible to build multifamily housing." The result is that North Shore elevator condos are few, varied, and often on unusual sites.

Hills and split levels

The streets that climb from the ferry terminal toward Grymes Hill and Stapleton Heights put some buildings on slopes steep enough that the garage opens onto one street and the lobby onto another, a full story or two higher. Camera coverage has to follow both entrances, and the recorder location has to make sense for both. We usually place it on the lobby level or above, run a riser cable down to the garage, and label views by street so whoever reviews footage knows which door is which.

Lobby and mail area

Lobby cameras in a North Shore building watch the street door from inside at face height, the mailboxes and the elevator doors. Many of these lobbies face the harbor or a bright street through glass, so we choose cameras with wide dynamic range and mount them to look across the glass rather than straight into it. Where the building has a package shelf or room, it gets its own camera aimed at the shelves, not just the door.

Elevator cabs

An elevator camera is a common request and a common source of confusion about who does what. We install the camera in the cab and the wireless link that carries its video from the cab to the building network, which avoids touching the elevator's traveling cable. Anything inside the elevator controller, machine room equipment or the car's own wiring belongs to the building's elevator contractor, and we coordinate the visit with that contractor rather than work around them.

Garages, stairs and the roof

Garage cameras follow the same logic as parking courts: one camera inside the overhead door looking in, so every car is seen after it clears the door, then views along the drive aisles and at each stair and elevator vestibule. Stair doors to the roof and the cellar, the trash room and the service entrance are the back-of-house doors boards most often leave uncovered, and they are where a building's propped-door problems show up first.

Mixed-use buildings along Bay Street, Victory Boulevard and Forest Avenue add one more layer. The stores below often run their own camera systems for their own spaces. The condo's system covers the residential entrance, the residential halls and whatever the declaration makes common, and we keep it on a separate recorder unless the board and the commercial owners agree in writing to share one.

Equipment

Condo Security Camera Systems Built for Spread-Out Staten Island Sites

The hardware in a Staten Island condo system is the same family of equipment we use everywhere: cameras, a recorder, a network switch that powers the cameras, storage, and the software owners and agents use to view it. What changes is the proportion. A tower spends its budget on many indoor cameras over short runs. A townhouse community spends a bigger share on fewer outdoor cameras, the links that reach them and the hardware that survives the weather.

Cameras

Turret cameras, with a ball-shaped lens in a short housing, are our default for eaves, carports and building corners, because they shrug off water and have no glass dome to fog or scratch. Domes suit covered spots that people can reach, such as clubhouse ceilings and carports, where a vandal-resistant housing matters. Bullet cameras, longer and narrower, are easy to aim down a long lane or parking court and give a visible "this area is recorded" signal. Pan-tilt-zoom cameras earn a place only where someone will actually steer them, such as a superintendent's desk overlooking a large lot; on a volunteer-run community they mostly sit in one position. Full-color low-light cameras help where the court has some lighting, and models with a built-in white light can add both color and deterrence at a dark entrance.

Recorder and storage

A network video recorder in a locked utility closet, office or clubhouse room records every camera continuously or on events, depending on the policy. FirstService Residential, a large condo management firm, says condo and co-op footage is generally kept 30 to 90 days depending on the building; we size the recorder's drives to whatever retention the board picks at the camera count it plans to reach, not just the first phase. The recorder sits on a battery backup so short outages do not erase the minutes that matter.

Reaching distant cameras

Standard network cable carrying both power and video runs about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, between the switch and a camera. In a city building that is rarely a limit. On a Staten Island site it often is. When a camera sits beyond that, we choose between a small outdoor-rated switch at a midpoint, fiber optic cable between buildings with media converters at each end, or a point-to-point wireless bridge with a clear line of sight between rooftops. Each is listed separately in the proposal with what it costs and what could go wrong, such as a tree that will leaf out in May across a wireless path.

Privacy & the Law

Condo Cameras and New York Privacy Rules: What Staten Island Boards Should Know

Boards ask us about the law on almost every walk-through. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice; a board with a specific question should ask its counsel. What we can do is point to the published rules that shape where cameras go, and tell you how we design around them.

The board's authority comes from the declaration and bylaws

New York's Condominium Act requires bylaws to cover matters such as the board's powers and the care of the common elements (Real Property Law §339-v). Camera projects are normally a common-element decision made by the board under those documents. Where a Staten Island community also has a homeowners association, the HOA's own documents govern the land and facilities it owns. Habitat magazine, writing on condo surveillance in August 2023, quoted attorney Andrew Lieb on the limits of board rules: "A house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable." So the first question is not whether cameras are allowed in general but which body's documents authorize them on that piece of ground.

Video, yes; audio, no

New York's eavesdropping statute, Penal Law §250.05, makes "mechanical overhearing of a conversation" (defined in §250.00) without the consent of at least one party a felony. Many cameras ship with microphones turned on. We disable audio recording on every common-area camera we install, including the pool deck and the clubhouse, and we note that in the handover so a future installer does not quietly switch it back.

Places cameras never go

Penal Law §250.45 lists bedrooms, changing rooms, fitting rooms, restrooms, toilets, bathrooms, washrooms and showers as places where recording a person without consent is unlawful surveillance. FirstService Residential's guidance for condo and co-op boards adds that New York's Labor Law §203-c bars video recording of employees in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas. In a townhouse community that means pool house changing rooms and restrooms, clubhouse restrooms and any staff changing area in a maintenance building are off the plan entirely.

Homes are off limits

A lawyer answering a reader's question in CooperatorNews in 2025 noted that the law gives the inside of a neighbor's unit considerable privacy protection, and that a camera which "peers into a neighboring unit" can interfere with that. Townhouse communities make this easy to get wrong, because front windows, sliding doors and back decks face the same lanes and lawns the cameras watch. We aim every view at shared ground, test angles from inside the frame at night when lit windows stand out, and black out any window that remains in view using the recorder's privacy masking, which removes that part of the picture from live view and recordings alike.

Owner-installed cameras

The same CooperatorNews column pointed out that condominiums typically bar owners from altering common elements on their own and that board consent is advisable before an owner mounts a camera on them. On Staten Island the line is often blurred: an owner's front wall, stoop and patio may be part of the unit, a limited common element or a common element depending on the declaration. Boards that adopt a short rule on owner doorbell and porch cameras, saying where they may point and what they may not record, avoid most neighbor disputes. We do not install a camera on a common element for an individual owner without written board approval.

Smart access and face identification

New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021 regulates smart access systems, including biometric entry, in certain residential buildings. Whether and how it applies to a particular condominium is a question for the board's counsel. Separately, we do not set up face identification on condo common-area cameras unless a board asks for it after legal review; person and vehicle detection, which only tell the recorder that a human or a car is in the frame, are different and are what most boards actually want.

Who may install

Footage Policy

Who Sees the Video: A Footage Policy for a Staten Island Condominium

The camera plan decides what gets recorded. The footage policy decides what happens next, and in a volunteer-run community it matters more than the hardware. Without one, recordings end up on a board member's personal phone, clips get forwarded around a group chat, and the board has no answer when an owner asks why a neighbor saw a video of them. FirstService Residential recommends that boards keep a log of footage requests and a list of everyone who has accessed footage, and that residents not receive footage or copies without approval from the board and legal counsel. We give every board a short template that covers the same ground, for its counsel to adapt.

Six decisions a Staten Island policy should settle

  • Who has live access. Usually the managing agent and one or two officers; on self-managed communities, a named review officer and a backup.
  • Who may export a clip. Exporting should be rarer than viewing, logged every time and limited to the reviewer and the agent.
  • How owners ask. A written request naming the date, time window and reason, answered within a set number of days, with the board deciding what is released and to whom.
  • Police and insurers. Who hands over a copy, in what format, and how the request is recorded.
  • Retention. How many days the recorder keeps before overwriting, and how an incident clip is preserved beyond that.
  • Seasonal and outside accounts. When a pool company, landscaper or security patrol gets access, which cameras it sees, and the date it ends.

Two governance layers, one policy

Where a master HOA owns the entrance, pool and clubhouse and a condominium association owns the buildings and parking, both bodies' cameras may share one recorder for cost reasons. The policy then needs to say which board controls which cameras and who reviews footage of shared areas. We can set the recorder up so each body's officers see only their own cameras, which keeps the system on one box without blurring responsibility.

Telling owners and renters

Signs at the entrance and the amenities, plus a notice to owners with a simple map of what each camera covers, head off most complaints before they start. Many Staten Island condo homes are rented, and renters live with the cameras too; the notice should go to them through the owners, and the policy should say renters can ask for footage the same way owners do.

Conditions

Storms, Flood Lines, Salt, Snow and Wildlife

Camera spec sheets assume mild conditions. Staten Island condominiums sit on a coastline, in woods and on hills that test equipment in ways a city interior never does. Most of these problems are cheap to plan for on the walk-through and expensive to discover after the first storm.

Where the recorder lives in a flood area

The NYC Building Code's flood-resistant construction appendix (Appendix G) requires mechanical equipment and outside appliances in flood hazard areas to be elevated to or above the design flood elevation, which for many structures is the base flood elevation plus two feet. A camera recorder is not named in that rule, but the logic carries over: if the clubhouse office or a ground-floor utility room has taken water before, the recorder, switch and battery backup go higher, on an upper floor or a raised shelf, with cables brought up from below. A recorder that drowns takes the recordings of the storm, and everything before it, with it.

Wind-driven rain and storm mounts

Nor'easters and tropical storms drive rain sideways into connections that stay dry in a normal shower. We seal every outdoor cable entry and keep connectors inside junction boxes rather than taped to the wall, and we mount cameras under eaves or overhangs where the building allows. Short, rigid mounts and properly anchored poles resist the vibration that blurs night video in gusts.

Salt air near the water

Within a few blocks of the beach on the East and South Shores, salt spray corrodes plain steel brackets and screws and fogs lenses. We use stainless or coated hardware and housings rated for outdoor exposure, and we put lens cleaning on the maintenance list more often than for inland communities.

Snow, plows and ice

Associations that plow their own lanes push snow onto the same curbs and islands where entrance and mailbox poles stand. Poles go behind the plow line, low-mounted equipment gets a protective post or none at all, and cameras are mounted high enough that ice sliding off a roof will not knock them out of line.

Trees and leaf-out

Wooded edges block views and wireless paths once leaves grow in. We check sightlines with summer canopy in mind, not just the bare branches of a winter walk.

Power at the far end

Cameras on the PoE switch run off the battery backup with the recorder. A remote pole that needs its own outlet gets one from the community's electrician; we do not do electrical work.

Reality Check

Seven Things Staten Island Condo Boards Hear About Cameras, Checked

National cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, store displays and well-meaning neighbors give boards plenty of confident advice. Much of it is written for a single house. Here is how seven common claims hold up in a Staten Island condominium.

"Every owner already has a doorbell camera, so the community is covered"

Doorbell cameras watch doorsteps. They do not watch the entrance, the lanes, the parking courts, the mailbox pad or the pool, which is where most community incidents happen. They also store video in dozens of separate personal accounts, so after an incident the board has to ask owners one by one for clips, some of whom will not have kept them.

Owner cameras and a community system complement each other. The community system covers shared ground under one recorder the board controls; owners keep their own doorstep cameras under whatever rule the board adopts about where they may point.

"Wireless cameras mean no wiring"

Most "wireless" cameras still need power, so they need either a wire to an outlet or a battery that someone has to recharge. Battery models also record only on motion and wake up a second or two late, which can miss the event the board most wants to see.

Wi-Fi is also the weak link across a Staten Island site. Signal from a clubhouse router fades long before it reaches the far parking court, and every leaf, wall and car in between weakens it further. A wired camera on PoE, or a dedicated wireless bridge built for long outdoor links, is far more reliable.

"One 4K camera can watch the whole parking lot"

Resolution is spread across the whole frame. A 4K camera aimed at a hundred-foot parking court gives each car a modest number of pixels, and at night, with less light, the usable detail falls further. It will show that something happened; it usually will not show who.

Two ordinary cameras, one wide and one tight on the court's only entry point, beat one premium camera trying to do both jobs. Lighting beats both: an unlit court records shapes no matter what camera watches it.

"Solar cameras are free to run and need no installer"

Solar cameras are useful at a mailbox pad or a far gate where running power is impractical. But they record on events rather than continuously, they depend on enough winter sun reaching the panel, and many send video to a cloud subscription rather than to the community's recorder.

On a shaded, north-facing or tree-lined Staten Island lot, a panel that works in July may struggle in January. We check panel placement against winter sun angles and the tree canopy before recommending one.

"Cameras at the gate will stop the break-ins"

Cameras deter some people and record the rest. They do not stop a determined thief, and footage of a hooded figure at night does not guarantee an arrest. What cameras reliably do is establish what happened, when and in which direction, which helps police, insurers and the board.

Deterrence improves with visibility: a camera people can see, a sign at the entrance and good lighting. Some cameras add a white light or a siren on detection, which some communities find useful at a pool fence after hours and others find too intrusive for a residential lane.

"Cloud storage is cheaper than a recorder"

Cloud plans look cheap per camera per month, but a community system has many cameras and runs for years. Multiply the subscription by the camera count and by five or ten years, and a recorder with local drives usually costs less. Cloud upload also depends on the community's internet upload speed, which many clubhouse connections cannot spare for a dozen cameras.

Local recording keeps the footage on equipment the association owns, under its own accounts, and it keeps recording when the internet goes down. Remote viewing still works when the connection is up.

"Any handyman can mount cameras"

New York State requires security system installers to be licensed by the Department of State, and its regulations at 19 NYCRR Part 195 cover video surveillance used to detect or monitor intrusion. An association that hires an unlicensed installer takes on the risk if something goes wrong.

Beyond licensing, the hard parts of a community system are design decisions a handyman is not set up to make: links between buildings, recorder sizing, network separation, privacy masking and account control.

Questions We Hear

Questions Staten Island Condo Boards, HOAs and Owners Ask Us

Our entrance sign and mailbox pad are hundreds of feet from any building with power. How do cameras get out there?

There are three workable routes, and the walk-through decides which. If the association will allow a trench, a conduit carrying network cable or fiber from the nearest building gives the most reliable result; fiber is needed beyond about 100 meters. If digging is off the table, a point-to-point wireless bridge from a building roofline to a small pole-mounted enclosure carries the video, with power coming from an outlet the community's electrician adds at the pole. For a low-traffic spot with no power at all, a solar-assisted camera can work, recording on events. We price each option the site allows so the board can compare cost against reliability.

Deer and raccoons set off alerts all night on our wooded back lawn. Can the cameras ignore animals?

Mostly, yes. Plain motion detection reacts to anything that changes in the picture, including animals, swaying branches and headlights. Cameras and recorders with person and vehicle detection classify what moved and alert only on the categories you choose. We also draw the detection zone to exclude the tree line and set alerts for the hours the board cares about. No setting is perfect, and a large dog at night can still fool a camera now and then, but the alert volume usually drops from dozens a night to a handful a week.

Our St. George building sits on a hill, with the garage entrance a full story below the lobby on a different street. Can one recorder handle both?

Yes. One recorder can serve both levels as long as the cable between them is planned. We usually put the recorder at lobby level or above, away from any water that could enter a lower garage, and run a riser cable down to a switch serving the garage cameras. Views are named by street and level, so whoever reviews footage knows instantly which entrance they are looking at, and the garage and lobby door cameras share one timeline when a car comes in below and the driver walks up into the lobby.

We hire a pool company every summer. Can its staff see the pool cameras without getting into the rest of the system?

Yes. We create a separate account for the pool company that shows only the pool gate, fence and deck cameras, with viewing rights but no export or settings rights. The board's officer or agent switches it on at opening day and off after closing, and the account log shows when it was used. That is far safer than sharing an officer's login, which tends to stay on a lifeguard's phone long after the season ends.

Do we need a permit to put a camera pole by our community's entrance?

We place camera poles on the association's own land, inside the property line shown on the community's survey, and we keep them out of the public sidewalk and roadway. Anything in the public right-of-way would need city approval, which is why we design around it rather than into it. Whether a particular pole, footing or sign change needs approval from your own association or from any agency is a question for the board and, where needed, its counsel or engineer; we mark exact pole positions on the site plan so that review can happen before the vote.

Owners rent our clubhouse for parties. Should the inside camera keep recording during a private event?

That is a board decision, and boards go both ways. Some keep the main-room camera on to resolve damage claims after rentals; others turn it off during bookings out of respect for guests. A common compromise is a camera on the clubhouse entrance and vestibule that always records, plus a main-room camera scheduled off during reserved hours. Whatever the board chooses, the rental agreement should say so, and restrooms and changing areas are never on camera.

When a hurricane is forecast, should we take the outdoor cameras down?

No. Properly mounted outdoor cameras are built to stay up, and the storm is exactly when the board will want recordings of what happened to cars, trees and buildings. The preparation that matters is elsewhere: confirm the recorder and battery backup are above any past flood level, check that the backup's battery is healthy, and make sure someone knows how to look at the system remotely after power returns. Anything temporarily mounted, such as a camera on a seasonal tent, should come down.

Our association maintains its own storm drains and a retention area. Can a camera show us when they back up?

Yes, within limits. A camera aimed at a known problem drain, catch basin or the retention area's outlet lets the board or the superintendent check water levels from a phone during a heavy storm instead of walking out into it, and the recordings show how high water rose and how long it stood, which helps when the board talks to engineers or contractors. A camera is not a flood sensor and will not send a water-level alarm by itself; it gives you eyes on the spot.

Cars speed through our private lanes. Can a camera measure their speed?

Not in a way anyone can enforce. Some cameras offer analytics that estimate speed, but those estimates are not calibrated measurements and the association cannot issue tickets on private roads. What a lane camera can do is show which vehicles regularly drive too fast and when, which supports a letter to an owner about a guest or contractor, and shows the board where signs, speed humps or a stop bar would help.

Our letter mail comes to a cluster mailbox unit at the entrance. Can a camera watch it?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful single cameras in a townhouse community. One camera on a nearby pole or wall, framed on the front of the unit and the person standing there, records who used the boxes and when. We set it so it cannot read envelopes or the inside of an open box, set late-night alerts if the board wants them, and keep it low enough to see faces but high enough to be out of easy reach.

Our townhouses have vinyl siding. Can cameras be mounted on it without causing leaks or cracks?

Yes, with the right hardware. Screwing a camera straight onto vinyl siding cracks the panel and lets water in behind it. We use a siding mounting block that sits flat in the siding profile, fasten through it into the sheathing and framing behind, and seal every penetration. Where the board prefers no holes in the homes at all, cameras can go on soffits, fascia boards, carport structures or poles instead, which also keeps the association's equipment off walls that may belong to individual owners.

People who don't live here leave their cars in our guest spaces all day to catch the express bus. Can cameras help?

Cameras give the board a dated record of which cars use the guest spaces and for how long, which is what it needs before it acts under its own parking rules. A camera covering the guest area, with the recorder's clock kept in sync, shows a car arriving at 6:40 a.m. and leaving at 7:15 p.m. on five weekdays running. Whether to post signs, issue warnings or tow is the board's call under its documents and with its counsel's advice; the cameras supply the evidence.

Is $999 enough for a whole Staten Island condo community?
+

Usually not, and we say so up front. $999 is our Staten Island price for a four-camera house-brand system with a recorder, installed, the same figure as on our Staten Island camera price page. Should condo cameras record all the time or only when something moves?
+

For most common-area cameras we recommend continuous recording, with motion or person detection used to mark events for quick searching. Continuous recording avoids the gap of a second or two that event-only cameras can miss at the start of an incident. Solar or battery cameras at remote spots are the exception, since they record on events to save power.

What camera resolution do we need to identify a person at the entrance?
+

Resolution matters less than how close and how well lit the subject is. A 4-megapixel camera framed tightly on a doorway or a lane where people pass within a few yards will usually show a face; an 8-megapixel camera aimed at a whole parking court usually will not. We choose resolution camera by camera, matched to the distance and the lighting at each spot.

How far can a camera see across an open lawn or lot at night?
+

Built-in infrared typically reaches somewhere between about 60 and 130 feet depending on the model, but it shows shapes and movement at the far end, not detail. For useful night detail beyond that, the area needs some light, either a fixture the association adds or a camera with its own white light. We test night views on the walk-through when the schedule allows, or describe what to expect in the proposal.

Can the association mount a camera on the outside wall of an owner's townhouse?
+

Only if the declaration makes that wall a common element or the owner agrees in writing, and the board approves. On many Staten Island communities exterior walls are part of the unit or a limited common element, so we prefer soffits, carports, light poles or association poles. The proposal shows exactly which surfaces each camera mounts on so the board can confirm ownership first.

Can owners watch the entrance camera from their own phones?
+

It is possible, and some communities offer it, but it widens who sees footage. If the board wants owners to have it, we create a view-only account limited to one or two non-sensitive cameras, such as the entrance overview, with no playback or export. Most boards keep owner access to live entrance views only, or none at all, and spell that out in the footage policy.

Can you give us a rough camera plan from our site map before the walk-through?
+

Yes. Send the community's site map or a survey and we can mark likely camera positions and a planning range for the camera count, which helps the board decide whether to schedule the walk. The firm per-camera price still comes after we see the distances, mounting surfaces and cable paths in person.

Should the cameras be on the same network as the clubhouse Wi-Fi?
+

No. We put cameras on their own network segment so they can talk only to the recorder, and the recorder reaches the internet only for the remote viewing the board approves. Guests and party renters who join the clubhouse Wi-Fi never share a network with the cameras.

How do we give a clip to our insurance company after a car is damaged?
+

The officer or agent named in the footage policy exports the clip from the recorder or the app to a file, logs the request, and sends it to the insurer or saves it to a USB drive. At handover we write out those steps for your recorder and show the reviewer how to export a clip with the date and time stamp intact.

What warranty comes with a condo camera system?
+

The installed system carries the 30-day warranty listed on our website. One-year and longer warranty plans are available as paid add-ons, priced by the year, and the cameras, recorder and drives also carry their manufacturers' own warranties. If we start with the house-brand system, can we add Reolink or another partner brand later?
+

Often, for basic video. Many network cameras follow the ONVIF standard and will show live and recorded video on another maker's recorder, but features such as person detection, two-way audio and the phone app's full functions may work only within one brand. Before adding a different brand we test a sample camera on your recorder and tell the board what will and will not work.

Is it appropriate to put a camera on the community playground?
+

It is lawful in a common area and many boards do it for vandalism and after-hours use. We set the playground camera to a wide view rather than close-ups, and recommend the footage policy say playground footage is reviewed only after a reported incident, exported only for that incident and never posted or shared beyond it.
