The Neighborhood

Why Williamsburg Properties Need Access Control

Williamsburg began as the Village of Williamsburgh, incorporated on April 14, 1827, and became part of the City of Brooklyn in 1855, the year it lost the final “h,” according to Wikipedia’s history of the neighborhood. For most of the next century and a half its waterfront was industrial and its North Side, above Grand Street, was a Polish and Italian district of walk-ups and small frame buildings. The Williamsburg Bridge, opened in 1903, tied it to the Lower East Side. None of that history explains the doors we are called to now as much as two later events: the arrival of a music and bar scene in the late 1990s and 2000s, and the waterfront rezoning the City Council passed on May 11, 2005.

The bar and venue scene filled old ground floors. Wikipedia’s account names places such as the Lucky Cat, Zebulon, Monkeytown and the electroclash club Luxx; the venue now called Music Hall of Williamsburg opened at 66 North 6th Street in the spring of 2001 as Northsix and holds about 650 people; Brooklyn Bowl opened on July 7, 2009 inside the Hecla Iron Works building at 61 Wythe Avenue, an 1882 foundry that became a city landmark in 2004. Many smaller bars and restaurants took storefronts on Bedford, Metropolitan, Grand and Driggs, very often in buildings with apartments upstairs. In 2016 New York YIMBY and Bisnow reported plans to add three floors of apartments, one per floor, above the former Living Room venue at 134 Metropolitan Avenue, with a bar or restaurant staying on the ground floor. That is the Williamsburg building in miniature: a business that works until the early morning, and households that sleep above it.

The rezoning then put towers next to those businesses. It covered the North Side and Greenpoint waterfront, promised a continuous two-mile esplanade and produced high-rise condominiums such as The Edge and Northside Piers on Kent Avenue. Further south, the Domino Sugar Refinery site has been rebuilt since 2017: 325 Kent Avenue with 522 apartments, One South First with 330, the Ten Grand office tower, the converted Refinery building that reopened on September 27, 2023 with about 460,000 square feet of offices, and the two One Domino Square towers that formally opened in June 2024. The city’s own Office of Nightlife wrote in May 2026 that when new housing rises near existing venues, incoming residents may file complaints that threaten long-standing businesses. The same tension runs through entrances, service corridors and lobbies.

It shows up first as a door question. Brick Underground published the account of a woman who lived from 2006 to 2009 in a ground-floor apartment next to a Metropolitan Avenue bar: patrons sat on her stoop until 4 or 5 a.m., and the building’s shared vestibule had no lock and no doorknob at all. Residents have fought over late-night spaces in more public ways too. In 2011 Community Board 1’s public safety chair called noise from backyard drinking “the number one problem we have in the community,” and the board adopted a policy that bars seeking a license with a rooftop or backyard provide seated food service; in 2017, CBS New York reported nearly 400 residents signing a petition against a fifth-floor rooftop bar at the Pod Hotel on Metropolitan Avenue at North 3rd Street. Every one of those disputes involves a door that someone wants open and someone else wants shut.

The second door question comes after closing. On January 8, 2023 ABC7 reported six burglaries in one early morning, three of them on Kent Avenue: at one restaurant the burglars cut through the metal locks on the front door, at another they took a sledgehammer to the door, and the total taken passed $16,000. On March 13, 2026, News 12 reported that a group had broken into fifteen businesses between February 22 and March 9, nine of them in Williamsburg, with nine incidents in a six-hour window on March 4, including one at South 4th Street. A bar or restaurant is empty, and full of cash, bottles and equipment, for the hours just after its staff leave. A door that locks behind the last person out and reports what happens next is worth more in this neighborhood than almost anywhere else in Brooklyn.

So the work here starts from a different question than “fob or keypad.” Which doors belong to the residents, which belong to the business, and which are honestly shared? Once that line is drawn, the hardware follows: an inner door that only residents can open, a back-of-house door and storeroom on staff credentials with schedules matched to the closing hour, exits that stay exits, and alerts that reach the right phone at 5 a.m. If your building has both kinds of occupant, most owners who call us also pair this work with cameras on the shared entrances.

Two Shifts, One Building

The Night Trade Downstairs, Homes Upstairs: Drawing the Line Between Two Populations

Most access control assumes a building has one kind of user. A Williamsburg mixed-use building usually has at least three: the people who live upstairs, the people who work in the business on the ground floor, and the public that business invites in, sometimes several hundred of them on a busy night. Each needs a different set of doors, at different hours, and the failures we see nearly always come from a door that was meant for one group being used by another. The patron who follows a resident through the inner door looking for a restroom. The cook who props the residents’ hallway door because it is the shortest way to the trash. The roof guest who takes the stairs down instead of the elevator and ends up on the fourth floor.

The fix is rarely more hardware on the street door. It is a clear line inside the building, enforced by a door that latches every time, with the right people on each side of it. Below is how we draw that line, in roughly the order we work through it on a survey.

Start with the vestibule and the inner door

In a typical North Side walk-up with a bar or restaurant on the ground floor, the business has its own storefront entrance and the residents come in through a narrow street door into a vestibule or a long hallway beside the shop. That hallway often leads past a door into the business’s back room or kitchen and on to the stair. If the residents’ street door is the only controlled door, anyone who gets past it, by tailgating, by being buzzed in by mistake or by walking through the kitchen, reaches the stairs. We therefore treat the inner door at the foot of the stair as the residents’ real front door: an electric strike or electrified mortise lock, a reader, a closer that is strong enough to beat a draft from the street, and a door-position switch so the owner hears about it when it is left open.

The street door can then stay simpler. In some buildings it becomes a plain latching door on a key and a reader; in others, where the business is allowed to use the hallway for deliveries during the day, it is unlocked on a schedule while the inner door stays locked around the clock. The account Brick Underground published of a Metropolitan Avenue vestibule with no lock and no knob is the worst case; the common case is a vestibule with a lock that nobody trusts, so people prop it. A latching inner door gives the residents something that holds even when the outer door does not.

Give the business doors of its own, and keep it off the residents’ doors

A bar or kitchen often has a door from its back room into the building’s hallway or cellar. Sometimes that door is legitimately needed: it may be the only route to the meters, the trash area or a required second exit. When it is, we make it one-way in the sense that matters. From the business side it opens freely as an exit if the code requires it; from the hallway side it opens only for staff credentials during the business’s hours. What it must never do is give staff, or patrons who wander back, an unlocked route into the residents’ part of the building after the residents’ inner door.

Where the business has no need for the hallway, the best answer is usually to stop using that door at all and give the business a controlled service door of its own to the yard or the street. That is a conversation between the building owner and the tenant before it is a hardware decision, and we are glad to be in the room for it: a door schedule written into a lease is easier to live with than one argued about after the first noise complaint.

Match staff schedules to the closing hour, not to office hours

Off-the-shelf access software assumes a working day of nine to six. A Williamsburg bar opens in the afternoon, serves until 2 or 4 a.m. depending on its license and the night, and has staff leaving after cleanup well past that. Prep cooks and delivery drivers may arrive before noon. A schedule that ends at midnight locks out the closing bartender; a schedule that runs all night lets anyone with a code in at 6 a.m. We set staff groups around the real shifts: closers whose codes work until an hour after the latest possible close, openers whose codes start when deliveries begin, managers who can enter at any time, and a dead window in between when nobody but a manager should be inside.

State law sets the outer edge. Alcoholic Beverage Control Law §106(5) ends on-premises sales at 4 a.m. and keeps them stopped until 8 a.m. on weekdays and 10 a.m. on Sundays. A licensee’s own method of operation or community board stipulations may set earlier hours for a yard or roof. We do not interpret licenses, but we can build the door schedule from whatever hours the license and the operator give us, and change it in minutes when those hours change.

Roofs, yards and terraces that close on time

Williamsburg’s fights over late noise have centered on outdoor space. Community Board 1 adopted its backyard and rooftop policy in December 2011 after its public safety chair called backyard drinking noise the neighborhood’s first problem; the Pod Hotel roof petition in 2017 was about a fifth-floor bar. An access system cannot make guests quieter, but it can make a closing time real. A roof door or yard door that releases to the public during permitted hours and locks to the outside afterward, while always opening from the roof or yard side so people can leave, takes the decision away from whoever is working that night.

Roofs reached through a residential stair or a shared elevator need more thought. Guests should arrive at the roof and leave from it without stopping on residential floors. In an elevator building that means destination control or floor restriction, so a roof-level call takes guests only between the lobby and the roof after a set hour; in a walk-up it usually means the stair doors on residential floors are locked from the stair side while still opening from the floor side, which the code generally requires. We draw both on the plan before anyone buys hardware.

Closed hours: the doors that matter when nobody is there

The January 2023 Kent Avenue burglaries and the 2026 North Brooklyn run that News 12 described both happened while businesses were closed. In 2023 the reported methods were blunt: cutting the metal locks on a front door and taking a sledgehammer to another. No reader stops a sledgehammer. What a system can do is make sure every door is actually locked when the last person leaves, record that it was, and send an alert within seconds when one is forced or left open, so the owner or a monitoring center can call the precinct while the people are still inside rather than finding the damage at opening.

For that to work the alerts have to reach a person who will act on them at 5 a.m. on a Sunday. We set up routing by schedule: forced-door alarms to the owner and to a central station if the business has one, held-open alerts to the closing manager first, and nothing at all to the residents upstairs, who did not sign up to be the bar’s night watch. If the building has an intrusion alarm, the door system can arm it when the last staff credential exits.

Exits stay exits

Venues and bars are assembly spaces, and their exit doors are not ours to lock. Under NYC Building Code §1010.1.10, rooms in assembly occupancies above the panic-hardware threshold need panic or fire exit hardware, and the electrified lock rules in §1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9 require doors to release on loss of power and on a fire alarm. When an operator asks us to stop patrons using a fire exit as a smoking door, the answer is an alarmed exit device or a local horn and a camera, not a lock. When an owner wants the residents’ inner door locked, it must still open from the stair side with one motion. Every door plan we hand over says, door by door, how it lets people out.

Ownership, logins and a business that changes hands

Ground floors in Williamsburg turn over. A bar closes, a restaurant takes the space, the restaurant becomes a café; when the Living Room at 134 Metropolitan Avenue closed, Bisnow reported in 2016 that its landlord planned apartments above whatever came next. Every time that happens, someone has to answer a plain question: whose door system is this, and who has the password? We set every mixed-use job up so that the answer is written down before it matters. The building owner holds the master administrator account and the controller, which lives in the building’s own space. The business tenant gets an administrator role limited to its own doors and staff groups. The residents’ doors never appear in the tenant’s view at all.

When a tenant leaves, the owner disables the tenant’s administrator role and its staff groups in one step, and the next tenant is given a fresh role on the same hardware. Nothing has to be rewired and no codes survive from the previous business. When the new tenant wants different doors controlled, the owner decides what goes on the building’s system and what the tenant pays to add. We recommend that leases name the system, the two roles and who pays for changes; we can supply a one-page description of the equipment for the lease schedule. The same split works in towers with retail at the base, where the managing agent holds the building’s side and each retail tenant runs its own service door.

Records follow the same line. The building owner keeps the residents’ door records under the limits of HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law; the business keeps its staff door records for its own use; neither side sees the other’s. That separation is what lets a bar show the precinct when its back door was used without exposing which resident came home at what hour, and lets the building review its inner door without reading the bar’s staff log.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Williamsburg’s Architecture

The building stock is unusually mixed for one neighborhood. Old walk-ups and frame buildings on the North Side side streets, converted factories and foundries near the water, glass towers from the post-2005 rezoning, the Domino site’s new buildings, hotels with rooftop bars and storefront rows along Bedford, Metropolitan and Grand. The door that matters, and the person who controls it, is different in each.

Walk-ups with a business downstairs

Three to five stories, a storefront bar or restaurant at street level, a narrow residents’ door and hallway beside it, a cellar shared by both. The inner door at the foot of the stair is the one we put on the system first.

North Side frame and brick row buildings

Smaller buildings, some clad in siding, with two or three apartments over a shop or a garage. Wood doors in old frames, short cable runs, and a yard door that is often the weakest point.

Converted industrial buildings

Foundries, warehouses and factories turned into venues, offices and lofts, such as the 1882 Hecla Iron Works building that houses Brooklyn Bowl. Heavy steel and cast-iron fronts, freight doors, big floor plates and several tenants.

Waterfront towers

Condo and rental towers from the 2005 rezoning and the Domino site, with staffed or unstaffed lobbies, garages, amenity floors and retail at the base. Larger systems, usually expanded rather than started from scratch.

Hotels and rooftop spaces

Small and mid-size hotels with roof bars and event rooms open to the public. Elevators and stairs that must deliver guests to the roof but not to guest floors after a set time.

Storefront rows and offices

Shops, cafés and studios on Bedford Avenue and its cross streets, plus office floors in converted buildings such as Domino’s Refinery. Glass fronts, rear service doors and tenant suites.

Methods that suit the local buildings

  • Cast iron and landmarked fronts. On a designated facade we mount readers and call stations on the frame or the jamb inside the reveal, or on a freestanding post, and bring cable through the door frame rather than the iron. Work on a designated building may need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval; the owner applies and we supply the drawings.
  • Shared cellars. Many walk-ups have one cellar for the business’s stock and the building’s meters and boilers. We often divide it with a partition door on its own credential group rather than locking the whole cellar to one party.
  • Vibration and slamming. Venue doors take heavy, constant use. We specify heavy-duty strikes and closers, through-bolted hardware and armored door loops, and recheck the closer and latch after the first month.
  • Wet service doors. Bar and kitchen doors get ice, mop water and spills. Readers go on the dry side or under a hood, and cable enters from above.
  • Controllers away from the business. The controller and lock power supply for residents’ doors sit in a locked box in the building’s own space, not in a tenant’s back room that changes hands.

Rules that apply to Williamsburg doors

We are installers, not lawyers or code consultants, and nothing here is legal advice. These are the rules we design around most often in this neighborhood; your architect, expediter or counsel decides how they apply to your building.

  • Free exit. NYC Building Code §1010.1.9.8 (sensor-release doors) and §1010.1.9.9 (electromagnetic locks) require electrically locked egress doors to release on loss of power and on activation of the fire alarm or sprinklers, with listed hardware.
  • Assembly spaces. §1010.1.10 requires panic or fire exit hardware on exit doors from assembly rooms at or above its occupant-load threshold. Bars and venues usually fall here; we never fit a lock that would defeat it.
  • Apartment entrances. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and intercom systems in larger multiple dwellings; HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what landlords may collect and keep from electronic entry systems.
  • Liquor licenses. ABC Law §106(5) sets the 4 a.m. end of on-premises sales; a licensee’s method of operation and any stipulations agreed with the community board or the State Liquor Authority may set earlier hours for parts of the premises. We schedule doors from the hours the operator gives us.
  • Landmarks. Work visible on a designated building, such as the Hecla Iron Works facade at 61 Wythe Avenue, may need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Williamsburg: Questions Answered

These are the questions operators, owners and residents in Williamsburg’s mixed buildings bring to us, grouped by the problem behind them. Each answer is short on purpose; the survey fills in the details for a particular building.

A venue downstairs and homes upstairs

Patrons from our bar keep slipping through to the apartment stairs whenever a resident is buzzed in during a show. Should the vestibule be split, or the residents’ door moved?

Usually neither at first. The quicker fix is to make the inner door at the foot of the stair the controlled door, so being buzzed through the street door no longer means reaching the stairs. Fit a strike and reader there, set the closer so the door latches in a few seconds, and add a door switch with a held-open alert to the owner. If the hallway also gives onto the bar’s back room, that door goes on staff credentials from the hall side. Moving a residents’ entrance is a construction and permit project; splitting a vestibule with a new partition and door is sometimes worth it in long hallways, and we will price both if the first step is not enough.

The building’s only cellar holds our walk-in cooler, the residents’ storage cages and the boiler. How should one cellar be divided between a bar’s staff and the tenants?

Divide the cellar, not the building. A partition with a controlled door, or a reader on an existing internal door, can give bar staff the stock area and the route to the cooler while keeping the tenants’ cages, meters and boiler behind a second door that only the super, the owner and the tenants’ credentials open. The staircase down and any sidewalk hatch then belong to whichever side uses them most, with the other side given a schedule. Utility companies and inspectors still need the meters, so the owner keeps a master credential and a mechanical key on the boiler side. We draw the split on a sketch first; partitions themselves are built by a contractor.

Our staff prop the stage door with a crate for smoke breaks during sets. Can the door itself stop that?

It can make it visible and short-lived. A door-position switch with a held-open timer sounds a local chime after, say, a minute, then sends an alert to the manager’s phone if the door stays open. A heavier closer and a strike that catches every time remove the excuse that the door “doesn’t close anyway.” Many venues add a reader outside so the smokers can get back in without propping, which fixes the real reason the crate is there. If the stage door is also a required exit, its inside hardware stays free; the alarm watches the door, it does not lock anyone in.

Our venue’s fire exit lets out into the residents’ rear courtyard. Can we stop patrons using it to step outside without making it less of an exit?

Yes, with an alarmed exit device rather than a lock. The panic bar still opens the door instantly, but doing so sounds a loud local horn and can send an alert, and a sign says so. That keeps the courtyard quiet on ordinary nights and leaves the exit fully usable in an emergency. From the courtyard side the door can stay locked with no outside trim, or take a reader for staff only, so the residents’ yard does not become a way into the venue. Whatever we fit must keep the release required for that exit under the Building Code and must not conflict with the fire alarm; the venue’s architect or code consultant confirms the exit requirements before we start.

At 4 a.m. people leaving the bars sit on our stoop and lean on the buzzer. Is there anything a door system can do about it?

Less than people hope, and we would rather say so. The stoop is outside the door, and a lock does not move a crowd. What helps is narrower: a call panel that does not ring apartments after a set hour unless a visitor enters a valid code, lighting at the door, a camera that records the entrance, and an inner door that stays latched so nobody who gets into the vestibule goes further. Noise and loitering outside a licensed premises are matters for the business, 311, the precinct and, through the Office of Nightlife’s MEND NYC program, mediation between venues and neighbors. A door system can support those conversations with a clear record of when the buzzer was pressed; it cannot replace them.

After the last call

A crew pried open storefronts across North Brooklyn in one early March morning. When our bar is closed and a door is forced at 5 a.m., who should the system call first?

Whoever can act fastest, and more than one person. For a forced or broken door we route the alert to the owner and, if the business pays for one, a central monitoring station at the same time, with the general manager next; a monitoring station can call the precinct while the owner is still waking up. Held-open or not-latched alerts at closing go to the closing manager first, because they usually mean a door was simply not pulled shut. Residents upstairs should not get the bar’s alerts. We test the routing at handover by forcing a door ourselves at an agreed time and timing who answers.

Bottles go missing from our liquor room between inventory counts. Should each bartender have a code to that room, and what should the log show?

Each person who may enter should have their own credential, and the room should be on its own group, separate from the kitchen door. During service the door can stay locked with the bar manager and senior bartenders enrolled; after close only managers. The log then shows each opening by name and time, so a count that comes up short can be checked against who was in the room since the last count. It shows who opened the door, not what they carried out; for that, owners usually add a camera inside the room. Shared codes defeat all of this, which is why we disable a credential the moment the system sees it used by two people on the same shift.

Our bar was referred into the Office of Nightlife’s CURE process with the precinct. Can our door records help show what has changed since?

They can help with the parts that involve doors. The Office of Nightlife describes CURE as an education-first process where operators, the office and NYPD precinct leadership review incidents and agree corrective steps. If the concerns included a back door used after hours, a roof or yard open past its permitted time, or staff letting people in after closing, a door log and schedule report can show that those doors now lock on time and when they were used. Records show door activity, not crowd behavior, and they belong to the business; share them through whoever is advising you in the process. We can export a plain report for the dates you need.

Towers, hotels and landmarked fronts

Our rental tower faces a venue that lets out after midnight. Should the lobby ask for a fob plus a PIN at night?

Two-factor entry after midnight is easy to set up and sometimes worth it, but it slows every resident coming home late, and residents then hold the door for each other, which defeats it. Where the problem is crowds drifting into the vestibule, we usually do better with a second door: the outer lobby door stays on a single credential, and the inner door or the elevator lobby asks for the PIN at night. A video panel that shows the concierge or the managing agent who is at the door, and a camera on the vestibule, round it out. We set it as a schedule so it can be loosened on quiet nights and tightened when the venue has a show.

Our hotel’s roof bar is open to the public, and its elevator also serves guest floors. Can the elevator carry bar guests only between the lobby and the roof after a set hour?

Yes, if the elevator controller supports floor restriction, which most modern ones do through an interface the elevator contractor connects. A reader in the car, or destination control at the lobby, unlocks guest floors only for room keys or staff credentials; a roof call is allowed for everyone during bar hours and switched off afterward. The stairs need matching treatment: stair doors on guest floors locked from the stair side, always opening from the floor side, so a guest who walks down from the roof comes out at the lobby. The elevator contractor’s work is billed by them; we coordinate the schedule and the readers.

Our venue occupies a landmarked cast-iron building. Where can a reader or call panel go without drilling the iron?

Usually inside the door reveal on the wood or steel frame, on the door itself with a power transfer hinge, or on a slim post just inside the entrance; cable runs through the frame and up from the cellar rather than across the facade. Wireless and battery locks can avoid wiring altogether on some doors. On a designated building such as the 1882 Hecla Iron Works at 61 Wythe Avenue, visible exterior changes generally need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval, which the owner applies for; we provide product sheets, photos and a mounting drawing for the application, and we do not drill historic fabric before the permit is in hand.

A tenant in our building is subletting for the summer with the board’s approval. How should the subtenant’s fob be set up?

Enroll the subtenant as a person, under the apartment, with an end date that matches the approved sublet, and suspend the leaseholder’s own credentials for the same period if the board’s rules ask for that. The fob then stops working on the last day without anyone remembering to cancel it, and the log shows the change was authorized. Hand-me-down fobs from the leaseholder are the thing to avoid, because nobody can tell later who had which one. Keep in mind HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits on what you collect about any resident; a name, an apartment and dates are normally all the system needs.

A promoter rents our event room for one night and brings outside security. Which doors should their security staff be able to open?

Only the ones they are there to guard. Give the security lead a temporary credential that opens the event room’s entrance, its back-of-house corridor and the exit route they manage, valid from load-in to an hour after the event ends. It should not open your office, stock rooms or any residential or tenant doors elsewhere in the building. The guards control who comes in the front; your system controls where they can go once inside. Ask the promoter for names in advance so credentials are enrolled per person, and collect them at the end of the night.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Williamsburg

Google’s AI Overview could not be loaded for Williamsburg searches from our build environment, so nothing below is quoted from it. These are the generalizations that keep turning up in quick online answers and in cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what we see on doors in this neighborhood.

“Lock the street door and the building is secure.”

In a building with one population, maybe. In a Williamsburg walk-up with a bar downstairs, the street door is opened all evening by residents, guests, delivery workers and sometimes the bar’s own staff.

The account Brick Underground published from Metropolitan Avenue, a shared vestibule with no lock and no knob, is an extreme example of a common pattern: the street door is the door everyone stops trusting.

The door that protects the homes is the one at the foot of the stair. We treat it as the residents’ front door and design the rest around it.

“Access control costs a set amount per door.”

National per-door figures assume a new door, an easy cable route and one tenant. Williamsburg doors often come with a cast-iron frame, a shared cellar, a business tenant and a landlord who both want a say.

The biggest swing in price here is rarely the reader; it is splitting a shared space, rebuilding a frame that has been slammed for twenty years, or coordinating an elevator contractor.

Our prices come from the Brooklyn list on this page, and the quote names every door and what is done to it, so you can see where the money goes.

“A magnetic lock is the easy option for a business door.”

It is cheap and quick to fit, which is why so many bar back doors have one. It is also the lock most often found disconnected, because staff learn that pulling its power opens it.

On an exit it must release on loss of power, on a request to exit and on the fire alarm under Building Code §1010.1.9.9, so it is not a security lock for closed hours.

A heavy strike or an electrified mortise lock keeps a mechanical latch and a key, and stays locked through a power cut from the outside while always opening from the inside.

“Business hours are nine to five.”

Software defaults and many online guides assume that. A Williamsburg bar opens in the afternoon and may serve until 4 a.m. under ABC Law §106(5), with cleanup after that and deliveries before noon.

A default schedule either locks out the closing staff or leaves codes live all night. Both happen, and both lead to doors propped or codes shared.

We build schedules from the actual shifts and the license hours, with a window between close and opening when only managers can enter.

“A smart lock and an app are all a small venue needs.”

An app-based lock is fine for one office door. A venue has a front of house, a back of house, a storeroom, a stage door, exits and sometimes a roof, used by dozens of people on rotating shifts.

Consumer locks rarely carry the listings required on egress doors, cannot alarm an exit, and are hard to manage when staff turn over every few weeks.

One controller with readers, exit devices and door switches covers all of it, and one login removes a departing bartender from every door at once.

“Better locks stop burglars.”

The Kent Avenue burglars in January 2023 cut through door locks at one restaurant and used a sledgehammer at another, according to ABC7. No electronic lock survives that by itself.

What makes the difference is that the door was locked in the first place, that its frame and strike are solid, and that someone hears about the break-in within seconds.

That is why our closed-hours work focuses on confirming every door at closing and routing a forced-door alert to a person or station that answers at 5 a.m.

“New luxury towers already have all this.”

Big towers do come with lobby systems, but they were specified for the building as designed, often more than a decade ago after the 2005 rezoning.

Retail and restaurant tenants at the base, amenity floors opened to guests, and new venues across the street all arrive later, and the original system rarely anticipated them.

We usually extend what is there, adding readers, night modes and elevator rules to the existing platform where it can take them, rather than replacing a working lobby.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Color Every Door Before Choosing a Lock

This describes how we survey mixed-use buildings in general, not any particular customer. We walk the ground floor with the owner and, when we can, the business’s manager, and mark each door on a sketch in one of three colors: residents, staff, public. Then we ask the question that matters most: who actually uses this door at midnight? The answers are often different from the lease. The kitchen door into the residents’ hallway turns out to be how staff reach the trash. The yard door the bar is supposed to close at ten stays open because it is also the smoking area. The roof door has no lock on the stair side because the original tenant wanted it that way.

Only after that map exists do we pick hardware. It is the step that keeps a reader from being fitted to the wrong door, and the one owners tell us afterward was most useful, because it puts the two groups’ needs on one page.

The map also settles arguments before they start. When the owner and the operator both look at a door colored two ways, the conversation turns to who pays for the second door or the schedule, rather than to whose fault the propped door was. It is also the sketch we update whenever a ground-floor tenant changes, so the next operator starts from an accurate picture of the building rather than from the previous tenant’s habits.

On towers the map is bigger but the method is the same: lobby, garage, amenity floors, retail service doors and roof, each colored by who uses it and when.

FAQ

Williamsburg Access Control FAQ

At Brooklyn prices, what does it cost to put a bar’s back door, its liquor room and the residents’ inner door on one controller?
+

Three doors on one cloud controller fall on the multi-door line of the Brooklyn list, $4,000 to $15,000, and a building of that size usually lands toward the lower part of the range. Priced door by door, the residents’ inner door is a single-door job at $1,500 to $2,500 and each staff door is $1,500 to $2,000. What pushes the figure up is dividing a shared hallway or cellar, rebuilding a worn frame, or adding alarmed exit hardware. The written quote lists each door separately so the owner and the business tenant can see what each is paying for if they split the cost.

Will drilling and cabling disturb the tenants upstairs if you work while the bar is closed in the morning?
+

Some of it is noisy, mainly drilling through masonry and frames, so we agree quiet hours with the owner and do the loud work late morning rather than at dawn, when residents are asleep and the bar has only just closed. Cable pulling, mounting and programming are quiet and can run earlier. Every day ends with each door latching and locking as it did before, so neither the residents nor the business is left with an open door overnight.

Can you represent us before Community Board 1 or the State Liquor Authority about our door hours?
+

No. We are installers, and license conditions, stipulations and hearings are matters for the licensee and its counsel or consultant. What we can do is provide a written description of how each door is scheduled and an export of door events, if your adviser thinks it useful, and change a schedule quickly when the agreed hours change.

Should the door system share the network the DJ booth and the point-of-sale terminals use?
+

We recommend it does not. Readers and controllers work best on their own network or their own segment, so a sound engineer resetting a switch at midnight cannot take the doors offline, and so a problem on the guest Wi-Fi never reaches the locks. Where only one internet line exists, we separate the door system on its own segment and give the controller a battery so doors keep working if the network drops.

Will bass vibration and constant slamming loosen strikes and closers on a venue door?
+

Over time, yes, which is why we specify heavy-duty strikes, through-bolted closers and hinges, and thread-locking on the fasteners, and return after the first month to readjust. Readers themselves are not affected by sound. On doors that take very heavy traffic during shows we sometimes fit an electrified exit device instead of a strike, because it spreads the load across the door rather than the frame.

Will readers on a bar’s service door survive ice, spilled drinks and mop water?
+

Mount them on the drier side of the door where possible, under a hood when they must face the wet side, and choose units with an outdoor weather rating even indoors. Cable should enter from above so water does not run along it into the reader. Keypads with physical buttons suffer more from sticky spills than proximity readers, which is one reason we usually recommend fobs or cards for kitchen and bar doors.

If a lock fails at 3 a.m. while we are closing, will anyone answer in Williamsburg?
+

Call the Williamsburg line at (347) 934-8335. Outside our regular hours we cannot promise a technician at the door within the hour, but we will help the closing manager secure the door mechanically by phone, and book the first available visit. Most door systems also let an administrator unlock or lock a door remotely, which solves many late problems without anyone driving over.

Can the security staff at our front door release the residents’ inner door for a guest?
+

Only if the building owner wants that, and usually it is better not. The bar’s door staff work for the business; the residents’ inner door belongs to the building. Residents admit their own visitors through the call panel or their phones. Where a building has both a staffed venue entrance and apartments, we keep the venue’s staff credentials off the residents’ doors entirely and give the owner a log that shows it.

Is the 4 a.m. closing time set in state law, and does it affect how doors are scheduled?
+

New York’s Alcoholic Beverage Control Law §106(5) stops on-premises sales from 4 a.m. to 8 a.m., and until 10 a.m. on Sundays; a business’s own license conditions can be earlier. That is the outer limit we use for public-facing door schedules at a bar or venue, with staff closing codes running a set time beyond it for cleanup. We take the hours from the operator and do not interpret the license.
