The Neighborhood

Why University Heights Buildings Need a Different Camera Plan

University Heights got its name in 1894, when New York University moved its undergraduate college to the hilltop above the Harlem River. Before that the land was part of the rural Morris estate, and NYC Parks notes it was first known as Fordham Heights. The neighborhood we work in today came later. When the IRT Jerome Avenue line opened in 1917, the farms and suburban villas gave way to low-rise apartment buildings for middle-class families, and most of the walk-ups that line University Avenue, Loring Place, Andrews Avenue, Davidson Avenue and Grand Avenue date from those decades. Homes.com puts the median year built near 1910, which is a rough figure from a small sample, but anyone who has been down a University Heights cellar knows the type: brick bearing walls, plaster on wood lath or terra cotta block upstairs, a boiler room, a meter room and a tangle of old phone, intercom and cable-TV wiring nobody has labeled in decades.

The boundaries are West Fordham Road to the north, Jerome Avenue to the east, West Burnside Avenue to the south and the Harlem River to the west, with University Avenue as the spine and ZIP codes 10453 and 10468. Inside those lines the ground is the story. Sedgwick, Andrews and Davidson Avenues climb hard. The Old Croton Aqueduct runs as a walking trail through the middle of the neighborhood. University Woods, a 3.3-acre park created in 1899, sits on a rock slope between Sedgwick and Cedar Avenues. Bronx Community College holds the high ground, including the Hall of Fame for Great Americans, a 630-foot colonnade on the highest natural point in the borough. For a camera installer, a hill changes almost everything: where the cellar is, which door gets the most traffic, where the light falls at dusk, how a rear yard drops behind a retaining wall, and where cable can travel.

The people who live here are mostly renters. The area was long Jewish and Irish Catholic, became home to a large Vietnamese and Cambodian refugee community, and is now largely Dominican. Ten NYCHA developments sit within the neighborhood, including the Sedgwick Houses towers. Those buildings are served by NYCHA and its own contractors, so this page is about privately owned buildings, small houses, new construction and businesses, which is where an owner, a managing agent, a tenant association or a store owner actually decides what gets installed.

University Heights facts that decide where a camera goes

  • Street doors that do not close properly are a recorded problem here. HPD records on Augrented and Weverit show a non-compliant electronic lock at the main entrance of 2294 University Avenue, a five-story building with 27 apartments; entrance door and lock problems at 2230 University Avenue, a six-story building with 84 apartments; and a defective latch set on an entrance door at 2536 Grand Avenue, along with an open Class C violation concerning self-closing doors. A camera aimed at a door that never latches records a parade, not a breach.
  • Many buildings carry heavy violation counts. OpenIgloo's snapshot lists 141 open violations at 2170 University Avenue and 125 at 2280 Loring Place North. Owners trying to clear a building's record and tenants trying to document conditions both end up asking for cameras, for different reasons.
  • Some buildings are changing hands from landlord to tenants. News 12 and the Bronx Times reported that the city foreclosed on 2201-05 Davidson Avenue, a 49-unit rent-stabilized building with more than 800 open violations, and that more than 80 percent of its tenants signed on to become resident-owners. When residents become the owners, they also become the people who decide who sees the footage.
  • New buildings are on the way. New York YIMBY reported permits for a six-story, 13-unit building at 2424 Davidson Avenue between West 184th Street and Fordham Road, and a six-story, 25-unit building with a cellar and a 40-foot rear yard at 2603 Sedgwick Avenue, along with further filings on Andrews Avenue, University Avenue, Aqueduct Avenue East and West 181st Street.
  • The shopping streets are busy late and early. Fordham Road forms the northern edge and its main retail strip; Jerome Avenue concentrates groceries and restaurants near 183rd Street, and further along the el it is tire shops and muffler repair. Norwood News and News 12 have both run NYPD requests for help identifying robbery suspects from video on these streets.

Building on a Slope

Cameras for a Building That Steps Down the Hill

Walk down Andrews Avenue or Davidson Avenue and look at the cellars. On a level block, every building's cellar is a basement: you go down a stair from the lobby or through a hatch out front, and the only way in from outside is the cellar door under the stoop or the delivery hatch. On a University Heights hill, the building at the low end of the block may have its cellar fully above the sidewalk on one side, with a full-height door, sometimes a second street entrance for the super, and windows at eye level. Two buildings apart, the cellar is twelve feet underground. That changes what a camera system has to cover. A downhill cellar door is a second street door, and in many buildings it is the one with the weakest lock, the least light and the least traffic, which is exactly why people use it.

We start every hillside job with a simple question: how many ways are there to get in? The answer is usually more than the owner expects. There is the main street door and its inner vestibule door. There may be a side or cellar-level door on the low side. There is the roof door, which on a row of buildings with touching roofs can be reached from next door. There is the rear yard, which on a slope may sit a full story below the street, behind a retaining wall, with fire escapes coming down into it. And there may be a gate or a passage between buildings. Each of those gets its own camera with one plain job: show who came through, clearly enough to recognize them, in the light that is actually there.

Where the recorder lives on a hillside building

The recorder goes where it is safe, cool, dry and hard to reach. In most University Heights walk-ups that is a locked steel box or a small cabinet in the cellar, near the electrical service and the building's internet connection. On a building whose cellar opens to the sidewalk on the downhill side, we do not put it on that wall. Anyone who comes in through the low door should not walk straight past the box that records them. Where the cellar floods or sweats in summer, which happens in older buildings cut into rock, the recorder goes up on a shelf or wall bracket, never on the floor, and the box gets ventilation. Where the owner wants an extra layer, the recorder sends a copy of key cameras offsite, so smashing or stealing the box does not erase the evening.

How cable climbs six floors in an old building

Old buildings do not come with empty conduit. The cleanest path up a University Heights walk-up is usually one of three: an abandoned dumbwaiter shaft, a pipe chase beside the stair, or the old intercom or cable-TV riser if it has room. Failing those, we run up the stairwell itself in a painted metal raceway tight to the wall where a run along the stair stringer looks like part of the building rather than an afterthought. On every floor the cable crosses a fire-rated floor or wall, the opening is sealed with firestop. On top floors, the roof door camera often shares the path of the bulkhead's existing lighting. Exterior runs on the street side are kept short and sleeved, because a cable hanging down a brick front is an invitation.

Light on a slope

Hills change light. A west-facing street door on a block running down toward the Harlem River can take low sun straight into the lens in late afternoon, washing the face of anyone walking in. An east-facing door below the el on Jerome Avenue sits in shadow most of the day. We pick cameras with real wide dynamic range for doors that face bright sky, and we check every door view at the time of day the problem happens, not only at noon. A camera that looks perfect on install day and shows a white blur at 5 p.m. in March has not done its job.

The Front Door First

A Camera Cannot Lock a Door: Fixing the Entrance Before Recording It

The most common mistake we see in University Heights is a camera system bought to solve a problem that is really a door problem. People come in who do not live in the building. They sleep in the stairwell, use in the hallway, take packages from the mailbox area. The owner, or the tenants, decide a camera will fix it. Then the camera faithfully records the same street door standing open, night after night, because the closer was removed, the latch does not catch, or the electric lock was disconnected when the buzzer died.

The building records bear this out. HPD violations at 2294 University Avenue include a non-compliant electronic lock at the main entrance. An inspection at 2230 University Avenue found entrance door and lock problems. 2536 Grand Avenue has a defective latch set on an entrance door and an open Class C violation about self-closing doors. Elsewhere in the neighborhood, intercom and buzzer complaints show up at 2610 University Avenue, 2300 Loring Place North, 2175 Cedar Avenue and 30 Buchanan Place. City Limits reporters visiting former cluster-site shelter buildings on Davidson Avenue walked in through an unlocked entrance, and a tenant told them she worried about people using drugs in the hallways. In NYCHA buildings across the Bronx, the city Comptroller found broken locks on 47.6 percent of entrance doors in 2022, up from 15.4 percent in 2018, with residents saying doors were propped open because intercoms, locks and release buttons did not work. That is a borough-wide NYCHA figure, not a University Heights one, but the pattern is the same in private walk-ups.

So before we aim a single camera at an entrance, we look at the door. Does it swing shut on its own from fully open? Does the latch catch without anyone pushing? Is the strike plate lined up, or has the frame sagged so the bolt misses? Is the electric strike or maglock actually powered? Is there a gap a hand or a credit card can work through? HPD's self-closing door rules give owners a short window to correct violations and allow emergency repairs charged to the property if they do not, so a failed closer is both a security hole and a legal one. If the door needs a closer, a strike, a latch guard or work on the intercom, we say so and price it on the quote, or point you to our door buzzer repair and intercom installation pages for University Heights. Then the camera's job becomes clear: record the face of every person who comes through a door that should have stopped them.

Two cameras on a street door, not one

Most University Heights entrances are a street door, a small vestibule with the buzzer panel and sometimes the mailboxes, then an inner door into the lobby. One camera outside the street door sees hoods and caps from above. One camera inside the vestibule, aimed at the inner door from slightly above head height, sees faces as people stop at the buzzer panel or wait for someone to let them in. Where mailboxes are in the vestibule or lobby, the second camera covers them too, which matters in buildings with package and mail theft. Where budget allows only one, the inside view usually wins.

Recording the buzzer panel without recording the code

Some buildings use keypads at the door. A camera aimed squarely at the keypad records every code typed into it, and anyone with access to the recorder then has every code. We angle the vestibule camera to catch the person's face and body at the panel, not their fingers on the buttons, and we tell the owner which views would expose codes so they can decide.

When Tenants Hold the Keys

Cameras for Buildings Run by Their Own Residents

University Heights has buildings in an unusual position. At 2201-05 Davidson Avenue, after years on the city's worst-landlord list and more than 800 open HPD violations, 412 of them Class C, the city foreclosed on the owner and more than 80 percent of the tenants signed a petition to become resident-owners, with the Bronx Times reporting steps toward a co-op conversion. Buildings in that position are making decisions for the first time that a landlord used to make alone, and cameras are often near the top of the list, along with the doors, the boiler and the elevator.

A camera system in a resident-run building works differently in one important way: the people who will be recorded are also the people who own the recorder. That is a strength, because nobody is installing cameras over the residents' heads, and a challenge, because every neighbor has a view about where cameras should go and who gets to watch. We have found that the systems that last in buildings like this are the ones where the rules are written down before the install, not after the first argument.

The four decisions a resident board should make first

Where cameras may point. Common doors, stairs, the lobby, the mailboxes, the cellar, the roof door and the yard. Not into anyone's apartment, not into a laundry window across the yard, not down the corridor in a way that records exactly when one person comes and goes. Corridor cameras, if any, look at the stair door and the elevator, not at apartment doors.

Who can watch live. Usually the super and one or two board members, each with their own login, so the recorder's log shows who looked at what. A shared password that half the building knows is not a security system; it is a bulletin board.

Who can pull and release a clip. Name the people, and write down that clips go to the police on request, to the board for building business, and to a resident only for an incident involving them, by written request. Brick Underground notes that a landlord is generally not required to show tenants footage except to the police or under a court order. A resident-owned building can set a more open rule, but it should be a rule.

How long footage lives. Thirty days is common. Longer means bigger drives and more cost; shorter means a theft noticed late may already be gone. We size the drives to match the number you pick.

We will bring a one-page draft of those rules to the board meeting if it helps, leave room for the board to change it, and set the recorder's user accounts to match what was agreed. When the building's ownership or management changes later, the logins are changed with it, and we can hand the new manager a clean inventory of every camera, cable and setting.

Prewar walk-ups: the cellar and the stair do the work

In a typical University Heights walk-up, the recorder goes in a locked box in the cellar, and cable reaches the vestibule through the cellar ceiling directly under the lobby. Up the building, the stair is the backbone. A camera on the top landing facing the roof door, one on the ground-floor landing facing up, and, in larger buildings, one at the halfway landing give a record of anyone moving through the stair without putting a lens on any apartment door. Plaster walls mean careful drilling and patching; brick and terra cotta mean the right bit and patience. We protect floors and stairs during the work and clean up the dust every day.

Elevator buildings: more entrances, more decisions

The bigger buildings along Jerome Avenue and University Avenue add an elevator, a wider lobby, sometimes a courtyard, a service entrance and a separate route to the trash room. The camera count goes up, and so does the importance of a good layout on the viewing screen, so the super can look at the lobby, the elevator landing and the service door at once. An in-cab elevator camera needs a traveling cable or a wireless bridge and coordination with the elevator contractor, and we quote that separately when it is wanted.

Steep-block buildings: two street levels

For buildings that sit across a grade change, we treat the low side as a second front. It gets a camera, the door gets checked like the main entrance, and if the low side is dark, which it usually is, the camera is chosen for low light rather than relying on infrared alone, so colors of clothing and vehicles stay readable. Yards below the street get cameras mounted high on the rear wall where a ladder from the fire escape cannot reach them.

Small houses and townhouse buildings: simpler, but closer to neighbors

A two-family house or a townhouse building usually needs four to eight cameras: the front door, the side or driveway, the back, and maybe a package spot. Cable is easy compared to a walk-up. The care goes into aim, because houses sit close together and a camera that sees into the house next door, or over a neighbor's back yard, creates trouble. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a homeowner a right to sue over video surveillance of their back yard by an adjoining neighbor without consent, so we aim at your own property and use privacy masking where an edge of a neighbor's yard is unavoidable.

New construction: do it while the walls are open

The new buildings on file, such as the six-story, 13-unit building at 2424 Davidson Avenue and the six-story, 25-unit building at 2603 Sedgwick Avenue, are the cheapest place in the neighborhood to put cameras, if the wiring goes in during framing. More on that below.

Barber shops and salons: the chair, the register and the door

Norwood News reported that on January 3, 2022, around midday, five people robbed a man inside Innovation Barber and Beauty Shop at 2357 Jerome Avenue, two of them showing handguns while three kept watch, and then fled on foot south on Jerome; the NYPD released video and asked for help identifying them. In a shop like that, the cameras that matter are the one facing the door from behind the counter, which captures faces as people come in, and one high in a back corner covering the whole floor. The register or cash drawer gets its own view. Audio stays off; recording customers' conversations is not something a shop should do, and New York's eavesdropping law makes it risky.

Tire and muffler shops under the 4 train

An auto shop's risks are overnight: cars left outside, tires stacked by the bay, tools and parts in an open garage. Cameras go high on the building face to cover the cars and the bay doors, with one inside each bay. The el creates hard shadows by day and a mix of sodium light and headlight glare by night, so we favor cameras that keep color in low light and set them so a car's headlights do not blind them. Train vibration is real; mounts go into solid masonry with proper anchors, not into old signage or loose facade panels.

Groceries and delis: counters seen from both sides

A counter camera from behind the register shows customers' faces and hands; a second from above the customer side shows the register and the employee. Together they settle disputes about change, returns and missing stock, and they show what happened if a confrontation breaks out. Stock rooms and back doors get their own views, since a lot of loss walks out the back.

Stores under apartments

Many University Heights storefronts sit under residential floors owned by the same landlord. The store's cameras and the building's cameras are often best kept on separate recorders with separate logins, because a store owner should not be watching the residential lobby and the building's super should not be scrolling through the register camera. Where the two share a facade, we plan both so they cover the sidewalk without duplicating each other.

The Street Outside

What a Street-Facing Camera Can Really Capture on a University Heights Block

Police appeals in the neighborhood keep showing the same shape. Norwood News reported that on April 12, 2025, at about 5:15 p.m., a 68-year-old man walking near West 183rd Street and Aqueduct Avenue was approached by a man who took his bracelet and punched him when he tried to get it back; the attacker got into a black Ford pickup with a second man and drove west on West 183rd Street. News 12 reported in October 2025 that three masked men with weapons took more than $9,000 in jewelry from three people near Jerome Avenue around 5 a.m. and fled in a BMW. In both cases the NYPD released video and asked the public for help. Owners who read those stories call us asking for a camera that will "get the plate." It is worth being honest about what that takes.

A general-purpose camera covering a building's frontage, a stoop and a stretch of sidewalk is built to see people and the overall scene. It will usually show that a dark pickup drove west at 5:16 p.m., its shape and color, and maybe a dent or a roof rack. It usually will not show a readable plate on a moving vehicle, especially at night, because plates are small, reflective, and pass through the frame in a fraction of a second. Reading plates is a separate job with a separate camera: a narrow field of view aimed at a spot where cars slow down or stop, set with a fast shutter so motion does not blur the characters, and with infrared tuned so the plate's reflective coating does not bloom white. On a hill like Andrews Avenue or Davidson Avenue, the best spot is often where traffic has to brake for the slope or a corner.

Faces at the door versus the scene on the sidewalk

We split the jobs. One camera, close and at the door, is for faces. Another, higher and wider, is for the scene: direction of travel, the vehicle, the time. If a plate reader is wanted, that is a third camera with a single assignment. Trying to make one camera do all three is the most common reason footage turns out useless.

Early morning and late afternoon

The two incidents above happened at 5 a.m. and 5 p.m., both times when light is changing fast. At 5 a.m. in October it is dark; at 5 p.m. in April it is still bright but the sun is low. Cameras switch between day and night modes around dusk and dawn, and a badly set switch point gives a few minutes of grainy, gray video right when it matters. We test that switch at the real hour on the real block and adjust it, and we use low-light color cameras on fronts that have some street lighting so the switch to black-and-white happens later or not at all.

What a camera on your building may and may not look at

A camera on private property may record the public sidewalk and street. It may not be aimed into a neighbor's windows, and New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law section 250.45, makes it a crime to record people without consent in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, like a bedroom or a bathroom. We aim street cameras at the sidewalk and roadway, mask out windows across the street, and leave audio off.

Hard to Wreck

Building a System That Survives Someone Who Wants It Gone

In 2015 CBS New York reported that three men walked into several apartment buildings on Davidson Avenue, in the buildings numbered from 1985 to 2024 at the Morris Heights end of the street, around 1 p.m. and smashed surveillance cameras inside and outside before running off. Police released video of the suspects, which tells you the other cameras or another recorder caught them. That is the lesson: people who want cameras gone go after the ones they can reach, so a system has to keep working, and keep its record, after the most exposed camera is broken.

Height and housing

Inside stairwells and vestibules, cameras go as high as the view allows and in hard-shelled vandal-resistant domes, rated to take a strike. Outside, cameras on the street face go high enough that a person on the stoop cannot reach them, and where a fire escape or a dumpster could be climbed, the mount goes beyond that reach. Cable is in metal raceway or behind the wall, never hanging loose where a pull would rip a camera off.

Cameras that watch each other

We place cameras so that the approach to one camera is in the view of another. Someone who walks up to the vestibule camera with a hammer is recorded by the lobby camera behind them. Someone who climbs to the roof door camera is seen by the top-landing view on the way.

Tamper alerts

Most current cameras can raise an alert when the view is suddenly blocked, covered, sprayed or turned. We switch that on and send the alert to the super's or owner's phone. A camera that goes dark at 1 p.m. should produce a notification at 1 p.m., not a discovery a week later.

Protecting the recorder

The recorder is the real target. It goes in a locked steel box, somewhere other than the obvious room, and its own location is covered by a camera. Where the owner wants it, footage from the key cameras is copied offsite as it records, so taking or smashing the box loses nothing important.

Tenants, boards and owners

Someone took a package from our mailbox area on Loring Place and the landlord says he "can't" show me the camera. Is that true, and what can I do instead?

It is mostly true that he does not have to. Brick Underground points out that a New York landlord generally is not required to show a tenant surveillance footage, with the exceptions being a police request or a court order in a lawsuit, and that buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. What usually works is a police report the same day, with the date, the time window and a note that the building has a camera over the mailboxes, because detectives can ask the owner for the footage. Ask management in writing to save that time window before it is recorded over. If thefts keep happening, a group of tenants asking together for a reviewed clip, a package locker or a better door has more weight than one person asking alone.

Our tenants are on track to buy 2201-05 Davidson Avenue from the city. Should cameras wait until the co-op closes, or go in now?

Plan now, install when the people who will own and run the system are the ones signing for it. Until ownership changes, the entity holding the building is the one that can authorize drilling and wiring, so any work needs its approval. Meanwhile the tenant group can do the part that takes longest: agree where cameras should point, who will watch, who can release clips and how long footage is kept. We can walk the building, give you a written plan and a quote, and hold the plan until the board is ready to sign. That way the first meeting after the transfer can approve something already worked out, rather than starting the argument from scratch.

I rent on Andrews Avenue and want a doorbell camera on my own apartment door. Could management make me take it down?

It can happen. News 12 reported in May 2024 that management at a Mott Haven building told tenants to remove the cameras on their apartment doors or face lease violations, and one tenant took down her Ring doorbell reluctantly. Before buying one, read your lease for any clause about alterations, devices on doors or cameras in common areas, and ask management in writing. A camera inside your apartment, looking at the inside of your own door, is a different matter from a device mounted on the door facing a shared hallway, which records your neighbors' comings and goings. If management says no, a peephole viewer camera or an inside-the-door camera usually avoids the issue.

The cellar on the low side of our building opens right onto the sidewalk. Does that door really need its own camera if the front door has one?

Yes. On a steep University Heights block, a sidewalk-level cellar door is a second street entrance, often less watched, less lit and less sturdy than the front. Anyone who comes in that way never passes the front camera. It needs a camera inside, aimed at the door from the cellar side so it sees faces as people enter, and ideally a light. Check the door too: an old cellar door with a hasp and padlock, or one that the super props open for deliveries, is the real weak spot, and we will say so on the quote.

Cameras in buildings on Davidson Avenue were smashed by a group of men years ago. How do you stop that from wiping out a whole system?

You assume someone will try and build so it does not matter much. That means vandal-rated domes mounted out of reach, cable inside walls or metal raceway, cameras placed so each one's approach is seen by another, tamper alerts that ping a phone when a view is blocked or moved, and a recorder in a locked box away from the obvious room, with key cameras copied offsite. In the 2015 Davidson Avenue case the police still released video of the suspects, which is the point: a system designed this way keeps its record even after the nearest camera is broken.

The man robbed near West 183rd and Aqueduct got away in a pickup. Would a camera on our building have read its license plate?

Probably not with an ordinary building camera. A wide camera covering a stoop and sidewalk shows the vehicle's type, color and direction, which helps, but a moving plate is small and blurs, especially in low light. Reading plates takes a dedicated camera with a narrow view aimed where cars slow or stop, a fast shutter and infrared set for reflective plates. If that is what you want, tell us, and we will look for the spot on your block where traffic brakes, often at a corner or on the slope, and quote it as its own camera.

We are building a six-story building on Davidson Avenue. When in the schedule should the camera contractor come in?

Twice. First during design or early framing, to mark the head-end space, the camera locations and the cable routes on the drawings, and to make sure there is a dedicated outlet for the recorder. Second after framing and rough electrical, before drywall, to pull cable and set boxes. Cameras are mounted after paint near the end. Coming in only at the end means surface raceway across a brand-new lobby and drilling through finished, fire-rated walls, which costs more and looks worse.

Storefronts and businesses

Both matter and both are manageable. Owners, Tenants and the Law

The Rules Behind a University Heights Camera Job, in Plain Words

Camera work in a neighborhood of renters, resident-run buildings and busy shops touches several New York rules. None of this is legal advice, and a building with a specific dispute should talk to a lawyer, but these are the rules we design around on every job.

Recording where people expect privacy

New York Penal Law section 250.45, unlawful surveillance, makes it a crime to use a device to record people without consent in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as a bedroom, bathroom or changing area. In a building, that rules out any camera that sees into an apartment, a bathroom or a laundry changing area. Common doors, stairs, lobbies and yards are where cameras belong.

Audio

Recording a conversation that you are not part of, without consent of at least one person in it, can be eavesdropping under New York Penal Law section 250.05. Most cameras can record sound. In common areas and shops we leave audio off unless there is a specific, lawful reason, such as a two-way talk feature at a door station where the person is speaking to you.

Neighbors' back yards

Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a residential owner or tenant sue an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent, for the purpose of watching them. In University Heights, where rear yards sit side by side below the street, we aim yard cameras at your own yard and doors, and mask any part of the view that falls on the yard next door.

Doors and HPD

Self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and working intercoms are part of an owner's duty under the city's housing rules, and HPD treats failures seriously, including emergency repairs charged to the property. A camera does not satisfy any of those rules on its own. That is why we check the door first.

Who installs

Installers of security systems in New York State are licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D. We work under NYS license #12000287431, and we carry insurance. Ask any contractor for their license number before work starts.

Landmarks

The Bronx Community College buildings, including Gould Memorial Library and the Hall of Fame for Great Americans, are designated city landmarks, and exterior work on landmarked buildings needs Landmarks Preservation Commission review. The residential blocks of University Heights are not in a historic district according to the sources we found, so most private buildings do not need an LPC permit for cameras. If your building is landmarked, tell us and we will plan for the review.

Myths

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Security Camera Installation in University Heights

People increasingly ask a chatbot before they call anyone. These are claims we hear repeated that do not hold up for buildings here.

"A landlord has to give tenants the camera footage."

Not as a general rule. A landlord usually must hand footage to police on request or under a court order, but is generally not required to show it to a tenant who asks. The workable path is a police report and a written request to preserve the footage.

"Put a camera on the front door and the trespassing stops."

Not if the door does not latch. The HPD records for several University Heights buildings show entrance lock and latch problems. A camera records a broken door being walked through; it does not close it.

"Any outdoor camera will read license plates."

General outdoor cameras show vehicles, not readable plates, especially on moving cars at night. Plates need a dedicated camera set up for that one purpose.

"Wireless cameras are the answer for old buildings."

Thick brick and terra cotta walls and six floors of stair block Wi-Fi badly, and wireless cameras still need power. In walk-ups, wired cameras on one cable each are more reliable and cheaper over time. Wireless has a place for a single hard-to-reach spot.

"The city already has cameras covering the neighborhood."

City and council-funded cameras watch a limited number of public locations, and owners cannot review them. They do not cover your doors, your stairs or your stock room.

"Footage is kept forever on the recorder."

Recorders overwrite their oldest footage when the drive is full, often within days to weeks. If something happens, save the clip quickly.

"Cameras can go anywhere on your own property."

Not in New York. Recording where people expect privacy is a crime under Penal Law section 250.45, recording conversations you are not part of can be eavesdropping, and neighbors have a civil claim over back-yard surveillance under Civil Rights Law section 52-a.
