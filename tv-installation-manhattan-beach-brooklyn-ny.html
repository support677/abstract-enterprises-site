The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need TV Installation

Manhattan Beach sits at the very bottom of Brooklyn, a peninsula with Sheepshead Bay on one side, the Atlantic on the other, Brighton Beach to the west and Kingsborough Community College on the eastern tip. Its streets run in alphabetical order from Amherst to Pembroke, with Oriental Boulevard along the ocean side and Shore Boulevard along the bay. Brick Underground's guide to buying here describes a neighborhood of mostly detached single-family houses, with some attached and semi-detached homes and only a handful of condos, built mainly in the 1920s and 1930s and often in traditional European styles, especially Tudor, alongside newer modern houses. One broker quoted there summed it up as "the suburbs on the water." For a TV installer, that description is a list of walls: plaster from between the wars, brick and stucco on the outside, chimney breasts with fireplaces in the living rooms, and new framing wherever a house has been rebuilt.

The rebuilding is the part that makes Manhattan Beach different from most Brooklyn neighborhoods we work in. The same guide quotes a Corcoran broker saying new development here happens only when a poorly maintained house is torn down and replaced, and the listings it cites show what replaces them: a 4,197-square-foot contemporary on Ocean Avenue and a 7,000-square-foot new house a few doors along. Brownstoner's list of the largest homes in Brooklyn put two Manhattan Beach houses at the top, one on Coleridge Street at 16,914 square feet and one on Dover Street at 15,463 square feet. A house like that does not have a TV; it has a family room, a primary bedroom, a gym, a playroom and a lower level, each with its own screen, and often a builder and an electrician still on site when the first TV arrives. The TV plan has to be made while the walls are open, or it gets made later with a drill and a patch kit.

Then there is the water. When Sandy came in October 2012, City Limits reported that the surge arrived from both the bay and the ocean, and quoted the president of the Manhattan Beach Community Group saying, "Every house was flooded." Brick Underground's guide says the storm damaged many houses and flooded many basements, and that prices fell for only a month or two afterward. The city's flood rules changed in the years after, and the Department of City Planning's retrofitting guide explains that in new and substantially improved buildings in flood zones, the space below the design flood elevation can be used only for building access, crawl space, storage and parking, with utilities and equipment kept at or above that line. Many of the newest houses on the peninsula are built that way, with the garage and entry at street level and the living floors above. That changes where a family can put its TV room, and where we put the equipment that runs it.

The fourth thing is size and distance. In a 2,500-square-foot brick house on Beaumont Street, the TV is usually in one front room, a few feet from the outlet and the cable box. In a 7,000-square-foot rebuilt house, the internet may come in at one end of the lower level and the family room may be two floors up at the other, so a smart TV that streams 4K over Wi-Fi from the far end of the house buffers on a Sunday afternoon. Those houses are where a wired connection to the TV, a cabinet with proper ventilation for the receiver and a labeled run back to the network closet start to matter, and where a TV job turns into a small piece of cable planning. We do both, under the same license, and we price the TV part from our Brooklyn TV list and any structured cabling separately and in writing.

Same-Day Repair in Manhattan Beach

Need Repair in Manhattan Beach? A Mount Creeping Out of Old Plaster, a TV the Last Owner Left Behind, or a Den Screen Nobody Touched Since the Storm

Three kinds of urgent TV call come up again and again from houses like the ones on the peninsula. The first is a screen that has begun to lean forward, sit lower on one side or move when someone brushes past it. In a house from the 1920s or 1930s that usually means the bracket was fixed into the plaster alone, with plugs or small toggles that grip a thin, brittle surface and nothing behind it. Plaster is hard on the face and crumbly further in, and every time a full-motion arm is pulled out and pushed back, the fixing crushes a little more of it. A second, related call comes from brick and stucco walls, where a bracket was set into mortar joints instead of the brick itself, or where an anchor meant for solid masonry went into a hollow. Both are fixable, and both are much easier to fix before the screen comes down on its own.

The second kind of call comes from people who have just bought a house. Manhattan Beach houses change hands for a lot of money, and new owners often find a previous family's TV still on its bracket in a den or a bedroom, with a cable hanging out of a hole and no idea what is holding any of it up. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. When the bracket comes off and a new TV goes on a properly fixed mount on the same wall in the same visit, we price it as a new mount on that wall type, with the take-down added. If the old holes sit right where the new fixings need to go, we move the bracket a few inches or bridge the damage, and we tell you which before we start.

The third is particular to this neighborhood. Plenty of basements on the peninsula had a den or a playroom with a TV before October 2012, and some of those rooms were rebuilt while others were simply dried out, painted and left. If an old mount is still on a wall that sat in floodwater, we treat it as unknown: we look at the wall around every fixing, at what the fixings went into, and at whether the board or paneling behind it is still sound, and we do not hang a new screen on old holes in a wall we cannot vouch for. If a screen is leaning right now, do not push it back. If two people can lift it off safely, unplug it and take it down; otherwise leave it, keep people clear and call (347) 934-8335.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Manhattan Beach's Architecture

Almost every Manhattan Beach TV call comes from one of six kinds of building. They differ in what the walls are made of, how far the TV sits from the outlet and the internet, and how many other trades are in the house. Those three things decide the plan before anyone talks about brands or sizes.

The original brick house

Detached houses from the 1920s and 1930s, often Tudor or another European style, with plaster inside, solid brick outside and a fireplace in the living room.

The stucco house

Mediterranean-style houses with stucco over masonry or framing, deep window openings and arched doorways that leave fewer flat walls than you expect.

The enlarged house

An original house with additions on the back, the side or the top, where old plaster meets new drywall, sometimes on the very wall you want for the TV.

The rebuilt house

A new house where a small one stood, from about 4,000 to more than 15,000 square feet, often with the ground level built for flooding and the living floors above.

The attached and semi-detached house

Smaller homes that share a wall with a neighbor, where the best TV wall is often the shared one and the drilling has to respect the house next door.

The condo building

A small number of newer buildings such as The Wave at 102 West End Avenue, with a doorman, a management office and steel or wood framing behind the drywall.

The original brick house: thick plaster, a chimney breast and a living room built for a different kind of furniture

The houses that went up on the peninsula between the wars were built for radios and pianos, not 65-inch screens. The living room is usually across the front or along the side of the house, with a fireplace on one wall, windows on another and a wide opening into the hall or dining room on the third, so the obvious place for the TV is often over the mantel or on the one stretch of wall between two windows. Both work, and both need care. The interior walls are typically plaster, two or three coats on wood lath nailed to studs, which can add up to around an inch of material before the drill reaches anything that holds. The outside walls are often solid brick with plaster applied over it, and the chimney breast is brick all the way through.

The difference between those two walls is the whole price conversation. Plaster on framing is a $45 add-on on our list, and the fixing has to go through the plaster and the lath into the stud, with the pilot hole drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward around it. Brick is a $65 add-on, and the anchor has to sit in the brick, not in the mortar between bricks, which is softer in an old wall and can crumble around a fixing over the years. A brick chimney breast over a fireplace takes both the brick add-on and the $130 above-fireplace add-on, because it means working higher, protecting the mantel and planning a cable route through or around masonry that cannot be fished. We confirm which wall you have with one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, before the price is final.

A Brownstoner forum thread about older Brooklyn houses explains why we do not rely on a stud finder alone in these houses. One contributor pointed out that in many older homes, plaster was applied straight onto brick with no framing at all, and that knocking on plaster is a poor guide to what is behind it; another wrote that "most old homes front walls are constructed from older, unplaned framing," with studs that can sit more than an inch behind the surface. A scanner set for drywall reads that kind of wall badly or not at all, which is why our technicians use a deep-scan setting, a knock along the wall at screen height and the hidden pilot hole together.

The stucco house: deep openings, curved walls and a surface that does not forgive a wrong hole

Mediterranean and Spanish-style houses are part of the peninsula's original stock, with stucco outside, plaster inside, tile roofs, arched doorways and recessed windows. Inside, those curves and recesses leave fewer large flat walls than a plain brick house, and the flat ones are often the outside walls. What sits behind interior plaster on an outside wall varies from house to house: stucco over wood framing, stucco over block or brick, or masonry with furring strips. MantelMount's guidance for stucco covers both main cases, saying that over wood you drill through the stucco with a masonry bit until you reach the stud and then use a lag bolt, while over block you use concrete fasteners, and it recommends sealant where water is a risk.

Indoors, our concern is the inside face of that wall. A TV hung on the interior plaster of a stucco house is priced by what the pilot hole finds: plaster on framing at the plaster rate, plaster on brick or block at the brick rate. We never drill through to the outside, and we keep fixings away from the edges of window recesses, where the wall is carrying a lintel or a frame. If the only good wall is an outside wall with a deep window on each side, we show you with painter's tape how much room there really is, because a 75-inch screen between two arched windows can look cramped once it is up, and a slightly smaller one in the right place can look better than a bigger one in the wrong one.

The enlarged house: an old house and a new addition meeting somewhere behind your TV

A lot of Manhattan Beach houses were not torn down but added to: a second floor over a one-story wing, a family room built out into the back garden, a primary suite over the garage. That leaves houses where one room has walls of two different ages, and where a long family-room wall can be old plaster on the left and new drywall on the right with a seam somewhere in between that someone skim-coated so well you cannot see it. A stud finder that works on the new half gets nothing on the old half. A bracket that spans the seam can end up with two fixings in framing and two in a different material entirely.

We find the seam first. The knock changes from a hollow drywall sound to the duller sound of plaster, the scanner reading changes, and the pilot hole confirms it. If the bracket can sit entirely on one side, it does, and you pay only that side's add-on. If the screen has to be centered across the join, we fix each side by its own material and price the wall by the harder of the two, and we tell you that on the phone from your photos, not after drilling. Additions also explain a lot of strange wiring: an outlet that comes from the old house's circuit on one side of the room and the new one on the other, or a cable jack that was moved during the work and now leads nowhere. We trace what we need for the TV and leave the rest for your electrician.

The rebuilt house: the cheapest TV work happens before the drywall

When a small house is replaced by a large new one, the best time to plan the TVs is the week the framing is done and before insulation and drywall go up. At that stage we can do three things that are slow and messy later. We mark each screen position with the family standing in the room, because a TV spot drawn on a plan often moves once people see the windows and the view. We ask the builder to add solid wood blocking between the studs behind each bracket, so the fixings land in wood wherever the screen ends up across that wall. And we leave a recessed box behind the screen and a clear route down to the console or the equipment closet, with cable pulled, coiled and labeled, so the finished wall needs no cutting when the TV arrives. Power behind the screen in a new house is the electrician's job, on the house's circuits and under the house's electrical permit; we tell your electrician exactly where the TV will hang so the outlet ends up hidden behind it.

Rebuilt houses also bring the flood rules into the TV plan. In the newer houses built with the ground level for parking, access and storage, the city's guidance keeps utilities and equipment at or above the design flood elevation. A TV in a garage or a ground-floor entry may still be possible, but the equipment that runs it, the receiver, the streaming box, the network gear, belongs higher up, and the family room itself is almost always on the first living floor. We plan around your architect's drawings, not around a guess, and if the drawings show the flood elevation we use it.

The attached and semi-detached house: one good wall, and a neighbor on the other side of it

Brick Underground notes that alongside the detached houses, the peninsula has some attached and semi-detached homes, which it says start at a lower price than the detached ones. In these houses, the longest unbroken wall in the living room is often the one shared with the house next door. A shared masonry wall is usually an excellent place for a heavy screen, priced at the brick rate, but we drill only as deep as the anchor needs, keep every hole in the brick and away from the joints, and look for anything running along that wall first: a flue, a heating pipe, an old gas line. If you know what is on the other side, a kitchen, a closet, a bedroom, tell us; it changes where we put the soundbar as much as the TV.

Sound is the other half of a shared wall. A soundbar fixed to the wall under the screen sends less low-frequency sound into the floor than one sitting on a hollow console, but a shared wall carries sound of its own, so in these houses we set the bass and night modes during setup with you, at the volume you actually use. We are not acoustical engineers, and a soundbar cannot be made silent next door, but most complaints between neighbors come from bass turned up and sitting on the floor, and that part is easy to fix.

The condo building: a front desk, a management office and walls you share with the corridor

Condos are rare in Manhattan Beach, which is part of why the few that exist matter. The Wave at 102 West End Avenue is the clearest example: a seven-story building of 48 units built in 2016, with a doorman, a fitness center and a roof terrace. In a building of that kind, the TV job starts with the management office, not the wall. Brick Underground's guide to alteration agreements says contractors' insurance certificates should name the building, its unit owners and the managing agent, and that buildings commonly limit working hours and ask workers to show identification at the door. Hanging a TV is usually minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall kit may be treated differently, so we ask you to check the house rules before the visit, and we send the certificate free of charge.

Inside, the walls in a building of this age are typically drywall on framing, which may be wood or light-gauge steel; we do not assume either. A wall shared with the corridor or with the next unit may be a rated wall that the building will not let anyone open, so the in-wall kit goes into an interior partition if you want hidden power, and otherwise the cables run in a paintable cord cover or drop straight into the console. Many units face the water with large windows, so the glare from the bay in the afternoon is part of the decision about which wall the TV goes on, and we check it with tape on the wall at the time of day you usually watch, if we can.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Manhattan Beach Living Room

These notes describe how our technicians work in houses like the ones on the peninsula. They are our standard practice, not an account of any one address.

Ask the age of every wall. Before anything else, the technician asks when the house was built and whether this room was added or rebuilt later. In an enlarged house that one question tells us more than the scanner will.

Find the fireplace flue and the heat. If the TV is going over a mantel, we ask whether the fireplace is ever lit and look at how far the mantel projects, because heat and height both decide the bracket.

Knock, scan, then one hidden hole. The knock and a deep scan narrow it down; one small pilot hole behind the future bracket plate settles it. White dust then wood means plaster on lath. White dust then red grit means plaster on brick.

Tape it and sit down. Painter's tape marks the screen. You sit where you watch. We look for glare off the water and check the cable route to the console before a single fixing goes in.

Tell you before the price changes. If the wall is not what we priced from the photos, you hear it then, with the new figure, and you choose whether we carry on or move to another wall.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick, Stone, Stucco and New Framing: What Holds a TV on the Peninsula and What It Adds to the Price

The $179.99 base on our Brooklyn list covers labor for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs. In Manhattan Beach that describes the newest rooms in rebuilt houses and not much else, so here is each wall the peninsula actually has, what holds a TV in it, and what it adds. Every add-on exists because the wall needs different tools, different fixings or more time.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The standard wall of a house from between the wars: thin wood strips across the studs and several coats of plaster over them. MantelMount's own plaster guidance warns that plaster "is very thick and can crack more easily, so be careful as you drill," and recommends lag bolts into the studs behind plaster and lath. That matches what we do. The fixing goes through the plaster and the lath into the stud, after a slow pilot hole, and where a stud cannot be reached we use a toggle rated for the load rather than a plastic plug. On an Ask MetaFilter thread about a 55-inch TV on crumbly plaster, the agreed answer was the same: mount to the studs, and the state of the plaster matters much less.

Plaster on brick (priced at the brick rate). Outside walls and chimney breasts in the older houses are often brick with plaster laid straight onto it. Once the pilot hole shows red grit behind the plaster, the wall is priced as brick, and the anchors go into the brick through the plaster.

Brick or stone (+$65). Exposed brick, brick behind plaster, stone fireplace surrounds and stacked-stone accent walls in renovated family rooms are all on this line. Brick holds a TV very well when the anchor sits in the brick and not in the mortar. Natural stone varies: some is solid enough to take an anchor directly, and some veneers are a thin layer over another wall, in which case the fixing has to go through to whatever is behind. We check before choosing the anchor.

Above a fireplace (+$130). Any mount above a mantel, on top of the wall add-on. It covers working higher, protecting the mantel and the hearth, and planning the cables around masonry. If the fireplace is used in winter, we look at how much heat reaches the wall above it before agreeing the position, and we will tell you if we think a wall nearby is the better choice.

Stucco walls and block. Indoors, we price these by what the pilot hole finds behind the interior finish, and outside work on stucco is quoted separately from photos. Hiding the wires within New York City's rules

After "put it on the wall," the most common request is "and make the wires disappear." There are two different jobs hiding inside that request, and the city treats them very differently. The low-voltage cables, HDMI, network and the like, can be run inside a framed wall by a low-voltage installer. Power is another matter. The National Electrical Code does not allow a flexible cord, such as a TV's own power cord, to be concealed inside a wall, and the NYC Department of Buildings states that "no installation and modification to any electrical systems can be performed without a permit," with that work done by a licensed master electrician. So pushing the TV's cord into a hole behind the screen and pulling it out near the floor is not a shortcut; it is the one thing we will not do.

On a framed wall, the answer we install is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list including the kit. It has a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, its own rated cable inside the wall, and a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI and other cables. It does not connect to the house's electrical wiring, and it needs a hollow cavity between its two openings, so it suits plaster on lath and drywall but not brick, block or stone. An electricians' forum thread on exactly this point shows how unsettled the details can be in New York City, with one poster asking which in-wall TV power adapters are acceptable under the city's code and noting versions sold as compliant for New York. If your electrician or your building wants a true outlet on a circuit instead, that is the electrician's job under a permit, and we coordinate the position with them.

On masonry, the choices are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly up the surface and can be painted to match the wall, or a TV placed directly above a console so the cables drop straight down behind the furniture. In old houses with thick plaster on brick, the second is often the cleanest result of all. Angi's national cost guide puts hiding wires in the wall at close to $1,000 or more when it involves opening and repairing walls; on a framed wall in our list, the kit is $169, and on a brick wall the honest answer is that the wire does not go inside the wall at all.

Above the Flood Line

Ground Levels, Lower Levels and Equipment Closets: Where a TV Room Belongs in a House Rebuilt After 2012

Before Sandy, many Manhattan Beach basements were finished rooms: a den with a sectional and a big screen, a playroom with a console, an exercise room with a TV on the wall. Brick Underground's guide mentions listings with a finished basement family room and a 900-square-foot finished basement on Dover Street, so these rooms still exist. But the storm flooded many of them, and the rules for rebuilt and substantially improved houses in the flood zone now steer what can go below the design flood elevation. The Department of City Planning's retrofitting guide lists the permitted uses there as building access, crawl space, storage and vehicular parking, and says utilities and equipment belong at or above that elevation, or must otherwise be protected from water.

For a TV plan, that means three practical things. First, in a new or substantially improved house, the family room and its TV usually go on the first living floor, not the ground level, and the architect's drawings will already show that. Second, the equipment that runs the TVs, the router, the network switch, the receiver, the streaming boxes, should live above the flood elevation too, in a ventilated closet or cabinet on a living floor, with cables running down to any screen below rather than the other way round. A TV in a garage for watching a game while working on the car is not unusual, but the screen there is the cheap part; the equipment that would be expensive to lose stays upstairs. Third, in an older house with a finished basement that was never substantially improved, the rules may not require any change, but nothing stops you from following the same logic: screen on the wall if you want one, equipment on a high shelf or upstairs, and power strips off the floor.

We are not your architect or your code consultant, and we do not decide what a basement or a ground level may be used for. We read the drawings you have, ask your builder or architect where the flood elevation is in your house, and put the equipment above it. If you do not know whether your house was substantially improved under the flood rules, your architect, expediter or the city's records will; it is worth finding out before you spend money on a lower-level media room.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Manhattan Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Manhattan Beach owners actually describe: houses being rebuilt, rooms where two building ages meet, outside walls of stucco and brick, flooded dens, glass walls facing the bay and rooms that do two jobs. Houses being built or rebuilt

Our new house on one of the lettered streets is framed and the builder wants to close the walls in two weeks. Should the framer put solid wood between the studs where the TVs will hang?

Yes, and it is the single cheapest thing you can do for every future TV in the house. Ask the framer for horizontal wood blocking between the studs behind each planned screen, wide enough to cover the whole area where the bracket might end up, because positions often shift by a foot once the room is finished and furnished. With blocking behind the drywall, every fixing lands in solid wood, the bracket can sit exactly where the screen looks best rather than where two studs happen to be, and the wall is priced at our base rate rather than needing toggles or extra work. Take photos of the open wall with a tape measure in the frame before it closes, and keep them; they become the map we use on installation day.

The architect's plan shows an outlet behind every TV in our rebuilt house. Should your visit come before or after the electrician's, and who handles the permit?

Before, if you can arrange it, and the permit belongs to the electrician. The NYC Department of Buildings requires electrical work to be done by a licensed master electrician under a permit, so the outlet behind each screen is part of the house's electrical job, not ours. What we add is the position: we mark the center of each screen and the bracket's footprint with you and the electrician in the room, so each outlet ends up hidden behind the TV rather than six inches above it, and we leave our low-voltage cable and recessed boxes beside the electrician's work. If the walls are already closed by the time the TVs arrive, we fall back on an in-wall power and cable kit at $169 on framed walls, which plugs into an existing outlet and does not touch the house's wiring.

Half of our family room is the original 1930s house and half is an addition from about fifteen years ago, and the long wall for the TV runs across both. How do you fix a bracket over the join?

First we find where the join actually is, because a good skim coat hides it completely. The knock changes from the hollow sound of drywall to the duller sound of old plaster, the scanner reading shifts, and one pilot hole on each side confirms both materials. If the bracket can sit entirely on one side and the screen still looks centered, we do that and charge only that side's add-on. If it has to straddle the join, each fixing is chosen for the material it goes into, screws into the new studs on one side and fixings through plaster into framing or brick on the other, and the wall is priced by the harder of the two. You hear which from your photos before booking, and it is confirmed before any real drilling starts.

Old walls, outside walls and water

The wall we want for the TV is an outside wall of our Mediterranean-style house, with stucco on the outside and plaster on the inside. What actually holds the TV in a wall like that?

It depends on what is between the stucco and the plaster, and that varies from house to house. Some of these walls are stucco over wood framing, some are stucco over brick or block, and some are masonry with furring strips inside carrying the plaster. We find out with a pilot hole from the inside, where the bracket plate will cover it. Over framing, the fixings go through the plaster into the studs and the wall is priced at the plaster rate; over brick or block, masonry anchors go into the masonry and it is priced at the brick rate. We never drill through to the stucco face, we keep fixings away from the edges of the window recesses, and if the wall turns out to be furring strips over masonry we fix through them into the masonry rather than relying on the strips.

Our living room plaster has a hand-troweled, swirled texture from when the house was built. Will a paintable cord cover sit flat on it, or will it look stuck on?

A cord cover is a straight, smooth channel, and on a strongly textured wall it cannot follow every ridge, so there will be small gaps along its edges. On light texture, a thin bead of paintable caulk along both edges and a coat of the wall color make it nearly disappear. On heavy, swirled plaster it will always read as a channel, and we will say so from your photos. In that case the better options are usually a TV placed directly above the console so the cables drop straight behind the furniture with no cover at all, or, if the wall behind the plaster is framed, an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) so the only visible openings are behind the screen and behind the console. The cord cover is $59 on our list if you still prefer it.

Our basement den was under water in 2012. It was dried out and painted, and the old TV bracket is still on the wall. Can the new TV go on the same bracket?

Not until we have looked at what is behind it, and quite often the answer is no. A wall that sat in floodwater may have paneling, drywall or furring that softened, swelled or rotted around the fixings, and paint hides all of that. We look at the wall around each fixing, check what the fixings went into and whether they still bite, and see whether the bracket itself is rated for the new TV's weight and bolt pattern. If the bracket and the wall are sound, we hang the new TV on it at the price for that wall type. If not, the old bracket comes off and the new one goes into sound material, which may mean moving it. And whatever happens with the screen, we suggest putting the equipment that runs it, the cable box, the receiver and the power strip, on a high shelf or upstairs rather than on the floor.

Condos, glass and light

Our condo on West End Avenue has floor-to-ceiling windows facing the water, and the only solid wall gets the afternoon sun bouncing off the bay. Can the TV be placed or angled so we can actually see it at four o'clock?

The wall facing our bed is a run of built-in closets with a narrow panel between the doors. Can the TV go on the closet wall?

Not on the doors, and rarely on a narrow panel between them. Closet doors move, and a cabinet face or a thin panel is not built to carry a TV's weight on a bracket that pulls away from it. What can work is the solid wall above the closets, if there is enough height between the top of the doors and the ceiling and you are happy with the screen higher than usual, in which case a tilting bracket brings it down toward the bed. The other answer is the wall beside the closets, with the TV turned toward the bed on an articulating arm. We look at the closet construction from your photos, because some built-ins are framed walls with doors set into them and some are furniture standing in front of a wall, and that decides which of these is possible.

Rooms that do two jobs

It can, and in a big room with hard floors and lots of glass the sound is usually the part that needs most attention. A rug and soft furniture do more for echo than any speaker, and we will say so when we hear the room.

Our TV wall already has a thermostat, an alarm keypad and a bank of light switches on it. Can the TV go there without moving them?

Often, if the screen is sized and placed around them, and that is worth trying first because moving any of them is someone else's job. Light switches are electrical work for a licensed electrician. A thermostat may need its wire extended and has to stay where it reads the room fairly, not behind a warm TV. An alarm keypad belongs to the alarm company, or to us if we installed the system, and it must stay reachable at the door. We tape the screen outline with every device in view, check that the TV will not cover or overheat the thermostat, and keep the keypad and switches clear. "Plaster anchors will hold it"

Some answers suggest heavy-duty plastic anchors or small toggles in plaster when a stud is not where you want the TV.

Plaster is hard on the surface and brittle behind it, and a fixing that grips only the plaster can loosen over months, especially under an arm that gets pulled out and pushed back. The consensus on an Ask MetaFilter thread about a TV on crumbly plaster was blunt: put the mount on the studs, and then the plaster's condition matters much less.

Where a stud cannot be reached in a lath wall, we use a toggle rated for the load and the bracket's full set of fixing points, and on a heavy screen or an arm we move the bracket to framing or masonry instead.

"Over the fireplace is the natural spot"

Design-oriented answers often treat the space over a mantel as the obvious home for the TV.

In a peninsula living room with a real fireplace, it can be, but it is usually higher than comfortable, and the chimney breast is masonry that cannot hide a cable. On a Brownstoner thread about a TV over a decorative fireplace, one contributor warned that a mantel-height mount is "awfully high and invites a sore neck," and noted that fireplaces on party walls often have no studs, only plaster over brick.
