The Neighborhood

Why Ocean Parkway Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Ocean Parkway is a road first and a neighborhood second, and the camera work follows from that. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux laid it out in the 1870s as a grand boulevard running about five and a half miles from the Prospect Park end down to Surf Avenue in Brighton Beach. It is 210 feet across, with a 70-foot central roadway, two landscaped malls with benches and the country's first bike path, and a service road on each side. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it a scenic landmark on January 28, 1975. The landmark is the parkway itself: the roadway, malls, paths and rows of maple, oak, sycamore and elm. The homes and apartment buildings that line it are private property, and they are what we put cameras on.

Those buildings fall into a few clear groups. Along much of the parkway between Kensington and Gravesend stand large detached and semi-detached houses, many of them rebuilt or enlarged; The Real Deal reported in 2017 that city records showed 35 homes on the road enlarged since 1995, and that most owners belonged to the Sephardic community, which wants to live within walking distance of family, schools and synagogues. Between the houses, and in long runs near Kensington, Midwood, Homecrest and the southern end, are six-story apartment buildings from the 1920s and 1930s, postwar red-brick co-ops, and a handful of newer condos. Shuls, yeshivas and schools sit along the parkway and on the side streets. And the shopping is not on the parkway at all but on the avenues and streets that cross it or run beside it, from Church Avenue at the north end to Kings Highway, Avenue U and Coney Island Avenue further south.

What the record shows about crime here is mostly about getting into buildings. In October 2015, Bklyner reported that the 66th Precinct's commander, Captain Kenneth M. Quick, told the precinct community council that "in the past 28 days there has been a surge in Kensington burglaries," with burglars coming in through fire-escape windows in the Ocean Parkway area from Ditmas Avenue to Fort Hamilton Parkway. The precinct's own message was blunt: "male committing burglaries around Ocean Pkwy. Call 911 for susp person on fire escapes and backyards." A resident reported a sixth-floor apartment, not even next to a fire escape, entered through the top half of a window, possibly from the roof. In October 2024, BoroPark24 reported that a person entered a residential building near Ocean Parkway and Cortelyou Road in the afternoon, took a credit card from an envelope in the building's mail area and ran up about $1,859 in purchases. In August 2025, Hoodline reported a person who got into an apartment building near Ocean Parkway and Avenue M at about 4:40 a.m., pried open a mailbox and took a checkbook.

None of those cases needed a broken front door. They needed a way in behind someone, an unwatched mail area, or a window off a fire escape or a yard that nobody looks at. That is why we start a building's camera plan at the vestibule and the mailbox wall, then the rear yard and fire-escape lines, then the cellar and service doors, and only then the street. For a house, the order shifts: the side driveway and the back come before the front steps, because the front of a parkway house is the side most people are already looking at. The record also includes the street itself. In December 2025, police released surveillance video after a man allegedly slammed a 12-year-old boy's head into a car windshield in front of 510 Ocean Parkway on a weekday afternoon, and News 12 Brooklyn ran video of a 1:30 a.m. crash on the Kensington stretch that appeared to knock down a city traffic camera pole. Private cameras facing the service road and the curb are often what investigators ask for after events like these.

Ocean Parkway facts that shape a camera plan

  • A landmark road, private buildings: the parkway was designated a scenic landmark on January 28, 1975, and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983; the designation covers the roadway, malls and paths, not the houses and buildings beside them.
  • Fire escapes: in 2015 the 66th Precinct linked a burglary surge to fire-escape windows in the Ocean Parkway area between Ditmas Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway, per Bklyner.
  • The mail area: police appeals in 2024 (near Cortelyou Road) and 2025 (near Avenue M) both involved people inside apartment buildings going for mail, a credit card in one case and a checkbook in the other.
  • Parking indoors: under the Special Ocean Parkway District zoning, accessory parking for new developments with parkway frontage "shall be completely enclosed," so many newer buildings have indoor garages to cover.
  • No trucks on the parkway: NYC DOT lists Ocean Parkway among roads where commercial vehicles are not allowed, so deliveries and service vans for parkway buildings arrive on the side streets.
  • Older buildings, open violations: Openigloo lists 1609 Ocean Parkway (built 1929, 59 units) with 29 open HPD violations and 1850 Ocean Parkway (built 1938, 70 units) with 86, so a camera plan in buildings like these often has to work around ongoing repairs.

Many Ocean Parkway building owners who ask us about cameras also ask about the entry door itself, because a camera records who followed a resident in while a door that closes and locks properly keeps them out. We plan the vestibule cameras around whatever intercom or entry panel the building has, so the two work together rather than staring at each other. The Parkway Face

The Parkway Side and the Street Side: Planning Cameras for a Property With Two Fronts

Most Brooklyn camera plans have one front. An Ocean Parkway property usually has two. The parkway face is formal, set back behind a deep front yard, looking across a service road and a mall of trees toward the main roadway. The other face, on a numbered street or an avenue, is where the garage door, the service entrance, the trash area and often the real day-to-day front door are. Here is how each part of that layout changes what a camera can and should see.

The service road and the curb

The service road is where residents park, where guests are dropped off, where packages are carried in from a double-parked van and where a car gets clipped at night. A camera on the house or the building, aimed down at the curb in front of your own property, records who stopped and who got out. It does not need to see across the mall to the main roadway, and on most parkway lots it cannot, because the mall's rows of trees are in the way for half the year. We frame the curb camera on your own frontage, from the edge of the driveway to the edge of the lot, and set it to record continuously rather than on motion, because the steady stream of cars and bikes along the service road would otherwise trigger it all day. If you want plate detail on cars that stop, that is a second, narrower camera aimed at the stopping spot, not a wider lens on the first one.

The malls, the benches and the bike path

The malls belong to the city, and they are part of the scenic landmark. We do not mount anything on mall trees, benches, lampposts or the bike-path fencing, and we do not aim private cameras to study people sitting on the benches. A curb camera will inevitably show some of the nearer mall and the people passing along it; that is fine, and it is often exactly the context a detective wants. What we avoid is a camera whose main job is watching the public mall rather than your own frontage. Owners sometimes ask for a long zoom across to the far side of the parkway. Between the trees, the distance and the headlights on the main roadway at night, it rarely shows anything useful, and we will say so before you pay for it.

The deep front yard and the walk to the door

The zoning rules for the parkway keep a large yard between the sidewalk and the house, with no porches or stairs allowed to push into it, so the front door of a parkway house is a long walk from the street. For cameras, that means the doorway camera and the curb camera are two separate jobs. A single high camera on the facade trying to cover both ends up showing small figures at the curb and the tops of heads at the door. We put the door camera close to the entrance, a little above head height, and give the curb its own view. On houses where the front walk is lined with plantings or a low wall, a third view along the walk covers anyone who cuts across the yard to the side of the house.

The side street, the driveway and the garage door

On a corner lot, the side street is where the cars actually come and go. Zoning requires driveways in the parkway front yard to run straight in from the street, and many houses have their garage or driveway on the side street instead. The camera that matters here is the one watching the driveway and the gap beside the house, because that is the way into the back that the street cannot see. We mount it high on the side wall near the front corner, aimed back along the driveway, and a second camera at the back corner covers the yard and the back door.

Trees, leaves and the winter view

The parkway's trees are the reason people love living on it, and they change a camera's picture twice a year. A view that is clear in January can be half leaves by June, and a branch swaying in front of a lens at night will set off motion alerts until you turn them off. We look at the trees in front of every camera position, aim below the canopy where we can, and set motion zones that ignore the top of the frame. Where a summer canopy will block a view entirely, we tell you before mounting, not after the first spring.

Large detached parkway houses: a long front, a hidden side

The older houses along the parkway are big, often brick or stucco, with a center or side entrance well back from the sidewalk and a driveway down one side to a garage or a paved yard. The walls are usually masonry, so each outdoor camera means a hole drilled through brick or block from inside, sealed and sleeved, with the cable coming straight out behind the camera base. The attic and the basement are the cable routes: down from the attic for the front and side eaves, through the basement for the back wall. On these houses we usually recommend five or six cameras for a first system: the front door, the service-road curb, the driveway from the front corner, the back door and yard from the rear corner, and one inside the garage if there is one. The recorder goes in a basement or closet away from the front door, so a person coming in does not find it in a minute.

Rebuilt and enlarged homes: wire it before the walls close

Ocean Parkway has seen a long run of teardowns and expansions; The Real Deal counted 35 homes on the road enlarged under Department of Buildings permits between 1995 and 2017. If your house is in that process, the cheapest time to plan cameras is before the drywall goes up. We meet the general contractor or the electrician, mark camera positions on the plans or on the framing, and pull cable to each one, coiled and labeled, along with a home run to a closet where the recorder and switch will live. The cameras themselves can go up at the end of the job, when the exterior is finished and the stone or stucco is on. Owners who do this end up with no visible wire anywhere, which also means no wire anyone can cut. We work as a low-voltage trade alongside the builder; we do not do the electrical work, so if a camera position needs a new outlet, that goes on the electrician's list.

Side-street two-families: two households, one set of cameras

On the numbered streets off the parkway, two-family and semi-detached houses are common, with the owner in one unit and a tenant or relatives in the other. The cameras on shared doors, the driveway and the yard belong to whoever owns the house, but the people living there will want to know where they point. We can set up one recorder with separate accounts so each household sees its own door and the shared areas, or two small systems if the households prefer to keep things separate. Semi-detached houses have one party wall and one open side; the open side gets the driveway camera, and the back wall gets a camera aimed at your half of the yard.

Prewar six-story elevator buildings: vestibule, mail, fire escape, cellar

These are the buildings where the recent police appeals took place, and they share a layout: an outer door, a small vestibule, an inner door, a lobby with the mailboxes on one wall, a single elevator, a stair, fire escapes at the back or side, and a cellar with the boiler, laundry and storage. Openigloo lists 1609 Ocean Parkway as built in 1929 with 59 units on six floors, 1777 Ocean Parkway as built in 1926 with 75 units, and 2901 Ocean Parkway as built in 1926 with 60 units. Cable in these buildings runs up existing risers and through the cellar ceiling, and we agree the route with the super before we start. The first cameras are the vestibule face camera and the mailbox wall; the next are the rear yard and fire-escape lines, the cellar door, the laundry room and the ground-floor elevator landing. The recorder goes in a locked room the owner chooses, not the super's apartment.

Postwar co-ops: doormen, garages and a board decision

The larger postwar co-ops on the parkway often already have a doorman and a few cameras. Nest Seekers lists The Park Tower at 370 Ocean Parkway as a 12-floor, 131-unit co-op with a 24-hour doorman, outdoor parking, a waitlist for indoor parking, storage and a bike room. HL Real Estate lists 820 Ocean Parkway as a six-story, 72-unit co-op with a full-time doorman, a live-in super, a garage and a bike room. In buildings like these, the job is usually adding cameras where the doorman cannot see: the garage, the bike room, the laundry, the package area, the service door and the roof door, and replacing an old recorder that nobody can get clips off. The board decides where cameras go and who sees them, and we write the proposal so it can be read at a board meeting.

Newer condos: an intercom with a camera, and everything else

Newer buildings usually come with a video intercom at the street door, as listings for the 24-residence Ocean 24 Condominium at 379 Ocean Parkway describe, along with an enclosed garage and storage units. The intercom's camera sees whoever presses the button; it does not see the garage ramp, the storage room, the roof deck, the gym or the trash room. We add cameras to those areas on a separate recorder or one the condo controls, and we leave the intercom system and its network alone unless the board asks us to tie them together.

Kings Highway: shared facades and apartments above

Many Kings Highway stores sit under apartments that belong to someone else. The store wants its door, counter and stockroom covered; the building owner wants the residential door and the sidewalk. Two systems on one facade can work if they are planned together, so that the store's door camera does not stare into the residential entrance and neither looks up into the apartment windows. Inside, the plan starts with a face camera just inside the door, a counter camera behind the register aimed at the customer side, and an overview camera high at the front looking back through the store. A stockroom or basement stair gets its own camera.

Avenue U and Coney Island Avenue: long stores and back doors

Stores on these avenues are often long and narrow, with shelves or racks down the middle and a back door onto a lot, an alley or a loading area. After-hours trouble usually starts at the back, and daytime trouble at the counter. We put one camera just inside the back door and one outside it looking along the rear wall, and keep the counter camera on continuous recording rather than motion, because in a busy store motion recording is either always on or set so loosely that it misses things. Bright windows behind a customer can turn a face into a silhouette, so we set the door camera's exposure for the people walking in, and check it on site at midday.

McDonald Avenue: yards, gates and the elevated line

Under the elevated tracks, contractors' yards, supply houses and workshops keep vans, tools and stock behind gates. The camera plan here covers the gate from inside the yard, the doors to any building on the lot, and the van parking. Long yards often need a varifocal camera zoomed on site to the far end, and cable runs across a yard go in conduit or overhead rather than lying on the ground. The noise and vibration of the trains overhead are no problem for the cameras, but they do mean motion alerts should be set on people, not on movement in general.

Rules and limits for business cameras near Ocean Parkway

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance; ask any installer for both before work starts. Facial recognition. New York City's biometric identifier law requires retail stores, food and drink businesses and places of entertainment that collect biometric data, such as facial recognition, to post a clear and conspicuous sign at their entrances, and bars them from selling or profiting from that data. Ordinary recording cameras do not collect biometric data, and we leave face-matching features switched off unless the owner has taken advice and posted the sign. Restrooms and changing areas. No camera views a restroom, fitting room or changing area, and we will not install one that does. Sound. We leave audio recording off unless the owner has taken advice on it. Deliveries. Because commercial vehicles are barred from Ocean Parkway, our vans park on the side streets, and we plan the work so ladders and cable are carried in from there. Public property. We do not mount private cameras on street trees, mall lampposts, light poles or the elevated structure.

Apartment Buildings

Vestibules, Mail Areas and Fire Escapes: A Camera Plan for an Ocean Parkway Apartment Building

The most useful camera plan for an Ocean Parkway apartment building follows the path a person takes from the sidewalk to a mailbox, an apartment window or a cellar storage room. Here is that path, step by step, with what each camera has to show and the building conditions we plan around.

The outer door and the vestibule

In both recent police appeals, the person got into a residential building first. The records do not say how; in many buildings like these, it is simply by walking in behind a resident or waiting for the door to drift shut too slowly. The vestibule camera's job is a clear face of everyone who comes through the inner door, and especially of anyone who comes through right behind someone else. We mount it inside, above and slightly to one side of the inner door, aimed back at the person walking toward it, so the light behind them from the street does not blank out the face. If the building has an intercom panel with its own camera, we place ours so the two complement each other: the intercom sees whoever rings, our camera sees whoever actually enters.

The mailbox wall

The 2024 theft near Cortelyou Road came from a mailing envelope in the building's mail area. The 2025 burglary near Avenue M involved a mailbox pried open at about 4:40 a.m. A camera on the mailbox wall needs to show three things: the face of whoever is standing there, the hands, and which box they are at. That usually means a camera mounted across the lobby from the mailboxes, at a height that sees over the head of a person standing at them and down onto the boxes, plus the vestibule camera's face shot as they arrive. We set it to record continuously, because a slow, careful theft at a mailbox may not move enough to trigger motion recording. The camera covers the boxes and the people at them; it does not read the names on the boxes or the mail in a resident's hands in detail, and the building's footage policy decides who can review it.

The lobby ceiling, leaks and where equipment goes

In April 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported that water had been coming through the lobby ceiling at 514 Ocean Parkway for nearly two months, soaking mail and packages, while the building's single elevator was out for about three weeks. Lily Schwab, a tenant of more than 50 years, told the station: "Nothing gets fixed. Nothing gets done." For a camera installer, the lesson is practical: in an older building with known leaks, we keep cameras, power supplies and cable joints out from under the wet spots, mount lobby cameras on walls rather than on a sagging ceiling, and put the recorder somewhere dry and locked, not in a cellar corner beside the boiler. A camera that records the leak, and when it started, can also be useful to a tenants' association or an owner trying to show HPD what happened.

The rear yard and the fire escapes

The 2015 surge the 66th Precinct described came in through fire-escape windows, and in one case through a sixth-floor window reached from above. A camera on the building's own rear wall, mounted at the second or third floor and aimed along the line of fire escapes and down into the yard, shows anyone climbing onto the ironwork or moving along it. It must not look into any apartment. We aim along the face of the wall, not at the windows, and use the recorder's privacy masking to black out any window that cannot be kept out of frame. A second camera at roof level aimed at the roof door and the top of the fire escape covers the route from above. In a courtyard building, the courtyard gets its own camera, framed on the ground and the base of the fire escapes.

The cellar, the laundry room and the bike room

Cellars are where storage cages, bikes and strollers are broken into, where packages are sometimes stacked, and where residents do laundry alone at night. We put a camera at the cellar door or the bottom of the cellar stair, one on the laundry room's door side, and one on the bike room or storage area. The cellar is also where the cable for the rest of the building usually runs, so we plan these cameras together with the riser routes. The recorder often ends up in a locked cellar room, which is fine as long as it is dry, ventilated and behind a door that is not left open for the boiler service.

Who decides, and who gets to see

In a rental building, the owner decides where common-area cameras go and who can review them. A tenant who wants a camera in the hall needs the landlord's permission; attorney Jeffrey Reich told Brick Underground in 2022 that a common-area camera should be positioned so it would not film inside any apartment, and that without permission, a tenant should not install one. In a co-op, the board decides; Habitat Magazine reported in 2023 that there is no city law banning doorbell cameras in apartment hallways, but co-op rules govern, and shareholders need board approval for anything in a common area. FirstService Residential's 2024 guidance for New York co-ops and condos suggests keeping footage for about 30 to 90 days, limiting access to the manager or a security provider, and logging every request. We set the recorder up to match whatever policy the building adopts, with separate accounts for the super, the agent and the board contact.

Field Notes: Starting an Ocean Parkway Building Walk-Through at the Mailboxes

This describes how we approach buildings here, not one particular job. On an Ocean Parkway apartment building, we start inside the vestibule, not on the sidewalk. We stand at the mailbox wall and ask the super three questions: when does the mail arrive, who has keys to the inner door, and where do packages end up when nobody is home. Then we watch the inner door close by itself, and time it. From there we go down to the cellar and find where the risers are, because that decides where every other camera's cable can run. The 2024 theft near Ocean Parkway and Cortelyou Road started with a credit card taken from an envelope in the mail area. If our building only has a camera on the street door, would it have helped?

Partly. A street-door camera shows who came into the building and when, which is a start. It does not show which mailbox they went to, what they took or whether they were the person who later used the card. A second camera aimed at the mailbox wall ties the face at the door to the hands at the boxes, and that is what turns a guess into something police can act on. In most prewar Ocean Parkway lobbies, both cameras run to the same recorder for little more than the cost of one extra run.

The August 2025 mailbox burglary near Avenue M happened at about 4:40 in the morning. What should a lobby camera be set to do in the hours before dawn?

Record continuously, see faces in low light, and send an alert to the right phone. Lobby lights in older buildings are often dimmed or partly out by early morning, so we check the picture at night and add infrared or a brighter fixture where needed. We can set an alert for any person at the mailbox wall between, say, midnight and 6 a.m., sent to the super or the agent rather than every tenant. That way someone knows the same morning, not days later when the checkbook is missed.

In 2015 the 66th Precinct said burglars were getting in through fire-escape windows near Ocean Parkway. Can the landlord put a camera on the fire escape itself?

We do not mount cameras on the fire escape ironwork, because it is an exit route that must stay clear and it would put the camera right outside a tenant's window. The better spot is the building's rear wall, at a floor where the camera can look along the line of fire escapes and down into the yard without facing any window. That records someone climbing up or moving along the ironwork, and the recorder's privacy masks black out any window that cannot be kept out of view.

A tenant in our 1920s building wants to put her own camera in the hallway facing her door. As the owner, what should we say?

The hallway is a common area, so it is your decision, and the safest answer is a written yes or no rather than a shrug. If you allow it, the usual conditions are that it faces only her own door, does not look into any other apartment when a neighbor's door opens, is removable without damage, and does not record sound. Many owners prefer to install one building camera on that floor instead, which solves the tenant's worry without creating a precedent for a camera at every door.

Our co-op board on the parkway is fielding complaints about a shareholder's video doorbell. Can the board require us to take ours down too?

It can adopt a house rule on video doorbells, and an attorney told Brick Underground in 2022 that a board can pass one without a shareholder vote; some boards ban them, others only bar cameras that face directly into other apartments. If yours goes that way, the usual alternative is a building camera on each hallway, managed by the board. If the rule only limits the angle, we can re-aim or replace a doorbell so it shows your own doorway and nothing across the hall.

The 2017 Real Deal story said dozens of homes on the parkway have been enlarged. We are rebuilding ours now. When should cameras come into the plan?

Before the walls are closed, ideally when the electrician is roughing in. We mark camera positions on the plans or the framing and pull labeled cable to each one, plus a run to the closet where the recorder will go. The cameras themselves go up after the facade is finished. Doing it this way means nothing shows on the outside, and you are not drilling through new stone or stucco a year later. If the house is still at the permit stage, a camera layout sheet can go to the builder with the low-voltage drawings.

Our house faces the parkway, but our driveway and garage open onto the side street. Which side should get cameras first?

The side street, in most cases. The parkway front is visible to everyone walking on the mall and the service road, while the driveway and the gap beside the house are the way to the back of the lot that nobody sees. We would start with a camera at the front corner aimed down the driveway, one at the back corner on the yard and back door, and the front door after that. A service-road curb camera comes next if you park on the parkway side.

Can a camera on our parkway house record who parks and gets out on the service road in front of us?

Yes, if it is aimed at the curb along your own frontage and set to record continuously. Mounted on the facade or at the eave, framed from one edge of your lot to the other, it shows cars that stop, people who get out and which way they walk. It will catch some of the passing traffic and the nearer mall in the background, which is normal. For plates, a second narrow camera aimed at the main stopping spot works better than trying to zoom one wide camera.

In December 2025, police released video after a boy was attacked in front of 510 Ocean Parkway in the afternoon. If something happens on the sidewalk in front of our building, how quickly can we get the clip to detectives?

The same day, if the recorder is set up properly and someone in the building knows how to export. We show the owner, the super or a board contact how to find a time window on the right camera and copy it to a USB stick or a phone in a few minutes. If nobody on site is comfortable doing it, call us and we will help copy it before it is overwritten.

The Kensington end of the parkway had a 1:30 a.m. crash in December 2025 that knocked down a traffic camera pole. Will a city camera like that cover our car on the service road?

Do not count on it. City traffic cameras are aimed at the main roadway for traffic enforcement and monitoring, not at private cars parked along a service road, and the one in that crash was knocked over. A camera on your own building, aimed at your frontage, is the only view of your car you control. Car damage at night is often a sideswipe from the service road itself, so the curb camera should see the side of the car facing the traffic, not just the plate.

Our yeshiva's front entrance is on the parkway, but buses unload on the side street. How should the cameras split between the two?

Each door in use gets its own face camera, and the bus area gets an overview camera that shows children getting off and walking toward the door. The parkway entrance is often used by parents and visitors, the side entrance by staff and buses, so both need faces. Alerts are set for the hours the building should be empty, and the recorder is sized so a second phase of hallway cameras can be added later without replacing it.

Our building has an enclosed garage under it, as the parkway zoning requires for newer buildings. Should the garage cameras be on the same recorder as the lobby?

Usually yes, on the same recorder but with separate accounts, so the doorman or super can watch the lobby while the board or a garage manager reviews the garage. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Ocean Parkway

The claims below turn up in AI-written answers, cost guides and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack when people ask about cameras for a Brooklyn building or house. Where a claim comes from a named source, we say so. Each is followed by what we see on Ocean Parkway properties.

"Ocean Parkway is a landmark, so you can't put cameras on the buildings."

The scenic landmark designated in 1975 is the parkway itself: the roadway, the malls, the paths and the trees. The Historic Districts Council's description of the designation covers those features, not the homes and apartment buildings that face them.

What that means in practice is that the malls, benches and lampposts are off limits for private equipment, which we would not use anyway. The walls of your house or building are yours, subject to your board, your landlord or your lease.

If a particular building sits inside a separate historic district or is an individual landmark, its own rules apply, and we check that before quoting. For most properties on the parkway, the approval that matters is the board's or the owner's.

"Security camera installation in Brooklyn averages about $1,400."

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn guide gives an average of $1,416 and a usual range of $680 to $2,145 for camera installation or repair. That is a reasonable picture of a small house job, and it says very little about a six-story building.

An Ocean Parkway elevator building with a vestibule, a mailbox wall, a rear yard, fire escapes, a roof door, a cellar and a laundry room may need eight to twelve cameras and cable up a riser, which is commercial work. A big detached house with a garage and a long driveway may need six.

That is why we publish starting prices, from $850 for four cameras installed, and then quote each property in writing with every camera and run on its own line, so you can see what is driving the number.

"Put a camera over the front door and you're covered."

On an Ocean Parkway building, the recent police appeals were about what happened inside, at the mail area, after someone was already through the door. The 2015 burglaries came in from fire escapes. A front-door camera alone would have recorded the arrival and nothing else.

On a parkway house, the front door is the most visible part of the lot. The side driveway, the gap beside the house and the back door are where nobody on the mall or the service road can see.

The front-door camera is worth having, but on most properties here, it is the third or fourth camera, not the first.

"Wireless cameras work anywhere."

They work where the Wi-Fi reaches. In a masonry apartment building, a router in one apartment or the super's office rarely reaches the rear yard, the cellar or an enclosed garage, and a battery camera on a fire-escape wall will need climbing up to recharge.

A wired camera gets its power and its connection through one cable, records to a recorder in the building whether the internet is up or not, and does not run flat in January.

We use wireless only where a cable truly cannot go, and say so on the quote. For lobbies, rear walls, cellars and garages, we wire.

"A tenant can put a camera anywhere outside their own door."

Not in a common hallway without permission. Brick Underground's 2022 expert answer was that a renter needs the landlord's permission for a camera in a common area, and that it must be placed so it would not film inside any apartment.

In a co-op, Habitat Magazine reported in 2023 that board approval is needed for anything in a common hallway, and Brick Underground reported in 2022 that boards can adopt a ban on video doorbells as a house rule.

Inside your own apartment or house, you have far more freedom. For anything in a hall, a lobby or on a building's facade, ask first, and we will help you write the request.

"Keep footage forever, just in case."

Buildings that keep everything forever end up with drives that fail without anyone noticing, and a pile of recordings of residents that nobody has a reason to hold. FirstService Residential's guide for New York co-ops and condos suggests about 30 to 90 days, with a plan for deleting footage after that.

The right number depends on how quickly incidents come to light. A missing check may not be noticed for weeks, so a mailbox camera in an apartment building benefits from a month or more.

We size the recorder's drives to the retention the board or owner chooses, and longer storage is a one-time upgrade on the recorder, not a subscription.

"Facial recognition is the best way to secure a store."

It is the most regulated. New York City's biometric identifier law requires retail stores, food and drink businesses and places of entertainment that collect biometric data to post a clear and conspicuous sign at their entrances, and makes it unlawful to sell or profit from that data, with a right for customers to sue.

For a shop on Kings Highway or Avenue U, ordinary cameras that record clear faces at the door and the counter do the job most owners need, without collecting biometric data at all.

If a store wants face matching, we will explain what the law asks for and leave that decision with the owner and their adviser. By default, we leave those features switched off.
