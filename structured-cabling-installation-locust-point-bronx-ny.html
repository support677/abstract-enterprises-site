The Neighborhood

Why Locust Point Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

On Locust Point, the internet reaches the house easily. What it rarely reaches is the far bedroom, the upstairs office or the rental on the lower level, and the spot where it does arrive is often the lowest, wettest corner of the building. That combination is what makes cabling here its own kind of job. The point is small: Wikipedia and City-Data put it at about 0.416 square miles, bounded by Harding Avenue on the north, the Throgs Neck Bridge on the east and Eastchester Bay, with CityNeighborhoods.nyc adding Hammond Creek on the west. NYC's tax-lot data for the blocks that hold Locust Point Drive, Tierney Place, Glennon Place, Hatting Place, Giegerich Place and the east end of Chaffee Avenue shows roughly 265 lots and 319 homes. About 167 of those lots are one-family houses, 68 are two-family houses and 5 are three-family houses, and not one building on those blocks holds four or more homes. The tallest building is three floors. In other words, every cabling job on the point is a house job, usually bought by the person who lives in it, and usually decided at the kitchen table rather than at a board meeting.

The houses themselves are old, and they were not built with a network in mind. City-Data's census tables count about 90 homes built in 1939 or earlier, 41 in the 1940s, 100 in the 1950s, 21 in the 1960s, 24 in the 1970s and 25 between 2010 and 2019. The tax-lot records stretch from a building dated 1899 to one dated 2013. Listings give a feel for the older end: Zillow describes 4408 Locust Point Drive as a 1,764-square-foot single-family home built in 1915, and a Compass listing gives a nearby lot as 25 by 100 feet. CityNeighborhoods.nyc explains why the stock looks the way it does. In the nineteenth century the shoreline here held small boatyards and wharves. In the 1920s and 1930s the land was subdivided and small bungalows replaced the farms, many of them first used as summer places by city families, according to a local history site. The neighborhood's own site describes what came later: classic Cape Cods, modest colonials and a few newer houses with views across Eastchester Bay, in a place that remains mostly owner-occupied. Many houses are still one- and two-story bungalows, CityNeighborhoods.nyc notes, though renovations and new construction have changed a good number of them.

The ground under some of those houses has a history too. Wikimapia records that Locust Point was once an island, called Wrights Island after Captain J.T. Wright and later Locust Island, and that the channel separating it from the mainland was filled and built over, partly for the Throgs Neck Bridge's northern ramp and toll plaza. The MTA lists Locust Point, Throggs Neck and SUNY Maritime College as the bridge's Bronx neighbors. The bridge, built between 1957 and 1961 and opened in January 1961, cut the point off from some of its older connections, and the Bronx Times quoted the civic association's president in 2011 describing a neighborhood with only one access road. For a cabling job that matters in small, practical ways: when the crew arrives, how materials get to a house at the end of a cul-de-sac, and why we plan a visit so that nothing needs a second trip for a forgotten part.

The water is the bigger factor. The Bronx Times reported that during Sandy, Eastchester Bay came over the seawall behind houses on the south side of Tierney Place, flooding streets, basements and ground floors, and one tenant in a downstairs rental described three feet of water and walls and floors that had to be replaced from scratch. Councilman Vacca was reported as shocked to see Pennyfield Avenue underwater. The National Weather Service's coastal flood notes list inundation of Tierney Place as a recurring impact and record a foot of water on Chaffee Avenue along Hammond Creek on January 23, 2016. The tax-lot data flags about 231 of the point's 265 lots in the 2015 preliminary flood maps, and the civic association's president told the Bronx Times her mortgage company had moved her from Zone B into Zone A. NYC Planning's 2017 briefing to the Throggs Neck Homeowners Association said Community District 10 holds about 60 percent of the Bronx's floodplain population and about 80 percent of its floodplain buildings. If you are going to put a network cabinet, a switch, a battery backup and the hub of every cable in the house somewhere, a Locust Point house tells you very clearly where not to put it.

This is where structured cabling earns its keep. A typical arrangement in an older house on the point grew one service at a time: a telephone line from decades ago, a coaxial cable for television, and then a fiber or coax drop from whichever provider the owner switched to most recently, each entering wherever the installer found it convenient and very often ending at a box on the basement wall. The Wi-Fi router sits beside that box or on a shelf near it. The signal then has to climb through a floor framed in old lumber, past a chimney or a stair, and out to rooms at the far end of a long, narrow house on a 25-foot lot. Additions made over the years, such as a rear room, a dormer or a raised lower level, put a different wall material between the router and each room. A wired cable plant replaces that chain with something simpler: one cabinet in a dry, chosen spot, a tested cable to each room that needs a steady connection, and access points fed by cable so the Wi-Fi starts where people actually are, not where the provider happened to drill.

Supply is not the problem. ISPReports says Verizon offers fiber service to about 96.6 percent of homes in the Throgs Neck–Edgewater Park area and Optimum to about 91.8 percent. Those figures describe the street, not the inside of a 1915 house. The provider connects the house; it does not design a path from its box to a bedroom two floors up, and it does not decide whether its equipment should live above or below the line the water reached in 2012. The owner decides that, and the owner pays for a cable plant that belongs to the house just like the plumbing does. Because Locust Point is overwhelmingly owner-occupied and almost entirely one- and two-family, that decision usually sits with one household, which makes the job simpler to approve and easier to plan around the way the family actually lives.

Locust Point facts that change a cabling plan

  • All houses, no towers: about 265 lots and 319 homes on the core blocks, with 167 one-family, 68 two-family and 5 three-family lots, none over three floors and no building of four or more homes, per NYC tax-lot data.
  • Old framing: about 90 homes built before 1940 and 100 more in the 1950s, per City-Data, so most jobs are retrofits in walls that were closed long before anyone owned a router.
  • The floodplain is the rule, not the exception: about 231 of 265 lots carry the 2015 preliminary flood map flag, and Tierney Place and Chaffee Avenue appear in the Weather Service's flood impact notes.
  • Elevation rules for new and substantially improved buildings: NYC's flood construction rules require the lowest floor and mechanical equipment to sit above the Design Flood Elevation, per NYC Planning's briefings to the civic association.
  • Fiber is on the streets: about 96.6 percent Verizon and 91.8 percent Optimum fiber availability across Throgs Neck–Edgewater Park, per ISPReports, which means the inside path is usually what is missing.
  • One road in: the civic association has described the point as having a single access road, which shapes how we schedule a visit and load the van.

What owners on the point usually add next. Once a house has a dry cabinet and a labeled cable to each room, the next request tends to be a camera at the side passage, a video doorbell at the door people actually use, or a wired access point for a deck facing the bay. All of those travel on the same kind of cable, so we ask about them at the walk-through and leave a spare run or two where they would land, even if the family is a year or two away from buying the devices.

Flood Elevation and the Network

The Design Flood Elevation, the Provider's Box and the Height of Every Jack in a Locust Point House

Most cabling advice starts with the cable. On Locust Point it has to start with a height. NYC Planning's briefings to the Locust Point Civic Association in October 2017 and to the Throggs Neck Homeowners Association that September described the city's flood construction rules: in the floodplain, new and substantially improved buildings must put the lowest floor and mechanical equipment above the Design Flood Elevation. A network cabinet is not a boiler, and we do not decide how the Department of Buildings classifies a modem. But the logic of the rule fits a cable plant exactly. The parts of a network that cost money and take time to replace, such as the switch, the battery backup, the patch panel and the access points, should live above the water, and the parts that have to go lower should be cheap, simple and quick to swap. We plan every Locust Point job around three heights: where the provider's line enters, the highest water the owner knows the house has seen, and the lowest floor people actually live on.

Start with the water the house has actually seen

Flood maps describe probabilities. A house remembers specific water. Before we draw anything we ask the owner where water reached in Sandy, in the January 2016 tide the Weather Service recorded on Chaffee Avenue, and in any storm since. Some families have a tide mark on a basement wall or a line on a stair stringer; some have photos; some remember that the water stopped two steps below the kitchen. If the house has an elevation certificate or an engineer's report from a raise or rebuild, that gives us a Design Flood Elevation to work above. Where there is neither, we use the owner's remembered high mark plus a sensible margin and write that figure into the plan, so everyone agrees on the line before the crew decides where the cabinet goes.

The provider's box is theirs to move

Many point houses have a fiber terminal or a coaxial amplifier screwed to the basement wall a foot or two above the floor, because that is where the line came through the foundation. That equipment belongs to the provider. We do not unscrew it, relocate it or splice its line. What we can do is build the rest of the network so it does not depend on that box staying dry: a cable from the box up to a cabinet on the main floor, with the router, switch and backup up there. If the owner wants the provider's equipment itself moved higher, the right step is to ask Verizon or Optimum to relocate it; once they agree on a new spot, we can make sure there is a clean pathway and a labeled cable waiting for them. Any new outlet the provider's equipment needs is electrician's work, and our plan says so.

Feeding the lowest floor from above

In many houses on the point the lowest floor is lived in: a family room, a bedroom, a laundry and sometimes a rental with its own door, as a Coldwell Banker listing on Locust Point Drive describes. Those rooms still need connections. The usual way to wire them is along the basement ceiling, which puts the main runs in the very space most likely to flood. We prefer to drop each lower-level cable down from the floor above inside a wall, entering the room high, and to keep the jack at a height the owner is comfortable with. If the lower level floods again, the damage is limited to a short run and a jack in that room; the cabinet, the switch and every upstairs cable stay untouched. It costs a little more labor at installation, and it is the single most useful design choice we make in a floodplain house.

Sealing what we open in an exterior or foundation wall

Every new hole through an outside wall or a foundation is a possible path for water, and on a lot where the bay has come over the seawall that path matters. We avoid new foundation penetrations below the known water line wherever an existing entry or an above-grade route exists. Where a cable has to pass through an exterior wall, the hole slopes down toward the outside, the cable gets a drip loop, and the opening is sealed with a product suited to the wall material. Where a cable crosses a fire-rated separation inside the house, such as between the units of a two-family or the wall between an attached garage and living space, the opening gets a listed firestop seal. Each sealed opening is photographed and the photos go in the owner's file.

The room list beats a total

"Six jacks" tells us very little. "A jack at the desk in the back bedroom, one behind the living room TV, one in the lower-level family room and an access point in the upstairs hall" tells us the routes, and the routes are the price. A jack on an interior wall directly above the cabinet may take minutes; a jack at the far end of a long house on the other side of a chimney may take an hour. Walk through the house, note where each connection is needed and what will plug into it, and mark anything you expect to add in the next couple of years, such as a camera at the side passage or a door station. We would rather run a spare cable now than open the wall twice.

Photograph the outside of the house, too

Stand on the street and photograph the front of the house, then the side passage and the rear if you can reach it. We are looking for where provider cables come down the wall or from a pole, any old lines left from previous services, the attic vents or a dormer that might offer a route, and the lowest openings in the foundation. On houses that have been raised, we also want to see where the old utility entry was and whether it moved when the house went up. Those pictures save time on site and let us arrive with the right drill bits and the right length of cable for the routes we expect.

Tell us about past repairs

Many point houses had their lowest floors rebuilt after Sandy, and some have been raised or remodeled since. If you know that a wall was replaced, a ceiling was furred down, or a contractor added insulation or a vapor barrier, tell us before the visit. A wall that was rebuilt in 2013 with new studs and drywall fishes very differently from a 1920s plaster wall next to it, and spray foam or rigid insulation in an exterior wall changes how we approach that route. Knowing the repair history is the fastest way to avoid a surprise once the drill is in hand.

Early bungalows: small houses, crowded walls

A bungalow from the 1920s or earlier looks like the easiest house to cable because it is small. It often is not. Older framing tends to have blocking between studs at mid-height, and decades of electrical, plumbing and heating changes have left wall cavities with obstructions nobody remembers. The roof space over a one-story bungalow may be too low to crawl through, and many have been closed up or insulated. So we look first for the routes the house already offers: a closet wall shared with the room that needs a jack, a gap beside a chimney chase that can be used without touching the chimney itself, a run along the top of an interior wall from a reachable spot in the roof space. Where the only route is low, we weigh a neat painted surface raceway along a baseboard or a door casing against opening and patching plaster, and the owner chooses. We never notch a rafter or a joist to make a route easier.

Cape Cods and colonials: use the stack

Houses with a central stair and a full or half second floor usually give us the best vertical route in the neighborhood: closets that sit directly above one another, or a partition wall that continues from floor to floor. Running down from the upper floor or the attic is usually cleaner than pushing up from the basement, and on the point it has the added benefit of never routing a cable through the space that floods. Knee walls under the slope of the roof offer a sideways path to bedrooms at the ends of the house, provided the space behind them is reachable and not packed with insulation that the owner wants left undisturbed. Where we pass close to a chimney, we keep the required clearance and route around it rather than through any chase that carries a flue.

Two-family houses: two networks, one entry

The 68 two-family lots on the core blocks are where we spend the most time on planning. The owner may live upstairs and rent the lower level with its own entrance, or the reverse, and each household is entitled to its own service. We usually bring both providers' lines to a shared point, often a cabinet with a lockable section for each unit or two small cabinets side by side, and give each unit its own cable routes from there. That way each household can choose and change its provider freely, neither can reach the other's devices, and the landlord never needs a tenant to let anyone in to reach shared equipment. Where a cable crosses the separation between the units, the opening is sealed with a listed firestop product and photographed.

Raised and rebuilt houses: time the cable to the walls

A house lifted above the flood line, or rebuilt after storm damage, is a rare chance to put cable in exactly where it belongs. While studs are open, a run to each room takes a fraction of the labor it takes to fish later, and pull strings and a spare conduit to the cabinet location cost little. The trick is timing: the cable has to go in after framing and rough plumbing and electrical, and before insulation and drywall. We ask the general contractor or the owner for the schedule and plan the visit around it, so nobody is waiting on us and the drywall crew does not close a wall we still need. On these houses we also check where the provider's line will enter after the work is finished, because a utility entry that sat at the old basement level may need to move once the living floor has gone up.

Field Notes: The Three Heights We Write Down in Every Locust Point House

We do not tell stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is a routine, not an anecdote. It is what a technician writes on the first page of the notes before discussing a single jack on the point.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Locust Point: Questions Answered

Flood maps, flood rules and where the water reaches

Does a modem or network switch count as "mechanical equipment" under the city's flood construction rules?

We are a cabling contractor, not the Department of Buildings, so we will not tell you how DOB classifies a particular device; for a raise, a rebuild or a substantial improvement, your architect or engineer is the one who confirms what the rules require. What we can say is that the reasoning behind the rule, as NYC Planning explained it to the Locust Point Civic Association in 2017, is to keep the costly, hard-to-replace parts of a building above the Design Flood Elevation. We apply that reasoning to the network whether or not the code demands it. The switch, router, patch panel, battery backup and the outlet that feeds them belong above the elevation on your paperwork, or above the highest water you know the house has seen if there is no paperwork. Only cable and jacks for the lowest rooms go lower, and they are designed to be cheap to replace.

How can we check whether our lot sits in the mapped floodplain before we decide on a wiring plan?

Start with what you already have. If your mortgage company requires flood insurance, it has a map determination for your lot; the civic association's president told the Bronx Times her lender moved her from Zone B to Zone A, which is the kind of letter many point owners have received. An elevation certificate, if the house has one from a sale, a raise or an insurance purchase, lists the relevant elevations directly. The city and FEMA publish flood maps you can look up by address, and the tax-lot data we reviewed flags about 231 of the point's 265 lots in the 2015 preliminary maps. If none of that is in hand, the honest working assumption on Locust Point is that the lowest floor can take water. We write the water line we are designing to into the plan so you can compare it with any paperwork you find later.

Can the rooms on our lowest floor be wired from above, so a flood would only cost us the cable on that floor?

Yes, and in most point houses it is what we recommend. Instead of running the lower-level cables along the basement ceiling back to a cabinet in the basement, each one drops down from the cabinet on the floor above, inside a wall, and enters its room high. The jack can sit at a height you are comfortable with rather than at the usual outlet height near the floor. If that floor floods, the job afterward is limited: inspect and replace the short runs and jacks in the affected rooms, retest, and update the map. The cabinet, the switch and every cable serving the upper floors never got wet. The labor is a little higher at installation, because dropping down inside finished walls takes more care than stapling along an open basement ceiling, and the plan shows that difference line by line.

Providers and the box on the basement wall

Verizon's fiber box is mounted low on our basement wall. Can you move it upstairs, or does that have to be Verizon?

That has to be Verizon. The fiber terminal and the line feeding it are the provider's equipment, and we do not unmount, extend or splice them. The same goes for Optimum's equipment. What you can do is call the provider and ask about relocating it higher, explaining that the basement has flooded; once they agree on a spot, we can make sure a clean pathway and a labeled cable are waiting there, and the electrician can add an outlet if one is needed. If they will not move it, we leave it where it is and run a single tested cable from it up to a cabinet on the main floor, with your router and switch there. Then the only parts at risk in the basement are the provider's box and that one cable, and the provider is responsible for its own equipment.

Does it matter for the wiring inside whether we choose Verizon or Optimum?

Very little, which is one of the main reasons to wire the house properly. ISPReports puts Verizon fiber at about 96.6 percent of Throgs Neck–Edgewater Park homes and Optimum fiber at about 91.8 percent, so many point owners have a real choice. Each provider brings its line to the house and terminates it in its own box. From that box, your own router connects to a standard network cable, and from there your cabinet, switch and room cables do not care which company supplies the signal. If you change providers later, the new one connects at the same spot and your house wiring stays exactly as it is. The only provider-specific question is where their line enters, which affects the first short cable from their box to your cabinet. In a two-family house, each household can choose differently and still use the same kind of inside cabling.

The ground, the house and its history

Our house stands on fill where the channel between Locust Island and the mainland used to be. Is it safe to bury conduit in the yard?

Wikimapia records that the channel separating Locust Island from the mainland was filled and built over, partly for the bridge's northern ramp and toll plaza, so some lots near the bridge approach likely sit on made ground. We have no soil data for any particular lot and will not guess at it. What we do in practice anywhere near the shoreline is assume buried conduit will hold water at some point, from groundwater or from a flood above it. So we use cable rated for wet and direct-burial conditions inside the conduit, seal both ends, bring the conduit up into the building above the known water line rather than through the foundation below it, and slope it to drain away from the house where we can. Before digging anywhere, utility lines must be marked. If a short wireless bridge can do the job instead of a trench, we will tell you, because on low, wet ground the trench is not always the better answer.

Our 1915 house has been added onto more than once. How do you find out what is inside the walls before anyone drills?

We read the house first. An addition usually shows itself in a change of floor level, a thicker wall where the old exterior used to be, different trim, or a step in the basement foundation. Inside the original core we look for the obstacles older framing tends to have, such as blocking between studs, old plumbing and heating runs and abandoned wiring, using a stud finder, a moisture meter, a small inspection hole where the owner agrees, and the view from the basement and attic. Where an old exterior wall now sits inside the house, it may still have sheathing or siding behind the plaster, which changes how we fish it. Each route in the plan notes what we expect to find. If we open a wall and find something we did not expect, we stop, show the owner, and agree on a new route before continuing.

Storms, seasons and the civic house

Before a hurricane or a nor'easter, what should we do with the network equipment?

If the cabinet is above the water line, very little. If the provider's box or any of your equipment is still in the basement, the safest step is to unplug anything of yours that sits low, such as a router or switch still on a basement shelf, and move it up, then switch off the circuit to any outlet on that floor if your electrician has told you how. Do not try to remove or open the provider's box. Upstairs, a battery backup will carry the cabinet through a short outage, but in a long one it is better to shut the equipment down cleanly than to let it run the battery flat. After the storm, check for water before restoring power to anything on the lowest floor. If water reached any jack, cable or outlet, call us to inspect and test before you plug devices back in there.

We close the house for part of the year. Can the network be left off and brought back without a service call?

Yes, if it is set up for that from the start. Locust Point began as a summer place for many families, according to a local history site, and some owners still split their year. We label the cabinet with a short start-up and shut-down card, put the router, switch and backup on one switched power strip or a single outlet, and keep the settings saved on the equipment so it comes back exactly as it was. The cable itself does not care whether the house is occupied; a sealed cabinet above the water line will sit through a winter without harm. If you want to keep something running while you are away, such as a camera or a water sensor, we plan which devices stay on, size the battery backup to them, and leave the rest switched off.

Our civic house doubles as a polling place. Can its new network be kept separate from anything election workers set up?

The Bronx Times has reported the civic house at 3300 Tierney Place serving as a polling site as well as a meeting place. We do not touch, connect to or plan for election equipment; that belongs to the people who run the election. What we do for a building like this is make the association's own network self-contained: its own cabinet, its own labeled ports, its own Wi-Fi name for members and events, and no open jacks in the rooms where outside parties set up, unless the association wants them and labels them as such. That way nothing anyone brings in on a given day can end up plugged into the association's network by accident, and the association's computer and printer are never sharing a network with guests. Every port is mapped in a file the association keeps.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Locust Point

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask results for this neighborhood from our build environment, so nothing below is a quotation from them. These are claims we see often in quick online answers about home network wiring and about houses like the ones on the point, checked against the public record and against how we work. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed a lot of those answers with averaged cost guides; they help with scale, but none of them has stood in a Tierney Place basement with a tide mark on the wall.

"Put the network equipment where the internet comes into the house."

That is good advice in a dry house and poor advice on most of Locust Point. In many older houses here the provider's line enters through the foundation near the basement floor, and the Bronx Times reported basements and ground floors on Tierney Place under water during Sandy.

The provider's box has to stay where the provider puts it, but your router, switch and backup do not. A single cable from that box to a cabinet a floor up costs little and moves the expensive part of the network out of harm's way.

The city's flood construction rules, as NYC Planning described them to the civic association, already push mechanical equipment above the Design Flood Elevation in new and substantially improved buildings. A network deserves the same thinking.

"A small bungalow only needs a good router."

Square footage is not the problem in a small point house. Floors, chimneys, old plaster and additions built in different decades are, and a router in a basement corner is fighting all of them at once.

A single tested cable to a ceiling access point on the main floor often does more for a bungalow than a more expensive router, because the Wi-Fi starts where the family actually lives.

Desks, TVs and anything that streams all day are better on a cable regardless of the house's size. A small house usually needs only a few runs, which keeps the job modest.

"Low-voltage wiring isn't regulated in New York City anymore, so anyone can do it."

Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, removed the electrical permit requirement for wiring below 50 volts used for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A permit exemption is not the same as no rules at all.

A Bronx hiring guide says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, and a 2026 licensing roadmap says cameras, access control and fire alarm work require a state Article 6-D license. Fire alarm wiring has its own filings entirely.

Ask who is doing the work, under which license, and whether any part of it touches cameras, door hardware or fire alarm. Our low-voltage work is done under NYS license #12000287431.

"If fiber is available on your street, the whole house gets fiber speeds."

ISPReports puts fiber availability near 97 percent across Throgs Neck–Edgewater Park, and that is good news for point owners. But the fiber stops at the provider's box.

Everything after that, the router, the walls, the floors and the distance to each room, decides what a laptop in the back bedroom actually gets. In a 1920s house with the router in the basement, that can be a fraction of what the line delivers.

The fix is not a faster plan from the provider. It is a short, tested path from their box to each room that needs the speed you are already paying for.

"Network cable that got wet will be fine once it dries out."

Sometimes a cable that sat in clean water tests fine afterward, and sometimes it does not. Saltwater and the dirt it carries are harder on jacks and terminations than on the cable jacket itself.

The only way to know is to test each affected run on an analyzer after the area is dry, and replace the jacks and any run that fails. Guessing leaves a problem hidden in the wall until it shows up as a dropped connection.

The better answer on the point is design: feed lower-floor rooms from above so that only short, cheap runs are ever at risk, and keep every expensive part of the plant well above the water.

"A national cost-per-jack figure will tell you what your house will cost."

National averages blend new offices with open ceilings, suburban houses with easy attics and city buildings with masonry walls. A national how-to guide we read says New York City jobs trend toward the upper end because plaster, brick and building rules add labor.

On the point, the same five jacks might be quick in a house being rebuilt with open studs on Longstreet Avenue and slow in a 1915 bungalow with blocking in every wall and no usable attic.

That is why we price from a walk-through and a written plan, route by route. A national figure is a guess about someone else's house; the plan is a number for yours.

"Always install the highest category of cable you can buy."

Cat6A has real advantages for long runs and very high speeds, and in a house being rebuilt it is often worth the modest extra cost. But it is thicker and stiffer, which matters when it has to be fished through a narrow, blocked wall in an old bungalow.

Most runs in a Locust Point house are short, well within what Cat6 handles comfortably for today's home equipment. The right category is the one that fits the route and the use, chosen run by run.

The plan states the category for each cable and why, so the choice is visible rather than hidden in a total. A properly installed and tested Cat6 run will outperform a poorly terminated Cat6A run every time.

FAQ

Locust Point Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Bridge traffic sometimes backs up on the one road onto the point. Will that delay the job or add to the bill?
+

It should not add to the bill. Locust Point is billed at the standard Bronx rate, and we plan arrival times with the bridge and the single access road in mind, since the civic association has described the neighborhood as having one road in. We load everything for the job before we leave so nothing needs a second trip, and if traffic will make us late we call ahead rather than leave you waiting.

If we ever have to file a flood claim, will your paperwork help show what network wiring was in the house?
+

It should help. The handover file includes the port map, the test result for every run, photos of each concealed route and sealed opening, and the plan listing what was installed on each floor. Keep a copy somewhere other than the house, such as email or cloud storage. We cannot tell you what an insurer will cover, but a dated record of what was in the walls is far better than trying to reconstruct it after a storm.

Is there network cable that can sit underwater and keep working?
+

There are cables built for wet locations and direct burial, and we use them in conduit outdoors and underground. Indoors, the weak point in a flood is not the cable jacket but the jacks and connections, which are not made to sit in salty, dirty water. That is why we keep the cabinet high, feed lower rooms from above and treat any submerged jack as something to inspect, test and usually replace afterward.

Can the wall jacks on our lowest floor be set higher than normal outlets?
+

Yes. A data jack can go at any height that suits the room, and on the point we often set lower-level jacks at desk height or just below a TV rather than near the floor. It keeps the connection clear of shallow water and makes the cable easier to feed from the floor above. We agree each height with you on the walk-through and write it into the plan.

Could the hole for a new cable let tide water into our basement?
+

Any opening in a foundation is a possible path, which is why we avoid new holes below the known water line whenever an existing entry or an above-grade route will do. Where a cable must pass through an exterior wall, the hole slopes down to the outside, the cable gets a drip loop, and the opening is sealed with a product suited to the material. Each one is photographed for your records.

We are buying a house on the point. Can you test its network wiring before we close?
+

Yes, with the seller's permission to visit. We can trace and test the existing runs, note where the provider's line enters relative to any signs of past water, and tell you which parts of the wiring are usable, which are not, and what a plan for the house would likely involve. You leave with a written summary to factor into your decision, not a sales pitch.

Will a small network cabinet in a bedroom closet be noisy?
+

Usually not. A house-sized cabinet holds a patch panel, a small switch, the router and a battery backup, and most of that equipment runs quietly or silently. Some switches and backups have small fans, so if the closet is in a bedroom we choose fanless models where possible and tell you in advance which parts make any sound at all. A hall or utility closet on the main floor avoids the question entirely.

Will you leave us spare jacks or faceplates for future repairs?
+

We can leave a few spare jacks and faceplates matching the ones installed, along with the label scheme, so a future repair or a jack replaced after a flood looks and works like the rest. They go in a labeled bag in the cabinet. Tell us at the walk-through if you would like them, and they will be listed on the plan.

When the provider comes to install service, will they connect to the cable you ran?
+

In most cases the provider connects its line to its own box and hands you a connection there, and our cable from that box to your cabinet takes it from there. We label that first cable clearly so their technician can see what it is. If you are having service installed after we finish, tell the provider where your cabinet is and that the inside wiring is already in place.

Do you take the cable scraps and boxes with you when the job is done?
+

Yes. Cable offcuts, packaging, old jacks we replaced and any dead cable you asked us to remove leave with the crew. We vacuum the dust from any drilling, and if a wall needed a small inspection hole, it is closed and ready for paint unless the plan says otherwise. You should not have anything to carry out on garbage day.
