The Neighborhood

Why Jerome Park Properties Need Intercom Installation

Jerome Park is a neighborhood of shared lobbies. The Homes.com neighborhood guide describes it as full of postwar tenement buildings, with a mix of co-ops, rowhouses and Italian Renaissance brownstones, and another housing guide says most of the structures in the area are prewar courtyard buildings. Either way, the typical front door in Jerome Park is used by dozens of households, not one or two. That changes what an intercom has to do. A visitor needs to find the right apartment among many, the call has to reach that apartment and only that apartment, and the person upstairs has to be able to open the entrance without anyone else in the building hearing the conversation or getting a release they did not ask for. When any of those steps fails, tenants improvise. They walk down several flights to open the door themselves, they leave the lobby door unlocked when they expect someone, or they tell friends to call from the sidewalk. Each of those habits leaves the building a little less secure than its owners and residents think it is.

The second thing that sets Jerome Park apart is the age of its wiring. The apartment boom here followed the subway. According to the cityneighborhoods.nyc profile, the extension of the IRT Jerome Avenue Line in 1917 encouraged five- and six-story apartment buildings in Art Deco and Tudor Revival styles, and the Mosholu Parkway station opened on April 15, 1918. A building put up in the 1920s or 1930s has usually had its intercom replaced several times since, and each replacement was laid on top of whatever was already in the walls. The panel in the lobby may be fairly recent while the cable running up through the building is decades older, spliced in closets, rerouted around renovated kitchens and shared between apartments in ways nobody wrote down. The clearest local example is in the towers at the north edge of the neighborhood. In a Norwood News report on a dispute between Tracey Towers management and the Department of Buildings, residents said the intercom was not working to buzz people in when they were expecting visitors, so they had to leave the door open downstairs. Management's response was that the current system was "wired 'in series,' and this is outdated." That single phrase describes a problem we look for in older buildings all over the neighborhood, and we explain it in detail in the wiring section below.

Six Jerome Park facts that shape an intercom before any hardware is chosen

  • The front doors are shared by many households. The housing stock is dominated by prewar courtyard buildings, postwar tenements and co-ops, so most jobs involve a directory of dozens of apartments rather than two or three buttons.
  • The buildings date from the subway boom. The IRT Jerome Avenue Line extension in 1917 set off construction of five- and six-story Art Deco and Tudor Revival apartment houses, which means century-old cable paths and decades of layered repairs.
  • Most residents rent. About 87.8 percent of residents rent, according to Homes.com, so the owner, board or managing agent usually decides on and pays for the intercom, while tenants report when it fails.
  • The biggest buildings have documented intercom trouble. Tracey Towers, two towers of 41 and 38 stories at 20 and 40 West Mosholu Parkway South by the Paul Rudolph Institute's count, has had residents complain publicly that the intercom would not buzz visitors in and did not work in the elevators.
  • New buildings are on the way. Filings on East 202nd Street, East 203rd Street, Sedgwick Avenue, Webster Avenue and Valentine Avenue mean new entry systems will be designed from scratch on these side streets in the next few years.
  • The landmarks are institutions, not apartment blocks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission list places the High Pumping Station and the Kingsbridge Armory in Jerome Park, but we found no historic district covering its apartment buildings, which keeps most panel replacements simpler than they would be farther south on the Concourse.

Same-Day Repair in Jerome Park

Need Repair in Jerome Park? Dead Lines of Apartments, a Release That Does Nothing, or a Panel Nobody Can Read

Repair calls from Jerome Park buildings tend to follow the shape of the buildings. The most common is a system where whole groups of apartments go quiet at once: the A and B lines on the upper floors stop ringing, or every apartment above the third floor can hear the lobby but cannot open the door. In a building wired so that one cable passes through several apartments on its way up, a single failed handset, a loose terminal or a splice that corroded in a closet can cut off everyone beyond it. The second pattern is a release that hums upstairs but leaves the lobby door locked, which usually points to a strike that has worn out, a keeper that no longer lines up with the latch after years of the door slamming, or a power supply that can no longer push enough current to the door at the end of a long run. The third is the panel itself: buttons worn smooth, a directory that has not been updated in years, a speaker that crackles or a cracked faceplate letting water in. The fourth is the system nobody can identify, installed by a company that no longer exists, with no manual and no record of where its wires go.

If the lobby door will not lock or latch, deal with that first, because a door that stays open is the real danger. A door left on the latch so visitors can get in is exactly the situation Tracey Towers residents described to Norwood News. Most entrance doors can still be locked with their key cylinder while the intercom is down, and residents can meet visitors at the door rather than wedging it. If a breaker feeding the intercom keeps tripping, leave it off rather than resetting it over and over. Then call (646) 490-0629. Requests are booked within 2 hours, and on the phone we tell you what the photos let us promise and what they do not. Many single faults, such as a failed power supply, a corroded junction, a dead handset breaking a chain or a misaligned strike, are found and fixed in the same visit. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we give you an honest estimate of the tracing time before we start.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Jerome Park

Every Jerome Park job starts with the same three counts: how many apartments share the entrance, how many floors and lines the cable has to reach, and how many doors a visitor has to pass through before reaching the stairs or the elevator. The services below are built around those numbers.

Lobby panels for prewar buildings

A new entrance panel with a clear directory for buildings of 20 to 60 or more apartments, sized for the lobby or vestibule wall and wired to a station in every apartment.

Rewiring series-wired systems

Old chains where one cable passes through several apartments replaced with a layout that gives each apartment or each line its own run, so one failure no longer silences a whole floor.

Video for walk-up floors

A camera at the entrance and a screen upstairs, so a resident on the fifth floor of a building without an elevator can see who is waiting before buzzing them in or walking down.

Phone and app answering

Entrance panels that call a resident's phone as well as, or instead of, a wall station, for households out at work all day and for owners who manage from elsewhere.

Strikes, closers and release power

Door hardware fitted so the release opens the entrance for a few seconds and the door then closes and latches on its own, with free exit from inside at all times.

Courtyard and vestibule layouts

Plans for buildings with a sidewalk gate, a courtyard and a lobby door, or an outer door and an inner vestibule door, so the release opens the door that actually keeps strangers out.

New construction entry systems

Networked entrance panels, apartment stations, cable risers and an equipment location planned with the architect and contractor for new buildings on the side streets.

Mixed-use buildings

A residential entrance with its own panel, power and cable path, kept separate from the ground-floor shops that occupy many Jerome Park buildings.

Repairs and takeovers

Dead lines, weak releases, crackling panels and unlabeled systems left by companies that no longer exist, traced, tested, documented and repaired or replaced.

Many Jerome Park buildings pair a new intercom with access control on the same entrance, so residents use a fob or a code while visitors ring. Planned together, the two can share the strike, the power supply and the cable path, which costs less and looks tidier than bolting one onto the other later. In a building where residents worry about how many old keys are floating around, adding fobs to the street door at the same time as the intercom is often the most useful single change. If the building is thinking about both, say so on the first call and we will design them as one job.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Jerome Park's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every intercom call from Jerome Park. They differ in how many households share the entrance, how the cable has to travel, what the walls and floors are made of, and who besides the owner has a say. The sections after the cards explain what changes in the work for each one.

The Art Deco or Tudor Revival co-op

Five- and six-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s along Mosholu Parkway and the side streets, run by a board and a managing agent, often already on their second or third intercom.

The prewar courtyard building

A rental or co-op wrapped around a courtyard, where a visitor may pass a sidewalk gate, cross the courtyard and reach a lobby door before ringing anyone.

The postwar tenement or walk-up

Smaller apartment buildings that replaced older houses on streets like Villa Avenue, with one entrance, a narrow lobby and stairs instead of an elevator.

The tower

Very tall buildings such as Tracey Towers, with hundreds of apartments, elevators, a management office and, in that complex, two entrances at different heights.

The new side-street building

Eight- and nine-story buildings filed for East 202nd Street, East 203rd Street, Sedgwick Avenue, Valentine Avenue and Webster Avenue, designed with networked entry from the start.

Rowhouses and small houses

The few Italianate rowhouses left on Villa Avenue and the duplexes and one-family houses that sit among the apartment buildings, where one or two households share a door.

The Art Deco or Tudor Revival co-op: a board, a budget and a history of upgrades

The co-ops along Mosholu Parkway and the streets behind it are some of the best-kept buildings in the neighborhood, and many already have a reasonably modern system. The listing for the 1937 co-op at 340 East Mosholu Parkway South mentions a video intercom and cameras watching the entrance and common areas. When a building like that calls us, the question is rarely whether to have an intercom. It is whether the current one can be extended, what it will cost to keep it working, and what the board should plan for when it can no longer be repaired. A building that installed video ten or fifteen years ago may have good cable and a panel nearing the end of its life, or a sound panel connected to old wire that will keep causing faults until it is replaced. The survey separates those cases, because the right budget depends entirely on which one the building is in.

Co-ops also have a decision process, and the work has to fit it. A board usually wants a written scope it can circulate before a meeting, a clear breakdown of what is equipment and what is labor, and an honest list of what might change once walls or junction boxes are opened. We write quotes that way. We also plan the changeover around shareholders. Lobbies in these buildings are often the most carefully finished rooms in the house, with original floors, metalwork and lighting, so we protect finishes while we work, run cable through existing paths where they exist, and avoid opening plaster in the lobby unless there is no other way. Inside the apartments, a shareholder who has renovated a kitchen may have buried the old handset location behind new cabinets, so we check each apartment's station position on the survey rather than assuming every line is the same.

The prewar courtyard building: deciding which door the intercom controls

Courtyard buildings are common in Jerome Park, and they raise a question that does not come up in a simple rowhouse: where does the visitor actually stop to ring? In some buildings the sidewalk gate stands open and only the lobby door at the back of the courtyard is locked. In others the gate itself is the security line, with a lock and sometimes an old call box, and the lobby doors stay unlocked during the day. A few have both a locked gate and locked lobby doors, which means a visitor needs two releases to get inside. Each layout works, but only if the intercom matches it. If the panel sits at the lobby door while the gate is locked, visitors cannot reach the panel at all. If the panel sits at the gate but the release only opens the lobby door, visitors are left standing at a locked gate.

On the survey we walk the path a visitor takes, from the sidewalk to the apartment door, and mark every point where a door or gate can stop them. Then we agree with the owner where the security line should be. Usually that means putting the panel and the release at the first locked barrier and making sure every door behind it either stays unlocked or opens on the same release. Running cable to a gate across a courtyard is a real part of the cost, because it often means a buried conduit or a route along a wall, and we price it on its own line so the owner can decide whether the gate is worth controlling. Where a courtyard leads to several wings with separate lobbies, each lobby needs a clear directory for its own apartments, and the visitor needs signs telling them which wing to walk to.

The postwar tenement or walk-up: stairs, a narrow lobby and a long climb

Homes.com notes that on Villa Avenue many of the original houses were torn down and replaced with postwar tenement buildings. Buildings like these have one entrance, a small lobby or vestibule, and stairs rather than an elevator. That makes the intercom more important than it is in a building with a doorman or a lift. Every unanswered call means someone walking down four or five flights and back up again, and every release that does not work means the same climb. The upper floors are where video earns its cost: a tenant on the top floor who can see the visitor on a screen decides from the apartment whether to buzz them in, instead of shouting down the stairwell or walking down to look.

Walk-ups also tend to have the hardest cable paths. There is rarely a dedicated shaft for low-voltage wiring, so the old intercom cable often runs up a public hall in a molding, through closets stacked one above another, or along a dumbwaiter or pipe chase. When that cable has to be replaced, we look for the least disruptive route first: an existing chase that can take a new cable alongside the old one, a stack of closets where a small hole at each floor does the job, or a surface raceway in the stair hall that can be painted to match. We avoid opening apartment walls unless there is no other route, and when we have to enter apartments, the managing agent tells tenants in advance which day and roughly what time we need to be inside.

The tower: hundreds of apartments, elevators and more than one entrance

Tracey Towers is the clearest example of a Jerome Park tower, and the published details show how different that kind of building is. The Paul Rudolph Institute describes two towers, a 41-story and a 38-story, at 20 and 40 West Mosholu Parkway South, with one tower built over the tracks and the other on undisturbed ground, which puts the two entrances at different elevations. The developer's own project page lists them differently, as two 42-story towers built in 1974, and a secondary source counts 871 Mitchell-Lama apartments. Whatever the exact figures, a building of that size has entrance traffic all day, a management office, security staff and elevators, and the intercom has to work with all of them. Residents told Norwood News that their intercom would not buzz visitors in and did not work in the elevators, and management said the system was wired in series and outdated.

We do not claim to have worked at Tracey Towers, and the choice of system there belongs to its management. What we can explain is how towers differ in general. A tower panel needs a searchable directory rather than a wall of buttons, cable that runs up riser closets on many floors, and network equipment in a room with backup power, because hundreds of households lose their door at once if it fails. Where security staff answer calls, the system has to route visitor calls to the desk, to apartments, or to both, depending on the time of day. And where there are two entrances at different levels, each needs its own panel and each panel has to be clear about which apartments it serves. For residents of a tower, the most useful thing to do when the intercom fails is to report it to management in writing, note which apartments are affected, and keep a copy.

The new side-street building: getting the entry system into the drawings

The new buildings filed for Jerome Park are modest in size compared with the towers but large compared with the houses they sit beside: eight stories at 233 East 202nd Street, 30 residences at 3377 Sedgwick Avenue, 22 at 234 East 203rd Street, a nine-story filing for 62 residences at 2895 Valentine Avenue, and senior housing at 2850 Webster Avenue. Buildings like these usually get networked video intercoms, with an entrance panel, apartment stations or phone answering, and network equipment in a dedicated room. The hardware is not the hard part. The hard part is making sure the building is ready for it: a cable path from every apartment to the equipment room, power and backup in that room, conduit to the entrance and to any secondary door, and a door frame prepared for the strike before the door is hung.

For senior housing like the Webster Avenue project, the entry system also has to suit residents with weaker eyesight, hearing or grip. That means apartment stations that are easy to read and press, call volumes that can be turned up, release times long enough for someone who moves slowly, and phone answering for residents who would rather use a phone they already know. For buildings with affordable units required under the Jerome Avenue rezoning, management also needs a system that does not depend on every tenant owning a smartphone. We work with developers, architects and general contractors as the low-voltage contractor on projects like these, and we can provide a written scope for the drawings even when the stations will be bought later.

Rowhouses and small houses: one door, a few households

Homes.com notes that a few slatted Italianate rowhouses survive on Villa Avenue, and other guides mention duplexes and one-family houses scattered among the apartment buildings. These are the simplest intercom jobs in the neighborhood, but they still need planning. A two-family house where an owner lives downstairs and a tenant upstairs needs a button and a station for each household, a strike so the upstairs household can let a visitor in without walking down, and a door that latches again after every release. Old houses often still have a doorbell transformer in the cellar and thin bell wire running up to the hall, and that wire may or may not be good enough for a modern system. We test it before deciding. Because these houses sit next to much larger buildings, visitors sometimes ring the wrong door, so a lit house number on or near the station helps more than people expect.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Jerome Park Lobby From the Directory Down to the Riser

Compare the directory, the mailboxes and the apartment count. In the lobby the technician counts the names or buttons on the panel, then the mailboxes, then asks the super how many apartments the building really has. In buildings that have combined or split apartments over the decades, these three numbers often disagree, and the gaps usually point to cable that was rerouted or abandoned.

Press every button before touching anything. With someone upstairs or a list from the managing agent, the technician records which apartments ring, which can talk, and which can release the door. A pattern where whole lines or whole floors fail together suggests a series-wired chain; scattered failures suggest individual stations or splices.

Open the power supply cabinet. In the cellar or a closet, the age of the power supply, the number of wires leaving it and the condition of its terminals say a lot. Many cables leaving one cabinet usually means separate runs; one or two thick cables usually means a chain.

Follow one riser to the top. The technician traces at least one line from the cellar to the top floor, noting every junction, every closet it passes through, and every place it has been spliced.

Watch the door. The lobby door is opened fully and let go. If it does not close and latch on its own, the closer or the latch comes first.

Wiring Layouts

"Wired in Series": How Old Apartment-Building Intercoms Are Laid Out, and What That Means for a Jerome Park Rewire

When Tracey Towers management told Norwood News that their intercom was "wired 'in series,'" they were describing something that matters in many older buildings, not just towers. The way an intercom's cable is laid out decides how it fails, how hard it is to repair, and how much of it can be reused when the system is replaced. Most owners and boards never see this, because it lives in closets and risers. This section explains the layouts we find, what each one means for a Jerome Park building, and how we decide what stays.

Series, or daisy-chain, wiring. In a series layout, one cable leaves the power supply or the lobby panel and travels from apartment to apartment, connecting at each station before going on to the next. It was cheap to install, because one cable could serve a whole line of apartments, and in a new building it worked. Its weakness is that every station is a link in a chain. A loose terminal behind one handset, a station a tenant ripped off the wall during a renovation, or a corroded splice in a closet can cut off every apartment beyond that point. That matches the pattern residents often describe: a building where the lower floors work and the upper floors do not, or where an entire line goes quiet one day and nobody upstairs did anything to cause it.

Home-run, or star, wiring. In a home-run layout, each apartment, or at least each line of apartments, has its own cable back to a central point. When one station fails, only that apartment is affected, and the fault is easy to find because the cable that serves it can be tested from end to end without going through anyone else's apartment. Most modern systems are designed around home runs or around a short riser feeding a distribution module on each floor. Converting a series building to home runs is the single largest cost driver in many Jerome Park rewires, because it means pulling new cable up through the building rather than just changing the panel.

Mixed layouts. Many older buildings end up with a mixture. The original installer ran a chain up each line; a later company added a new cable for some floors; a tenant's contractor tied a renovated apartment back in with whatever wire was at hand. These buildings are the hardest to repair, because the drawing in the cellar, if there is one, no longer matches what is in the walls. The only reliable approach is to trace each line and draw a new map. That tracing takes time, which is why we estimate it honestly before starting and bill it at our service rate rather than burying it in an installation price.

Deciding what to reuse. A modern two-wire video system can often run over an existing pair, and a networked system can sometimes use existing cable as a riser if it is the right type. But reuse depends on more than the layout. The copper has to be continuous, the insulation must not leak between conductors, and the run must not be so long and thin that the voltage drops too far by the top floor. We test each run we might keep, look at the copper wherever it is exposed, and compare its length and gauge with what the chosen system needs. Runs that pass can stay. Runs that fail, or that pass but sit in a chain that would leave the building as fragile as before, are replaced. The quote shows which wires are being reused and why.

Replacing splices even when the cable stays. Joints made decades ago are usually the weakest point in an old system. Twisted, taped connections in closets and behind handsets loosen and corrode, and they cause the crackle, the faint calls and the releases that work only some of the time. Even when a run tests well, we cut out old splices and remake them in labeled junction boxes with connectors made for low-voltage work, and we record where every box is.

Getting new cable up a prewar building. Buildings from the 1920s and 1930s rarely have a dedicated shaft for low-voltage cable. The routes we use, in order of preference, are an existing chase or old intercom conduit that can take another cable, a stack of closets one above another, a pipe or dumbwaiter chase where the building allows it, and finally a painted surface raceway in a public hall. We do not run intercom cable alongside power wiring in the same pathway, and we keep cable clear of fire-rated assemblies unless a penetration can be properly sealed. Where a path crosses a floor, the hole is sleeved and firestopped.

Power in a dry place the building controls. The intercom's power supply goes in a closed enclosure in the cellar, a closet or a utility room the super can reach, on a circuit that is labeled at the panel. The Rules at the Entrance

Eight Apartments, Self-Closing Doors and Free Exit: What New York Rules Ask of a Jerome Park Entrance

Most Jerome Park apartment buildings are large enough that the city's intercom and door rules apply to them in full. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources and installer summaries say, so owners, boards and tenants can ask their attorney, managing agent or HPD the right questions.

Buildings with eight or more apartments. Installer summaries of Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a describe the intercom requirement as attaching at eight apartments: Class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercom from each apartment to the main entrance. For older buildings, the same summaries say the owner must install the doors and the intercom when tenants occupying a majority of apartments request or consent in writing. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 describes the same eight-apartment threshold for buildings erected or converted before 1968. Nearly every prewar building in Jerome Park is far above eight apartments.

Self-closing doors apply regardless of size. The same summaries say the self-closing and self-locking door requirement applies regardless of how many apartments a building has, and HPD's ABCs of Housing says that in every type of multiple dwelling, building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors must be self-closing. HPD's proactive self-closing door program recorded 3,151 self-closing door violations citywide in fiscal year 2024. An electric strike must preserve this: after every release, the door has to close and latch on its own. We fit strikes to where the latch actually lands and adjust the closer so the door does not stop short.

Enforcement and deadlines. Installer sources describe a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous violation that HPD enforces on tenant complaint, with 311 complaints triggering inspections, and one of them says a system where some apartments work and others do not is itself a violation. HPD's own page lists 30 days to correct Class B hazardous conditions and 14 days for a Class C self-closing door violation. One installer blog says Class B intercom violations carry fines of $250 to $500 per day; we did not confirm that on an HPD page, so treat it as unverified. The practical point is simple: a partly working intercom is not a working intercom in the eyes of the rules, and a repair that fixes the lower floors while leaving the upper floors dead is not finished.

Free exit always. An installer summary notes that any access control setup that impedes interior egress violates the city's fire code. Every door we fit with a strike or lock still opens freely from inside without a key, code or button. Where a building wants a magnetic lock instead of a strike, the exit side needs release devices that work every time, and the lock must fit into the building's fire and life-safety arrangements, which is one reason we usually recommend strikes on prewar entrance doors.

Mounting heights. Rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. In a lobby with steps up to the door, we measure from where the visitor actually stands. Where an entrance also serves the public, such as a building's management office or a ground-floor business, accessibility reach rules usually put the buttons lower than the residential maximum.

Landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission list places two designated landmarks in Jerome Park: the High Pumping Station, designated July 28, 1981, and the Kingsbridge Armory, designated September 24, 1974. Neither is an apartment building. We found no historic district covering Jerome Park's apartment blocks; the nearest districts we found are Perry Avenue in Bedford Park and the Grand Concourse district farther south. If you are unsure about an address, the commission's map will settle it.

Buildings run by agencies or programs. Where a building is operated under a government housing program or managed by a large management office, the entry system belongs to the owner and its management, not to individual tenants. Residents should report failures to management in writing and keep a record. We work in such buildings only when the owner or management brings us in.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Jerome Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Jerome Park owners, boards, tenants and developers describe: old chains of wiring that fail a floor at a time, elevators where nobody answers, keys that open too much, doors left on the latch, gates that may or may not count, and new buildings still on paper. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Old systems in big and old buildings

Our management says the intercom is "wired in series," the same phrase Tracey Towers management used. What does that actually mean for us?

It means the cable leaves the power supply or the lobby panel and passes from one apartment's station to the next, rather than giving each apartment its own run. The practical effect is that every station is a link in a chain. If one link fails, whether from a loose terminal, a damaged handset or a corroded splice in a closet, the apartments beyond it can lose calls, the ability to talk or the ability to release the door. That is why series-wired buildings tend to fail in groups: a whole line goes quiet, or everyone above a certain floor stops working. A repair can restore the chain, but the building stays just as fragile. The lasting fix is to rewire so that each apartment, or at least each line, has its own run back to a central point. On the survey we trace the layout, test what is there and quote the rewire on its own lines, so the owner or board can see what the chain is costing and what it would take to end it.

Residents in a tower near us said the intercom in the elevators did not reach security. Is the elevator's emergency phone part of the lobby intercom?

Usually not. The phone or speaker in an elevator car is part of the elevator's own emergency equipment, and in most buildings it is maintained under the elevator service contract rather than by whoever maintains the lobby intercom. It may call a monitoring service, the building's security desk or both, depending on how it was set up. The lobby intercom is a separate system that connects the entrance to apartments and sometimes to a desk. When residents say the intercom does not work in the elevators, as Tracey Towers residents told Norwood News, the first step is to find out which system they mean and who is responsible for it. Report the problem to management in writing, say which elevator and when it failed, and keep a copy. Where a building wants its lobby system and its desk to work together with elevator communications, we coordinate with the elevator contractor rather than altering the elevator's equipment ourselves.

Doors, keys and who gets inside

Tenants in a nearby Concourse building found that some keys opened both the front door and other apartments. Would a new intercom fix that?

Not on its own. Norwood News reported tenants in a Grand Concourse building saying that some keys fit not only the front door but other apartments as well. That is a lock and keying problem, and it belongs to a locksmith: the apartment cylinders need to be rekeyed so each apartment key opens only its own door and the entrance. An intercom controls how visitors are let in; it does not change what a resident's metal key opens. Where an intercom project does help is at the entrance itself. If the street door moves to fobs or codes as part of the job, the number of loose entrance keys in circulation stops mattering, and a lost fob can be switched off in minutes. We recommend owners in that situation do both: have a locksmith sort out the apartment keying, and use the intercom work to put the entrance on credentials that can be managed.

Since our buzzer stopped letting visitors in, neighbors have started leaving the lobby door unlocked. What should we do until it is fixed?

Keep the door locked, even though it is less convenient. An entrance left on the latch is open to everyone, not just the guest someone is expecting, and that is the exact situation Tracey Towers residents described when their system would not buzz people in. In the meantime, meet visitors at the door yourself, ask guests to call or text when they arrive, and ask delivery drivers to call your phone. Put a short note to management in writing that the intercom is not releasing the door, with the date and which apartments are affected, and keep a copy. If the building has more than one entrance, make sure the others are locked too. Once a technician is on site, the cause is often simple: a failed power supply, a worn strike, or a broken link in a chain. Fixing it quickly is the best way to stop the propping, because people stop wedging the door the day the release works again.

Our board wants a magnetic lock on the lobby door instead of a strike. What has to be true for people to get out safely?

A magnetic lock holds the door shut with power, so the whole question is what happens on the inside. Anyone in the lobby must be able to leave without a key, a code or special knowledge, every single time, including when the power, the intercom or the network fails. An installer summary of New York's rules puts it plainly: any access control setup that impedes interior egress violates the fire code. In practice that means release devices on the exit side that cut power to the lock reliably, and tying the lock into the building's fire and life-safety arrangements where those exist. That is more equipment and more things to maintain. On most prewar Jerome Park entrance doors, an electric strike paired with the door's existing exit hardware is simpler: the door always opens mechanically from inside, and the strike only controls entry. We explain both options to the board and recommend the one that fits the door.

Our courtyard has a sidewalk gate in front of the building. Does the city treat that gate like the building's entrance door?

The sources we reviewed speak about building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors, and say they must be self-closing in every type of multiple dwelling. They do not spell out how a courtyard gate in front of the building is treated, and we are not going to guess at a legal answer. Ask your attorney, managing agent or HPD if it matters for your building. What we can say is practical. If the gate is the point where visitors are stopped and buzzed in, it should close and latch on its own after every release just like a door, or the whole system is only as strong as a gate that hangs open. If the gate stands open and the lobby door is the security line, then the lobby door carries the panel and the strike, and the gate is simply a way into the courtyard. We help the owner decide which line to defend and set up the hardware to match.

Tenants, owners and the rules

In our building about half the apartments get calls and half do not. Does that count as a working intercom?

According to installer summaries of the city's rules, no. One of them states that a system where some apartments work and others do not constitutes a violation, and another describes a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous condition that HPD enforces on tenant complaint. HPD's own page lists 30 days to correct Class B hazardous conditions. For tenants, the useful step is to write down which apartments work and which do not, with dates, and send it to management. For owners, a partial failure is a signal worth taking seriously, because it often means the building's wiring is a chain that has broken at one point, and the next break may take out more apartments. A proper repair restores every apartment and tests each one, with the result written down, so nobody has to argue later about whether the system works.

Our building is a few blocks from the Kingsbridge Armory and the High Pumping Station. Do their landmark designations limit what we can put at our front door?

Not because of the distance. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designations for the High Pumping Station in 1981 and the Kingsbridge Armory in 1974 cover those buildings, not the apartment houses around them. We found no historic district covering Jerome Park's apartment blocks; the nearest districts we found were Perry Avenue in Bedford Park and the Grand Concourse district farther south. So for most Jerome Park buildings, replacing a lobby panel is a matter for the owner, the board and the building's own rules rather than Landmarks review. If your building's address might fall inside a district, the commission's online map settles it in a minute. Separately, many co-ops have their own rules about the lobby and facade, and boards often want the new panel to suit the building's original style, which we take into account whether or not Landmarks is involved.

New buildings on the side streets

We are developing one of the new buildings on the side streets. Should we pick the entry system now, or leave it to whoever will manage the building?

Pick the infrastructure now and keep the brand decision open as long as it is useful. The parts that are expensive to change later are the cable paths from every apartment to the equipment room, the conduit to the entrance and any second door, power and backup for that room, and a door frame prepared for the strike. Those have to be in the drawings before walls close, and they suit almost any modern system. The brand and the subscription terms, if any, can be chosen closer to completion, ideally with input from the managing agent who will run the building, because they will be the ones answering residents' questions. If the management company is not yet known, choose a platform the owner can administer without being locked into one installer, insist on written documentation and administrator logins, and make sure the system can call ordinary phones for residents without smartphones. We can write the infrastructure scope for the architect now and quote the stations later.

FAQ

Jerome Park Intercom Installation FAQ

Once HPD issues an intercom violation, how long does the owner have to correct it?
+

Installer summaries describe a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous violation, and HPD's own page lists 30 days to correct Class B conditions. A self-closing door violation is Class C, with 14 days. If your building has an open violation, tell us the date it was issued when you call, so the survey and the work can be scheduled inside that window.

There is a loud hum on our handset every time someone calls from the lobby. What usually causes that in an older building?
+

In older buildings the usual causes are intercom cable running right beside power wiring, a failing power supply or amplifier, a poor ground, or a corroded splice somewhere along the riser. A technician can usually narrow it down by testing at the power supply and at the affected apartment. If several apartments hum, the cause is probably shared; if only one does, it is usually local to that line or station.

Each apartment in our building has its own doorbell at the hallway door. Can those tie into the new intercom stations?
+

Often, yes. Many apartment stations have an input for a hallway door button, so a knock-free ring at the apartment door sounds on the same station with a different tone from a lobby call. Whether the existing hallway buttons and their wire can be reused depends on their condition, which we check on the survey. Where they cannot, a small new button and short run can replace them.

Our lobby has an automatic door opener with a push plate. Can a release from an apartment open the door automatically?
+

In many cases, yes. Most door operators accept a signal from an intercom or access system, so a release can unlock the strike and then swing the door open for a visitor using a wheelchair or a walker. We coordinate the timing with the operator's settings and confirm the door still closes and latches on its own afterward. If the operator is serviced by another company, we work with them rather than changing its settings ourselves.

The mail carrier gets into our lobby with a postal lock beside the old panel. What happens to it when the panel is replaced?
+

The postal lock belongs to the Postal Service, not to the building, so we do not remove or rewire it on our own. Some new panels have a space designed to hold it, and in other buildings it stays in its own small box beside the panel. If the new panel's position would affect the postal lock, the owner or managing agent should contact the local post office before work starts, and we plan the layout around their answer.

On a moving day, can the super keep the lobby door unlocked for a few hours and have it lock again by itself?
+

Many current systems allow a scheduled or timed unlock that the administrator sets for a set period, after which the door returns to locked automatically. That is safer than wedging the door, which is easy to forget. Someone should still keep an eye on the entrance while it is unlocked, and the door must keep closing on its closer even while the strike is open.

On our old system we can sometimes hear another apartment talking to the lobby. Is that a wiring fault, and will a new system end it?
+

Hearing other conversations usually means apartments share a talk path, either by design in a very old system or because of a fault such as damaged insulation or a crossed splice. Modern systems give each call its own connection, so the lobby talks only to the apartment being called. A new system on properly separated or rewired runs should end the problem, and we test for it apartment by apartment before handover.

After the new system is running, should the old intercom cable stay in the walls?
+

Old cable that tested well can stay in place, labeled as a spare, which is useful if a line ever needs a backup path. Cable that failed or is no longer connected to anything should be removed wherever it can be reached without damaging finishes, or at least cut, labeled and noted on the wiring record as dead, so a future technician does not waste time tracing it.

With a video system, can we see who is downstairs before deciding whether to answer at all?
+

Yes. On most video stations the picture appears as soon as the call comes in, before anyone presses talk, so a resident can see the visitor and choose whether to answer. Many systems also let an apartment look at the entrance camera without a call, although some buildings limit that feature for privacy. We set it up the way the owner or board decides.

A tenant is renovating and wants the intercom station moved to a different wall. Who arranges that?
+

The station and its cable usually belong to the building, so the request should go to the owner, the managing agent or the co-op board first. Moving a station is normally a short job if the new spot is close and the wall is open during the renovation. It is much easier to plan the move before new cabinets or drywall go up than to cut into finished work afterward.
