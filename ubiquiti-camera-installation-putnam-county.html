The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need Ubiquiti Camera Installation

Start with the houses. The Census Bureau’s 2023 American Community Survey counts 38,369 homes in Putnam, and the median one was built in 1968. Close to one in five — 6,857 of them — went up before 1940, while barely a thousand have been built since 2010. More than three-quarters are single-family detached houses. That is the building stock a camera installer meets here: plaster walls and fieldstone foundations in the older hamlets, split-levels and colonials from the commuter decades, and very few houses that were wired for data when they were framed. Every UniFi camera needs one Ethernet cable back to a switch, so the age of the house decides most of the labor.

Then the land. The county’s own hazard-mitigation profile describes a place that is about two-thirds forest, sitting on Hudson Highlands bedrock. Driveways climb, garages stand apart from houses, docks and sheds sit down at the water, and the ground between them is frequently ledge. A wired camera network does not mind distance as long as each run stays inside the roughly 100-metre (328-foot) limit for copper Ethernet, but it minds rock, trees and lightning a great deal. Those three things shape where we can put a cable, how we protect it and which camera goes on the end of it.

Then the people. Putnam’s working residents average about 39 minutes each way to work, and the American Community Survey lists 1,757 homes held for seasonal or occasional use — many in the lake colonies laid out from 1928 at Putnam Lake, Lake Carmel and Lake Peekskill. A thread on r/hudsonvalley in October 2026 asked second-home owners who keeps an eye on their places; the answers ran to caretakers, neighbours with cameras, battery backup and a cellular fallback. The internet those owners rely on is still filling in: in 2016 a county official said flatly, “We do not have 254 square miles of coverage,” and in September 2025 the state awarded Comcast $11.5 million to bring fiber to 390 Putnam locations that did not have it. A camera system watched from Manhattan or Queens has to be reachable through whatever line the house actually has.

And then the power. On the morning after Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020, NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers in the dark, including 12,717 in Carmel and 5,815 in Kent, and Central Hudson had 3,621 of 5,160 out on the river side. The smaller hits keep coming — the July 4, 2026 storms knocked out power across Westchester and Putnam, and an August 3 outage cut 740 Central Hudson customers in Cold Spring and Garrison. Because UniFi cameras take their power from the recorder or a PoE switch, one outage takes every camera down at once, and they all come back only when the recorder, switch, gateway and modem do. Designing that recovery is part of the install, not an afterthought.

Whole UniFi System Dark in Putnam? We Fix It the Same Day

When several UniFi cameras drop at once, the fault is almost never the cameras. It is the thing feeding them: a PoE switch or recorder that lost power and has not come back, a breaker that tripped in the garage, a surge that took out one port, or a modem that came back after an outage on a different address. Before booking a visit, three checks from home or by a neighbour usually tell us which:

  1. Look at the recorder’s front screen or lights. The UNVR Instant has a small 1.14-inch status display; if it is dark, the recorder has no power and the cameras on its ports will be dark too.
  2. Check whether only one camera, one building or everything is gone. One camera points at its cable or port; a whole outbuilding points at the switch or the run that feeds it; everything points at the recorder, the network or the power.
  3. Check the utility. NYSEG and Central Hudson both publish town-by-town outage maps. If the town is still out, nothing on the network will come back until the battery backup or the grid does.

Patterson and Putnam Lake

Putnam Lake was mapped as roughly 11,000 summer lots of 20 by 100 feet; many of those bungalows have since been joined and winterised, but they remain small houses on tight lots with crawlspaces rather than basements. The recorder rarely has a good home here: closets are small, and the unheated garage or shed is tempting but wrong for a recorder rated only to freezing. We usually wall-mount the compact UNVR Instant inside the living space, keep cable runs short, and aim cameras so they cover the owner’s lot rather than three neighbours’ yards on a 20-foot frontage.

Around the I-84 interchange and the village of Brewster, the work is more commercial: storefronts on Main Street and Route 22, offices and contractors’ yards along Route 312, and homes on the commuter routes near the Metro-North station. Putnam Valley

Wooded, hilly and full of lake communities, Putnam Valley declared a flash-flood emergency on July 29, 2026. For cameras that means two things: equipment in basements that take water needs to sit off the floor, and cameras along streams and roads that washed out should be aimed and mounted so a re-graded driveway does not leave them looking at nothing. Short-term rentals are common enough that the town requires a local manager within 30 miles of Town Hall — one more person who needs a limited view of the cameras.

Philipstown, Cold Spring, Nelsonville and Garrison

The river side runs on Central Hudson rather than NYSEG, has the county’s weakest cell coverage — a county legislator called Cold Spring “notorious for having blackout spots” — and is where Optimum has been building fiber. Cold Spring’s 691-acre historic district needs the review board’s approval for exterior changes visible from the street, so a camera on a village façade is planned for position and colour before anyone drills; the black UniFi housings often disappear against dark trim better than the white ones.

Before you buy: a Putnam UniFi planning checklist

Whether you hire us or do some of the work yourself, these are the questions that decide a UniFi system on a Putnam lot. Answering them before buying anything avoids most of the return trips we see.

  • Where will the recorder live? It needs a spot that stays between freezing and 104°F, close to the router, on battery backup and out of a burglar’s first sight line. If the only candidate is an unheated garage, solve that first.
  • How far is each camera, really? Measure the path the cable will take, not the straight line, including the climb up walls. Any camera whose path approaches 100 metres needs a switch or fiber in the plan.
  • What will power them? Add up the published maximum draw of every camera and compare it with the PoE budget of the recorder or switch. Leave headroom; winter nights run infrared hard.
  • Which walls are exposed? Mark north walls, gables and posts in the open; those get the models Ubiquiti rates to −22°F.
  • Which cables leave the house? Every one of them needs grounded surge protection at both ends, and the longest may be better as fiber.
  • Who needs to see what? Owner, family, caretaker, rental manager, staff: list them, and decide which cameras and which rights each one gets.
  • How long are you away? Your longest absence sets the minimum recording history, which sets the drive size and which cameras record continuously.

Ubiquiti Camera Installation Services in Putnam County

Every Putnam UniFi job is a mix of the services below. We price them together because they depend on each other: the camera choice sets the power draw, the power draw sets the recorder or switch, and the building sets where all of it can live.

Complete UniFi Protect systems

Cameras, a UniFi recorder with its drive, every cable, and UniFi Protect set up on each phone in the household. The standard Putnam starting point is four cameras on a UNVR Instant, which has six PoE ports of its own, so a small house needs no separate switch at all.

Power budgets worked out first

We add up each camera’s published maximum draw before we order anything. A recorder or switch that is asked for more than its PoE budget will simply refuse to power the last camera plugged in, which is a common reason owners call us after a do-it-yourself expansion.

Cameras on outbuildings

Garages, barns, studios, docks and pool houses. Short runs go straight back to the recorder; long ones get a small PoE switch in the outbuilding, and the most exposed get fiber, which carries no electrical surge across the yard. Every run that leaves the house is planned for lightning.

Recorder placement and cooling

The recorder goes where it will stay between freezing and 104°F, near the modem when possible, off the floor in basements that take water, and somewhere a burglar will not find it in the first minute. Wall and DIN-rail mounts for the UNVR Instant come in the box.

Outage-proof network core

A battery backup sized for the modem, gateway, recorder and any PoE switch, settings that bring everything back without anyone on site, and a pulled-plug test before we leave so you know how long the cameras ride through a NYSEG or Central Hudson outage.

Recording set for how you use the house

Continuous recording on doors and drives, detection-only recording on quiet woodland views, retention planned around your longest absence, and Enhanced Retention configured where a longer history at lower quality is worth more than a short one at full resolution.

Smart alerts that ignore the trees

UniFi cameras sort motion into people, vehicles and animals on the device itself. We switch on only the detection types you care about, draw zones away from roads, branches and water, and set phone alerts on a schedule so the house is quiet while you are in it.

UniFi doorbells and gate cameras

UniFi doorbells on the front door and the side door the family really uses, and a camera at the end of a long driveway that can read plates on vehicles turning in. Both report into the same UniFi Protect app as the rest of the system.

How the wiring changes by building

In a mid-century house, a four-camera install is mostly a basement job. The recorder sits beside the modem, a single cable runs from it to each corner of the house, and each cable leaves the basement through the rim joist, travels up behind a downspout or inside a soffit, and ends in a junction box behind the camera. The junction box matters: it hides the cable’s bend, keeps water out of the hole, and gives the next technician something to work with.

In an older village house, the same four cameras can take twice as long. Plaster walls do not fish well, fieldstone foundations are thick and irregular, and attics may be too low to crawl. We route cable through closets and chases where we can, and on the outside of the house in raceway painted to match where we cannot, rather than cracking walls that are a century old. Hidden in-wall wiring is priced separately because these houses are where it is genuinely hard.

On a ridge or woodland lot, the problem is exposure. Cameras on north walls and open gables see the coldest air, long runs pick up surges from nearby strikes, and every tree in the frame is a source of false motion. We favour the cameras Ubiquiti rates to −22°F for the most exposed positions, put a surge protector on both ends of any run that leaves the house, and draw detection zones before the first night of alerts.

Camera choice by wall

  • Exposed north walls, gables and poles: G5 Turret Ultra, G6 Turret or G6 PTZ — each rated down to −30°C (−22°F) in Ubiquiti’s tech specs.
  • Sheltered eaves, porches and garage fronts: G6 Bullet or G6 Dome — rated down to −20°C (−4°F), fine under a roof overhang but not our first pick for an open, windward mount.
  • Within reach of people: the G6 Dome, which carries an IK10 impact rating against the IK04 of the turret and bullet models.

Rules a Putnam business should know before adding UniFi cameras

  • Licensing under county Chapter 145. Camera cabling counts as regulated work here: Putnam folds video surveillance into its low-voltage and limited data communications trade, and that trade requires a county licence or registration. Before signing with anyone, look the firm up on the technician lists kept by Putnam Consumer Affairs — that goes for us as well.
  • Sound needs a reason. Several UniFi cameras have microphones. New York’s eavesdropping law makes recording a conversation without at least one party’s consent a felony, so we leave audio recording off at counters, offices and break rooms unless the owner has a lawful reason and posts notice.
  • Neighbours’ yards. Civil Rights Law § 52-a lets a homeowner sue someone who points a video device at their backyard from neighbouring property without written consent. UniFi Protect’s privacy masks black out parts of a view; we use them wherever a camera’s frame crosses a fence.
  • Village façades in Cold Spring. A camera that can be seen from the street on a building inside the historic district is an exterior alteration, and the Historic District Review Board signs off on those. A camera on a rear wall or under a porch out of street view does not go before the board.
  • A state credit for small retailers. New York’s Commercial Security Tax Credit can return as much as $3,000 per store to retail businesses of 50 or fewer employees that buy eligible security equipment, cameras among it. The certificate application closes on October 31 each year.

Many Putnam shops pair cameras with access control for the back door and stockroom, and yards often add dedicated parking-lot cameras once the first system is in.

Your Questions Answered

Ubiquiti Camera Installation in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the UniFi questions Putnam owners ask once they are past “which camera” — the ones about power, placement, the internet, recording, weather and local rules. Each is answered for the houses, lots and lines found here.

Power and the PoE Budget

We added a fourth camera to the UniFi NVR Instant at our Carmel house and it never powered on — why?

Almost certainly the recorder ran out of power to give. The UNVR Instant has six PoE ports, but Ubiquiti lists its total PoE budget as 40 watts and the most any single port can deliver as 15.4 watts. Three G6 Turrets at their published maximum of 12.5 watts each already account for 37.5 of those 40 watts; a fourth pushes the total past the budget, and the recorder declines to power it. A G6 PTZ is a different problem: it needs PoE+ and draws up to 24.5 watts, more than any one port on the Instant can supply at all.

The fix is a small PoE+ switch between the recorder and the cameras, sized with headroom, or a lower-draw camera on the less critical views — the G5 Turret Ultra is listed at 4 watts. Before we order equipment for any Putnam job, we add up each camera’s published maximum against the recorder or switch budget, so the arithmetic is done on paper rather than on a ladder.

How many cameras can a single UniFi NVR Instant record before we need a bigger recorder?

Ubiquiti rates the UNVR Instant for six 4K cameras, eight 2K cameras or fifteen HD cameras, with a single 3.5-inch drive bay. That makes it a good fit for most Putnam houses: four to six cameras of mixed resolution, recorded on one large drive. Past that, or when you want longer recording than one drive can hold, the four-bay UNVR handles up to eighteen 4K cameras, and the UNVR Pro adds seven bays.

The camera count is only half of it. A recorder that supports the cameras may still need a separate PoE switch to power them, and a single-drive recorder keeps no second copy if the drive fails. For houses where the footage really matters — a rental, a business, a property empty for weeks — we usually recommend a multi-bay recorder with drive redundancy rather than stretching the Instant.

When NYSEG drops the power at our Mahopac house, which pieces should the battery backup keep running?

Everything between the cameras and the internet: the modem, the UniFi gateway or router, the recorder, and any PoE switch. Because the cameras take their power over the network cable, a backup that only covers the recorder will still lose any camera fed from a separate switch, and a backup that skips the modem leaves the cameras recording but unreachable from your phone. Ubiquiti’s own guidance on drive health also lists avoiding unexpected shutdowns, which a battery backup prevents.

How long it lasts depends on the load: four low-draw cameras and a compact recorder will ride through far longer on the same battery than a PTZ and a rack recorder. We measure the real draw at install, test with the plug pulled, and tell you how long you have. For houses on generators, the battery also covers the seconds between the grid dropping and the generator taking the load.

Where the Recorder Lives

Is the unheated garage at our Patterson place a reasonable home for the UniFi recorder?

No. Ubiquiti lists the UNVR Instant’s ambient operating range as 0 to 40°C, or 32 to 104°F, and an unheated Putnam garage spends much of the winter below that. Cameras are built for the cold; recorders are not, and the hard drive inside one dislikes both cold starts and summer heat. A garage is also the first place a burglar walks into.

We put the recorder in the conditioned part of the house — a closet, a basement shelf above flood height, a utility room — and run the garage cameras back to it, or to a small switch in the garage, which tolerates the cold better than a hard drive does. If a building must hold the recorder, it needs heat, and the drive needs to be one Ubiquiti lists as compatible.

Does the UniFi recorder have to sit beside the internet modem?

No, but it has to be on the same network, and the closer the better. The recorder needs a cable to the router or gateway; the cameras need cables to the recorder or a PoE switch. In a basement where the modem already lives, putting the recorder on the same shelf keeps that link short and lets one battery backup cover the whole core.

When the modem sits in a spot that cannot take the recorder — a living-room bookcase, an upstairs office — we run one cable from the router to wherever the recorder belongs, usually the basement where the camera cables converge. What we avoid is linking the recorder to the house over Wi-Fi. Recording traffic is constant, and a wireless link between the recorder and the router is the first thing to fail when the family starts streaming.

The Internet Side

Do we have to open ports on the router for UniFi Protect to reach our phones in the city?

No. Ubiquiti’s Help Center says its remote access needs outbound connections on TCP ports 443 and 8883, and states plainly that you do not need to open those ports for inbound traffic. The console reaches out to Ubiquiti’s Site Manager, which verifies who you are and sets up an encrypted session between your phone and the recorder. Nothing on the house router has to be exposed to the internet.

Where remote viewing fails in Putnam, it is usually because something blocks that outbound traffic or the time and name lookups the console depends on — an ISP-supplied router with filtering turned on, or a clock that is wrong because time requests are blocked. We check those at install, and we close any old port forwards left by a previous camera system, because an open port to a recorder is the one thing UniFi does not need.

Should our UniFi console be tied to a UI account, or kept as a local-only login?

For most owners who watch from somewhere else, a UI account with remote management is the practical choice: it is how the phone app reaches the house, and it is what lets you recover access if a password is forgotten. A local-only login keeps everything inside the house and works during an internet outage from a device on the home network, but Ubiquiti warns that local-only credentials cannot be recovered unless an email server was set up manually.

Many Putnam owners want both: a UI account for everyday remote viewing, plus the knowledge that, when the line is down, someone on the home Wi-Fi can still sign in locally and pull a clip. We set it up that way, record who holds the owner account, and give caretakers or managers their own limited logins rather than sharing the owner’s.

Recording for Long Absences

Should the cameras that face the woods record around the clock or only when something is detected?

Usually detection-only. UniFi Protect offers three recording modes — Always, Detection Only and Never — and Ubiquiti itself suggests Detection Only for low-activity areas to save storage. A camera on a tree line records almost nothing of value at 3 a.m. on a still night, and its footage crowds out the views that matter. Pre-event and post-event buffers mean a detection recording still includes the seconds before and after the trigger.

Doors, driveways and the approach to the house are different. In July 2026 the Sheriff said the county courthouse cameras had captured only portions of an incident because the system relied on motion sensors. That is the risk of detection-only on a key view, so we record entrances continuously and let quieter cameras save the drive.

How do we stop one important clip from being written over while an insurance claim drags on?

Flag it, export it, or put it in a case. UniFi Protect’s Storage Manager deletes the oldest footage as the drive fills, and Enhanced Retention may downgrade older footage to low quality. Ubiquiti lets you flag a clip so it is not downgraded, export or archive it to outside storage, or add it to Case Manager, which keeps footage indefinitely.

For anything an adjuster or the police may want, we suggest all three: flag it in the app the day it happens, export a full-quality copy to a laptop or USB drive, and keep a note of the camera, date and time. We show every owner how to do this on handover day, because the time to learn it is not the morning after a break-in.

Will deer, wind and swaying branches flood our phones with UniFi alerts?

They will if alerts are set to plain motion. A Putnam woodland view moves all day — leaves, shadows, snow falling off branches, deer at dusk. UniFi cameras classify what they see as people, vehicles or animals on the device itself, and Ubiquiti says that detection runs on your local network rather than in a cloud. Turning off plain-motion alerts and notifying only on people and vehicles removes most of the noise.

The rest comes from setup: detection zones drawn to exclude the road and the trees, sensitivity tuned in the first week of real use, and alert schedules so your phone is silent while you are home. We return to adjust after the first week if the alerts are still wrong.

Weather, Lightning and Plates

What stops a lightning surge from riding a camera cable back into the recorder?

Grounded surge protection at both ends of any run that leaves the house, and fiber where a run is long and exposed. Ubiquiti sells an outdoor Ethernet surge protector rated for 10 kA that guards against electrostatic discharge and surge events, and installers on Ubiquiti’s own community forum consistently say the same thing: protect both ends, bond the protector to ground, and use shielded cable with a drain wire and shielded connectors or the protector has nowhere to send the surge.

On a Putnam ridge, the cable to a garage or a pole camera is a long copper antenna. Within the house, short runs inside the walls are lower risk; between buildings, a nearby strike can take out a camera, a switch port and the recorder in one go. Where a run crosses open ground to an outbuilding, fiber with a small switch at each end removes the copper path entirely.

Can a UniFi camera read the plate of a car turning into our driveway at night?

The right one, placed properly, can. Ubiquiti lists licence-plate recognition on its G6 Turret, G6 Bullet and G6 PTZ, and sells an AI LPR camera “optimized for recognizing license plates”. What decides success is geometry: a camera aimed down the length of the drive at a narrow point the car must pass, close enough that the plate fills the frame, set with a fast shutter so headlights and motion do not smear it.

That matters in a county where Mahopac residents were asked in August 2026 to check their footage for overnight vehicle break-ins between about 3:30 and 5 a.m. A wide driveway camera shows that a car came in; a plate camera at the choke point shows which car. We usually recommend both: one for the scene, one for the plate.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Ubiquiti Camera Installation in Putnam County

Search-engine AI summaries change constantly and none could be captured for this page, so none is quoted. Instead, these are seven claims about UniFi that recur in forum replies, how-to videos and machine-written answers. Each holds a grain of truth, and each goes wrong somewhere between Ubiquiti’s spec sheet and a Putnam house lot.

“UniFi Protect is a cloud camera service”

It is easy to see why people assume so: you sign in through a Ubiquiti account, the app works from anywhere, and the video turns up on your phone in seconds.

But Ubiquiti’s own documentation says the opposite. Recordings and AI detections stay on your local console, there is no cloud backup of video by default, and Ubiquiti’s Site Manager only verifies your identity and helps build an encrypted connection; it does not handle the video itself.

For a Putnam owner, that cuts both ways. Your footage is private and keeps recording when the internet drops — but if the recorder is stolen or its single drive fails, the footage goes with it. That is why we hide the recorder, keep it on battery backup, and recommend a multi-drive recorder where the footage really matters.

“You have to forward ports for remote viewing”

This one is a leftover from older camera systems, where remote viewing really did mean opening a port on the router and pointing it at the recorder.

Ubiquiti’s Help Center says its remote access needs only outbound traffic on TCP 443 and 8883, and that you do not need to open those ports for inbound traffic. Forwarding ports to a UniFi console adds risk without adding anything.

Where we take over an older Putnam system, we often find forwards left behind for a recorder that no longer exists. We close them as part of the install.

“Any UniFi camera is fine for winter outdoors”

Every outdoor UniFi camera we install carries an IP66 weather rating, so rain and snow are not the issue.

Cold is where they differ. In Ubiquiti’s tech specs, the G5 Turret Ultra, G6 Turret and G6 PTZ are rated to −30°C (−22°F), while the G6 Bullet and G6 Dome are rated to −20°C (−4°F). And the recorders are indoor equipment: the UNVR Instant is rated only from freezing to 104°F.

So the generic answer is half right. The cameras can live outside; the recorder cannot, and on the most exposed Putnam walls we choose the wider-rated models rather than assuming every UniFi camera is the same.

“PoE means you can hang as many cameras as the recorder has ports”

Power over Ethernet really does let one cable carry both power and data, which is what makes UniFi so tidy to install.

But every PoE device has a total budget as well as a port count. The UNVR Instant has six ports and a 40-watt budget, with 15.4 watts per port at most. Four 12.5-watt G6 Turrets exceed that budget, and a 24.5-watt G6 PTZ needs PoE+ that the Instant’s ports do not provide.

We do this sum before ordering: published maximum draw for every camera, against the budget of whatever powers them, with headroom left over for winter nights when infrared runs all night.

“Wiring a UniFi system is a weekend job”

On a new house with an open attic and a basement under every wall, a handy owner can wire a few cameras over a weekend, and many do.

Putnam’s houses are rarely that. The median one dates from 1968, close to a fifth predate 1940, and many sit on rock with a garage across the yard. Plaster, fieldstone, long runs, lightning exposure and the 100-metre copper limit turn a weekend into a project, and Putnam County’s Chapter 145 brings video surveillance work under its low-voltage licensing rules.

We are happy to install equipment an owner already bought, on our published labor-only pricing; the cable runs are where professional time pays for itself.

“If the internet is down, the system is useless”

It is true that you cannot watch from the city while the house has no internet, and in parts of Putnam that happens — the county said in 2016 that it did not have coverage across all 254 square miles, and fiber was still being funded for 390 locations in 2025.

But the cameras keep recording to the local drive as long as they have power, and Ubiquiti documents local management specifically for internet outages: anyone on the home network can sign in by browser or app and review footage.

So the design priority in Putnam is power first. A recorder on battery backup with no internet still records the night; a recorder with no power records nothing at all.

“More megapixels always means better evidence”

A 4K camera does capture more detail than a 2K one, and on a wide yard view that extra detail can be the difference between “a person” and a recognisable one.

But resolution is not reach. A plate 80 feet away on a wide-angle lens is a handful of pixels whatever the sensor size; the AI LPR and the zoom-lens Pro models exist because placement and focal length matter more than megapixels for that job. Higher resolution also costs drive space and upload.

We match the camera to the job: wide 4K for scenes, a zoom or plate camera at the choke point, and lower-resolution, low-draw turrets where the view is simply “is anyone there”.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Watts, Then Walk the Cable

Two habits save more Putnam UniFi jobs than any piece of equipment. The first is arithmetic. Before we order, we write down every camera, its published maximum draw and the port it will use, and add them up against the recorder or switch budget with room to spare. It takes five minutes and it is the difference between a system that works on the first night and one where the last camera stays dark.

The second is walking the route. On a Putnam lot, the straight line from the basement to the garage is rarely where the cable can go: there is a stone wall, a septic field, a tree root mat or bare ledge in the way. So we walk the real path with a measuring wheel, add the climb up the wall and the drop at each end, and compare the total with the 100-metre limit for copper Ethernet. If it does not fit with margin, the plan changes on paper — a small switch in the outbuilding, or fiber — rather than on install day.

Then, before we leave, we pull the plug on the battery backup’s input and watch the cameras ride through, and we open UniFi Protect on a phone using cellular data rather than the house Wi-Fi. If the owner will be watching from the city, that is the test that counts.

FAQ

Putnam County Ubiquiti Camera Installation FAQ

Do the outdoor UniFi cameras each need an electrical outlet nearby?
+

No. UniFi cameras are powered over the same Ethernet cable that carries their video, a method called Power over Ethernet, so a camera on a garage gable or a post along the drive needs only its one cable back to the recorder or a PoE switch. Ubiquiti lists PoE as the power method for its G5 and G6 cameras, and PoE+ for the PTZ and Pro models. The only outlet involved is the one indoors that powers the recorder or switch, which we put on battery backup.

Does UniFi Protect send our video to a cloud service we have to pay for?
+

No. Ubiquiti’s Help Center says UniFi Protect stores recordings locally on your own console, that there is no cloud backup of your video by default, and that Ubiquiti cannot view, store or access your streams. Remote viewing passes through an encrypted connection to your recorder rather than a storage plan. We charge no monthly fee for recording; how much history you keep depends on the drive in the recorder.

Can the cameras be watched on a TV in the house as well as on our phones?
+

Yes. The UNVR Instant has an HDMI port that Ubiquiti calls Viewport, so a television or monitor can show live cameras without a computer. For larger recorders, Ubiquiti sells a separate Viewport device. We put the screen where it is useful, such as a kitchen, a front office or a shop counter, and set the layout to the views people actually check.

Can a UniFi camera go on a fence post or a lamp post along the driveway?
+

Often, yes. Ubiquiti includes a pole mount with the G6 Bullet for posts of 1.5 to 2 inches in diameter, and junction boxes and other mounts are available for wider posts. The real question is the cable: it has to reach the post underground or along a protected path, and a post far from the house is exposed to lightning, so we fit grounded surge protection. Trees are poor mounts because they move and grow.

How does the system warn us that the recorder’s hard drive is wearing out?
+

UniFi OS watches the drive’s health and reports it in plain terms: Optimal, Drive Failure Detected, or Drive at Risk of Failure, based on things like bad sectors and the drive’s own SMART reports. Ubiquiti says a drive showing early failure should be replaced as soon as possible. We switch on notifications so the owner hears about it, and on multi-bay recorders we set up redundancy so one failed drive does not lose the footage.

What should be ready at the house before the install day?
+

Access to the basement or closet where the modem lives, the attic hatch if there is one, and every outbuilding that gets a camera. Clear a little room along the walls where cable will run, have the internet working, and have the owner’s Ubiquiti account email to hand, or let us create one with you. If you will not be there, a caretaker or neighbour can let us in and we finish the phone setup with you by call.

How long does a four-camera UniFi install usually take at a Putnam house?
+

Most four-camera jobs on a house with a basement or accessible attic take one working day, including setup and testing. Older houses with plaster walls, stone foundations or no attic access take longer, as do cameras on outbuildings that need a separate switch, fiber or a trench. We give you the expected time with the written quote, after the site walk.

Is half the price due before the work starts?
+

Yes. Our service agreement asks for a 50% deposit, with the balance due on completion. The agreement also sets out the warranty terms and the late-fee schedule, and your written quote shows every line before you sign anything.

We already run a UniFi Dream Machine Pro for our Wi-Fi — can Protect record on it instead of a separate recorder?
+

Yes. Ubiquiti lists the UDM-Pro among the consoles that take a 3.5-inch drive for Protect recordings, so cameras can record to it without a separate recorder. It has no PoE ports for cameras, so a PoE switch powers them, and it shares its processor with your network duties. For more cameras, longer recording or drive redundancy, a dedicated recorder is the better choice, and we can add one to the same UniFi system.

How well does a UniFi camera within arm’s reach on a porch stand up to being knocked or tampered with?
+

It depends on the model. Ubiquiti gives most of its turret and bullet cameras an IK04 impact rating, while the G6 Dome carries IK10, the top of that scale. For a porch, a shop door or a rental entrance where people can reach the camera, we use a dome like that, mount it as high as the view allows, and run its cable inside the wall or a junction box so it cannot simply be pulled.
