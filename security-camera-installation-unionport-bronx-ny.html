The Neighborhood

Why Unionport Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Unionport is one of the older planned settlements in the East Bronx, and its houses carry that age into every camera job. In 1851 a building association bought a large tract west of Westchester Creek, and Industrial Home Association No. 2 filed its map of Unionport in 1854. The village stayed outside New York City until the 1890s; NY1 gives 1895 for when it joined, while CityNeighborhoods says 1898, and we would rather show both dates than pick one. The IRT Pelham Line arrived in 1918, and the housing boom that followed, running through the 1920s, 1930s and 1940s, filled the blocks with the brick rowhouses and two-family homes that still dominate them. Working-class Irish and German families came first after the subway; Holy Family parish had been founded in 1896 for German Catholics. Then, in the 1950s, the Cross Bronx Expressway was cut through the area. Local accounts describe apartment buildings and single-family homes demolished in its path, and blasting damaged the old Holy Family church so badly that a new one was built in 1962. The expressway was finished in 1963, larger housing replaced smaller buildings in places, and some of Unionport's ordinary streets became exit ramps. The result is a neighborhood where a prewar row, a postwar six-story building and a highway service road can all share a single block, and a camera plan has to work for all three.

What stands here now is mostly small and mostly rented. Community Board 9 calls Unionport largely two-family homes. NeighborhoodScout counts 35.6 percent of the residential real estate as two-, three- and four-unit buildings and reports that most homes are renter-occupied and that many were built by 1939. City-Data's count by year of construction shows 1,205 units from the 1940s, 768 from the 1950s, 1,028 from the 1960s and 1,369 from 2010 to 2019, plus 150 more since 2020, so a technician here moves between plaster-and-brick houses and buildings with modern low-voltage risers in the same week. Homes.com describes postwar high-rises, detached Colonial Revivals with siding on small lots, and large prewar attached rowhouses with seven to nine bedrooms, and puts the median year built at 1945. Along Unionport Road, co-op listings run up to the twelfth floor; on Havemeyer Avenue and Ellis Avenue there are low-rise co-op walk-ups; on Castle Hill Avenue, a 140-unit rental at 1140 Castle Hill Avenue shares the corridor with two-family buildings that have a store at street level and a full basement. Because so many homes are rented, a Unionport camera conversation almost always involves two parties: the owner who pays for the system and the household who lives in front of it. Who sees which camera, who holds the recorder and what the tenant is told are decided at the start, not after a dispute.

The crime picture is mixed and deserves plain words. The 43rd Precinct, which covers Unionport along with Castle Hill, Parkchester and Soundview, names Westchester Avenue, Castle Hill Avenue, White Plains Road and Parkchester as its four main commercial strips. Wikipedia's Castle Hill entry lists the precinct's 2022 totals as 747 robberies, 302 burglaries, 1,039 grand larcenies and 561 grand larcenies of motor vehicles, and a local ranking still places the 43rd among the less safe parts of the Bronx. This year brought two cases on residential blocks: Norwood News and News 12 reported a fatal shooting on Haviland Avenue between Castle Hill Avenue and Havemeyer Avenue on May 24, 2026, and News 12 reported a daytime double shooting in the courtyard of the 124-unit building at 2035 Newbold Avenue on September 26, in which a 19-year-old later died. A camera system does not stop violence, and we will never sell one as if it did. What cameras do well here is narrower and more practical: they record who came through a door, who took what from a store counter or a laundry room, which car stopped at the curb and which way it left, and they give an owner, a board or the police something concrete to work from afterward.

The paperwork side of Unionport is lighter than in some Bronx neighborhoods, and that is worth knowing. None of the Bronx historic districts on the Historic Districts Council's list fall in Unionport, so most houses and buildings here carry no Landmarks review for exterior cameras. No general city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings, although multiple dwellings must keep a working intercom or buzzer under the Housing Maintenance Code. Public cameras are arriving slowly: PIX11 reported the Borough President budgeting $1.2 million for NYPD surveillance cameras and $500,000 for sanitation cameras aimed at illegal dumping across the Bronx, and Community Board 9's own FY2026 needs statement includes the blunt line that new cameras are needed and new mailboxes are too small. Owners who want investigators to know their cameras exist can list them, voluntarily, in the Bronx District Attorney's BronxCAMS program, which already holds NYPD and NYCHA cameras and is never published as a public map. None of that watches your stoop, your side gap or your stock room. That is the job a private system does.

Unionport facts that decide where a camera goes

  • Two households per house, often: Community Board 9 describes the neighborhood as largely two-family homes, and NeighborhoodScout counts 35.6 percent of residential property as two- to four-unit buildings.
  • Mostly renters: most Unionport homes are renter-occupied, so owner and tenant permissions shape nearly every camera plan.
  • Four building eras: City-Data counts large groups of units from the 1940s and 1960s and 1,369 built between 2010 and 2019, each with a different path for cable.
  • Stores under homes: Castle Hill Avenue listings include two-family buildings with a store and a full basement, like the one at 1240 Castle Hill Avenue near the train station.
  • Highway edges: the neighborhood is bounded by the Cross Bronx Expressway and Bruckner Boulevard, and the expressway's construction turned some local streets into exit ramps.
  • New buildings arriving: Bruckner Heights finished 330 apartments at 2070 Chatterton Avenue and 1017 Olmstead Avenue in 2026, and permits have been filed for 1933 Lafayette Avenue, 2069 Bruckner Boulevard and 900 Castle Hill Avenue.

What Unionport owners tend to add next. Once the front and rear cameras are working on a two-family, the next request is usually a door station at the street door that rings both floors, or a keypad on the side gate. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an entry upgrade looks likely, a spare cable to the front door, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

Front Camera Dark on the Two-Family, or a Store Clip That Has to Be Pulled Today? Fast Camera Help in Unionport

Unionport calls tend to arrive in two forms. In the first, a camera has quietly stopped: the rear-yard view on a two-family after a summer storm, the lobby camera in a 1960s building after the internet provider swapped the modem, the camera over a Castle Hill Avenue register after someone unplugged a power strip to charge a phone. In the second, something has happened, a package has gone from a stoop, a laundry machine has been pried open, a car at the curb has been entered, and the clip has to be saved before the recorder writes over it, which on a small drive can happen within days. For a dead camera, write down which one it is and when it last showed a picture, check whether the recorder and any switch beside it still show lights, and do not reset the recorder to factory settings, which can wipe passwords and remote access along with the fault. For an incident, leave the recorder running, note the camera name and the exact time, and if you know how, copy that window to a USB stick or lock it in the recorder's playback screen. Beside the Expressways

Where the Cross Bronx Meets the Bruckner: Camera Work on Unionport's Highway Edge

Few Bronx neighborhoods sit as close to two expressways as Unionport. The Cross Bronx runs along one edge, Bruckner Boulevard and the Bruckner Expressway along another, and the two meet nearby. When the Cross Bronx was finished in 1963, some local streets became exit ramps and service roads, and houses that once faced a quiet residential block now face traffic that rarely stops. That changes the camera job in ways an installer from elsewhere can miss. A front camera on a highway-edge two-family sees hundreds of vehicles an hour. It is lit, then darkened, by a steady sweep of headlights after sunset. It records a background that is always moving, so a careless motion setting will fire an alert every few seconds until the owner turns alerts off altogether, which is the opposite of what a camera is for. None of these problems needs exotic equipment. They need placement and settings chosen for the block. The table below shows what we look at.

Draw the alert zone on the house, not the road

The single most useful setting on a highway-edge camera is the alert zone. Modern recorders and cameras let us draw an outline on the picture and tell the system to raise an alert only when a person appears inside it. On a Unionport front, that outline usually covers the bottom of the stoop, the walk to the door and the gate, and leaves the sidewalk, the curb lane and the roadway outside. Recording continues across the whole frame all the time, so the street is still on file if anything happens there, but your phone only lights up when someone steps onto your property. On a house where the owner wants to know about cars stopping in front, we add a second, smaller zone on the curb right outside, set to vehicles that stay for more than a short time rather than vehicles passing through.

Aim along the facade, and let the light fall behind the camera

A camera pointed straight out at a busy road stares into oncoming headlights all night. A camera mounted at the corner of the house and aimed back along the front, toward the door, keeps the headlights at its side and sees the faces of people approaching the stoop. Where the front door sits in a shallow vestibule, as it does on many semi-detached brick pairs, we look at whether the porch light falls on the visitor or on the lens, and re-aim or shade it. A steady light that stays on keeps most current cameras in color all night; a light that snaps on only when someone arrives makes the first seconds of the clip the worst ones.

Which vehicles a front camera can really identify

Owners on blocks near the ramps sometimes ask for a camera that reads every plate going by. That is a specialized job with its own camera, lens, angle and lighting, and on a fast multi-lane road it is not realistic from the front of a house. What a well-placed camera can do is show the make, color and shape of a car that stops at your curb, the direction it came from and went, and, at the moment it slows or parks, often the plate. We set the curb camera for that moment, not for the stream of traffic, and we say so on the quote so nobody expects more than the angle can give.

Blocks that end at a fence or a ramp

Where the expressway cut through, a few blocks end abruptly at a fence, a wall or an on-ramp. The last houses on those blocks have a side that faces no neighbor at all, and that side is often dark. We treat it as a second front: a camera on the corner of the house, covering the side yard and the rear gate, with its own alert zone, and lighting checked on the walk-through. The same goes for houses whose rear yards back onto a retaining wall, where the only way into the yard is from a neighbor's yard or over the wall.

Stores Below, Homes Above

What Two Unionport Fires Teach About Camera Work in Mixed-Use Buildings

Two fires in the last few years are part of how many Unionport owners think about their buildings. In October 2022, PIX11 reported three people dead and several injured in a two-alarm fire on the top floor of a multi-use building at 2165 Quimby Avenue near Castle Hill Avenue. In July 2023, News 12 reported a four-alarm fire on Castle Hill Avenue, at 1101 to 1103, that left seven firefighters and a civilian injured, with the roof sagging and partly collapsing. We are not suggesting cameras had anything to do with either fire, or could have prevented them. We bring them up because they are a reminder that the mixed-use buildings on Castle Hill Avenue and the streets around it, with a store at street level and homes above, are exactly the buildings where camera work has to respect fire separation, and where the camera system itself should be planned to survive a bad night.

Every hole through a rated wall or floor gets sealed

In a building where a store sits under apartments, the floor and walls between them, and the walls around the stair, are meant to slow fire and smoke. Camera cable that travels from a store's back room up to a recorder, or from a cellar to a residential landing, has to cross those barriers somewhere. Each crossing is a hole, and an unsealed hole is a path for smoke. We keep crossings to as few as possible, group cables through a single sleeve where we can, and seal every opening through a rated wall or floor with firestop rated for that assembly before we leave the building. On the written quote, firestopping appears as its own line, so an owner can see it was done.

Keep the stair clear and the exit signs visible

Stair and hallway cameras are useful in mixed-use buildings because the residential stair is often the one space the store owner, the building owner and the tenants all share. A camera there is mounted where it does not block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, an emergency light or the swing of a door, and its cable runs in a way that does not hold a self-closing door open or pass through the door frame. We never mount anything that changes how a stair door closes and latches.

Plan the recorder so it is not the first thing lost

A recorder on a top-floor shelf or in a store's back room sits where heat and water may reach it first. Where the building allows, we put the recorder in a locked, dry spot low in the building, away from the boiler and above any floor that has taken water, on a battery unit so a power dip does not corrupt the drive. For owners who want an extra layer, we set the recorder to send copies of selected clips, or of alert clips from the most important cameras, to a phone or a cloud account, so the record of what happened at the door does not depend on one box surviving. That is a choice with its own trade-offs in internet use and privacy, and we walk through those before switching it on.

Cameras are not fire detectors

Some cameras advertise smoke or flame detection. These features can be useful extras, but they are not a substitute for smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms or a building fire alarm system, and we never present them that way. If your building needs fire alarm work, that is a separate system with its own rules, handled separately. What a camera system does well in a mixed-use building is record who used the stair, who propped a door, and who went in and out of the store's back door after hours.

Semi-detached brick pairs: a shared center wall, two front doors close together

On a semi-detached pair, your stoop and your neighbor's stoop often sit only a few feet apart, separated by a railing or a low wall. A front camera on your side has to see your door and your visitors without making a habit of recording your neighbor's door. We usually mount it on the brick on the open side of your entrance, aimed back across your own stoop toward the center wall, so the frame ends near the railing. The shared center wall stays clear of mounts; anything fixed there affects both houses. Cable comes up from your own cellar through a single sealed opening low on the front, or down inside the stoop soffit, so nothing runs across the face of the brick. At the rear, these houses usually have a fenced yard and a back door to the cellar; one camera on the rear wall, high enough to be out of reach but low enough to see faces at the back door, covers both.

Detached houses in siding: mount on the framing, not on the skin

The detached Colonial Revival houses Homes.com describes on small Unionport lots are mostly frame houses clad in siding. Siding is a skin, not a structure. A camera screwed only into siding loosens, drifts and lets water in behind the panel. We fit a mounting block designed for the siding profile, fix it through into the sheathing and framing behind, seal the cable opening behind the block, and mount the camera on the block. Where the house has wood trim at the corners or under the eaves, the camera often goes there instead. Cable runs inside: down from the attic into the eave for upper cameras, up from the cellar through the sill for lower ones. On these lots the side gaps are narrow, and the back of the house often holds a garage reached by a driveway, so the typical plan is front, both side gaps from the corners, the rear yard and the garage.

Prewar attached rowhouses: everything comes through the cellar

An attached rowhouse has no side to work from. Every cable for the front and the back has to travel through the house itself, which in practice means the cellar. We run cable along the cellar joists from the front wall to the rear wall, come up through the front foundation or the stoop for the door camera, and through the rear wall for the yard camera. Cameras for upper floors, such as one watching a rear roof or extension, reach the recorder through a closet, a chase or a pipe opening rather than the face of a plaster wall. The large prewar rowhouses in Unionport, some with seven to nine bedrooms, may hold several households or a large family across three floors; the recorder goes wherever the owner wants control to sit, and each floor gets only the views it needs.

1940s brick apartment buildings: the cellar is the backbone

The 1940s buildings that make up the biggest single decade in City-Data's count generally share a layout: a street door into a small vestibule and lobby, a stair or a single elevator, a cellar with the boiler, meters, storage and often a laundry room, and a rear door to a yard or alley. The cellar ceiling is the natural route for camera cable, carrying it from a locked room to the street door, the lobby, the laundry room and the rear door. Landing cameras on upper floors are reached through a stair chase or pipe opening so hallway plaster stays intact. These buildings rarely have staff on site at night, so the cameras that matter most are the ones that show a face at the street door and at the rear and cellar doors, and alerts go to the super or the managing agent after hours.

1960s buildings and Unionport Road co-ops: block cellars and board paperwork

The 1960s buildings and mid-rise co-ops, like those along Unionport Road with apartments up to the twelfth floor, tend to have concrete block in the cellar and around the core, an elevator lobby, and sometimes a parking area. Block is hard on cable routes but good for mounts. We plan one or two core-drilled openings that carry all the cables for a floor or a zone, rather than many small holes, and seal each one. In a co-op, the camera project is the board's decision, usually run through the managing agent with a contractor agreement that names insurance requirements; we supply what that agreement asks for. Because officers, the super and the managing agent all touch the system, each gets a separate account with separate permissions, written down.

Buildings finished since 2010: inheriting someone else's system

Field Notes: What We Look At First on a Unionport Walk-Through

A Unionport walk-through begins at the curb, because the curb is where the expressway traffic, the parked cars and the front door all meet. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Unionport: Questions Answered

Houses, side gaps and driveways

The detached houses on our block stand only a few feet apart. How does a crew reach an eave in a side gap that narrow?

Usually with a narrow-base extension ladder on a leveled, footed base, a stand-off stabilizer that holds the ladder clear of the gutter, and two people, one climbing and one holding the base. When the gap is too tight to angle a ladder safely, we move the camera to the front or rear corner of the house and aim it down the gap instead, which often works better anyway, because it sees faces walking toward it rather than the tops of heads passing under it. If the ladder would have to stand on your neighbor's side of the line or lean near their windows, we ask you to clear it with them before install day, and we never set anything against their siding or gutter. Cable for a side-gap camera is fed from the attic or cellar so the time spent up the ladder is only the time it takes to mount the camera.

Our garage sits at the back of the lot at the end of a concrete driveway. How does camera cable get there without a trench scar across the concrete?

There are four common routes, and we pick after seeing the lot. If there is already conduit to the garage, for power or an old light, we pull outdoor cable through it. If the garage is close to the house, an overhead run on a support wire, high enough to clear any vehicle, can span from the eave to the garage. If there is soil along the edge of the driveway or the fence, a shallow run of direct-burial cable can follow that edge instead of crossing the slab; any digging waits until underground utilities have been marked. Where none of those works, a pair of small wireless bridge radios, one on the house and one on the garage, carries the picture with no cable between them. Cutting a groove in the concrete is the last resort, and we say so plainly on the quote if it is the only option.

On a small Unionport lot, should our cameras have fixed lenses or adjustable zoom lenses?

Mostly fixed, with one or two exceptions. On a two-family or a detached house in siding, most views are short: the stoop, the side gap, a rear yard of modest depth. A fixed wide lens covers those well, costs less and has nothing to drift. The exceptions are views where the thing that matters is farther away or narrower than the frame: the curb across a sidewalk on a wide avenue like Castle Hill Avenue, the gate at the back of a long yard, or the door of a garage at the end of a driveway. For those, a varifocal camera, adjusted on site until the target fills the picture, earns its extra cost. We mark on the quote which cameras are fixed and which are varifocal, and why.

Would a solar battery camera on our back fence save us from running cable to it?

Sometimes, but go in with clear expectations. Rear yards on many Unionport blocks are hemmed in by neighboring houses and trees, and in winter a panel on a fence may get only a few hours of direct sun. Battery cameras also work differently from wired ones: most sleep until they sense motion, then wake and record a clip, so the first moment of an event can be missed and there is no continuous recording to scroll back through. They depend on the house Wi-Fi reaching the fence, which brick walls often block. A solar camera can be a good fit for a secondary view, such as a shed or a garden gate that is rarely used. For the back door, the yard as a whole or anything you might need as evidence, a wired camera on the house is the better choice.

Apartment buildings and co-ops

The coin boxes on the machines in our building's basement laundry room keep getting pried open. Where does a camera go?

Two positions cover it. The first is high in the corner opposite the laundry room door, angled to record the face of every person who comes in; that is the camera that tells you who. The second looks along the row of machines at the coin boxes or card readers, so it shows which machine someone works on and what tool they use. Both should be hard-cased domes, because a laundry room is exactly where a camera gets hit, and the room should be lit well enough at night to keep them in color. If the machines belong to an outside laundry operator rather than the building, tell them before the cameras go in, since they may want copies of footage when their equipment is damaged. As with any common area, the owner or board decides who may review the recordings.

Would one 360-degree fisheye camera on our lobby ceiling do the work of two ordinary cameras?

It does one job very well and another poorly. A fisheye in the middle of a lobby ceiling sees the whole room at once, the street door, the mailboxes, the elevator and the stair, which makes it an excellent overview and hard to dodge. But it spreads its picture across the entire room, so a face near the edge is small and bent, and even with the recorder flattening the image, identifying someone by face is harder than with a camera aimed straight at the door. In the low-ceilinged lobbies of many 1940s and 1960s buildings, our usual answer is both: a fisheye for the overview, and a separate camera at head height facing the inner door, so every person coming in is recorded face-on.

A tenant asked our management office for the lobby clip that shows her package being taken. Do we have to hand it over?

Generally, no: Brick Underground's reporting on landlord footage notes that a landlord is not obligated to share surveillance video with a tenant. Many owners do share in a case like this, because it costs little and settles the matter. The sensible approach is a written policy, decided before the first request. It can say that the owner will save any clip a tenant reports within a set number of days, will give it to police when a report is filed, and will show or give it to the tenant at the owner's discretion. Whatever you decide, save the clip as soon as the request arrives, so it is not overwritten while you think it over. We can show the super how to lock and export a clip during the handover.

Stores on the avenues

Can the sales from our register show up on the camera picture, so we can match each sale or void to the video?

Often, yes. Many recorders can take the text that a point-of-sale system sends, item, price, void, refund, no-sale, and print it on the video from the counter camera as each transaction happens. When you search later for a void or a no-sale opening, the recorder jumps to that moment and you see the drawer and the hands. Whether it works with your register depends on the point-of-sale system and whether it can send that text out; some can, some cannot, and some need an extra interface box. We check your system's make and model before quoting, and if it cannot connect, we say so and set the counter camera so the drawer and the receipt printer are clearly in frame instead.

This section covers the part of camera work the rest of this page only touches on: what happens to your footage after it's recorded, how it fits with the cameras the city already runs, and how to set up a system so a clip can be found, trusted and handed over without a scramble.

Setting up a system so a clip holds up. The most common problem we see with footage isn't image quality. It's that nobody in the house or shop can find the clip, or the recorder's clock is wrong. Cameras at the Front Door: Working Around Unionport's Buzzers, Intercoms and Self-Closing Doors

A large share of the camera calls we get from Unionport landlords and co-op boards are really about the front door. Someone wants to see who's ringing, who's propping the door, who's following residents in, or why packages keep disappearing from the vestibule. A camera can answer those questions, but it sits on the same doorframe as equipment the law treats very differently. Getting the entry right means knowing which parts of that door are an owner's legal obligation and which parts are a choice, and making sure the camera never gets in the way of the obligation.

The buzzer is required; the camera usually isn't. Under the Housing Maintenance Code, Administrative Code §27-2045, landlords of multiple dwellings have to keep a working intercom or buzzer. There's no general New York City law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. The Multiple Dwelling Law, §50-a, goes further for one group of buildings. Class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have an intercom at the self-locking main door. It has to allow two-way voice communication and let the occupant release the door lock from inside the apartment. Sources agree that the law requires a working intercom but not a video one. A video intercom or a camera over the door is an upgrade an owner may want, not something these rules demand.

This matters in Unionport because of what the neighborhood is built from. Community Board 9 describes Unionport as largely two-family homes. One profile puts 35.6% of local real estate in two-, three- and four-unit buildings, a higher share than in 96.3% of US neighborhoods, and says most homes are rented. Many of those buildings fall under §50-a's eight-unit threshold, its 1968 cutoff, or both. That's our reading of the statute's own terms, not a Unionport-specific ruling. For many owners here, then, the §50-a intercom requirement may not apply. The Housing Maintenance Code still expects working entry hardware, and HPD's "ABCs of Housing" tells owners, managers and supers to immediately repair broken locks or intercoms to keep out drug dealers and vandals. When we look at an entry, we check the buzzer and lock first and the camera second, because a beautiful camera over a door that doesn't buzz is the wrong order of priorities.

The door, the lock and the intercom work as one system. One compliance source makes a point we see in the field constantly: an intercom beside a door that no longer latches leaves the building out of compliance. A two-way voice panel and a release button mean nothing if the door swings open on its own. Camera installation can quietly cause this kind of failure. A bracket mounted where the closer arm travels, a cable clipped across the strike side of the frame, or a junction box that keeps the door from fully seating can all stop a door from latching. Before we mount anything near an entry, we open and close the door several times, watch where the closer arm and hinges move, and pick a mounting point that's clear of all of it. After mounting, we check the door again to confirm it closes and latches on its own.

Self-closing doors are inspected. HPD enforces self-closing doors through inspection. Local Law 71 of 2022 followed the Twin Parks fire in the Bronx. Anyone working on or near entry hardware should keep a door's self-closing function working, and that includes a camera installer. In practice, we never take power for a camera from the door closer's mounting area, we never wedge a door open and leave it that way while we run cable through a vestibule, and we don't route cable along the hinge side, where it can rub and pull the door out of alignment over time. If a self-closing apartment or stair door is already failing when we arrive, we tell the owner. We don't install a camera that will just record the problem.

Exit always has to stay open. Entry systems must not block exit from inside without keys or codes. That's a firm line for us. Some owners, after a theft or a break-in, ask whether a camera system can tie into a lock so the door only opens after someone reviews the video. Inbound control like that is a question for access control and the building's lawyer. Outbound, nothing we install will stand between a resident and the street. A camera watches the door. It doesn't decide who leaves.

Violations, and what sources disagree on. Owners often ask what happens if an intercom fails. The sources conflict. Two contractor guides call a broken intercom a Class B hazardous violation, and one vendor says Class B intercom violations carry fines of $250 to $500 per day until fixed. Another guide lists "non-functioning intercom" under Class A, the non-hazardous class. We don't settle that argument on this page. If you've received a notice, the class shown on HPDONLINE for your building is the one that counts. Tenants report building-wide problems such as a broken lobby lock, an entrance door that won't close or a dead intercom through 311. We couldn't find Unionport-specific counts of intercom complaints or HPD intercom violations, so we won't claim the neighborhood has more or fewer than anywhere else.

What a door camera should actually see. Once the buzzer, lock and closer are working, a camera at the entry is worth placing carefully. A lens mounted too high over a stoop shows the top of a hood and nothing else. A lens aimed straight down a long vestibule gets washed out by daylight from the street door. For the small brick two-family and semi-detached homes common here, we usually aim for a view that catches a face at the height of the buzzer panel and the area where packages get left, while keeping the camera itself out of reach from the stoop. In the courtyard buildings, like the 124-unit property at 2035 Newbold Avenue, the entry from the courtyard and the entry from the street are separate problems. Each needs its own view. A single wide lens trying to cover both usually covers neither well.

Both create awkward questions about who sees what. Rent-stabilized buildings and tenant notice. For owners of rent-stabilized buildings, one vendor advises documenting the security reason for common-area cameras in writing before installation and notifying tenants in advance. Owners, Tenants and the Law

The Rules Behind a Unionport Camera Job, Laid Out Plainly

Unionport has no historic district, so the Landmarks layer that governs camera work in parts of the Bronx does not apply here. What does apply comes from five places: New York's installer licensing law, the city's building and housing rules, the relationship between owners and tenants, co-op and condo documents, and the state's privacy laws.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a license to be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems. The Department of State's application explains that the license covers systems that detect intrusion or movement and that installing purely passive, nondetecting security devices does not require one; modern cameras with motion and person detection sit close to that line, and the sources do not settle it cleanly. NYC's business portal notes that master electricians are exempt from the education and exam requirements but must still register. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to any owner, board or managing agent who asks. New line-voltage power, such as a new outdoor outlet, is a licensed electrician's work, and the quote says so when a job needs it.

Permits. Most camera installs on Unionport houses and buildings do not need a Department of Buildings permit. Vendor guidance notes that the picture can change when the job includes electrical work, conduit in co-op or condo common areas, or a connection to a fire alarm or access control system. If any of those is part of your job, we raise it before work starts rather than after.

Buildings and intercoms. No general city law requires cameras in apartment buildings. The Housing Maintenance Code requires owners of multiple dwellings to keep a working intercom or buzzer, and Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a sets the rule for buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. Many Unionport two- and three-family houses fall outside that rule. Cameras do not replace an entry system the law requires, and adding them does not fix an intercom that has failed.

Owners and tenants. In a rented two-family or apartment building, the common areas, the shared front door, the hallways, the laundry room and the cellar belong to the owner, who decides where cameras go and who may review the footage. Cameras never look into an apartment, through a window or at a tenant's private doorway. In a two-family, each household's logins show only the views agreed in writing. NY Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor whose video recording system is installed to watch the back yard of an adjoining residence, so rear-yard cameras here are framed to stay on the owner's own yard.
