The Neighborhood

Why Edenwald Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Edenwald is a neighborhood of houses on their own lots, and that is the single biggest fact behind any camera plan here. The name comes from an estate. NYC Parks records that John H. Eden owned land here called Edenwald, German for "Eden's forest," between 1900 and 1913, and before that William Seton had built a fifty-one-acre estate called Cragdon in what became the middle of the neighborhood. Wikipedia treats Edenwald as a section of Eastchester, inside Community Board 12, with ZIP codes 10466 and 10475. The housing listings describe what grew up on the old estates: prewar brick rowhouses, modest duplexes, multifamily brick colonials, Colonial Revival cottages and some newly renovated Cape Cod-style houses. NeighborhoodScout puts most homes in the 1940 to 1969 range, with a number built before 1940. One listing on Duryea Avenue describes a 1925 two-family with about 2,600 square feet across two units. That is the typical customer here: an owner with a front door, a side path, a yard, perhaps a garage at the back, and often a tenant upstairs or downstairs.

A house like that has more ways in than an apartment door, and most of them are out of sight from the street. The front porch faces the sidewalk, but the side path between two houses, the back door, the basement entrance and the garage face the yard, where nobody passing by can see what is going on. In Edenwald some of those yards back onto woods. Seton Falls Park, which became parkland in 1930 with the final parcels added in 1985, is a wooded preserve around what remains of Rattlesnake Brook, bounded by East 233rd Street, Baychester Avenue, Crawford Avenue, Marolla Place and Pratt Avenue, with private property along part of its edge. A rear fence on the woods gets no streetlight, no passing traffic and no neighbor across the way. That is where a camera does more than anything else on the lot, and where it is hardest to get right, because darkness and moving branches defeat cheap equipment quickly.

The neighborhood's largest landmark has its own camera story, and it shapes what we can and cannot do. The Edenwald Houses, built on the former Hebrew Orphan Asylum site and finished on October 15, 1953, are bordered by Grenada Place, East 225th Street, Baychester Avenue and Laconia Avenue, and mix three-story buildings with fourteen-story towers. NYCHA and the Housing Development Corporation count 2,035 apartments in 40 buildings and nearly 5,000 residents. Since 2023 the campus has been in a $783 million renovation under NYCHA's PACT program, and the planned work includes new entryways, enhanced security and access control, and full lobby and common-area upgrades. The resident FAQ answers the question "Will there be cameras?" with a plain yes, describing site-wide cameras, and NYCHA Journal reports that C+C Management will employ security staff responsible for actively monitoring all cameras. Those cameras belong to the project, not to residents, and we do not install or touch them. They also do not look at the houses on the surrounding blocks.

There is a longer history behind that promise. The Bronx Times reported that from August 2012 NYCHA used City Council money to install 3,879 closed-circuit cameras in 500 buildings at 84 developments, Edenwald among them. After Police Officer Brian Mulkeen was killed near Edenwald in 2019, Wikipedia notes, Mayor Bill de Blasio discussed installing security cameras in the development. Across the street, the Center for New York City Affairs described Baychester Houses before its renovation as having half a dozen cameras, frequently broken and sporadically manned, replaced afterward by hundreds of cameras and round-the-clock patrols. On top of that, local officials announced $1,000,000 for NYPD ARGUS cameras at the 47th Precinct, which sits at 4111 Laconia Avenue, though the announcement gave no locations, timeline or vendor. None of these systems sends a picture to a homeowner's phone, and none was placed to see the back of a house on Edson or Wilder Avenue. A private camera answers a narrower question, and the owner controls the answer.

The public record shows why that narrower answer matters. Wikipedia's Eastchester entry lists the 47th Precinct's 2022 figures as 300 burglaries, 461 robberies, 758 grand larcenies and 461 grand larcenies of vehicles, across a precinct that covers much more than Edenwald. ABC7 reported a 2013 shooting on Laconia Avenue, two blocks from the precinct, that was caught on video, and in July 2024 Norwood News reported that the NYPD released video of three people firing shots at the corner of East 223rd Street and Needham Avenue. We are not going to tell you whether your block is safe; that depends on the block, the hour and your own experience. What those reports do show is that when something happens on an Edenwald street, the first question investigators ask is whether anyone's camera saw it, and the answer usually comes from a house or a shop, not from a city pole.

The money side points the same way. Homes.com puts the median sale price in Edenwald over the last twelve months at $593,888, and Niche reports that about 40 percent of residents own their homes while about 60 percent rent. An owner who has put that much into a brick two-family usually wants three things from cameras: a record of who comes to the doors, a view of the parked car and the yard, and a way for the tenant to see the shared front door without seeing the owner's yard. A renter wants something simpler and removable. And new owners keep arriving: NY YIMBY reported permits for two three-story buildings with six residences, most likely condos, at 1237 East 224th Street, after earlier filings at 3545 Edson Avenue and 1978 Needham Avenue. Each new building is a chance to put camera cable in the walls before they close, which is far cheaper than going back later.

The owner's lot: front, sides, yard and the doors nobody watches

For an owner, the job is the whole lot, and we start at the back, not the front. The front porch is where packages arrive and visitors ring, and a doorbell or porch camera covers it well. But on a typical Edenwald house the side path, the back door, the basement steps, the yard gate and the garage are all out of sight from the street, and they are where an uninvited visitor feels safest. So the survey goes around the house in order: the front door and porch, each side path, the back door, any outside stair to the basement, the yard and its gate, the garage or parking pad, and the street in front. For each, we note what the camera would sit on, what light is there after dark, where the cable would travel, and whose windows would be in the frame. The result is a plan where every door into the house is seen by at least one camera, and nobody else's yard is.

The two-family: one building, two households, one set of rules

Most of the camera questions we expect in Edenwald come from owners of two-family houses, and they are really questions about fairness. The front door and front hall may be shared; the yard may be the owner's or may be shared by lease; each apartment is private. We plan the shared views so they help both households: a camera on the front steps that both can see, set on a tenant login that shows only that camera. The yard, the garage and the driveway stay on the owner's login unless the lease shares them. No camera ever looks into an apartment, through a window or down the inside stair to a tenant's door. And if the tenant is renting from you, we suggest telling them in writing which cameras exist and what they cover, because that conversation is easier before the install than after.

Brick fronts: anchors in the joint, one sealed hole

Old brick is strong but unforgiving. Drilled through the face of a brick, it can spall, leaving a chipped scar that stays long after the camera comes down. So on a prewar brick house we set anchors in the mortar joints, which can be repointed later, and keep each camera to a mount with a junction box behind it so the cable is never exposed at the camera. The cable then needs one way into the house. On most Edenwald brick houses the shortest path is straight through the wall into the cellar, below the first floor, where a masonry bit makes a single neat hole with a slight downward slope toward the outside, so rain cannot follow the cable in. We sleeve the hole, seal it with an exterior-grade sealant, and run the cable along the cellar ceiling to the recorder. Where several cameras need cable, we gather them to one or two entry points rather than making a hole under every camera.

Wood and vinyl: mounting blocks and attic runs

The cottages and some duplexes are wood-framed, sided in clapboard or with vinyl laid over the original wood. Siding is the wrong thing to hang a camera from: vinyl flexes and cracks, and clapboard is uneven, so a camera screwed straight on sits crooked and lets water behind it. We mount to a proper mounting block that fits the siding profile and screws through to the framing behind it, and we seal the top edge so water runs around it. Wood houses do have one advantage over brick: an attic or crawlspace. A camera under the eaves can often be cabled up into the attic and across to an inside wall, so there is no outside cable at all and only a small sealed hole under each camera. We check for old knob-and-tube wiring and insulation in the attic before anyone crawls through it, and we never bury camera cable in loose insulation near a hot light fixture.

Porches, stoops and the front door

The front of an Edenwald house is often a porch, open or enclosed, or a brick stoop with a door set back under it. A porch ceiling is a good spot for a camera: sheltered from rain, close to the door, and high enough to be out of easy reach. A camera there looks down the steps and catches a face as someone comes up toward the door. A doorbell camera adds the close-up at the door itself. On older doors, the brick or wood beside the frame is often narrow and uneven, and a flat doorbell would point at the wall opposite. Angled and wedge mounts turn the doorbell back toward the steps. Where an old bell transformer is in the cellar, we test it before wiring a video doorbell to it, because many old transformers are too weak to run one.

Shared stairs and the second household

In brick multifamily colonials and three-family houses, a shared stair connects the street door to the apartments, and the owner often wants it covered. A stair camera can be useful for seeing who came in the street door and when. It must never become a view into anyone's home. So we place the camera to look at the street door from inside, or at the stair landing, and stop the frame before any apartment door. Tenants get to see the street door camera on their own logins if the owner wants that, and nobody but the owner gets the yard. Where cable has to pass between floors in a multifamily house, each opening is sealed with rated fire-stopping material, because a hole between floors is a path for smoke as well as wire.

The plaza and the roll-up gate

Many Edenwald storefronts close behind a metal roll-up gate, and that changes the camera plan more than shop owners expect. A camera mounted inside the window sees the street during the day and nothing but the back of the gate at night. A camera mounted outside above the gate sees the sidewalk and the parking, but it is the landlord's wall and the camera is exposed. So a store plan here usually has three parts. Inside, cameras cover the counter, the register, the aisles and the stock room door, and they work whatever the gate is doing. At the door, a camera just inside the entrance, at about head height and aimed so customers walk toward it, gives the faces that make footage useful. Outside, if the landlord agrees, one camera high above the gate watches the gate itself and the strip of sidewalk in front of it, which is where any tampering at night would happen. In the plaza, where parking is shared, that outside camera should be aimed at your own frontage, not across the whole lot.

Recorders, staff and the after-hours alert

Most small businesses here have an owner who is not always on site, a manager who opens and closes, and staff who change. We set the owner as the only account that can change settings or delete footage, give managers a login with the cameras they need, and give staff, if anyone, view-only access to the front door. The recorder goes in the back office, locked, on a battery backup so a short power cut does not stop recording, and out of sight from the front door so it cannot be carried off with the till. After closing, the owner's phone gets person alerts from the cameras that should see nobody at night, usually the back door and the stock room. During opening hours, those alerts are switched off on a schedule, so the phone is not buzzing all day. Where the shop's internet is shared with a card terminal, we put the cameras on their own network segment and change every factory password.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Edenwald

  • The landlord decides outside your unit. Before any camera goes on an exterior wall, a shared sidewalk canopy or the plaza parking, we ask for the landlord's answer in writing.
  • Private places stay private. Recording where people reasonably expect privacy, such as restrooms and changing areas, is a crime under New York Penal Law 250.45, and we will not aim a camera at one.
  • Light comes before the camera. A camera on an unlit back door or loading spot gives no useful picture; we test each position after dark and say where light is needed.
  • Electrical work is an electrician's. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable from the recorder or a switch. If a shop needs a new outlet, our quote says so and names it as an electrician's job.
  • Fire-rated walls stay rated. Where cable passes through a rated wall or ceiling, the opening is sealed with rated fire-stopping, and every hole we make is sealed before we leave.
  • Churches and programs need clear limits. Doors, parking and halls can be covered; counseling rooms, nurseries used for changing and restrooms stay off camera.
  • Who installs matters to an insurer. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which is what a landlord or insurer usually asks first.

Field Notes: Walking an Edenwald Lot From the Back Fence Forward

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house, so this is the routine rather than an anecdote. It is what we do on the first visit to a house in Edenwald, before any camera comes out of a box.

We start at the back fence, not the front door. Standing where an uninvited visitor would come over, we look back at the house and ask what that person would see first: a dark yard, a lit back door, a basement stair, a garage. Then we walk the side paths and note how narrow they are and whose windows face them. We look at the walls: brick or siding, sound or crumbling, and where a cable could slip inside to the cellar or attic. In the cellar we find the bell transformer, the router, the electrical panel and a dry, ventilated spot where a recorder could live off the floor. Only then do we go to the front and the porch. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Edenwald: Questions Answered

Houses on the Edenwald grid

Our Colonial Revival cottage is sided in wood clapboard, not brick. What does a camera mount need on old wood?

It needs something flat and solid to sit on, which clapboard is not. Each board overlaps the one below, so a camera screwed straight onto the siding tilts and leaves a gap where rain gets behind it. We fit a mounting block shaped to the clapboard profile, screw it through to the framing, seal its top edge, and mount the camera on the block. Where possible we place cameras on wood trim instead, such as the corner boards or the soffit under the eaves, which are flatter and easier to seal. Cable usually goes up into the attic rather than along the outside wall.

Could the people who sold us our house still be watching through the cameras they left behind?

They could, if their cloud account or app login was never removed. Many cameras and recorders stay linked to the account of whoever set them up, and a sale does not change that. We check the recorder and each camera for linked accounts and remote-access settings, reset them to factory defaults where needed, set them up again under your own account with new passwords and two-step sign-in, and update the firmware. If a device cannot be reset because it is locked to someone else's account, we tell you, and it usually has to be replaced.

Strangers have talked their way into Bronx homes by pretending to be plumbers. How can a door camera help us check who is really outside?

The Bronx Times reported a 2021 case near East 227th Street and Barnes Avenue in Edenwald where two men posed as plumbers to get into an apartment. A video doorbell lets you see and talk to the person from anywhere in the house, or from your phone, without opening the door. Ask for the company name and a work order, then check it yourself by calling the utility or your landlord on a number you already have. The doorbell records the person's face and the conversation's video either way. We set the doorbell at a height and angle that shows a face clearly, not the top of a cap.

Our back fence is the only thing between our yard and the Seton Falls Park woods. What should a rear camera actually watch?

It should watch the fence line and the path from the fence to the back door, not the woods. A camera aimed into the trees records leaves moving all night and nothing useful, because nothing in the dark beyond the fence is lit. Aimed along the fence and back toward the house, it catches anyone who comes over or through, and as they walk toward the lit back door, it gets a usable face. We usually pair that camera with a motion light at the back of the house and keep a second camera on the back door itself.

My building at the Edenwald Houses is getting new self-closing doors and a new intercom in the renovation. Should I wait before putting anything on my apartment door?

Yes, for anything that attaches to a door. The resident FAQ says doors are being replaced with new ones that close automatically, and a new intercom is coming. A camera stuck to an old door may end up in a dumpster, and any device on a door needs the manager's agreement anyway. In the meantime, a small indoor camera on a shelf facing the inside of your front door gives you a view of who comes in without touching the door at all. Once the new doors are in, ask C+C Management in writing what, if anything, residents may attach.

The renovation promises free development-wide Wi-Fi at the Edenwald Houses. Can an indoor camera run on that network?

The Mayor's Office lists free development-wide Wi-Fi as part of the modernization, but we have not seen its terms, and we will not guess. Shared networks like this sometimes need a web login page that many cameras cannot complete, or they keep devices on the network from seeing each other. A camera may connect and then drop every few days. If you rely on the camera, a home internet plan of your own is the safer choice. If you want to try the shared network first, we can set the camera up and test it, and tell you plainly if it is not holding a connection.

The Edenwald Houses was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2024. Does that change what a tenant may attach outside?

For a tenant, the answer is the same either way: nothing goes on the outside of the building without the management's approval. What the listing does explain is how careful the renovation has been with the brick. The Center for New York City Affairs reported that the façades must be preserved at significant cost rather than reclad, and even flooring choices went through state preservation review. Anything drilled into that brick would be a matter for the PACT team, not for a resident or for us. Cameras inside your apartment do not touch the façade at all.

Corners, cars and clips the police can use

The NYPD released private video of gunfire at East 223rd Street and Needham Avenue in 2024. What makes a corner clip like that usable to investigators?

Usually three things: the clip shows people at a size where clothing and build are clear, it has an accurate time stamp, and it was saved before it recorded over. A camera high on a house corner looking down the block shows movement and direction well; a second camera lower down near the steps shows faces. We set the recorder's clock to sync automatically, keep enough storage for several days, and show every owner how to export a clip to a phone or a USB stick. If police ask for your video, share a copy and keep the original.

Stores, takeouts and churches

Our store in the plaza at East 233rd Street and Baychester Avenue closes behind a metal roll-up gate. Where does a camera go so it still protects the shop at night?

Inside cameras do most of the work, because they keep watching the counter, register and stock room whatever the gate is doing. One camera outside, high above the gate and aimed down at it, watches the gate and the sidewalk in front, which is where tampering would happen. That outside camera needs the landlord's written agreement, a sealed cable path into the store, and a mount out of easy reach. A camera behind the window glass is fine for daytime views of the street, but at night it sees only the back of the gate.

Our pharmacy on Laconia Avenue has a pickup counter and a locked stock area. Which views should we pay for first?

Start with the two that matter most when something goes wrong: a camera over the pickup counter that sees hands, bags and the customer's face, and a camera on the door to the stock area that shows who goes in and when. Next comes the entrance camera at head height, then overview cameras for the aisles. Patient privacy matters at a pharmacy, so we aim the counter camera to show the transaction, not screens or paperwork, and keep consultation spaces off camera. Each view is listed on the quote so you can phase the work.

Our takeout on Boston Road hands orders to delivery couriers at a side door all evening. Can a camera keep a record of each handoff?

Yes. A camera inside the side door, at head height and aimed at the handoff spot, shows the courier's face, the bag and the time. A label on the order ticket and the camera's time stamp together settle most disputes about missing or wrong orders. We set the recorder to keep enough days for a complaint to come in, and give the manager a view-only login so they can check a handoff without calling the owner. A plain notice at the door that video is recording is good practice.

Our church building on Baychester Avenue sits empty most weekdays. Can the cameras alert someone only when nobody should be there?

Yes, with a schedule. Most recorders and camera apps can turn person alerts on and off by day and time, so the pastor or building manager is alerted on a Tuesday night but not during Sunday service or Wednesday Bible study. The cameras keep recording all the time either way. We set the schedule with whoever keeps the calendar, give two or three trusted people alert access, and show them how to change the schedule when special events come up.

New small buildings

We bought one of six condos in a new three-story building on East 224th Street. Should the building have one shared camera system or should each owner have their own?

Usually one shared system for the common parts, decided by the board, plus whatever each owner wants inside their own unit. A shared system means one recorder in a locked utility room, cameras on the entrances, mailboxes and parking, and clear rules on who can view and export footage. Six owners each putting their own doorbell and floodlight on the building leads to a jumble of devices on the façade and arguments about who sees what. If the building is still under construction, cable for the shared cameras should go in the walls now.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Edenwald

These are claims that turn up repeatedly in quick online answers and installer pages about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about Edenwald and what we see on house jobs. Cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they show rough scale, but none of them has walked an Edenwald lot.

"Edenwald is basically the Edenwald Houses."

Quick answers often treat the neighborhood and the housing project as one thing, partly because the project is the largest landmark and gets most of the news. Even one installer's Edenwald page describes Grand Concourse co-ops and Art Deco buildings, which describe neither.

The record is different. Around the 40-building campus are blocks of prewar brick rowhouses, duplexes, brick multifamily colonials and cottages, and about 40 percent of residents own their homes.

For cameras, that matters because the two kinds of customer need opposite things: a whole-lot wired system for an owner, and a small indoor camera for a campus tenant whose building already has site-wide cameras coming.

"The NYCHA and NYPD cameras already cover the neighborhood."

There are public cameras here: NYCHA's 2012 program reached Edenwald, the PACT renovation is adding site-wide cameras to the Houses, and $1,000,000 was announced for ARGUS cameras at the 47th Precinct.

But the ARGUS announcement gave no locations or timeline, and the campus cameras watch the campus. None of these systems was placed to see the back yard of a house on Edson or Wilder Avenue, and none sends a picture to a homeowner.

A private camera answers a different question: who came to your door, your yard or your car, and when, on a recorder you control. The public and private systems do not overlap much.

"A wireless kit from the store is all a Bronx house needs."

For a renter's indoor camera, or a single front-door view on a cottage with the router nearby, that can be true. A good battery doorbell does a decent job at a front door.

On a brick house with the router on the second floor and the camera at a back fence fifty feet away, Wi-Fi often fails. Brick soaks up signal, and a battery camera that has to wake up and connect misses the first seconds of every event.

That is why most whole-lot jobs we plan in Edenwald are wired: power and picture over one cable to a recorder inside, with no batteries to charge and nothing to drop off the network.

"Camera installation in the Bronx has a set price per camera."

Online calculators and installer pages publish per-camera ranges and package prices for the Bronx, and AI answers repeat them as though every camera were the same job.

On an Edenwald house, one camera may go on a porch ceiling with a two-foot cable into the attic, and the next may need a run along a cellar ceiling, a hole through old brick and a mount at a back fence. The labor behind them is completely different.

So we do not print a camera price for the neighborhood. Every Edenwald address is priced on our Bronx rate, quoted in writing after photos or a visit, and booked within 2 hours.

"You need a city permit for any camera on a house."

Installer pages disagree here. One says low-voltage camera wiring in a single-family house in NYC generally does not need a Department of Buildings permit; another says permits come in only when the job involves electrical work.

Both point the same way for most house jobs: cameras on low-voltage cable from a recorder are not electrical work in the permit sense, while a new outlet or circuit is an electrician's job and follows electrical rules.

Licensing is separate. New York General Business Law §69-m licenses security installers, while the Department of State's application says passive, non-detecting devices do not require it. We are licensed, under NYS license #12000287431, so the question never comes up.

"New laws require landlords to allow tenant cameras and to post camera signs."

One installer blog says recent Real Property Law changes require landlords to allow tenant cameras. Another says an NYC "Local Law 50" requires signs for cameras in common areas. We could not confirm either from a primary source.

What Brick Underground does state is that a renter needs the landlord's permission for equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside the apartment. That is the rule we work to.

A posted notice that cameras are recording is good practice for a store or a shared entrance either way. If a firm legal answer matters to you, ask a lawyer before relying on any blog, including this page.

"Any camera with night vision will identify someone in a dark yard."

Product listings show night-vision footage from a lit driveway. A yard backing onto woods, with no street light and only a porch bulb, is a different test.

Infrared-only cameras turn a dark yard grey, lose color, and can be washed out by a fence or wall close to the lens. Faces at twenty feet in the dark are often unrecognizable.

The fix is placement and light: a camera that catches people as they walk toward a lit back door, a motion light on the yard, and a camera with its own light where the yard is darkest. We test every view after dark before it is fixed.

FAQ

Edenwald Security Camera Installation FAQ

Branches on the Seton Falls Park side keep setting off our backyard camera after dark. What stops the false alerts?
+

Three changes usually fix it. First, aim the camera along the fence and toward the house rather than out into the trees, so most of the frame is still. Second, draw a motion zone that leaves out the tree line and covers only the yard. Third, use person detection where the camera offers it, so alerts come for people and not for moving leaves. Wind and rain still trigger the odd alert, but the phone stops buzzing all night.

Can a camera be strapped to the big tree in our yard so it sees all the way to the back fence?
+

We advise against it. A tree sways in wind, so the picture moves and sets off motion alerts, and it grows, so straps and screws loosen or cut into the bark over time. The cable also has to cross the yard to reach it. A camera on the back corner of the house, aimed along the fence, or a varifocal camera that zooms in on the far end of the yard, usually covers the same ground from a fixed spot.

I rent the first floor of a two-family and set up my own camera. Can the owner upstairs get into my recordings?
+

Not if the camera and its account are yours and nobody else has the password. A camera on your own Wi-Fi, under your own account with two-step sign-in, is only visible to the people you share it with. If the camera runs on the owner's Wi-Fi, they cannot see the video, but they control the network. If you bought the camera and the owner set it up for you, change the password and remove any users you did not add.

Online calculators give a per-camera price for Bronx installs. Should we expect that number on an Edenwald house?
+

Treat those numbers as a rough guide, not a quote. A per-camera figure assumes each camera is the same job, but on an Edenwald house a camera under the eaves with a short cable into the attic and one at a back fence with a run through old brick take very different amounts of work. We quote each camera, mount and cable path on its own line after photos or a visit, on our Bronx rate, so you see exactly what drives the total.

The brick beside our 1920s front door is too narrow for a flat doorbell. Can a video doorbell still go there?
+

Usually yes. Slim doorbells fit narrower spaces, and angled or wedge mounts turn the doorbell toward the steps instead of the wall opposite. The mount is anchored into the mortar joint rather than the face of the brick. If there is truly no room, the doorbell can sit on the door trim, or a porch-ceiling camera can do the watching while a simpler button stays at the door. We test the old transformer before wiring anything to it.

Our gutters overflow down the front of the house in heavy rain. Does that change where a camera can go?
+

Yes. A camera under a gutter that spills will have water running over the lens every storm, which blurs the picture just when you might need it, and water down a wall finds its way into any hole that is not perfectly sealed. We look for the spill marks on the wall and keep cameras away from them, or under a soffit that stays dry. It is worth having the gutters cleared or repaired as well, for the house's sake.

Our mailbox hangs beside the front door and letters go missing. Can the doorbell camera watch the mailbox too?
+

Often it can, if the mailbox is close to the door and the doorbell has a wide or tall view. Where it sits out of frame, a porch-ceiling camera aimed down at the door and the mailbox together covers both. We set a motion zone on the mailbox so you get an alert when someone opens it. A locking mailbox does more to stop theft than any camera; the camera shows you who tried.

The Edenwald Houses' security staff will be monitoring the site cameras. Does that mean someone can see my own indoor camera too?
+

No. The site cameras the renovation is installing are the project's own system, monitored by the property manager's security staff, and they cover the campus, not the inside of apartments. An indoor camera you buy is on your own account and your own internet connection, and only the people you share it with can see it. Keep a unique password and two-step sign-in on it, and nobody else has a way in.

The neighbor's wall is only a few feet from our side camera. Why does the night picture turn almost white?
+

The camera's infrared light is bouncing off the nearby wall straight back into the lens and overwhelming the picture. It is common on the narrow side paths between Edenwald houses. Moving the camera so it looks along the path rather than across it, turning down or off the built-in infrared, or using a camera with a separate light source usually fixes it. We check side-path cameras after dark for exactly this.

Can a yard camera let us speak to someone standing in the yard, and is that a good idea?
+

Many cameras have a speaker and a talk button in the app, so you can speak through them from your phone. It can be useful for a delivery driver at the side door or a neighbor's child fetching a ball. For a stranger at night, calling 911 is the safer first move, and talking can tell someone the house is empty. If you want two-way audio, we test it at setup and explain what the speaker can and cannot do.

Our detached garage holds the car and the tools. Is a camera inside it better than one outside?
+

Usually both have a job. The outside camera on the house shows anyone approaching the garage door and their face. An inside camera shows what happens if someone gets in, and it is protected from weather and tampering. A detached garage needs power and a connection, either a cable buried or run from the house, or a wireless bridge if the distance and the walls allow. We look at the route before recommending either.

Our garbage bins keep disappearing from the curb on collection night. Can a front camera show who takes them?
+

It can, if the camera sees the spot where the bins stand. A camera high on the front of the house aimed down at the curb shows who takes the bins and when, though at night someone moving fast may only show as a shape. Marking the bins with your house number helps as much as the camera. We set a motion zone on that patch of curb so you are not alerted for every passing car.
