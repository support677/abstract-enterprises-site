The Neighborhood

Why Canarsie Properties Need Intercom Installation

Ask most Canarsie owners about their front door and they will describe more than one. The city's commercial district study for the neighborhood describes a housing stock made up mainly of one- and two-family detached and semi-detached homes and row houses, and two-family rentals with separate entrances turn up constantly in local listings. In those houses the upstairs household may climb the front steps to the main door while the downstairs household comes in at grade, beside or below the steps. Some houses share one front door and split inside at a small hall. Others have a basement apartment with its own door down a side path. An ordinary doorbell rings one chime in one room. It cannot tell a visitor which button belongs to which family, it cannot let the person upstairs talk to someone at the downstairs door, and it cannot open anything. That is where an intercom earns its place in a Canarsie house: one call per household, routed to the right people, with a release that opens the right door and nothing else.

The second thing that sets Canarsie apart is water. The Department of City Planning's 2017 Resilient Neighborhoods report for Canarsie counted roughly 12,000 residential buildings, about two-thirds of them in the floodplain, and noted that many houses keep critical building systems in basements that sit below the design flood elevation. During Hurricane Sandy, water came in from Fresh Creek and Paerdegat Basin and rose five to seven feet above grade in places, and HUD has said Sandy damaged 83 percent of homes in Canarsie and neighboring Flatlands. Smaller events matter too. In March 2020 a water-main break flooded basements on East 78th and East 79th Streets, and one resident told CBS New York his electrical equipment had been on the floor and none of it could be saved. Intercoms have exactly that kind of equipment: a transformer or power supply, a distribution box and a handful of splices, usually installed wherever the original electrician found it easiest, which in a Canarsie house is often a basement joist a foot or two above the floor.

The third thing is how the houses have changed since they were built. The city's flood report describes basements and cellars converted into apartments and garages turned into dens or living space, and City Limits reported after Sandy that many immigrant families lived in the basements of houses owned by other families. Every one of those changes adds or moves a door. A basement unit that opens onto a side path needs its own call station and a clear way for visitors to find it. A garage that became a den may still have a people door at driveway level that nobody thought of as an entrance until a delivery driver started using it. When we plan an intercom here, the first job is an honest map of who lives where and which door each person really uses, because the wiring follows that map and nothing else.

Same-Day Repair in Canarsie

Need Repair in Canarsie? A Release That Clicks but Won't Open, a Dead Line After Rain, or the Basement Button Ringing Upstairs

Urgent intercom calls from Canarsie houses tend to fall into a few patterns. The most common is a release that buzzes but does not open: someone presses the door button upstairs, the strike hums, and the latch stays put. That usually means the strike's keeper has drifted out of line as the door settled, a storm door or weatherstrip is pressing on the latch so hard the strike cannot let go, or the power supply is too weak to pull the strike when the call comes from the farthest unit. The second pattern is a system that dies after heavy rain or a basement leak. Here the cause is almost always low in the house: a corroded splice, a junction box that filled with water, or a transformer that shorted and tripped its circuit. The third is a crossed button, where the basement unit's button rings the main floor or the upstairs bell rings the downstairs tenant, which typically follows a renovation when someone reused a pair of wires. The fourth is a hum or crackle on the handsets, often a damaged cable where the run passes along a foundation wall or through a wet area.

If the main door will not lock, deal with that first, because an unlatched door is the real risk. Most Canarsie front doors can be secured with the key lock or deadbolt while the intercom is down. If the system died after water, do not keep pressing buttons or resetting a breaker that keeps tripping; leave it off and tell us. Then call (347) 934-8335. Single faults on systems we can work on are often traced and fixed in the same visit, and we tell you on the phone what the photos let us promise and what they do not. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our service rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and we give you our honest estimate of how long the trace should take before we start. If a repair would cost a large share of a new system, or parts for an older brand cannot be found, we put both numbers in front of you so the owner can choose with the full picture rather than paying twice for the same fault.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Canarsie's Architecture

Seven kinds of building account for nearly every intercom call from Canarsie. They differ in how many doors a visitor might use, how many households share each door, how wire can travel from a dry spot to every station, and who besides the owner gets a say. The City Planning report describes lots of 20 to 50 feet wide for detached and semi-detached houses and 20 feet or less for attached rows, which already tells you how much room there is beside a door and along a side path.

The postwar attached row

Narrow houses in long rows, often with front steps up to the main door and a lower level at or below grade, where two households can share one facade a few feet wide.

The semi-detached two-family

Two houses joined at one wall, each holding two households, with side paths, shared walks and a neighbor whose wall is part of the plan.

The detached house

A freestanding one- or two-family house on a wider lot, where doors can sit on three sides and the main floor is raised above a basement.

The house with a basement apartment

A cellar or basement converted to a unit, with its own door down a side path or under the steps, often below the flood elevation the city mapped.

Older frame houses and bungalows

Wood houses with siding, and bungalow-style houses on blocks such as eastern Conklin Avenue, from before the postwar boom.

NYCHA mid-rise campuses

Breukelen's three- and seven-story buildings and Bay View's eight-story buildings, with lobby doors controlled by NYCHA or its PACT partner.

Low buildings on the avenues

One- to three-story mixed-use buildings on Flatlands Avenue, Rockaway Parkway and Avenue L, with stores below and offices or apartments above.

The postwar attached row: two households on a narrow front

A postwar attached row house in Canarsie often gives a visitor a choice of doors in a very small space. There may be a main door at the top of a short flight of steps, a lower door at or just below the sidewalk, and on some houses a garage door between them. The City Planning report describes front yards that slope down one to four feet toward a garage on many houses, and backyards that sit several feet below the street. When two households share a front like that, the panel has to make the choice obvious. Our usual answer is a station at each door that a household actually uses, each one ringing only that household, rather than a single two-button plate that sends visitors up the steps to ring someone who lives downstairs. Where both households really do come in through the same upper door, one two-button station by that door works well, and the downstairs household's release opens only the shared door, not anything else.

The narrow front also limits where wire can go. On an attached row there is usually no side path, so cable to the lower station travels through the basement and comes out through the front foundation or the frame of the lower door. We avoid running it along the face of the steps or across a sloping walk, where water collects and feet scuff it. Inside, the station for the upstairs household goes where the person who answers the door spends the day, which in many Canarsie rows is the kitchen at the back of the main floor rather than the front hall. That can mean a longer run up through a closet or along the basement ceiling, and we show the owner the route before drilling. Because the power supply for the whole system often ends up in the basement of a row house, it goes as high on the wall as it can, in an enclosure, with every splice above it rather than below.

The semi-detached two-family: one shared wall and two sets of neighbors

Semi-detached houses are a large part of Canarsie's stock, and they bring one complication the other types do not: half the building belongs to someone else. The shared wall is not a place to drill for a station or to fasten a junction box, and a shared front walk or side path may be jointly used or even jointly owned. On these houses we keep every station, strike and cable on the owner's side of the line, and if the only sensible route touches the shared wall, the owner talks to the neighbor before we do anything. Most of the time, the side yard on the open side of the house gives a clean route from the basement to a side or rear door, and the front station mounts on the owner's own door frame or the masonry beside it.

A semi-detached two-family usually holds an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, or two related households. The owner often wants control of the system and the tenant wants a station that just works. We program each household separately, with its own button, its own indoor station and, where the system calls phones, its own account. If both households share one front door and then split inside at a small hall, the street release opens the shared door for either household, and each unit's own apartment door keeps its own key lock. If each household has its own outside door, each release is wired to its own door only. Getting that wrong is how a tenant ends up able to buzz someone into the owner's floor, which nobody wants, so we test every release from every station before we leave.

The detached house: doors on three sides and a raised main floor

A detached Canarsie house on a wider lot often has doors on the front, the side and the back, and a main floor raised above a basement. For a one-family house the intercom question is simple: where does a visitor stand, and where is the person who will answer? Many families want a video station at the front door and a monitor in the kitchen and one upstairs, so nobody has to walk down a flight to see who is there. For a two-family detached house the front door may be shared while the side door serves the lower unit, or the reverse. We decide which doors get a station by asking how visitors and deliveries arrive today, not how the house was laid out decades ago.

The house with a basement apartment: the door below the water line

The basement apartment is the hardest Canarsie intercom to get right, for two reasons. First, visitors often cannot find its door. It may be down a side path, under the front steps or at the back of the house, and a courier who sees one obvious front door rings there and wakes the household upstairs. Second, the basement is exactly where water arrives first. The City Planning report describes many basements and cellars below the design flood elevation, and City Limits found, after Sandy, basement tenants who lost everything they owned. A station mounted low beside a basement door, with its wire running along the floor of a stairwell, can be under water before anyone upstairs knows there is a problem.

Our approach starts with the visitor. If the basement unit's door can be seen from the sidewalk, it gets its own station there, mounted at a sensible height on the frame or the wall above any splash line. If it cannot be seen, the main station by the front door gets a clearly marked button for the lower unit, with a small sign pointing visitors to the side path once the tenant answers. Inside the unit, the station goes high on a dry interior wall, and the wire comes down to it from above rather than up from the floor. The basement unit gets its own release, wired only to its own door, and its own code or app account if the system uses them. Whether a basement apartment is legal is a question for the owner and the city, not for us; what we can do is make sure every household that lives in the house can answer its own door safely.

Older frame houses and bungalows: siding, shallow walls and settling doors

Canarsie's older blocks hold a different kind of house. Forgotten New York's 2026 walk through the neighborhood noted attached wooden houses with siding near East 95th Street, bungalow-style houses on eastern Conklin Avenue and older houses south of Bedell Lane, and the City Planning report dates the area between Remsen Avenue and Rockaway Parkway to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Wood-frame houses are easier to route wire through than masonry, but they bring their own care. Siding has to be cut cleanly and sealed so water does not get behind it, and a station should mount on a flat block or a trim board rather than straight onto vinyl or aluminum, which flexes and lets water in around the screws.

NYCHA mid-rise campuses: Breukelen and Bay View

Residents of these buildings cannot hire a contractor to change the building's intercom, because the lobby doors, panels and riser wiring belong to the housing authority or, at Bay View, the PACT partners now responsible for the property. Problems with a lobby intercom or entrance door go to NYCHA or the building's management. Where we can be useful is as a low-voltage installer when a management company or general contractor brings us onto a project, and inside an apartment where the lease and management allow a resident to add something of their own. Mid-century buildings like these are often masonry and block, and when a wall turns out to be block or concrete, mounting a station or running cable means hammer drilling, proper anchors and surface raceway rather than fishing wire through a hollow wall.

Low buildings on the avenues: shops below, offices and apartments above

The City Planning report describes the commercial and mixed-use buildings on Flatlands Avenue, Rockaway Parkway and Avenue L as one to three stories tall, and notes that these corridors sit outside the floodplain and saw little Sandy damage. That puts the avenue buildings in a different position from the side streets. Water is less of a worry; sharing is more of one. A typical building has a store at street level and an upstairs door beside it, leading to an office, a clinic, or one or two apartments. The upstairs door needs its own station and release, and the people upstairs need a system that keeps working whatever happens in the store.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Canarsie House From the Curb to the Bottom Step of the Basement

Count the doors before the households. From the sidewalk, the technician notes every door a stranger might walk up to: the main door, a lower door at grade, a side door down a path, a garage people door. Then the owner says who uses each one. A door nobody lives behind does not need a station, but if couriers keep trying it, it may need a sign.

Look for the water line. In the basement, the technician looks at the lowest few feet of the walls, the bottom of the stairs and the utility area. Stains, newer drywall or a fresh coat of paint at the same height all around the room tell a story, and the owner usually confirms it. Nothing that powers or joins the intercom goes below that line.

Test every release from the far station. With someone watching the door, each household presses its own release. A strike that clicks but does not open points to the keeper, the closer or a storm door. A release that opens the wrong door points to the wiring.

Follow the old wire end to end. The tone goes on at each existing station, and the probe finds it in the basement. Splices that sat in water are cut out and replaced, and any run that crosses a wet area gets tested before it is trusted.

Ask who answers the door. The last question is about people. Who is home during the day, and on which floor? Does anyone struggle with stairs? Does the basement tenant work nights? Those answers decide where each station hangs and whether the system should also call a phone.

Water and the Wiring

What Water Does to an Intercom, and How a Canarsie System Is Built to Come Back

An intercom has four parts that do not like water: the power supply, the junction points where wires are joined, the stations themselves, and the strike in the door frame. In most older Canarsie houses the first two sit in the basement, because that is where the original installer found power and space. When a basement floods, even by a few inches, a transformer mounted low can short and trip its circuit, and splices wrapped in tape soak up water and begin to corrode. Sometimes the system dies at once. Sometimes it keeps working for weeks and then starts to hum, ring the wrong unit or drop calls as the corrosion spreads along the copper. That delay is why a system that "still worked after the storm" is not proof that the wiring is sound.

Power goes up and stays dry. On a new system, the power supply goes in a closed enclosure mounted high on a basement wall, on the main floor in a closet or utility space, or wherever the house gives us a dry location that the owner controls. The City Planning report recommends that Canarsie owners move critical building systems above the design flood elevation, and we treat an intercom's power the same way. Where the supply needs a dedicated or relocated electrical circuit, that is work for a licensed electrician, and we coordinate with one rather than improvising.

Joints are made high, in boxes, and recorded. Every place a wire is joined goes in a junction box above the water line, and the joint is made with connectors meant for low-voltage work, not twisted and taped. The location of each box goes on the wiring record we leave with the owner, so after a storm the owner or the next technician knows exactly where to look.

Wire comes down to stations, not up from the floor. Where a station has to be in a basement unit or at a lower door, the cable reaches it from above whenever possible, so water running along a floor or down a stairwell does not follow the wire into the station. Outdoor stations on lower doors are mounted with the seals and covers the manufacturer specifies, above the level where water pools at the foot of a sloping walk.

After water, test before you trust. If water has reached the basement since the intercom went in, the right response is a short check: power off, inspect the supply and every box, test each run, and only then switch back on. That check takes far less time than tracing a corroded splice months later, and on a well-built system it is often a matter of looking and confirming rather than replacing anything.

The Rules at the Front Door

Houses, Multiple Dwellings and the Front Door: What New York Rules Say About Canarsie Intercoms

Most Canarsie owners live in houses, and the rules for a house are not the rules for an apartment building. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, so you can ask the right questions of your attorney, your managing agent or the city before choosing a system.

One- and two-family houses. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law covers buildings occupied by three or more households living independently. A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, and the intercom requirements written for multiple dwellings do not reach it. Families install intercoms in two-family houses because two households share a lot, a facade and sometimes a door, not because the law says so. Once a tenant relies on a door system, though, keeping it working is part of keeping the rental in good order.

Buildings with eight or more apartments. The New York Attorney General's Residential Tenants' Rights Guide summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a as requiring a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door, along with self-closing and self-locking entrance doors, in buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. For older buildings, the guide says an intercom is required when a majority of the apartments ask for one. The Department of Buildings rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, sets the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. Canarsie's low-rise apartment buildings and its larger developments are where these rules come into play.

Doors that close and lock. HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors in every type of multiple dwelling must be self-closing, and that the owner, manager or superintendent should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms. HPD's self-closing door rule adds that the agency selects 300 buildings a year for self-closing door inspections. An intercom is only as good as the door it releases: if the door does not close and latch on its own, the panel is decoration.

Heights. The same DOB rule says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must sit between four and five feet from the floor. On a Canarsie house with a raised main door, or a lower door down a step or two, we measure those heights from where a visitor stands. For businesses and public-facing offices, the ADA's reach rules for operable parts also apply, which usually means keeping buttons lower than the residential maximum.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Canarsie: Questions Answered

These are the situations Canarsie owners, tenants and businesses describe when they call: houses with more doors than households, basements that have taken water, apartments in the big campuses, and offices over the avenues. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Two households, two or three doors

The upstairs family's door is at the top of the front steps and the downstairs family's door is at driveway level. Where does a visitor ring, and whose door opens?

Each family's visitors should ring at that family's own door, and each release should open only that door. In practice that means a one-button station at the top of the steps for the upstairs household and another at the lower door for the downstairs household, each wired to its own indoor station and its own strike. A single two-button plate at the top of the steps looks tidier but sends the downstairs family's visitors to the wrong door, and then someone has to walk out and redirect them. If you would rather have one place to ring, put the plate where both doors can be seen and label the buttons with the door as well as the unit, such as "Upper, steps" and "Lower, driveway." Either way, we test both releases from both stations so that neither family can open the other's door.

Visitors to our basement tenant keep ringing us upstairs because they never find the side entrance. How do we fix that?

Give the basement unit its own call point where visitors look first, and make the path obvious. If the side entrance can be seen from the sidewalk, a station on that door, mounted at eye level and above any splash line, usually solves it within a week, especially with a small sign at the front pointing to it. If it cannot be seen, add a clearly marked button for the lower unit on the station at the main door, so the tenant gets the call directly and can tell the visitor which way to walk. What does not work is asking the upstairs household to answer and redirect, because the call still wakes them and the tenant still misses deliveries. Whichever route you choose, the tenant's station should ring only the tenant, and the tenant's release should open only the tenant's door.

We live on the main floor and our tenant upstairs shares the same front door. Can the tenant let visitors through the street door without being able to open ours?

Yes, as long as the house has two lockable doors in the right places. The intercom's strike goes on the shared front door, and both households can release it. Each household's own apartment door, inside the hall, keeps an ordinary key lock that the intercom does not touch. A visitor for the tenant comes through the front door and up the stairs; they never reach your door unless you open it. If your apartment door currently opens straight off the front vestibule with no lock of its own, fix that first, because no intercom setting can make a single door serve two households privately. We check this layout on the survey and suggest door hardware changes before any intercom work if the hall arrangement needs them.

Water, basements and wiring

Our basement took water during Sandy and again in a water-main break. Can any of the old intercom wire that sat in water be reused?

Sometimes, but only after testing, and never at the joints. Intact jacketed cable that was briefly wet can test perfectly well once it has dried, and if it does, there is no reason to pull it. Splices, connectors and junction boxes that sat in water almost always need to be cut out and redone, because corrosion starts at the exposed copper and creeps along it. A transformer or power supply that was submerged should be replaced, not dried and reused. On the survey we test each run end to end, look for green or black discoloration where the jacket is cut, and check for leakage between the wires. Runs that pass can be kept and rejoined above the water line; runs that fail are replaced along a higher path. The test result goes on the written quote, so you see what is being kept and why.

Our front walk slopes down toward the lower door, and in heavy rain water pools right in front of it. Is that a bad place for a call station?

It is a place to be careful, not a place to avoid entirely. The City Planning report describes many Canarsie front yards sloping down one to four feet toward a garage or lower level, and a trench drain at the bottom does not always keep up. A station on that door should be mounted well above the highest water you have seen, on the frame or the wall beside it rather than low on the door itself, with the manufacturer's seals and a rain hood if it has one. The cable should enter from the top or the side, never from below, and the hole behind it should be sealed. If water regularly reaches the bottom of the door frame, we may suggest placing that household's station at the top of the slope instead, with a short sign pointing down to the door.

Breukelen, Bay View and older apartment buildings

I live in Breukelen Houses and the intercom in my building's lobby is broken. Can I hire you to fix it?

Not directly. The lobby panel, the entrance door and the wiring that runs to each apartment belong to NYCHA, and a resident cannot authorize a contractor to work on them. The right step is to report the broken intercom and door to NYCHA, keep the ticket number and the date, and follow up if it is not repaired. HPD's guidance that broken locks and intercoms should be repaired immediately is useful language to include when you report it. We do work on housing authority and managed-building projects when a management company or general contractor hires us as the low-voltage installer, and in that case the work is scheduled and approved by them. Inside your own apartment, what you can add depends on your lease and management's rules, so ask them before buying anything.

Bay View is partway through its PACT renovation. Should tenants expect a new entry system, and who do they ask?

The published scope for the Bay View renovation includes upgraded electrical systems, painted doors with new hardware, and security and accessibility upgrades, with construction expected to run through summer 2029. It does not say whether the intercoms will be replaced or what kind of system will be installed, so we cannot tell you. The PACT partners and Wavecrest Management, which is handling day-to-day operations, are the people to ask. Tenant association meetings are a good place to raise it, because questions about entry systems affect every building. If you want a sense of what to ask, the useful ones are whether each apartment will get its own station or a phone app, whether the lobby panel will have battery backup, how residents who do not use smartphones will answer the door, and how broken stations will be reported and repaired.

Our nine-unit building from the 1950s has a buzzer that rings each apartment but has no voice. Does that still meet the rules?

The written rules point in two directions, which is why this is worth asking a lawyer or managing agent about. The Department of Buildings rule, 1 RCNY 42-01, describes the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. The Attorney General's tenants' guide describes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a as requiring a two-way voice intercom in buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, and in older buildings when a majority of the apartments request one. A ring-only system may satisfy the minimum in a pre-1968 building that nobody has asked to upgrade, but it gives tenants no way to know who they are letting in. Our practical advice is that a voice or video system is worth planning for, because the tenants can request one, and a ring-only buzzer invites people to release the door blind.

Family, care and neighbors

A home health aide visits my mother every morning. Can the aide get in without my mother walking to the door, and without us handing out keys?

Yes, and there are two common ways. The simplest is a video station with a monitor near where your mother sits, so she sees the aide at the door and presses one button to release it, no walking required. If she cannot manage even that, a system that also calls phones lets a family member answer from work and open the door remotely. The other option is a keypad or code on the front door, with a code assigned only to the aide's agency. That code can be changed or turned off the day the aide changes, which is better than chasing a copied key. Whatever you choose, the inside door your mother uses should still lock, and the code should open only the front door, not anything else in the house.

Our semi-detached house shares a wall and a front walk with the neighbor. Can our intercom be installed without touching their side?

Almost always. We keep every station, strike, junction box and cable on your side of the property line, and we do not drill into or fasten to the shared party wall. The front station goes on your own door frame or the masonry beside your door, and cable to it comes up from your basement or along your side of the house. If the only reasonable route crosses the shared walk or sits close to the shared wall, we will show you before doing anything, and you can talk to your neighbor. A shared walk also affects video: a camera at your door will see the neighbor's comings and goings, so we aim it at your own step and, where the station allows, mask out the part of the image that shows their door.

Offices on the avenues and choosing a system

Our office on the second floor over a store on Flatlands Avenue sees patients by appointment. How do we let them in without leaving the street door open all day?

Put a video station at the street door with a clear listing for the office, and a monitor at the front desk. The receptionist sees each patient, presses release, and the door locks again a few seconds later. If the desk is sometimes empty, the station can also ring a phone, so whoever is on duty answers from an exam room. A self-closing door with a properly adjusted closer matters as much as the station, because a door that bounces open defeats the whole system. If patients who use wheelchairs or walkers come through that door, the buttons go within ADA reach and the release time is long enough for someone to get through. If other tenants share the stair, each office gets its own button and its own listing.

Should both households in a two-family share one system, or should each choose its own doorbell?

One system serving both households is almost always better than two unrelated doorbells. A single system means the stations match, one power supply feeds both, one wiring record covers the whole house, and each household still gets its own button, release and, if wanted, its own app account. Two separate consumer doorbells usually cannot open a door at all, often depend on two different Wi-Fi networks, and leave the owner with two sets of hardware to maintain. The owner should hold the system's administrator login; each household controls only its own station and its own phone settings. If the two households have very different needs, such as one wanting video and the other a simple handset, most systems allow different stations on the same backbone.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Canarsie

"A video doorbell is all a home needs"

For a one-family house with one door and one household, a good video doorbell may be enough. Generic answers assume that is every home, and in Canarsie it is not.

A two-family with separate doors, or a house with a basement unit, needs each household to receive only its own calls and to open only its own door. Most consumer doorbells ring one account and open nothing. Two of them on one house means two apps, two Wi-Fi dependencies and no release.

The useful question is not "doorbell or intercom" but "how many households, and which doors?" Once those are counted, the answer is usually a small intercom with one station per household.

"Every residential building in New York must have an intercom"

This gets repeated often and is wrong for most of Canarsie. The intercom requirements in the Multiple Dwelling Law apply to multiple dwellings, and the best-known one, as summarized by the Attorney General, applies to buildings of eight or more apartments.

A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling. Nobody is going to cite a Canarsie two-family for lacking an intercom. Families install one because the house has more than one household and more than one door.

Where the rules do bite is in apartment buildings, and in the expectation that once tenants rely on a system, it gets repaired. HPD's guidance is that broken locks and intercoms should be fixed immediately.

"Intercom installation costs fall within a national average range"

Cost guides on sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national ranges, and AI answers tend to repeat them. We do not repeat those numbers, because they describe nobody's house in particular.

In Canarsie, the cost moves with things a national average cannot see: how many doors need a station, whether old wire passed a test after flooding, whether the power supply has to move above the water line, and whether a strike has to be fitted to a door that has settled.

The honest way to compare quotes is line by line: stations, wire, door hardware, power and labor. Our quote is built that way so you can lay it next to anyone else's.

"If the intercom still works after a flood, the wiring is fine"

"An outdoor-rated station can go on any exterior wall"

"Tenants can upgrade their building's intercom with a few smart devices"

AI answers aimed at renters often suggest adding a smart device to the building's panel. In Breukelen, Bay View or any rented building, the front door, the panel and the riser belong to the owner or the housing authority.

Tenants who change them risk damaging the system, breaching the lease, and leaving neighbors without a working door. In NYCHA and PACT buildings the right step is a written report to management, with a record kept.

In a two-family, the same logic holds. The tenant can ask for changes, but the owner decides, and a single system planned for both households beats a tenant's add-on every time.

"App-based intercoms have replaced wired ones"

App systems are useful, especially for owners who rent both floors and do not live in the house. Generic answers present them as the only modern choice.

An app system depends on the house's internet and on everyone having a smartphone and keeping it charged. In a household with an older parent at home all day, or a basement tenant who prefers not to install another app, a wired indoor station is often what actually gets used.

Many systems do both: a wired station on the wall and an optional app for whoever wants it. That way the door keeps working when the internet or a phone does not.

FAQ

Canarsie Intercom Installation FAQ

Will the intercom release the door if the deadbolt is also locked?
+

No. An electric strike releases the latch, not a deadbolt thrown into the frame. If a household locks the deadbolt, visitors cannot be buzzed in until it is unlocked, so families usually keep the deadbolt for nights and times away and rely on the latch and strike during the day.

Does a storm door in front of our main door cause problems for the intercom?
+

It can. A storm door that presses on the main door can bind the latch so the strike cannot release it, and a locked storm door stops a visitor even after the release works. We check how the two doors interact on the survey and adjust the hardware or the station placement so the release actually lets someone in.

Will drilling for the outdoor station damage vinyl or aluminum siding on our house?
+

Not if it is done properly. We mount the station on a flat mounting block or a trim board rather than directly on the siding, cut only the opening the cable needs, and seal it so water cannot get behind the panels. The finished station sits flat and the siding around it stays intact.

Does the outdoor station need an electrical outlet outside the house?
+

No. Door stations run on low voltage supplied from the power unit inside the house or through their own cable. There is no outdoor outlet involved, which also means nothing at the door is plugged into anything that could be soaked in a storm.

Can the intercom wire go through a hole the cable or internet company already drilled?
+

Sometimes, if the hole is in the right place, large enough and properly sealed. Many existing holes are rough, unsealed and low on the wall, so we often reseal them and make our own clean entry higher up instead. Either way, the opening is sealed when we finish.

How do we keep the basement tenant's entry code private from the household upstairs?
+

On systems with codes, each household gets its own code, set up with only that household present, and each code opens only that household's door. The owner can see that codes exist and can delete them, but does not need to know what they are. When a tenant leaves, that code is removed the same day.

Should anything be done to the outdoor station before a hurricane or nor'easter?
+

A properly sealed station can stay on the wall. Before a big storm, check that its rain hood and cover are in place, clear leaves from any drain at the foot of the lower door, and know where the intercom's power supply is so it can be switched off if water reaches the basement. Afterward, have it checked before switching back on if water got near the equipment.

As a tenant, can I ask you for a quote on my landlord's building?
+

You can ask questions and send photos, and we are glad to explain what the building may need. The work itself has to be approved by the owner, who controls the doors and the wiring. Many tenants pass our explanation to the landlord, who then contacts us for the survey and written quote.

If we sell the house, does the intercom stay with it?
+

Wired stations and door hardware are normally treated as part of the house, but that is for the sale contract to settle. Before closing, transfer the administrator login and any app accounts to the buyer, delete your family's codes, and leave the wiring record with the house so the new owner knows where everything is.

Will installing an intercom change our homeowner's or flood insurance?
+

We cannot tell you how your insurer will treat it. Some owners ask their agent whether moving equipment above the flood line or improving door security matters to their policy. We can give you a written description of what was installed and where, which is what an agent will usually want to see.

Can we see exactly where the wire will run before anything is drilled?
+

Yes. On the survey we walk the route with you from the power supply to each station, point out where each hole and junction box will be, and note it on the written quote. If you would prefer a different route, such as through a closet instead of along a hallway, we discuss it before work starts.

How many years should a well-mounted outdoor station last?
+

There is no single number, because it depends on the model, how much sun and rain the door gets, and how the station is mounted and sealed. Stations mounted above standing water, sealed at every opening and wiped clean now and then last much longer than ones mounted low on a wet door. We tell you the manufacturer's rating for the model you choose.
