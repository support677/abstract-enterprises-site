The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need Alarm.com Camera Installation

New York's buildings were not designed with cameras in mind, and the city's newest buildings are only a small share of what we wire. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council says more than 80 percent of the city's housing is over 50 years old, and that over half of all units, about two million, went up before 1947. The same source puts half of the city's housing in Brooklyn and Queens, a quarter of the units in Manhattan on 7.5 percent of the land, and five percent on Staten Island. Construction industry data cited by Dodge puts the total near 3.7 million units and says only five percent of the current stock sits in buildings finished after 2010. When an Alarm.com camera goes onto a building in this city, it usually goes onto a wall that predates Wi-Fi, the internet and the electric doorbell by decades.

That age shows up in the building types. A guide to prewar investing describes the range: four- to six-story walk-up tenements built before 1929, eight- to twelve-story elevator buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, and larger doorman buildings from the 1920s and 1930s along Park Avenue, the Upper West Side and Central Park West. The Architectural League notes that most of the city's tenements were built quickly and in vast numbers between 1860 and 1910 in Manhattan, Brooklyn and later the Bronx, and that the rowhouse neighborhoods filled in between the Civil War and the First World War. An analysis citing the city's PLUTO data says about 57 percent of Brooklyn's residential buildings predate 1940, rising above 75 percent in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Crown Heights North and parts of Sunset Park, mostly in three- and four-story walk-ups with original lath-and-plaster ceilings. That analysis comes from a low-authority site, so we treat the figures as a guide, but they match what the older sources say about the borough.

This matters for Alarm.com cameras in particular. Battery cameras and Wi-Fi cameras are attractive because they promise no wiring, but the walls of a brownstone or a prewar walk-up are dense brick and plaster. A local installer writes that brownstone masonry walls are dense enough to significantly degrade Wi-Fi, especially between floors and between the rear yard and the front of the building. Wired cameras avoid that problem, but they need a route for the cable, and in a building like this the route runs through a cellar, up a closet, along an old dumbwaiter or pipe chase, or out through a core-drilled hole in a block wall. Planning that route is most of the work, and it is the part a self-install kit cannot do for you.

Then there is the question of why people in this city want cameras in the first place. CBS New York has reported a string of lobby thefts caught on video: a man taking packages from an apartment lobby on Lawton Street in Bushwick; thieves in Park Slope seen cutting open packages and taking items from them inside a lobby; a theft near 36th Avenue and 31st Street in Astoria where police said the suspect used a screwdriver to disable the door lock; and four men who walked into the Robeson, a building on Lenox Avenue in Harlem, at about 2:30 in the morning and left with lobby furniture, in a building where a key code is required to enter and police found no forced entry. That last case is worth pausing on. A locked, coded door did not stop the theft. What it left behind was a question about who came in and how, which is exactly what a camera at the entry answers.

Package theft is the most common reason we hear. One delivery-industry blog says NYPD package theft complaints rose 23 percent in 2024; that is a secondary source and we have not seen the underlying figure, so we cite it with that caution. The same blog collects secondhand Reddit accounts, including a Bushwick resident whose packages were stolen twice from an entry area that was supposed to be locked, and another building where residents suspected the lobby cameras were not even working. A camera that nobody can watch, or that records to a box in the basement that nobody can reach, protects nothing. Part of what Alarm.com offers is the app: alerts when someone is in the lobby at an odd hour, clips you can review from your phone, and, on business accounts, video tied to alarm events.

The city itself is putting up cameras too. Police1 reported that new NYPD cameras are going into East Harlem, Harlem, Washington Heights and Inwood, several of them aimed at bodegas. Wikipedia's entry on the NYPD's Domain Awareness System says that in 2026 the department announced plans for real-time access to cameras at NYCHA developments. The Intercept, reporting on a 2025 lawsuit, described footage from tens of thousands of private cameras outside shops, businesses and banks reaching the NYPD through the same system. Those stories point in two directions for a property owner. On one side, the city's own guidance on 311 says businesses can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. On the other, tenants and customers are paying closer attention to who sees the footage, which is one reason privacy rules come up on nearly every multi-unit job.

Finally, ownership. Rent Guidelines Board figures show that rental units make up 68.1 percent of the city's housing, based on the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey. That survey counted about 3.7 million housing units and a net rental vacancy rate of 1.41 percent. Most New Yorkers who want a camera do not own the wall they want to put it on. A renter needs the landlord's agreement before drilling. A co-op shareholder needs the board. An owner of a building in one of the city's historic districts may need the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which oversees 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, plus every building inside a designated district. Alarm.com itself suggests its self-install systems for renters and small spaces. We think that is right for some apartments and wrong for most buildings, and the sections below explain where the line falls.

New York facts that shape an Alarm.com camera install

  • Old buildings, mostly: more than 80 percent of housing is over 50 years old, and over half of units, about two million, were built before 1947, according to the Citizens Housing & Planning Council.
  • Mostly renters: rental units are 68.1 percent of the housing stock, per the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report, so landlord permission is part of most residential jobs.
  • Small buildings are common: in one rental-listings sample, 47.7 percent of prewar buildings had fewer than ten apartments and 41.5 percent listed an elevator, which means many camera jobs are in walk-ups.
  • Thousands of protected facades: the LPC oversees 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, in addition to its historic districts.
  • Cameras are not mandatory, intercoms are: installer guidance on city rules says no general NYC law requires cameras in apartment buildings, while Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires intercoms at the main entrance of Class A buildings with eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968.
  • Lighting decides footage: the Multiple Dwelling Law requires adequate common-area lighting, and a camera aimed down an unlit corridor records nothing useful.

What owners often add once the cameras are in. Many Alarm.com accounts in this city start with one or two cameras at the front door and grow from there. The most common follow-up is a camera inside the package area or mailroom, then the rear yard or the cellar door, then a second entry. If you think you will want more cameras later, tell us at the first visit. We can pull an extra labelled cable to each likely spot while the ceilings and closets are already open, which costs far less than coming back to open them again.

The walk-up tenements

The four- to six-story walk-up is the building we most expect to see on a multi-unit job. The entry is a street door, then a vestibule with the buzzer panel and mailboxes, then a stair that climbs the full height of the building. Most of the trouble in these buildings happens in that first ten feet: packages left in the vestibule, a door propped open, someone who followed a tenant in. A camera inside the vestibule facing the street door, mounted high enough that it cannot be reached and low enough to catch faces under a cap brim, does most of the work. A second camera at the foot of the stair, looking back toward the door, catches whoever went past the mailboxes. The cable for both usually runs down to the cellar, where we find or make a path across to the building's internet line.

Walk-ups bring their own snags. Cellars are often reached from outside the building, through a hatch or a door under the stoop, so the cable path from the hall to the cellar may not exist. Brick party walls between buildings carry no wiring and should not be drilled carelessly. And the internet line may belong to a single tenant rather than the building, which means the cameras need their own connection. We work out each of those at the site walk, before anything is ordered.

The brownstones and rowhouses

Brownstones and rowhouses are usually one- to four-family homes, and the owner often lives in one of the units. The coverage list is short and consistent: the stoop and front door, the garden-level entrance under the stoop, the rear yard, and any side passage or garage. One installer describes exactly that pattern and notes that old masonry and plaster mean cable routes are planned through basements, closets and existing chases to avoid exposed wire on the facade. We agree. A camera run on the outside of a brownstone, with cable stapled down the stone, looks bad and, in a historic district, may be the kind of work the LPC objects to.

The rear yard is where Wi-Fi usually fails. A router in the parlor floor at the front of the house has to reach through two or three brick walls to get to the yard, and the signal that arrives is often too weak for a camera to stream reliably. That is the most common reason we recommend a wired camera at the back, even on a job where the front door uses a battery camera.

The prewar elevator buildings and co-ops

Larger prewar buildings have an advantage: a super, a cellar with organized risers, and usually a building-wide internet or telecom room. They also have a board or a managing agent, and an installer guide on city building rules notes that building-wide upgrades such as new camera systems may need board approval. In practice that means insurance paperwork, set working hours, and sometimes a review of where each camera will point. In these buildings we plan the lobby, the mailroom or package room, the elevator landings, the service entrance and the laundry room, and we run cable through existing risers wherever the super allows.

Garden complexes and multi-building properties

Complexes with several buildings around a courtyard, like the garden apartments the Jackson Heights Beautification Group dates to 1917, spread the entries out. Each building has its own door, the courtyard needs watching, and the distance between buildings may be too far for a single cable run without equipment in between. We either put a small recorder in each building and bring all of them into one Alarm.com account, or run cable to a central SVR where there is a protected path. We never route cable across a courtyard where it could be tripped over or cut.

Storefronts with apartments above

The store on the ground floor of a mixed-use building is often the tenant of a different landlord than the apartments upstairs, even in the same building. Its cameras need to see the register, the door and the sidewalk, and after hours a roll-down gate may hide anything mounted inside the window line. We plan at least one camera outside the gate and one inside, and we keep the recorder somewhere a thief would not reach in a hurry. Business accounts with an Alarm.com alarm can also use video verification, which Connextivity, a commercial integrator in the city, describes as linking camera footage to a triggered alarm so the scene can be checked before dispatch is called.

Lobby, Stoop, Cellar, Yard: What to Check in Your Building Before the Site Visit

Most of the delays on an Alarm.com camera job happen before the first camera goes up: an account that was never set up for video, a cellar nobody has a key to, a board that wants paperwork, or a camera spot with no light at night. Please do not open walls, move building wiring or take down an old camera yourself; just look, measure, photograph and send us what you find.

Stand where the camera will stand

The best way to judge a camera spot is to stand in it and look. Hold your phone up at the height a camera would sit, roughly where the door frame meets the ceiling in a vestibule, and take a photo. If you can see the face of someone coming through the door in that photo, the spot works. If all you see is the top of their head, the camera needs to be lower or further back. Do the same at night with the hall lights at their normal setting. Most disappointing footage comes from cameras mounted too high and aimed too steeply, or from a perfectly placed camera in a corner with no light.

Think about where the packages actually land

In many buildings, couriers leave packages wherever they can: on the vestibule floor, on top of the mailboxes, inside the inner door if someone buzzes them in, or on the stoop. Before the visit, look at where packages sit on a busy day. The camera should cover that spot from an angle that shows a person picking something up, not just a pile of boxes. If your building has a package room or a shelf, tell us; that space usually gets its own camera.

Find the old wiring

Many older buildings already have cable in them: an old camera system, a disused alarm, cable TV runs, or a phone line nobody uses. Some of that cable can be reused, and some of it is useless or dangerous to rely on. Take photos of any cable bundles you see in the cellar, at the base of the stairs or in a closet, and of any old camera or recorder. We will trace what is there and tell you what can stay. On masonry and Wi-Fi. The biggest single mistake we see on self-installed camera systems in this city is a wireless camera placed two or three brick walls away from the router. It works on the day it goes in, when someone stands beside it with a phone. A week later, with the doors closed and the neighbors' networks crowding the airwaves, it drops off the app at night or sends clips with long gaps. Brick, block, plaster on metal lath, and the steel doors common on cellar and apartment entries all weaken the signal. Before we recommend a battery or Wi-Fi camera, we measure the signal at that exact spot with the building closed up. If it is weak, the choices are a wired camera, a network extension we wire to that part of the building, or a different spot.

On landmarked facades. The LPC's 2018 rules amendments codified staff practice for health, safety and utility equipment, including security cameras, and they bar many such objects from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative or an agency requires that spot. The LPC Permit Guidebook's Chapter 11, "Installing Security Cameras," says staff can approve cameras that meet its criteria, and that a camera on a primary facade must be as small as possible and painted to match. In practice we look first for spots that are not on the primary facade at all: inside the vestibule, under the stoop, at the rear, or on a secondary wall. The LPC's revised rules of August 2023 also describe a FasTrack program for many simple exterior jobs on non-visible facades, with a permit issued in ten working days. Where a camera truly has to go on the front, we choose the smallest model that does the job, plan the cable to enter the building behind it, and leave the application to the owner with our specifications in hand.

On plaster ceilings. Many walk-ups and brownstones still have original lath-and-plaster ceilings in the halls, which crack and drop chunks if cable is fished through them roughly. We avoid fishing cable across old plaster ceilings where any other route exists. If a hall ceiling has to be crossed, we use the smallest openings that will work, patch them, and tell you beforehand that a plasterer's touch-up may be needed.

What a business camera has to do that a home camera does not

A home camera mostly needs to show who came to the door. A business camera has to do more, because a store, a restaurant or a small office gets hundreds of people a day and the footage may end up with an insurer, a court or the police. That raises the bar on three things. First, coverage: the register, the door, the stockroom door and the sidewalk outside each need a camera aimed so faces and hands are visible. Second, recording: cloud clips triggered by motion are not enough when the question is what happened over the whole of a Saturday, so a business account usually needs continuous recording. Alarm.com's business camera page says its Commercial SVR records up to 16 cameras at up to 4MP, works with third-party ONVIF or VAPIX cameras and has built-in PoE. Third, response: if the business has an Alarm.com alarm, video verification lets whoever responds to an alarm look at the scene before calling for dispatch, which Connextivity describes as one of Alarm.com's main commercial benefits in the city.

The roll-down gate deserves its own mention. Many storefronts close behind solid gates at night, and a camera mounted inside the window sees nothing but the back of the gate. The city's 2009 law requiring new gates to be at least 70 percent transparent set a July 1, 2026 deadline, but NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the City Council unanimously passed Int. 910 to repeal that deadline, so a compliant gate would only be needed when a gate is replaced for other reasons; the bill was going to Mayor Mamdani, and DOB permit rules for gates were unchanged. For camera planning the result is the same either way: we put at least one camera where it can see the gate and the sidewalk from outside the gate line.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Low-voltage permits. A low-voltage contractor's guide says the city's AC 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025, removes the electrical permit requirement for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Another source disagrees and says low-voltage work needs DOB permits, so we check the current rule for each job rather than assume.
  • When filing is still needed. An access control compliance guide says DOB filing or approval is required if the job alters fire-rated door assemblies, changes means of egress under Building Code Chapter 10, or ties into the building's fire alarm system. Camera jobs rarely do any of those, but cable that penetrates a fire-rated wall or ceiling has to be sealed properly, and an installer guide notes such penetrations may need a permit.
  • Audio. Cameras that record sound must follow New York's one-party consent rule in Penal Law 250.00. On a shop floor full of customers, we leave audio recording off unless the owner has a clear, lawful reason and has taken advice.
  • Biometrics. The city's biometric identifier law requires certain commercial establishments that collect biometric information to post notices at customer entrances, but legal commentary notes the disclosure provision generally does not apply to information collected through photographs or video recordings such as security cameras. If a business turns on any face-matching feature, it should take advice first.
  • Sharing with the NYPD. The city's 311 guidance says any business with cameras can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and can register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. That is voluntary, and the owner decides.
  • Insurance papers. Many commercial landlords and managing agents ask for a certificate of insurance before contractors work in a space. Send the request with the booking and we will have it ready.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Walk-Up Vestibule Before Any Camera Goes Up

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow in the vestibule of the kind of building this city is full of: a walk-up with a street door, an inner door, a buzzer panel, a row of mailboxes and a stair climbing out of sight.

We stand outside first and watch the street door open and close a few times, because the camera has to catch a face in the second after the door swings in. We look at where packages are lying, not where the building says they should go. We check the light, then come back after dark if the visit allows it, because a vestibule that looks bright at noon can be a single dim bulb at night. We run a Wi-Fi test where the camera would sit with both doors shut. We find out where the cellar is and whether a cable can get there without crossing the facade. We look for old cameras and old cable, and we trace anything we cannot name. Then we hold a phone up where the camera would go and show the owner the picture it would take. Only when the owner has seen that picture, and the landlord or board has agreed, do we drill.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm.com Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Recording and storage

What does an SVR add that Alarm.com's cloud video does not already do?

Continuous recording. On a Surety support forum thread about exactly this, the answer was that the SVR is an add-on to Alarm.com cloud video, and its main benefit is 24/7 recording. Cloud video alone saves clips, usually triggered by motion or an event. You still need the cloud video service to use an SVR, so the SVR adds to the plan rather than replacing it. In a building lobby, a store or anywhere you may need to see what happened between the clips, an SVR is usually worth it; for a single apartment door, cloud clips are often enough. We help you decide based on what the footage needs to prove.

We have a home account but want wired PoE cameras from another brand on an SVR. Can a residential plan handle that?

Partly. In another Surety forum thread, the answer was that a commercial SVR can be used on a residential account, but support for third-party ONVIF and Axis cameras needs a commercial video plan. So the recorder itself can work on a home account, while the cameras you connect may decide which plan you need. Before we buy or mount anything, we confirm with your Alarm.com provider what your plan covers, and we choose cameras to fit it. That check takes a phone call and saves you from mounting cameras the account will not accept.

Existing systems

Our walk-up already has older analog cameras on the stairs. Do they have to come down to use Alarm.com?

Not necessarily. Alarm.com's help pages say analog cameras and cameras you already have can be connected through an Alarm.com video server. Whether that is a good idea depends on the cameras. Analog cameras from many years ago may give a picture too soft to identify anyone, and the coax cable they run on may be damaged in places. We test each camera and its cable first. If the picture is good enough for what you need, connecting them through a video server can save the cost of rewiring a stair hall; if not, we replace the cameras that matter most and leave the rest for later.

We just bought a house that came with Alarm.com equipment already on the walls. What happens to it?

Alarm.com's help pages list this exact situation as a common question. The equipment belongs to an account held through a service provider, so the first step is to find out who the provider is and set up a new account in your name, often by contacting that provider. Once the account is yours and video is on it, the existing cameras can usually be enrolled to it. We check each camera to see whether it works, whether it is worth keeping, and whether the cable behind it is sound. We also reset anything the previous owner may still be able to see, so the cameras answer to you alone.

Permits, landmarks and licensing

Does the city's newer low-voltage rule mean camera cabling needs no electrical permit?

Often, but we check job by job. A low-voltage contractor's guide says AC 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025, removes the electrical permit requirement for wiring under 50 volts used for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Another source still says low-voltage work needs DOB permits, and a separate guide lists cases where filing is required, such as altering fire-rated doors or tying into a fire alarm. Because sources disagree, we confirm the current rule before quoting, and if any part of your job needs filing or an electrician, the quote says so.

Our brownstone is in a historic district. Can a camera go above the front door at all?

Sometimes, with limits. The LPC's rules cover security cameras and bar many such objects from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative or an agency requires that location. Its Permit Guidebook says a camera that does go on a primary facade must be as small as possible and painted to match. One secondary source also mentions a limit of two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings; we have not confirmed it, so we treat it as something to check with LPC. We look first for spots off the primary facade, such as inside the vestibule or under the stoop, and if the front is the only option we give you the specifications for the LPC application.

Renters and apartment owners

My lease says no drilling. Is there any Alarm.com camera option that leaves the walls alone?

Yes, within limits. Alarm.com says its self-install systems suit renters and small spaces, and its March 31, 2026 release announced the ADC-V731B Battery Spotlight Camera, with microSD recording for continuous video. A battery camera can sit on a shelf or a no-drill mount inside your apartment. Two cautions apply. Anything on a hallway wall or the outside of your door is usually a common area and needs the landlord or board, and battery cameras depend on Wi-Fi, which brick and metal doors weaken. We test the signal first and tell you plainly if a battery camera will drop out.

Can the cameras record sound in a building hallway or vestibule?

Recording sound brings in New York's eavesdropping law. Cameras that record audio must follow the one-party consent rule in Penal Law 250.00, which generally means someone in the conversation has to consent. A camera in a shared hallway or lobby picks up conversations its owner is not part of, which is why installers commonly leave audio off in common areas. Separately, legal commentary notes that a camera hidden inside a living space can support a charge under Penal Law 250.45, a felony. We set up common-area cameras with audio recording off unless the owner has taken legal advice and has a clear reason.

Landlords, boards and tenants

As a landlord, do I need to tell tenants before putting cameras in the halls?

We could not confirm a specific city law requiring notice. One installer blog says a "Local Law 50" requires notice of common-area cameras, but we found no official source for it, so we do not repeat it as fact. What we do know is that New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law 250.65, as legal commentary describes it, protects systems where a written notice is conspicuously posted or where cameras are clearly obvious. Posting a sign and telling tenants in writing costs nothing and avoids disputes, and one installer advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to document the security reason in writing and notify tenants in advance. We recommend both, and suggest your attorney review the wording.

A camera in our hallway seems to point straight at one apartment door. Can it be moved?

Yes, and it should be. A CNET writer described a camera placed directly outside her door in her first NYC apartment, and one installer warns that a camera installed specifically to watch one tenant's door, without covering the broader hallway, can look like harassment in a legal dispute. When we place hallway cameras, we aim them along the corridor, toward the stairs, elevator and entry, not at any single door. Alarm.com cameras also support privacy settings in the app that can mask part of the view. If you manage a building and inherited a camera aimed this way, re-aiming it is a short job.

Our building uses app or fob entry. Do any privacy rules apply when cameras are added?

Yes. HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners of buildings using a smart access system may only use the data it collects for granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas. Legal commentary says the law applies to Class A multiple dwellings in the city that use a smart access system. If cameras are tied into that system, the footage and any access logs should be handled with that limit in mind. We set up the cameras; the owner decides, with advice, who in management can view footage and for what purpose.

Footage, police and response

If something happens outside our store, how do we get footage to the NYPD?

There are two routes. For a single incident, you can save the clip from the Alarm.com app and give it to the officer or detective handling the case; we show every owner how to export clips at handover. For ongoing cooperation, the city's 311 guidance says businesses can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registration is voluntary. Some owners want it and some do not, especially after the 2025 reporting on how private camera footage reaches the NYPD's Domain Awareness System, and the decision is yours.

What is video verification, and is it worth setting up on a business account?

Video verification links camera footage to an alarm event. When an alarm triggers, whoever responds can see what the cameras saw at that moment before calling for dispatch. Connextivity, a commercial integrator in the city, describes it as one of the main reasons businesses choose Alarm.com. It is worth setting up if the business has an Alarm.com alarm and cameras that cover the same areas as the sensors: a camera on the door that has a contact, or on the stockroom with a motion sensor. We place cameras to match the sensors for that reason.

Our lobby camera works in the day but the night footage is useless. What is going on?

Usually lighting, sometimes placement. The Multiple Dwelling Law requires adequate common-area lighting, and installer guidance notes that a camera aimed at an unlit corridor gives no usable footage. Other common causes are a camera facing a glass door with street lights behind it, so faces turn into silhouettes, or infrared light bouncing off a nearby wall or ceiling and washing out the picture. We check the scene at night, re-aim or move the camera, and suggest lighting changes the building can make.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm.com Camera Installation in NYC

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Alarm.com cameras and about camera work in this city, set against what Alarm.com, the city's agencies and our own field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, but they cannot price a cable run down a five-story stair hall.

"Alarm.com cameras work on their own once you plug them in."

This is the claim that causes the most disappointment. Alarm Grid's FAQ is direct about it: every Alarm.com camera needs active monitoring service, and the provider has to add the video surveillance add-on to the account before the cameras will work. That page is several years old, but we have seen nothing from Alarm.com that changes the basic point.

So buying Alarm.com cameras secondhand, or keeping them after cancelling a plan, does not give you a working camera system. The cameras are part of a service, not standalone products.

Before any Alarm.com camera job, we confirm who the account is with and that video is on it. If you have no account yet, we discuss how that will be handled before anything is mounted, so nobody ends up with cameras on the wall that cannot connect.

"You buy Alarm.com straight from Alarm.com."

Quick answers sometimes treat Alarm.com like a camera brand you order from its website. Alarm.com's own page on getting and installing its systems says it is sold, installed and serviced by licensed service providers. Some providers ship pre-configured self-install systems; others send installers.

In New York, that means the account side of your system comes through a provider. Guardian, for example, lists an Alarm.com partnership for New York City. The installation side can come from that provider or from a licensed installer like us working with your account.

The practical point is to know who your provider is. When a camera needs adding, a plan needs changing or a camera goes offline, the provider controls the account, and we work alongside it.

"An SVR means you no longer need cloud video."

An SVR sounds like a replacement for the cloud: a box that records locally, so why pay for cloud storage? The answer from Surety's support forum is that the SVR is an add-on to Alarm.com cloud video, its main benefit is 24/7 recording, and you need cloud video to use it at all.

That matters in buildings where owners want to save on the plan. Taking cloud video off the account does not leave you with a local-only system; it leaves the SVR without the service it depends on.

We set up SVRs where continuous recording is worth having, such as lobbies, package rooms and stores, and we tell owners plainly that it adds to the plan rather than replacing part of it.

"Any camera you already own will show up in the Alarm.com app."

Partly true, and the details matter. Alarm.com's help pages say analog and existing cameras can be connected through an Alarm.com video server, and its business camera page says the Commercial SVR works with third-party ONVIF or VAPIX cameras.

But a Surety forum thread notes that third-party ONVIF and Axis camera support needs a commercial video plan, even though a commercial SVR can sit on a residential account. A brownstone owner with a box of other-brand cameras may find the plan, not the camera, is the deciding factor.

We check every existing camera against the account before promising it will connect. Old analog cameras in particular may connect perfectly well and still give a picture too soft to identify anyone.

"Battery cameras can only record short clips."

This was a fair point about many battery cameras for a long time. Alarm.com's March 31, 2026 release changes it for its lineup: it announced the ADC-V731B Battery Spotlight Camera with microSD recording for continuous video, alongside new AI video search.

That makes a battery camera more useful in this city than it used to be, especially on rentals where drilling is limited and on facades where cable would cross protected stone.

The limit that remains is the building. A battery camera still needs Wi-Fi to stream, and brick, block and steel doors weaken it. We test the signal at the exact spot before recommending one.

"Camera wiring in New York always needs a DOB permit," or "it never does."

Both versions circulate, and the sources behind them disagree. A low-voltage guide cites AC 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025, removing the electrical permit requirement for wiring under 50 volts for alarm and data circuits when done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person. Another source says low-voltage work needs DOB permits.

There are also cases where filing is clearly needed: work that alters fire-rated doors, changes means of egress, or ties into a fire alarm. Cable penetrating a fire-rated wall needs proper sealing.

So neither "always" nor "never" is a safe answer. We check the current rule for each job and say in the quote if anything needs filing or a licensed electrician.

"NYC apartment buildings are required to have cameras."

Installer guidance on city building rules says there is no general NYC law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. HPD may recommend them where crime-related violations persist, but the legal requirements are for intercoms and lighting.

The intercom rule comes from Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, which covers Class A buildings with eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968, and HPD can issue violations for intercoms that do not work. We have also seen online claims that a local law requires camera notices, or that a Real Property Law change forces landlords to let tenants install cameras. We could not verify either, so we do not repeat them as fact.

For a landlord, that means cameras are a choice, not a mandate. They should be chosen for a reason, placed to cover common areas rather than single doors, and backed by working lighting.

Our Installation Process

Every Alarm.com camera job we do in the five boroughs follows the same eight steps, whether it is one battery camera in a studio or a dozen wired cameras across a garden complex. The order matters most in older buildings, where cameras put up before the cable route, the lighting and the permissions are settled are the usual cause of footage nobody can use.

1. Tell us the building and the account

The address, building type, who owns or manages it, whether an Alarm.com account with a provider already exists, and what you want the cameras to see.

2. Book the estimate

We book every request within two hours and set a site visit. Photos of the entries, the cellar and any existing cameras or recorder help us plan ahead.

3. Walk the site

Every entry, the stoop, lobby, halls, yard and storefront, with lighting and Wi-Fi measured at each camera spot and the brick, block and plaster along the cable path noted.

4. Clear approvals and fix the plan

Landlord, board or managing agent sign-off, an LPC check for landmarked facades, and a check for fire-rated walls or fire alarm tie-ins, then every camera location and cable route written down.

5. Run, label and certify cable

Cable pulled through cellars, closets and chases, core-drilled through block where needed, labelled at both ends and tested before a camera goes on it.

6. Mount and aim the cameras

Fixed into masonry or framing with anchors suited to the material, aimed for faces at the door, and checked against the lighting after dark.

7. Add cameras to the Alarm.com account

With the provider's account and its video add-on, cameras are enrolled, named by location, and set for recording, alerts and any SVR continuous recording.

8. Test and hand over

Every camera checked live and in playback on your phone, alerts and clip export shown, and a labelled map of every camera, cable and recorder port left with you.

FAQ

NYC Alarm.com Camera Installation FAQ

Will Alarm.com cameras keep working if there is no service plan on the account?
+

No. Alarm Grid's FAQ says every Alarm.com camera requires active service, and the provider must add the video surveillance add-on to the account before the cameras work. Without it the cameras will not stream or record through the Alarm.com app. If you are inheriting cameras or cancelling a plan, plan for that before the cameras are mounted or moved.

Could I put up Alarm.com cameras myself instead of hiring an installer?
+

In some cases. Alarm.com says DIY-capable providers ship pre-configured systems, and its app has a Video Installation Wizard for adding video devices. That suits a battery camera on a shelf in an apartment. It suits a brownstone yard, a walk-up stair hall or a store much less, because those jobs depend on cable routes through masonry, Wi-Fi testing, and approvals from a landlord, board or the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

How are Alarm.com camera installation prices worked out for different boroughs?
+

Each borough and county has its own price page, because the cost of reaching and working in buildings differs across the region. Within an area, the number of cameras, wired versus battery, wall materials, cable distance, whether an SVR is added and building access decide the total. The Alarm.com service plan is billed separately by your provider.

Does the person installing security cameras in New York need a license?
+

New York State requires security and alarm system installers to be licensed, according to the installer and industry sources we reviewed. AESS does this work under NYS license #12000287431. Ask any company you are considering for its license number and proof of insurance, and check that the name on the license matches the company doing the work.

Is a landlord legally required to have cameras in a New York City apartment building?
+

Installer guidance on city rules says there is no general NYC law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. What the law requires is working intercoms in covered buildings and adequate common-area lighting. HPD may recommend cameras where crime-related problems persist, but cameras are the owner's choice.

Who should be allowed to view footage from a building's common-area cameras?
+

That is a decision for the owner, ideally with legal advice and a written policy. In buildings that use a smart access system, HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law page says the collected data may be used only for granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas. With Alarm.com, access is controlled by user logins on the account, so we set up separate logins for the people the owner names.

Am I allowed to put a camera inside my own NYC apartment?
+

Generally a camera inside your own unit, watching your own space, is something residents do, but it must never record people where they expect privacy without consent; legal commentary notes a hidden camera of that kind can support a felony charge under Penal Law 250.45. Audio brings in the one-party consent rule. Drilling and anything on a hallway wall may need your landlord or board.

Can my landlord add cameras to the lobby and halls of my building?
+

Owners generally can place cameras in common areas such as lobbies, halls and entries, which is why most building camera systems are landlord projects. Good practice, which we follow, is to aim cameras along corridors and at entries rather than at any single apartment door, keep audio off in shared spaces, and post visible notice. Tenants with concerns should raise them with the owner in writing.

Can cameras, the alarm and door access all run from one app?
+

Alarm.com is built around one app for security, video and connected devices, so cameras and an Alarm.com alarm on the same account appear together, and business accounts can use video verification to tie footage to alarm events. Whether a particular door access system can join depends on the product and the provider's plan, which we check before quoting.

If the job includes access control on the front door, does that need DOB filing?
+

It can. An access control compliance guide says DOB filing or approval is needed when the work alters fire-rated door assemblies, changes means of egress under Building Code Chapter 10, or ties into the building's fire alarm system. Cameras alone rarely trigger that, but door hardware often does, so we check before any door work is quoted.

How soon can someone come out after I send a request?
+

We book every request within two hours of receiving it, and same-day estimates are available. The installation date then depends on the building: approvals from a board or landlord, LPC review for a landmarked facade, and access to the cellar can all set the schedule. We tell you the earliest realistic date when we book.

What should I have ready on the day the cameras go in?
+

Your Alarm.com app login or your provider ready to add devices, keys to the cellar, roof or any locked closet on the cable route, written approval from the landlord or board if needed, and the super's contact in a larger building. If the router is in a tenant's apartment, arrange access to it. With those in place, the visit runs without stops.
