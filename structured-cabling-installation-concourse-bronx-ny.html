The Neighborhood

Why Concourse Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Concourse was built for elevators, light and air, not for a network jack in every room, and almost every cabling job here starts with that gap. The boulevard that gives the neighborhood its name was first sketched by Louis Risse in 1890, built between 1894 and 1909, and opened to traffic on November 25, 1909, with a southern extension added in 1927. Risse modeled it on the Champs-Élysées. The apartment houses came after. Six to Celebrate, the Historic Districts Council's profile, describes a first building boom set off by the Jerome Avenue subway and tax exemptions, and a second set off by the 1933 opening of the IND line under the Concourse itself, a boom that ran until about 1945. By the mid-1930s, according to Wikipedia, almost three hundred apartment buildings stood along the boulevard. They were wired, when they were wired at all, for lights, a few outlets, a doorbell and, later, a telephone line per apartment.

Those buildings are still here, and most of them are still full. Wikipedia describes the stock along the Concourse as mostly six-story, high-class, semi-fireproof elevator apartment houses, mixed with five-story New Law walk-ups and a handful of taller fireproof buildings. The Welcome2TheBronx account of the historic district notes that even the smaller buildings were typically built out to their lot lines around large light courts. For a cabler, every one of those words means something. "Semi-fireproof" means masonry walls and fire-resistant floors that we have to drill through and then seal. "Elevator apartment house" means a hoistway that looks like a perfect vertical path but is reserved for the elevator. "Light courts" mean a building that wraps around open air, so the shortest line between two apartments may cross a courtyard rather than a hallway. And "built to the lot line" means there is often no side yard to bring a cable around.

The newer demand sits on top of that old structure. Tenants work from home and want a wired connection in the room where they work. Owners who have answered HPD complaints about entry systems, like the bell, buzzer and intercom complaints that augrented.com's summary of city records shows at 1515 Grand Concourse in May and June 2025 and at 860 Grand Concourse in June 2025, increasingly want a backbone that a modern entry panel or camera can ride on. Co-op boards in buildings such as 1100 Grand Concourse, and in the growing number of Grand Concourse buildings that Lehman College describes as moving toward cooperative ownership, want infrastructure they can document and hand to the next board. Shops on 161st, 165th and 167th Streets run card terminals, kitchen printers, cameras and back-office computers that all expect a reliable wired connection. None of that fits into a building whose only vertical cabling may be a bundle of old telephone wire in a corner of the basement.

There is also the question of which internet service a building can actually get, and the sources do not agree. One commercial provider-comparison site says that in many pre-war multi-family buildings along the Grand Concourse and 161st Street, Optimum is functionally the only wired option, and that Verizon Fios coverage is patchier in older walk-ups where Verizon has been slow to reach building-access agreements. A second comparison site says fiber service is widely available along the Grand Concourse corridor. We present both because we cannot settle it from here, and because the answer is building by building. What we can say is that whichever provider brings service into the basement, the cable from that point to your desk, your counter or your camera is inside wiring, and inside wiring is the part we design, install, certify and document.

The Grand Concourse Historic District adds a layer that most of the city does not have. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it on October 25, 2011, covering properties along the Concourse between 153rd and 167th Streets and parts of Walton and Gerard Avenues; it had already been placed on the National Register in 1987. LPC's own permit guidebook says that where an installation on a facade needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed, and its 2018 rules amendments wrote into the rules the practice of reviewing equipment such as intercoms and cameras on facades and areaways. Most of a cabling job happens indoors, where those rules usually do not reach, but the moment a new service line, a camera cable or an entry-panel feed has to come through the front of a landmark-district building, the plan has to account for LPC before the drill comes out.

Finally, fire. Cabling crosses floors and walls, and every hole through a fire-rated floor is a path for smoke unless it is sealed. The Concourse has had reminders of how fire and smoke move in these buildings. ABC7 reported a fire at 1166 Grand Concourse, a six-story building off McClellan Street, in which the two most critically injured people were found on an upper floor, not in the apartment where the fire began. CBS and ABC7 reported a four-alarm fire on February 2, 2026, at 1215 Grand Concourse at 167th Street that burned through the top floor and the cockloft. We have no information that cabling played any part in either fire and we do not suggest it did. We mention them because they show why we firestop every penetration we make, including the small ones nobody will ever see.

We want to be direct about the limits of our research. We searched for Reddit threads, Patch coverage and community posts about network wiring, internet access or cabling problems in Concourse buildings and found none. We found no DOB permit data or 311 totals about cabling in the neighborhood. We could not capture Google's People Also Ask boxes or AI Overview from our build environment. Where this page describes what we expect to find inside a particular type of building, it is drawing on the age and type of the building in the public record and on what we find in buildings of that age generally, not on a survey of Concourse basements. The walk-through is how we find out about yours.

What the public record tells a cabler about Concourse

  • Where it is: Wikipedia bounds Concourse by East 169th Street to the north, Webster Avenue to the east, East 149th Street to the south, and Jerome Avenue and the Harlem River to the west, split into West Concourse, East Concourse and Concourse Village, in ZIP codes 10451 and 10452 and Bronx Community Board 4.
  • How many people: the neighborhood counted 75,371 residents in 2010, according to Wikipedia, which means a lot of apartments sharing a small number of building risers.
  • What the district holds: 61 apartment houses built between 1917 and 1959, two parks, the Bronx County Courthouse and the Andrew Freedman Home, according to the district summary on Welcome2TheBronx.
  • How tall the buildings are: the LPC designation report notes that 1000 Grand Concourse is one of only three buildings in the historic district taller than eight stories, so most vertical runs are five or six floors.
  • Who designed them: Six to Celebrate credits the Felson firm with the most buildings in the district, alongside work by Emery Roth, whose 1937 Art Deco house at 888 Grand Concourse still has its original lobby floor.
  • Where business happens: Wikipedia places the large retail node on 161st Street at Concourse Plaza, with smaller nodes on 165th and 167th Streets, and the Bronx Terminal Market mall to the south along the Major Deegan.

What owners usually add once the backbone is in. When a Concourse building gets a proper riser and a labeled patch panel for the first time, the next requests are almost always camera drops at the vestibule and stair landings, a cable for a modern entry panel, and ceiling drops for WiFi access points in the lobby or laundry room. Planned together, they share the same pathway and the same firestopped penetrations, which is far cheaper than opening the same floors twice. Tell us what you might want later, even if you are not buying it now, and we will leave spare capacity in the riser.

1927, 1936, 1955, 1963, Today: How a Concourse Building's Age Decides Where the Cable Can Climb

The hardest part of cabling a Concourse building is almost never the last ten feet into the apartment or the office. It is the vertical run: getting from the basement, where the provider's line and usually the main closet sit, up through the floors to the place the cable is needed. The 1920s six-story houses

The largest share of our expected Concourse work is the kind of building represented by 1515 Grand Concourse: six stories, dozens of apartments, built in the late 1920s under the rules of the 1901 Tenement House law, which Brick Underground notes pushed builders toward light and air. These buildings were not designed with a telecom riser. What they usually have instead is a history: decades of telephone pairs, coax from several cable companies, and sometimes abandoned alarm or intercom wire, stapled up the walls of a back stair or fed through holes that were drilled once and never sealed. Our first job is to trace what is there with a tone generator and probe, decide what can be removed, and find a route that can be sleeved, firestopped and reused. The best answer is often a dedicated sleeve at each floor in a stacked closet or utility corner, so every future cable can follow the same line without anyone drilling again.

The Art Deco boom years

The buildings from the 1930s and early 1940s are the ones people come to the Concourse to see, like 888 Grand Concourse at the southeast corner of 161st Street, which Untapped Cities describes as still having its original Art Deco geometric compass pattern on the lobby floor, or the Fish Building at 1150 Grand Concourse with its mosaics. For cabling, those finishes set the rules. We do not run surface raceway across a decorative lobby wall, we do not chase a terrazzo floor, and we plan the riser so it climbs where nobody looks: a service stair, a closet stack, the back of a light court. The masonry in these buildings is solid, which makes horizontal runs between apartments harder but makes a well-sealed vertical sleeve very durable once it is in.

The post-war brick elevator buildings

Brick Underground and the Reinvent guide both point to 1950s through 1970s brick elevator buildings near 161st Street and the courthouse, and 800 Grand Concourse, with 254 units over seven stories, is an example of the scale. These buildings trade the ornament of the 1930s for long double-loaded corridors. The vertical run is often easier than in the pre-war houses, because mechanical chases and compactor or service closets tend to stack neatly. The horizontal run is the challenge: a corridor may be well over a hundred feet from the riser to the last apartment, and the ceiling is frequently hard plaster or concrete with no space above it. We plan those runs in a corridor raceway or cable tray where the building allows it, and we watch the length limits for each cable category so the far apartments still certify.

The high-rise co-op across from the museum

Concourse Towers at 1020 Grand Concourse, a 23-story co-op from 1963 that CityRealty places in the Highbridge listing area but which sits across the boulevard from the Bronx Museum, represents the tallest kind of building we expect on a Concourse call. Its listings mention a full-time doorman, security staff and BuildingLink. A building like that has rules for contractors, and the risers and floor closets belong to the cooperative, not to any one shareholder. For a single apartment, our work is usually inside the unit, from the point where the building's or provider's service enters to the rooms that need a jack. For the building as a whole, the work is a backbone plan the board can approve, budget and hand over. In both cases we get the managing agent's requirements in writing before the visit.

New buildings inside the district

Some of the newest Concourse buildings sit inside the historic district itself, like 700 Gerard Avenue, which the Historic Districts Council testified about in 2019. A building designed in the last decade usually has a telecom room and conduit drawn into its plans, so the cabling question shifts from "how do we get up the building" to "how do we finish it properly." We terminate, dress, label and certify, and we check that the pathways the architect drew actually connect, which in our experience of new buildings generally is not always the case.

Count the outlets by what plugs into them

It is easier to decide how many drops you need by listing what will use them than by guessing a number. In an apartment that might be a desk computer, a television or streaming box, a game console and a WiFi access point in the middle of the unit. In a storefront it might be the card terminal, the register, a receipt or kitchen printer, a back-office computer, a camera recorder and two or three cameras. Write the list, mark roughly where each item sits, and add one spare at each location that is hard to reach. A spare cable pulled at the same time costs little; a single cable pulled alone through a masonry wall two years later costs nearly as much as the first.

Photograph the route you think the cable should take

You know your building better than we do. If you think a cable could follow the back stair, a dumbwaiter shaft that was closed decades ago, a closet that lines up floor to floor, or the ceiling of a basement corridor, take photos of it. Some of those routes will work and some will not; an old dumbwaiter shaft, for example, may be blocked or may connect spaces that should stay sealed from each other. But your photos give the walk-through a starting point and often save an hour of searching.

Ask the board or the landlord the right question

Boards and landlords tend to say no to "can the cable guy drill?" and yes to a specific plan. The better question is: "May we run one sleeved, firestopped data riser from the basement closet to the fourth floor through the closet stack in the back stair, with a certificate of insurance on file and a written plan for your records?" We provide that plan with our quote so you have something concrete to send.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling Installation for Concourse's Architecture

Concourse packs several building types into a few dozen blocks between Jerome and Webster Avenues. Each changes three things about a cabling job: what we drill through, which path the cable can take between floors, and who has to approve the work. These are the types the public record and neighborhood guides describe here, and how we handle each.

Six-Story Semi-Fireproof Elevator Houses

The most common type along the boulevard. Masonry walls, fire-resistant floors, an elevator whose hoistway is off limits, and usually a basement where the provider's lines arrive. The riser is the heart of the job.

Five-Story New Law Walk-Ups

No elevator, often narrower lots, and stairs that are the only common vertical space. We look for closets that stack and keep everything out of the stair's path of travel.

Landmark-District Houses with Original Lobbies

Buildings like 888 Grand Concourse with intact Art Deco floors and finishes. Cable stays out of sight, out of the lobby, and off the facade unless LPC's concealment rules are met.

Post-War Brick Elevator Buildings

Larger 1950s to 1970s buildings near 161st Street and the courthouse, like the seven-story, 254-unit house at 800 Grand Concourse. Long corridors and hard ceilings shape the horizontal runs.

High-Rise Co-ops and Condos

Concourse Towers at 1020 Grand Concourse and the 12-story condominium at 675 Walton Avenue. Boards, managing agents, insurance papers and building-owned risers and closets.

Shops Cut Into Residential Buildings

Ground-floor stores that, Welcome2TheBronx notes, appeared on many Concourse buildings during the 1970s and 1980s. The shop's cabling often has to stay separate from the apartments above.

On fire-resistant floors. Every time a cable passes through a floor or a fire-rated wall in one of these buildings, it makes a hole that smoke can follow. The fix is simple and it is the part most often skipped: a sleeve sized for the cable plus future growth, and a listed firestop sealant or device that restores the barrier around it. We firestop every penetration we make, we photograph it before it is covered, and we include those photos in the handover so the owner can show an inspector or an insurer what was done. Where we find open holes left by earlier trades in the same path, we point them out and price sealing them separately; we do not quietly route through them and leave them open.

On light courts and courtyards. Brick Underground and Wikipedia both describe the wide entrance courtyards and large light courts that give Concourse buildings their light. Inside, those courts mean a building can be shaped like a U, an E or a pair of wings around open air. The quickest route between two apartments may be across the court rather than through the building, and that is rarely a good idea: cable strung across a court is exposed to weather, birds and the next window installer, and it becomes a visible line in a space the district was designed to keep open. We prefer to stay inside the building, through the basement and up each wing separately, even when that means a longer run.

On walk-up stairs. In a five-story walk-up, the stair is the means of escape. We do not run surface cable up a stair wall, across a landing or along the stair ceiling, and we do not leave anything in the stair that could catch fire or snag a foot. A closet stack beside the stair, or a sleeved path in the back of each apartment's hall closet, keeps the stair clear and usually gives a straighter run anyway.

Rules and practical limits for business cabling in Concourse

  • Low-voltage permit exemption. NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. One Bronx hiring guide describes the exemption as applying when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, effective December 21, 2025. Data cabling still has to be done properly; the exemption is about the electrical permit, not about quality.
  • Fire alarm wiring is a different trade. A New York security systems guide notes that fire alarm wiring falls outside that exemption and needs specialized licensing and additional permits. We do not touch a building's fire alarm circuits as part of a data job, and we keep our cable out of fire alarm conduit.
  • New outlets and circuits are an electrician's job. Where a closet needs a new dedicated power circuit for a rack or a battery backup, that work goes to a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Firestopping every penetration. Any cable passing through a fire-rated floor or wall in a mixed-use building gets a sleeve and listed firestop, and the penetration is photographed for the owner's records.
  • Landmark facades. A storefront inside the Grand Concourse Historic District that needs a new line through its facade is covered by LPC's rules on equipment and conduit, which the guidebook says must be concealed. We plan the entry from the cellar wherever we can.
  • Keeping cable off the floor. No cable runs across a sales floor, an aisle or a path to an exit, where it becomes a trip hazard and an accessibility problem; everything is routed overhead or inside fixtures.
  • Insurance papers. Commercial landlords and managing agents usually want a certificate of insurance before a contractor starts. Send us their requirements with the booking and we issue it before the visit.

We look up at the cellar ceiling for a clear path toward the back of the building, and we look for the first floor penetration: an old hole near a closet line, a pipe chase, a sealed dumbwaiter. Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Concourse: Questions Answered

Licenses, codes and who may do the work

Since Local Law 128 dropped the electrical permit for data wiring, can our super or any handyman pull network cable through the building?

Not quite. The law, which added §28-105.4.7 to the Administrative Code, removed the need for an electrical permit for wiring under 50 volts used for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A Bronx hiring guide describes the exemption as applying to work done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. That is a narrower group than "anyone," and it says nothing about the firestopping, the cable rating for the space or the condition of the building afterwards, which are still the owner's responsibility. In a six-story Concourse house, the risk of an unqualified pull is less about the permit and more about open holes through fire-rated floors and cable of the wrong type left in a riser. We do this work under NYS license #12000287431 and document every penetration.

Our fire alarm company is upgrading the building at the same time. Can our data cable share their raceway to save money?

No, and a reputable fire alarm contractor will not want it to. Fire alarm wiring is outside the low-voltage permit exemption and is installed under its own licensing and permits, and its pathways are kept for the fire alarm system. Mixing data cable into fire alarm raceway makes inspections harder, invites someone to disturb a life-safety circuit while working on the network, and can violate the fire alarm design. What does save money is coordinating: if the fire alarm contractor is coring floors in the same stack, we plan our own sleeve next to theirs so both trades firestop at once and the owner pays for one disruption instead of two.

The historic district

Our building is between 153rd and 167th Streets on the Concourse. Does LPC have to approve cable that never leaves the inside?

Usually not. LPC's guidance says interior work needs an LPC permit when it requires a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior is a designated interior landmark, such as the lobby of the Bronx General Post Office at 558 Grand Concourse, designated in 2013. A data riser that stays inside the walls and floors of an ordinary apartment house generally falls outside all three. The exception to watch is anything that changes what people see from the street or the yard: a new hole through the facade, a box on the wall of the entrance courtyard, or conduit up the front. Those are exterior changes and we plan them around LPC before quoting.

The new line has to reach our cellar from the street. How do you get it into a landmark-district building without visible conduit on the front?

The best route almost always arrives below grade or at the rear. LPC's permit guidebook says that where an installation on a facade needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed, and the commission reviews equipment on facades and areaways under Section 2-20 of its rules. So we look first at how the existing services already enter the cellar, at the areaway, the rear yard or a secondary facade facing a light court, and at whether the new cable can follow an existing entry. Only if none of those work do we discuss a facade route, and then the plan goes to the owner with the LPC question flagged before anything is booked.

Getting cable up the building

Our six-story house has an elevator shaft running straight from the cellar to the roof. Why won't you use it for the riser?

Because elevator codes reserve the hoistway for the elevator's own equipment and wiring, and anything else in there is both a code problem and a safety problem for the elevator mechanic. Cable in a hoistway can also be damaged by moving equipment and cannot be reached for repair without taking the elevator out of service. In Concourse elevator houses we look instead for a closet stack, a utility corner or a pipe chase that lines up floor to floor, and if none exists we core a new sleeved path in a spot the owner agrees to. It is more work at the start and far less trouble afterwards.

Our building wraps around a big light court. Can a cable just cross the court from one wing to the other?

It can be done but we rarely recommend it. A cable strung across a light court is exposed to weather, sun and the next contractor working on windows, and in a historic-district building it becomes a visible line in a space designed to stay open. Inside the building, a run down to the cellar, across the cellar ceiling and up the other wing is longer but protected, easier to repair and invisible. Where the distance makes copper cable impractical, a fiber link through the cellar between two small closets, one in each wing, solves the length problem without crossing the court.

We own a 1920s walk-up with no elevator. How does cable get to the fifth floor without running up the stairwell walls?

We keep the stair clear and look for the apartments' own stacked spaces. In many walk-ups, hall closets, kitchen pantries or bathroom chases sit directly above one another, and a small sleeved hole through each floor in the back corner of a closet gives a straight, hidden route. Where apartments differ floor to floor, a concealed raceway in a back hallway or a short offset through a ceiling may be needed. We check each floor on the walk-through and show you the route before you agree to it, because it may involve entering several tenants' apartments, which takes notice and scheduling.

Internet service in pre-war buildings

Only one cable company serves our pre-war building. Does that limit what you can wire inside?

It limits who brings service to the building, not what we can build inside it. One provider-comparison site says that in many pre-war buildings along the Grand Concourse and 161st Street, Optimum is functionally the only wired option, while another says fiber is widely available along the corridor; we cannot settle that from here. Whatever arrives in your cellar, the inside cabling from that point to each apartment, office or counter is ours to design. A good backbone also helps if a second provider ever reaches an agreement with the building, because there is already a labeled path and spare capacity for their equipment to connect to.

We have heard Verizon has been slow to get into older Concourse walk-ups. Is there anything an owner can prepare in case fiber service arrives?

Yes. The same comparison site says Fios coverage is patchier in older pre-war walk-ups along the Grand Concourse where Verizon has been slow to gain building-access agreements. That agreement is between the owner and the provider and is outside our work. What we can do is make the building ready: a clean entry point in the cellar, a sleeved, firestopped riser with room for more cable, and a labeled closet with wall space. Providers generally find it easier to serve a building that already has organized pathways than one where every cable is stapled up a different wall.

Boards, renovations and the next project

Our building is converting to a co-op. What should the board ask for so the next board inherits something it understands?

Ask for the record, not just the cable. Lehman College describes a growing number of Grand Concourse buildings moving toward cooperative ownership, and a new co-op inherits whatever the rental owner left. A board should expect a drawing of the riser path, a port map that names every outlet and its patch panel position, test results for every permanent link, photos of every firestopped penetration, and a note of spare capacity left in each sleeve. With that, a future board can approve a camera upgrade or a new provider without paying someone to rediscover the building. We deliver all of it with every building job.

We are rebuilding the lobby and adding an elevator. When should cabling enter that plan?

Before demolition ends, which is earlier than most owners expect. While floors are open and walls are down, setting a sleeve, a pull string and a box for a future entry panel, lobby camera or display costs very little. Once new plaster, tile and terrazzo are in, every one of those needs coring and patching of finished work. The public example nearby is the reported renovation at 1130 Grand Concourse, where the lobby is being rebuilt and recessed display monitors were part of the exterior proposal reviewed by LPC. On any project like that, we ask the general contractor for the demolition schedule and plan our rough-in to match it.

Several tenants want cameras and a modern entry panel eventually. Can the riser you put in now carry those later?

If we plan for them now, yes. Camera and entry-panel cabling uses the same kind of pathway as data cabling, and a sleeve sized for future cable costs little more than one sized for today. We ask what the building might add over the next few years and leave spare capacity and pull strings in the riser, space on the patch panel and room for a powered switch in the closet. Then the camera or entry contractor, whether that is us or someone else, connects to labeled cable instead of drilling the same floors again.

Shops and offices

The parts that happen in the cellar or the back office can run while you are open. What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Concourse

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about network cabling in general and about the Grand Concourse in particular, set against the public record and our own field practice. They are useful for a rough sense of the work, but they are not a plan for a six-story masonry building on the Concourse.

"Low-voltage cabling in New York needs no license and no permit."

Half true, and the false half matters. Local Law 128 of 2024 did remove the need for an electrical permit for wiring under 50 volts used for data, communication, signaling and alarm, through Administrative Code §28-105.4.7.

But a Bronx hiring guide describes the exemption as tied to who does the work: a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. And a New York security guide notes that fire alarm wiring is outside the exemption altogether and needs its own licensing and permits.

In a Concourse building the bigger obligations are not on a permit at all: firestopping every penetration, using cable rated for the space, and getting the owner's or board's approval before drilling through common floors. We treat all three as part of every job.

"Historic district rules only cover the outside of the building."

Mostly, but not entirely. LPC says interior work needs its permit when the work requires a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated landmark.

Concourse has a designated interior: the lobby of the Bronx General Post Office at 558 Grand Concourse, designated in 2013. And plenty of interior cabling decisions affect the exterior, such as where a new provider line comes through the wall.

For an ordinary apartment house, a riser that stays inside is generally outside LPC's reach. We still check the address against the district map and plan every entry point before quoting.

"A mesh WiFi kit makes cabling unnecessary, even in old buildings."

Mesh systems are a good tool, but they carry their own traffic between units over the air, and every wall and floor the signal crosses costs speed. Concourse's semi-fireproof buildings are built of masonry and fire-resistant floors, the materials radio has the most trouble passing.

In a long pre-war apartment, a mesh unit at the far end can end up with a weak link back to the router and a fraction of the speed the provider delivers. In a building lobby or laundry room, a wireless repeater fed only by air is often the slowest point on the network.

The reliable answer is a wired drop to each access point. One cable to the middle of a long apartment, or to the ceiling of a lobby, often does more for the wireless network than several extra mesh units.

"Fiber internet is available everywhere on the Grand Concourse."

One provider-comparison site does say fiber service is widely available along the Grand Concourse corridor. Another says that in many pre-war buildings along the Concourse and 161st Street, Optimum is functionally the only wired option, and that Fios is patchier in older walk-ups.

Both are secondary sources, and neither one checked your building. Provider availability on the Concourse is decided building by building, often by whether the owner and the provider have reached an access agreement.

Check with the providers at your exact address before you plan around a service. Our part does not change either way: we wire from the point where service enters the building to the places you need it.

"An open shaft is the easiest riser."

Quick answers often suggest using the nearest open vertical space, and in a Concourse elevator house that means the hoistway. Elevator codes reserve the hoistway for the elevator's own equipment, so it is not available.

Old dumbwaiter shafts, pipe chases and abandoned flues are tempting too. Some can be used with care; others connect spaces that are supposed to be separated by fire barriers, or carry heat, gas or water that cable should not be near.

We look at every candidate on the walk-through and pick the one that can be sleeved, firestopped and reached for repair. Sometimes the right answer is a new cored path in a closet corner, which is more work at the start and less trouble forever after.

"Old phone and cable lines can just be reused for the network."

Old telephone pairs and coax were made for different signals and almost never certify as a modern data link. In a building of this age, a lot of the wire in the walls is also simply dead, left behind by services that ended long ago.

What old wiring can offer is a path. If a bundle of dead telephone cable runs up through a clean, reachable chase, removing it and pulling new cable along the same route can save a core drill.

We tone and identify old cable before deciding anything, remove what is confirmed dead and reachable, and tell you plainly which paths can be reused and which cannot.

"Cabling costs a set amount per drop wherever you are."

National guides give one figure per drop, and AI answers repeat it. A New York cabling cost guide notes that a pre-war building with long, difficult runs and after-hours labor can exceed the top of the usual range, which is exactly what many Concourse jobs are.

A drop to the counter of a 165th Street shop with a drop ceiling is quick. A drop to the fifth floor of a 1927 apartment house that needs a new sleeved riser and firestopping at four floors is a different job, even though both end in one jack.

Every Concourse cabling job follows the same eight steps, whether it is two drops in an apartment on Walton Avenue or a full riser through a six-story house. The port map, test results and photos of the pathway, penetrations and rack, with a walk-through for you and the super or office manager.

Will my internet provider run the wire to my back bedroom, or do I need my own installer for that?
+

Providers generally bring service into the building and to one point in your apartment, and their technicians install the modem or gateway there. Extra wired connections to other rooms are inside wiring, which is usually your responsibility or your landlord's, not the provider's. That is the part we install, terminate and test, starting from wherever the provider's service ends.

If no electrical permit is needed for data wiring, does it still have to meet any rules in NYC?
+

Yes. The permit exemption in Local Law 128 of 2024 is about the electrical permit, not about how the work is done. Cable still has to be rated for the space it runs through, penetrations through fire-rated floors and walls still have to be sealed, cable stays out of elevator hoistways and fire alarm pathways, and anything on a landmark facade still falls under LPC rules.
