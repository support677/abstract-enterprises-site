The Neighborhood

What Makes Condo Security Systems Different in Staten Island's Townhouse Communities

Ask someone in Manhattan to picture a condominium and they see a tower with a doorman. Ask a Staten Islander and the picture is a row of two-story attached homes facing a private drive, each with its own front door and driveway, a clubhouse near the entrance and a pool fenced off behind it. That difference changes almost everything about condo building security here. There is often no shared front door to put an intercom on. The things a board actually controls are the entrance road, the gate or guard booth if there is one, the pool and clubhouse, the mailbox kiosk, the visitor parking and the shared lanes behind the garages. A condo security system on Staten Island has to be designed around that layout, or the association pays for lobby equipment it has nowhere to mount.

We checked the borough's condo stock against the city's own property records before writing this page. The Department of City Planning's MapPLUTO tax-lot file on NYC Open Data tags every condominium with a Department of Finance building class beginning with R. Grouping Staten Island's condo lots by that class shows the shape of the market plainly: class R3, which Finance describes as a "Condo; Residential Unit In 1-3 Story Bldg." (class list), accounts for 141 lots and 7,233 homes. Elevator condos (class R4) add 17 lots and 1,948 homes, walk-up buildings (R2) six lots and 483 homes, and mixed residential-and-commercial condos (RM) 29 lots and 1,550 homes. Across the residential condo classes we counted 11,458 homes on 216 lots, and roughly 63 percent of them sit in low-rise one-to-three-story buildings.

Where the condos are: Mid-Island first

The same file carries a ZIP code for each lot, and the concentration is striking. ZIP 10314, which takes in Bulls Head, New Springville, Willowbrook, Travis, Graniteville and Heartland Village, holds 72 condo lots and 4,947 homes, about 43 percent of the borough's condo housing. The next tier is far smaller: 10304 (Stapleton, Clifton, Park Hill) with 1,500 homes on 13 lots, then 10306, 10305, 10301, 10309 and 10312 with roughly 600 to 850 homes each. A board in Travis and a board in Tottenville face the same basic questions, but the Mid-Island communities tend to be bigger, older and more likely to have a gated entrance and a full clubhouse.

Read the table with two caveats. PLUTO counts a condominium at the level of its tax lot, so one large community can be a single row while a phased development may be split over several. Two lots carry no ZIP code at all, which is why the rows add up to two fewer than the total. And a ZIP code is a mail route, not a neighborhood boundary. The pattern still holds: Staten Island's condo market is a Mid-Island townhouse market with pockets on the North and South Shores.

A generation of 1980s communities reaching middle age

PLUTO also records a construction year for most lots. Among the low-rise, walk-up, elevator and mixed-use condo lots with a year on file, 63 were built during the 1980s, more than in any other decade, with another dozen from the 1970s and a second wave after 2004 made up mostly of small buildings of two to six homes. That timing matters for security. A community that opened in 1986 is now about forty years old. Its entrance keypad, gate operator, clubhouse door strike and the low-voltage cable buried between the gatehouse and the clubhouse were chosen by a developer who left decades ago. Many of these associations have replaced roofs and repaved roads on schedule while the electronics carried on until something failed. When boards call us, it is often the first time anyone has looked at that equipment as one system.

Two layers of governance in many developments

Condominiums in New York answer to the Condominium Act. Under Real Property Law §339-e, common elements, unless the declaration says otherwise, include the "basements, cellars, yards, gardens, recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces" and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use." Many Staten Island developments add a second layer: a homeowners association that holds the roads, pool, clubhouse or open space for several condominiums or a mix of condos and fee-simple houses. State rules for those associations, in 13 NYCRR §22.3, require the offering plan to describe the "land, buildings, lots, parking facilities, recreational facilities" the association owns or maintains. In practice, the entrance gate might belong to the HOA, the mailbox kiosk to the condo, and the camera on the clubhouse to whichever body paid for it. We sort that out at the first meeting, because the contract, the deposit and the administrator logins all belong with the body that owns the equipment. Which body that is comes from the offering plan and declaration, and the association's attorney is the right person to confirm it.

What residents complain about

The complaints we hear from Staten Island condo owners are mostly about cars and packages. In 2022, the Staten Island Advance reported that residents of one community believed thieves were getting into locked cars, noting that police had spent "more than two years" urging borough residents to lock their doors "amid a surge in car break-ins and grand larceny." In a 2025 SILive story about a porch theft, another member of the victim's household summed up a familiar frustration: "It's unfortunate that people are having to set up lock boxes because of people (who steal)." In townhouse courts, packages land on dozens of separate stoops instead of one lobby, and the cars sit in open parking courts instead of a locked garage. Those are the two problems a Staten Island condo security plan has to solve first.

After that, the list is specific to the layout. The entrance gate arm is stuck up for weeks after a plow clips the loop detector. Former owners' remotes still open the gate. Teenagers from outside the community climb the pool fence on summer nights. The clubhouse key has been copied so many times nobody knows who holds one. The camera at the entrance records a blur of headlights after dark. Each of those has a narrow, affordable fix, and none of them needs a lobby.

Staten Island condo facts that shape the work

  • 11,458 condo homes on 216 lots across Staten Island's residential condo building classes, by our count of MapPLUTO (NYC Open Data, queried November 26, 2026).
  • About 63 percent low-rise: 7,233 of those homes are in class R3, Finance's category for condo units in one-to-three-story buildings.
  • 43 percent in one ZIP code: 4,947 condo homes in 10314 (Mid-Island).
  • 63 lots from the 1980s, the busiest decade of condo construction on the borough's dated lots.
  • 318,000 occupied condo apartments citywide, per the NYC Comptroller's 2024 homeowner housing report; Staten Island's share is small and mostly townhouse-style.
  • 1968 and eight apartments: the state lobby-intercom rule in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a targets class A buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments, which describes the borough's elevator condos far better than its attached-townhouse courts.

Associations that start by fixing the entrance gate usually move to the pool and clubhouse readers within a season, since a single fob list for every common door is what makes the gate upgrade pay off.

What We Install

Staten Island Condo Security Services: Call Boxes, Fobs, Amenities and More

Each part below answers what a Staten Island condo board, HOA or managing agent usually asks about that system, then points to the page where we cover that service in depth for the borough. Our condo security services are the same whether the association has 12 homes or 240: we install, repair, replace and maintain the equipment, and the board keeps control of it.

Entrance gates, call boxes and visitor entry

In a townhouse condo, the entrance is the intercom. A visitor pulls up to a call box on a post beside the drive, finds the owner in a directory, and the owner answers on a phone or indoor monitor and opens the vehicle gate or the pedestrian gate beside it. Where there is no gate, the same box often controls a clubhouse or mail-area door instead. Replacing that call box is the most common condo intercom replacement we do on Staten Island. Older units dial owners over a phone line, store a short directory that only the developer's software could edit, and sit in a weathered pedestal that lets rain into the keypad. A modern replacement calls owners' cell phones over a data connection, lets the board edit names from a browser, records a snapshot of every visitor and can issue a one-time code to a guest or a contractor.

For a board, the decision is less about the box and more about the plan around it: whether to replace the gate controls at the same time, whether owners will accept app-only answering, what the monthly platform fee will be if the chosen system carries one, and how to phase the work so the gate never sits wide open overnight. We answer each of those in a written condo intercom replacement proposal that separates the call box, gate interface, wiring and programming into lines a board can vote on. Associations working through a formal bid or a reserve-study item can send us their request for proposal and we follow its format. We cover installation in more detail on our Staten Island intercom installation page, and a call box or panel that has simply stopped working is handled through intercom repair on Staten Island. Our board-level guide to condo intercom replacement covers repair-versus-replace decisions, owner notices and how to compare bids.

Lobby intercoms in the borough's elevator and walk-up condos

About 2,400 of Staten Island's condo homes are in elevator or walk-up buildings, and those buildings do have a front door, a vestibule and a directory panel. Here the work looks more like the rest of the city: a lobby panel that rings apartments, in-unit stations or an app, a door release that actually pulls the door shut behind a visitor, and a service entrance on fobs. Several of the elevator condos date from the early 1960s or the 1980s, so the in-unit wiring is old but often reusable. We test a sample of apartments during the walk-through and tell the board whether the new system can ride the existing pairs or needs fresh cable to some floors. Where the building falls under the state's 1968 intercom requirement, the replacement keeps two-way voice and a release from each apartment, whatever the board decides about video.

Door buzzers in small condo buildings

Most condo lots built on Staten Island since 2004 are small: two to six homes in a single building, often with a shared front door and separate entries above. Those buildings need a simple, sturdy buzzer or a small video station rather than a platform with a subscription. When an existing buzzer goes quiet or the release stops clicking, the owners can book door buzzer repair on Staten Island; when the building needs a new plate, station set and release, we cover it under door buzzer installation. Small associations without a managing agent appreciate that a buzzer has nothing to log in to.

One credential list for gates, pools and clubhouse doors

The single most useful upgrade in a townhouse community is one credential list for every shared opening: vehicle gate, pedestrian gate, clubhouse, pool, fitness room, mail area and maintenance shed. Instead of a drawer of gate remotes, a ring of clubhouse keys and a padlock code everybody knows, each owner household holds fobs or phone credentials the board can switch off the day a home is sold. Our Staten Island access control installation page lists the borough's published ranges, including $1,500 to $2,000 for a pedestrian gate keypad and $4,000 to $7,000 for a combined vehicle and pedestrian gate system. Associations still running 125 kHz proximity fobs should know those older fobs can be copied with inexpensive tools; we quote encrypted credentials or phone credentials as the replacement.

Pools, clubhouses, fitness rooms and tennis courts

Amenities drive more board meetings than the entrance does. A pool that opens Memorial Day and closes Labor Day needs a reader that follows the season, honors the posted hours and refuses fobs from households behind on common charges if the board's rules allow that. A clubhouse party room needs a door that unlocks only for the owner who booked it. A fitness room needs a log of who came in after closing when a treadmill is damaged. A tennis or pickleball court gate needs a reader that survives sun, rain and the occasional ball. We install the readers, locks, schedules and logs; the rules about who may use what belong to the board.

Mail kiosks, stoops and packages

Many Staten Island developments collect mail at a cluster box kiosk near the entrance or the clubhouse, while packages go to each front door. That splits the package problem across every stoop in the community. Boards have three practical options: cameras that cover the lanes and stoops from shared property, a staffed or self-service parcel room in the clubhouse with a reader on its door, or smart lockers from a locker provider that we integrate with the association's access system. We install the access and video side of each option; locker hardware and its service plan come from the locker company.

Cameras in brief

Condo security camera installation on a townhouse property is mostly an outdoor job: the entrance lanes, the parking courts, the pool deck, the mail kiosk and the clubhouse doors. Our page on condo security camera installation on Staten Island covers placement, retention and pricing for condo associations. For multi-family layouts in general, see apartment building security camera installation on Staten Island.

Elevator access control

The borough's elevator condos can restrict cars to the floors an owner lives on, lock the elevator out of the garage level after hours or send a visitor straight to one floor after the owner answers the lobby call. Those projects involve the elevator maintenance company and a separate interface in the controller room. We describe the work on elevator access control installation for Staten Island.

Alarms for unit owners and the clubhouse

Because Staten Island townhouse owners each have their own front door, back door and often an attached garage, many want an intrusion alarm of their own. That is a contract between the owner and us, separate from the association's systems, and it is covered on our Staten Island alarm installation page. Central station monitoring is $30 a month with a keypad or $45 a month with the phone app. Associations sometimes add an alarm to the clubhouse, the pool equipment room or the maintenance garage, armed outside open hours.

Fire alarm work within our licensing scope

Clubhouses, elevator condos and mixed-use buildings can carry fire alarm obligations that townhouse homes do not. We handle fire alarm work within our licensing scope and the approval steps that apply in New York City; the details are on our Staten Island fire alarm installation page. Security equipment that touches the fire alarm, such as a mag lock that must release when the alarm sounds, is wired and tested to that requirement.

Buried cable, conduit and the gatehouse network

The hidden cost in a Staten Island condo project is distance. The call box may be 300 feet of buried conduit away from the clubhouse that holds the controller, and the pool gate another 200 feet beyond that. Original conduits from the 1980s are often crushed, flooded or full of abandoned cable. Our walk-through traces each path and tests what is in it, then gives the board a choice: reuse what tests clean, pull new cable through good conduit, bridge a gap with a point-to-point wireless link, or trench where nothing else will work. Trenching and paving restoration are the most expensive line in any gate project, so we look hard for alternatives before proposing them. Low-voltage signaling and data wiring under 50 volts generally does not need a city electrical permit under NYC Admin. Code §28-105.4.7, though life-safety work has its own exceptions; any new 120-volt circuit at a post or gate operator is the association's electrician's job, not ours.

Guests, contractors and short stays

Townhouse communities see a steady flow of landscapers, snow crews, pool staff, dog walkers, aides and relatives who stay for a week. Modern entry systems handle them without sharing a permanent code: a guest code that expires after a set number of days, a landscaper credential that works only on weekday mornings, a pool-staff fob that works only in season. Whether owners may rent their homes for short stays is a question for the declaration, bylaws and city law, not for us; we only provide the credential tools the board decides to allow.

A site-plan checklist for Staten Island boards

Before calling any contractor, a board officer can take the community's site plan, or a printed satellite view, and mark six things on it. Our own free walk-throughs follow the same order:

  • Every way onto the property: the main drive, any service road, pedestrian gates, gaps in the fence line and footpaths to neighboring streets.
  • Every door and gate the association controls: clubhouse doors, pool and court gates, the maintenance garage, the mail kiosk or mail room, and any lobby or service door in an elevator building.
  • The equipment closet: circle the gatehouse, clubhouse room or utility closet with the brains of the system in it, and note if it gets hot in summer or loses power in storms.
  • Every credential in circulation: gate remotes, windshield tags, fobs, keypad codes and clubhouse keys, with a rough count of each and when the list was last checked against the owner roll.
  • Today's camera coverage: mark each camera's position and draw an arrow for its view, then note the retention period and the one person who knows how to export video.
  • Who owns each piece: HOA, condo or individual owner, according to the offering plan and declaration.

A marked-up plan like that turns a vague request into a precise one, and it usually shows that the most urgent problem is a single gate, a single fence gap or a single recorder rather than the whole system.

Storm season, plows and power

Outdoor equipment on Staten Island takes a beating. Salt spray reaches the communities near the South Shore and the Kill Van Kull, wind drives rain sideways into call box faceplates, and every winter a plow or a delivery truck clips a keypad pedestal. We specify weather-rated enclosures, set pedestals back from the plow path with a bollard where the board agrees, and put a battery backup on each gate controller and clubhouse lock so the community does not lose entry with the power. Before the first storm of the season is the right time to test that backup.

Service calls, maintenance visits and support

Condo security repair on Staten Island starts with the $250 service call. Boards that prefer a planned visit can book a spring check of the gate, readers, cameras and recorder before the pool opens, or an autumn check before the snow and package seasons. Phone tech support on systems we installed is free. We do not run a managed security service, a monitored-video subscription or a virtual doorman; we install and maintain equipment the community's staff, an outside provider or the owners themselves use, and we can connect it to a provider the association already pays.

Building Specialists

Six Staten Island Condo Layouts and the Building Security Each One Needs

The six property types below cover nearly every Staten Island condo we are asked to quote. They come straight from the building classes and construction years in the city's tax-lot file, described by layout rather than by name.

A

Gated townhouse communities

Mid-Island developments of 60 to 160 attached homes with a single entrance drive, a gate arm or guard booth, a clubhouse and a pool. Entry control lives at the drive.

B

Open townhouse courts

Smaller groups of two- and three-story homes around a private lane with no gate. The shared assets are the lane, the parking court and the mail kiosk.

C

Garden-style flats

Two-story buildings with an upper and lower home behind a shared vestibule or separate doors. Small buzzers, local cameras and modest budgets.

D

Elevator condos

Seven- and eight-story buildings from the early 1960s and three- to seven-story buildings from the 1980s, with a lobby panel, mailroom and parking.

E

North Shore mixed-use

Newer buildings near Stapleton and St. George with stores or offices below, residential floors above and a garage.

F

Two-to-six-home infill

Small condo buildings put up since 2004 on former single-family lots, often without a managing agent.

Gated townhouse communities: the drive is the front door

The entrance of a gated community has to do four jobs: let owners in quickly, screen visitors, keep a record and fail gracefully when something breaks. Owners usually get a windshield tag or a remote for the vehicle gate plus a fob for the pedestrian gate. Visitors use the call box. Delivery drivers get either a time-limited code or a route through a separate service gate. A camera on each lane records the vehicle and, at a closer angle, the driver's side, and a second camera watches the exit lane where tailgaters most often slip through on the way in. We set the gate to stay open during busy morning hours only if the board wants that, and to lock down at night. The equipment closet is usually in the clubhouse or a small gatehouse; whichever it is, it needs ventilation and a battery backup.

Open townhouse courts: watching the lane, not the homes

Without a gate, a townhouse court relies on cameras, lighting and the mail-area door. Cameras go on shared structures such as the clubhouse fascia, light poles on common ground or the end walls of buildings where the declaration allows, aimed down the lane and across the parking court. The board's goal is to record who comes and goes on the association's property without looking into anyone's windows or garage. We draw the field of view for each camera on the site plan before installing anything, so owners at a board meeting can see exactly what will and will not be recorded.

Garden-style flats: small buildings, small systems

Two-story garden condos often pair an upper and lower home behind one outer door, or give each home its own entry under a shared porch roof. The right system is modest: a two- or four-button station, a release on the shared door, and a camera or two covering the entry and the parking spaces. The temptation is to buy a cloud platform built for a hundred-unit building. For a handful of homes, a wired station with no subscription is easier to own, and owners can still add their own doorbell cameras at private doors if the house rules allow.

Elevator condos: lobby, mailroom and garage

The borough's elevator condos behave more like mid-rises elsewhere in the city. The lobby panel, vestibule door and mailroom are the core; the garage ramp and the side door to the parking lot are where most unauthorized entries happen. Buildings from the early 1960s tend to have masonry walls and plaster corridors, so we reuse riser cable where it tests well and route new runs through closets and stairwells. Buildings from the 1980s usually have drop ceilings in the corridors and easier cable paths, but their original intercoms are long out of production and parts are scarce.

North Shore mixed-use: separate the stores from the homes

The newer mixed-use condos near the Stapleton and St. George waterfront put residential floors above commercial units and a garage. The residential board and the commercial unit owners need separate entrances, separate credential groups and clear lines about who pays for which camera. We set up those groups on one system so the managing agent administers a single platform while the shop tenants never receive a fob that opens a residential corridor.

Two-to-six-home infill: self-managed and practical

Small infill condos are usually run by the owners themselves. They need equipment that works without an administrator: a buzzer or small video station, a keypad or fob reader on the shared door, and a camera or two recording locally. We leave a one-page sheet with every code, login and warranty date so the next owner who joins the association is not left guessing.

Owners' own cameras and the association's rules

Because each townhouse has a private front door, many Staten Island owners have already installed their own doorbell cameras and driveway cameras. That is generally the owner's business at the owner's door, but cameras that look across a shared lane, into a neighbor's patio or onto the pool deck raise questions the board may want to settle in a house rule adopted under its bylaws. We do not draft those rules. What we can do is show the board what a typical doorbell or driveway camera actually records from a given spot, so the rule it writes fits the real equipment rather than a guess. Owners who want their own system tied into a professionally installed recorder can hire us directly for the work inside their own property line.

What changes with materials and methods

  • Vinyl and fiber-cement siding: common on townhouse end walls; cameras mount on a junction box fastened through to framing, sealed against wind-driven rain.
  • Brick veneer and masonry: anchors go into the brick, never the mortar joint alone, and cable enters through a sealed sleeve.
  • Gate pedestals and posts: concrete footings, a goose-neck or island pedestal set back from the plow path, and drip loops on every cable entry.
  • Buried conduit: tested end to end with a pull line before the board is told it can be reused; outdoor-rated, gel-filled or direct-burial cable where it runs underground.
  • Clubhouse interiors: drop ceilings and finished drywall; we fish cable rather than surface-mount it in rooms owners rent for parties.
  • Pool fences: locks and readers rated for wet, sunny locations, and a self-closing gate adjusted so the lock engages every time.

Hills, slopes and long sightlines

Many Mid-Island and North Shore developments sit on slopes, with townhouse rows stepping up a hillside and parking courts on several levels. A camera mounted at the top of a slope sees a long way but records faces poorly; one mounted at the bottom catches faces but loses the far end of the lane. We usually pair a wide view from high ground with a closer view at each pinch point, such as a stair between levels or the walk from a parking court to the homes.

Commercial Condo Security

Clubhouses, Shared Grounds and Storefront Condos: Security Beyond the Homes on Staten Island

A Staten Island condo association is a small business with a property to protect: a clubhouse that hosts parties, a pool that needs staff, a maintenance shed full of equipment and, in mixed-use buildings, commercial units with their own customers. This part covers the condo property security beyond the homes.

Where commercial and mixed-use condos cluster

  • Bay Street, Stapleton and Tompkinsville: the North Shore's newer mixed-use buildings, with ground-floor retail, residential floors and structured parking.
  • St. George near the ferry: older elevator buildings converted to condominium and newer buildings with offices or stores at street level.
  • Richmond Avenue near the Staten Island Mall: Mid-Island townhouse communities next to the borough's busiest retail corridor.
  • Hylan Boulevard: South Shore storefronts with condominium homes above, from New Dorp to Great Kills.
  • Victory Boulevard and Forest Avenue: mixed buildings in Westerleigh, West Brighton and Graniteville.

The clubhouse as a business

Clubhouses host birthday parties, board meetings, exercise classes and, in some communities, rentals to outside groups. That means a door that unlocks for a booked event and locks again afterward, a camera on the entrance and the parking area, and a record of who opened the building when something is broken or missing. Many boards also add a camera covering the pool equipment room and the chemical storage, both for theft and for insurance questions after an incident.

Maintenance sheds, staff and vendors

Mowers, snow blowers, salt and pool supplies are worth stealing. A fob reader on the maintenance garage, with credentials only for staff and the landscaping contractor, and a camera on its doors usually ends the losses. Vendor credentials can be scheduled to their working hours and switched off at the end of a contract, which is far easier than collecting keys from a departing landscaper.

Keeping commercial tenants and residents apart

In mixed-use condos, the commercial owner's employees need the service corridor and the trash room but not the residential elevator. Customers need the storefront and nothing else. We give each group its own credential profile, put a reader on every door that separates them, and record those doors on the same recorder so the managing agent can answer either side's questions. The commercial owner's own alarm and cameras inside the store remain the commercial owner's responsibility, and we can quote them separately.

Parking courts, carports and visitor spaces

Car break-ins are a parking-court problem, not a building problem. Good lighting does more than any camera, so we often start with floodlight cameras over carports and dark corners, then add fixed cameras at the court's entrance. Boards that want license plate capture need a dedicated camera at a lane where cars slow down, with infrared lighting tuned for plates; a general overview camera will not read them reliably at night.

The rules that apply

  • Licensing: security and fire alarm installers in New York need a state license under General Business Law Article 6-D; ours is NYS #12000287431.
  • Intercom requirement: MDL §50-a applies to class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments; whether a given condo building is covered is a question for the association's counsel.
  • Smart access and privacy: NYC's Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) sets consent, retention and policy rules for smart access systems in class A multiple dwellings; how it applies to an owner-occupied condo is for the association's attorney to judge, and we configure systems to whatever policy the board adopts.
  • Audio: New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, so we leave audio recording off on common-area cameras unless the board's attorney approves otherwise.
  • Egress: electrically locked doors and gates on a path of egress must release on a fire alarm and allow free exit; we wire and test them that way.

None of the above is legal advice. We cite the rules so boards know which questions to ask their own counsel.

Your Questions Answered

Condo Security Systems on Staten Island: Questions Answered

Grouped by topic below: what we are asked most often at Staten Island board meetings, clubhouse walk-throughs and on the phone. The short answer comes first in each.

Layout and governance

Our townhouse condo has no lobby. What does a building intercom even mean for a community like ours?

It means the call box at the entrance, plus any door the association controls. In a gated community, visitors call owners from a post at the drive and the owner opens the gate from a phone or indoor monitor. In an open court, the same idea moves to the clubhouse or mail-area door. Each home's own front door normally stays the owner's business, and many owners already have a doorbell camera there. The association's system should cover the shared entry points and leave private doors alone unless the board decides otherwise.

Our development has a master HOA and a separate condo association. Which one signs the contract with you?

Whichever body owns or maintains the equipment being installed. Often the master HOA owns the entrance road, the gate and the clubhouse, while each condominium owns its buildings and mail area. If a single project spans both, we can write one proposal with separate sections and separate deposits so each board approves and pays for its own part. The offering plans and declarations decide ownership; we follow what the associations' attorney or managing agent tells us.

Can a volunteer officer run the gate and pool access from a phone, without a managing agent?

Yes, with the right platform. Most current access and entry systems have a browser or phone dashboard where an officer can add a household, cancel a lost fob, change pool hours or issue a guest code in a minute. We set up at least two administrator accounts, never just one, so access survives a board election or an officer moving away, and we hand the board a written list of every login.

Entrance and gate

Visitors keep slipping in behind residents at our entrance gate. Is the fix a camera, a second arm or something else?

Usually timing and detection come first, cameras second. Tailgating at a vehicle gate happens because the arm stays up too long or the loop detector under the pavement no longer senses the car behind. Shortening the close timer, replacing a damaged loop and adding a "one car per entry" sign fix most of it. A camera on the entry lane then records the cars that still get through. A second arm or a pedestrian turnstile is the costly last resort.

The plow knocks over our keypad post every winter. What are the options?

Move the pedestal, protect it, or both. We can set a new pedestal farther from the curb line on an island, install a steel bollard on the plow side, or switch owners to windshield tags and remotes so the keypad is used only by visitors and can sit in a more protected spot. Telling the snow contractor where the post is, with a reflective marker, is the cheapest fix of all.

What should our entrance gate do when the power goes out?

The board should choose, in writing, before the storm. Options are to hold the gate closed on battery for a set number of hours, to open and stay open when the battery runs low, or to switch to a manual release that staff or the fire department can use. We install a battery backup sized to the board's choice and test it, and we label the manual release so whoever arrives in the dark can find it.

Amenities and access

How should pool fobs work for a season that runs only from late May to early September?

The pool reader gets its own schedule: active only between opening and closing day, and only during posted hours within that window. Owner fobs stay the same all year, so nobody collects or reissues anything; the reader simply ignores them out of season. Lifeguard and pool-company staff get credentials that expire at season's end, and the board can extend the dates with a few clicks if the weather is kind in September.

Can guest passes for the pool or clubhouse expire on their own?

Yes. A guest credential can be a one-day phone pass, a code good for one weekend or a temporary fob for a visiting relative that stops working on a set date. The owner who requested it appears in the log alongside each entry, which gives the board a fair way to enforce guest limits without anyone standing at the pool gate.

Can the clubhouse party room open only for the owner who reserved it?

It can. The booking can come from a shared calendar the board already uses or be entered by the officer who manages reservations. The room's reader unlocks for that household's credential during the booked hours, plus setup and cleanup time, and locks again afterward. If something is damaged, the log shows who had access and when.

Cameras, packages and parking

Where should a townhouse community put its first two cameras?

At the entrance and at the place where trouble actually happens. For a gated community, the first camera records the entry lane and the second the exit lane or the pool gate. For an open court, the first goes where the lane meets the public street and the second over the parking court with the most break-ins. Coverage of individual stoops comes later, once the board sees what the first two record.

Several of our garages open onto a shared lane. Can a camera watch the lane without looking into the garages?

Yes. We aim the camera down the length of the lane rather than across it, and modern recorders let us black out privacy zones so the opening of each garage, and any window that falls in the frame, is masked in the recording. We show the board the masked view during setup, and the board can share that screenshot with owners before the camera goes live.

Our townhouses get packages on dozens of separate stoops. Does a central parcel room make sense for a community like ours?

It does if the community has a clubhouse or a spare room near the mail kiosk and owners are willing to walk there. A room with a fob reader, a camera on the inside and a shelving system for carriers, or smart lockers from a locker provider, keeps boxes off stoops. In communities where owners would rather keep doorstep delivery, cameras on the lanes and individual owners' doorbell cameras are the realistic alternative.

Wiring, timing and older equipment

The original low-voltage cable runs underground from the gate to the clubhouse. Can a new system reuse it?

Sometimes. Cable buried in the 1980s may be fine, waterlogged or chewed, and the only way to know is to test it. If the conduit is intact, we can often pull new cable through it using the old cable as a pull line. If it is collapsed, a point-to-point wireless link between the gate and the clubhouse often costs far less than trenching and repaving. The walk-through report tells the board which case applies.

How long does a gate, clubhouse and pool access project take in a community of about 100 homes?

Plan on the calendar more than the labor. The installation itself usually fits in a few working days once equipment is on site. Before that, the board approves the proposal, pays the deposit, and the equipment is ordered; special-order panels ship on the maker's schedule, which we do not control, so we set the install date only after the equipment arrives. Fob distribution and owner app setup add a week or two of the board's time after go-live.

We are a mixed-use condo with stores downstairs. Can the residential board and the store owners share one system?

Yes, as long as the groups are separated inside it. One platform with distinct credential profiles, schedules and camera permissions lets the managing agent administer everything while the residential board sees only residential doors and footage. The cost split between the residential and commercial sides follows the declaration, and we can break the proposal out to match.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems on Staten Island

Search summaries, chatbots and national cost guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give condo boards generic answers built on Manhattan towers and suburban single-family homes. Here is where those answers miss on Staten Island.

1. "Every condo needs a lobby intercom"

Generic answers start with a lobby panel because most writing about condos is about apartment buildings. On Staten Island, most condo homes are in one-to-three-story attached buildings where each household has its own front door.

For those communities, the shared entry point is a gate, a clubhouse or a mail area. Buying a lobby-style system with a directory for every home and nowhere sensible to mount it is a common and expensive mistake.

The right first question is which doors and gates the association actually owns, and we answer it on the site plan before anything is priced.

2. "Every condo pays the same per home for security"

Per-unit pricing assumes every home adds the same amount of wiring and hardware. In a townhouse community, the homes often add almost nothing, because the association's equipment sits at a handful of shared points.

What drives cost here is distance and ground: how far the gate is from the clubhouse, whether the old conduit is usable, and how many separate gates, doors and lanes need coverage. A 150-home community with one gate can cost less than a 40-home community with two gates and no usable conduit.

That is why we publish the $250 service call and the repair ranges, and quote community-wide work only after walking the property.

3. "Wireless cameras solve the cabling problem"

Wi-Fi cameras are marketed as cable-free, but they still need power, and outdoors that means an outlet, a solar panel or a battery that fails in cold weather. Their signal also has to reach a router, which a gate far from any building will not.

On large properties, the reliable answer is wired cameras on outdoor-rated cable, with a purpose-built point-to-point wireless link only where trenching would be unreasonable.

That keeps every camera on one recorder the association owns, instead of a dozen apps on owners' phones.

4. "The HOA and the condo board are the same thing"

Many summaries treat the two terms as interchangeable. In New York, a condominium is governed under the Condominium Act, while a homeowners association that owns shared land or amenities files its offering plan under separate Attorney General regulations.

On Staten Island, plenty of developments have both, and the gate, pool or clubhouse can belong to a different body than the buildings. Signing the wrong entity to a contract, or setting up logins under the wrong board, causes real trouble later.

We ask for the governing documents' answer early and write the proposal to the owner of each piece.

5. "The entrance camera will capture plates on its own"

Overview cameras record the scene; they do not reliably read plates, especially at night when headlights wash out the image. Plate capture needs a camera aimed at a lane where cars slow down, set to a narrow field of view with infrared lighting and a fast shutter.

If the board wants plates, we place a dedicated camera at the gate's stopping point. If it does not, a general camera at the entrance is enough to show what happened.

Either way, the board should decide in advance who may see that footage and how long it is kept.

6. "Smart gate systems have no ongoing costs"

Many cellular call boxes and cloud access platforms carry a monthly or yearly fee per device or per door, and some need a data plan of their own.

Those fees can be worthwhile for app answering and remote administration, but they belong in the board's budget, not in a surprise invoice. Wired systems with a local controller can run with no subscription at all, at the cost of some convenience.

Our proposals show any recurring fee as its own line so the board sees the five-year cost, not just the installed price.

7. "Cameras and readers will earn the association an insurance discount"

Some guides promise insurance savings from cameras or access control. Whether a carrier gives any credit depends entirely on the carrier and the policy.

What a well-run system does provide is a record: who entered the clubhouse before a fire, which car hit the gate, when a fob was used at the pool after closing. That record can help with a claim or a dispute.

Boards that want a premium answer should ask their broker before counting on a discount. We do not promise one.

Field notes from our Staten Island crews

The first thing we do at a Staten Island townhouse community is walk from the call box to wherever the controller lives, following the cable. It is usually farther than anyone on the board thinks, and the path tells us most of what the job will cost.
