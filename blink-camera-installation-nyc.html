The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need Blink Camera Installation

Most people buy a Blink camera in New York for one reason: packages. Gothamist, writing about the city's missing Amazon deliveries, described the routine many New Yorkers know as the "package shuffle," and summed up one of its steps in a single line: "We install doorbell cameras to monitor the stoop." Gothamist also cited a SafeWise estimate that the total value of packages stolen in New York City is close to one billion dollars. A delivery blog drawing on Gothamist's reporting says New Yorkers filed more than 74,000 complaints about stolen packages over five years and that calls to police about package theft rise 45 percent during the holiday season. Wikipedia's entry on package theft records an estimate of 90,000 packages disappearing every day in the city in 2019, up 20 percent from four years before. And according to Axios, SafeWise ranked New York City ahead of Philadelphia and Chicago for package theft. When the New York Attorney General issued a consumer alert on package theft in 2024, one of the tips was plain: use a doorbell camera or video surveillance system to help deter thieves or help police identify them.

Blink is popular for that job because it is inexpensive, small and wireless, and because it does not need a contract. But New York does not make wireless easy. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council counts about 3.7 million housing units in the city, with roughly 62 percent of them in buildings of six or more units and only a little over a quarter in one- and two-family homes. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report says 54 percent of the city's units sit in buildings put up before 1947. One building-data analysis puts the city's median residential year built at 1931. Prewar walk-ups and elevator buildings were built with masonry walls that one real-estate writer describes as often 12 to 18 inches thick. Blink's own support pages tell owners to keep the Sync Module away from thick walls and materials like concrete, brick, stone or metal, and say a Sync Module can talk to cameras up to 100 feet away. A hundred feet sounds like plenty until it has to pass through a century-old brick bearing wall, a steel apartment door and a fire-rated stair enclosure.

The second problem is ownership. A camera on the inside of your own apartment door, or on the door itself, is a very different thing from a camera screwed into the hallway wall. Connextivity, a New York security writer, notes that no New York State or City law prohibits a resident from putting a camera on their own apartment door, but that a camera on a hallway wall sits in a common area and usually needs building approval in a co-op or condo. Another New York guide says management has authority to regulate hallway-facing devices that interfere with other tenants' privacy, and that whether a doorbell camera is allowed often comes down to building policy. On a renter question-and-answer site, a New York tenant who had put up a Blink doorbell asked whether the landlord could legally stop them, and was told that a landlord generally cannot remove the device without consent unless it violates the lease or building rules. That was an answer on a non-lawyer site, not a court ruling, and we treat it as one tenant's experience. The point for an install is simple: we read the lease and the house rules before we pick a mount, not after.

The third problem is power. In a house with a doorbell button and a chime, a wired Blink Video Doorbell can take power from the chime transformer, but Blink says that transformer has to supply 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz, with a maximum of 40 VA. If the wiring does not match, the Blink app shows "Wired power not supported" and the doorbell falls back to running on its batteries in what Blink calls Event Response mode. Most New York apartment buildings do not have a chime at each apartment at all. The Housing Maintenance Code, as summarized by an HPD filing guide, requires class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system, with a bell or buzzer system as the minimum. That buzzer is the "doorbell" for most of the city, and it is not a Blink power source. So in an apartment, a Blink doorbell nearly always runs on batteries, and the job turns on where the Sync Module sits and how much signal reaches the door.

Then there are the city's rules for the outside of buildings. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, along with whole historic districts full of row houses and brownstones. Its 2018 rules amendments wrote down staff practice for installing equipment on facades and areaway features, and its list explicitly included security cameras alongside intercoms, light fixtures, security grilles and mailboxes. The Commission's Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras that favors placement off the primary facade where possible and finishes that blend with the building. And the Commission does follow up: its July 16, 2013 public hearing calendar included an application to legalize security cameras and an intercom installed without permits on an 1887 to 1889 rowhouse at 52 West 76th Street in the Upper West Side/Central Park West Historic District. A small white camera on a stoop feels harmless, but on a landmark block it is still equipment on a facade.

Finally, there is the weather and the grid. A battery camera on a north-facing stoop in January works harder than the same camera on a kitchen shelf. A battery supplier notes that cold weather, weak Wi-Fi and frequent alerts all shorten battery life, and that lithium cells hold steady from minus 40 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit while alkaline cells lose much of their capacity in the cold. Asurion's setup advice for Blink outdoor cameras is to choose a spot sheltered from direct rain and extreme temperatures. And when the power goes out, cameras that rely on the home router go quiet with it. NY1 reported that on the evening of June 25, 2025, during a heat wave, 10,019 Con Edison customers were without power across the city, most of them in Brooklyn and Queens, and that one customer can be an entire building. Blink's Wired Doorbell 2K+ can run from a plug-in adapter that supplies 24 volts AC from an ordinary indoor outlet, but Blink says that setup has no battery backup and needs continuous power. None of this means Blink is a poor choice. It means a Blink system in New York should be planned around the building, the season and the network, not just unboxed.

We also want to be honest about what our research did not find. We looked for Reddit threads, Yelp reviews and hyperlocal news specifically about Blink installations in New York City and found none we could cite. We found no Department of Buildings permit data and no HPD or 311 complaint data specific to doorbell cameras. Where this page talks about what we see in prewar buildings, brownstones and storefronts, it draws on the public sources named in the stamp above and on our general field practice, not on a survey of Blink systems in the city.

New York facts that shape a Blink install

  • Mostly apartments: about 62 percent of the city's roughly 3.7 million units are in buildings of six or more units, per the Citizens Housing & Planning Council; Manhattan reaches 97 percent, while 83 percent of Staten Island's units are in one- and two-family homes.
  • Mostly old: 54 percent of units are in buildings built before 1947, according to the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report.
  • Packages go missing at scale: an estimated 90,000 a day in 2019 per Wikipedia's package theft entry, and security.org's annual report found apartment residents more than three times as likely as others to have had a package stolen in the past three months, 10 percent against 3 percent.
  • Not every home has broadband: the NYC Council's internet access data shows 8.1 percent of households with no internet at all, 12.8 percent on a cellular data plan only, and 24.5 percent without a cable, fiber or DSL subscription, which matters because Blink needs Wi-Fi to work.
  • Landmarks are everywhere: 1,460 individual landmarks plus historic districts, and the LPC's rules name security cameras among the equipment it regulates on facades.
  • The doorbell is often a buzzer: post-1968 class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments must have an intercommunication system, with a bell or buzzer as the minimum, under the Housing Maintenance Code.

What New Yorkers often add once Blink is up. After the door camera is working, the next question is usually the building's own front door: the lobby buzzer that does not ring, or the entry door that does not latch, which is where the package thief gets in. Those are separate building systems with their own repair, and if you tell us about them when you book, we will look while we are there and quote them separately so you can decide.

The same Blink Battery Doorbell 2K+ can be a ten-minute job on one front door and a two-hour puzzle on another. The borough is a rough guide to which you will get, because each one has a very different mix of buildings. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council's housing stock analysis gives the outline: Manhattan almost entirely apartments, Staten Island mostly houses, Brooklyn the most even mix, and Brooklyn and Queens together holding about half the city's units. Manhattan: the corridor door

In a borough where nearly every unit is in an apartment building, a Blink install usually means a doorbell or a small camera at an apartment door on an interior hallway. The questions are about permission and privacy before they are about hardware. Is the door yours to put a bracket on, or does the lease forbid any change to it? Will the camera's view reach across the hallway into a neighbor's doorway every time they open it? Does the building's alteration policy treat a doorbell camera as something that needs management sign-off? The Cooperator, writing about co-op alteration rules, notes that boards commonly want proof of a contractor's insurance and often limit work to weekday business hours, and that reserving a service elevator can take 48 hours' notice. We plan around those rules. On the hardware side, many Manhattan apartment doors are steel or metal-clad, and a doorbell vendor's guidance warns that walls, brick and metal doors cut the Sync Module's range and drain the batteries faster. We often put the Sync Module inside the apartment within a few feet of the door, rather than next to a router at the far end of a long railroad layout.

Brooklyn: the stoop and the parlor floor

Brooklyn has the widest spread of building types in the city, and its Blink jobs follow suit. A brownstone owner in a historic district wants the stoop and the areaway covered without a lump of white plastic on a carved lintel. A tenant on the top floor of a two-family wants a doorbell on their own entry upstairs and has no chime to wire into. A renter in a six-story walk-up wants the same thing as a Manhattan tenant. One Brooklyn installer notes that many of the borough's brownstones sit in Landmarks Preservation Commission historic districts, including Brooklyn Heights, Park Slope and Fort Greene, and the Historic Districts Council records that Brooklyn Heights was the first historic district the city designated, on November 23, 1965. For those homes we start with placement that keeps the camera off the primary facade where the view allows it: inside the vestibule glass, under the stoop, on the side of the areaway, or on the rear of the house. The other Brooklyn issue is brick. In a row house the router is usually on the parlor floor or in the back, and the signal to the front door has to pass through masonry, which is where a well-placed Sync Module, or a second one, earns its keep.

Queens: houses, driveways and garden apartments

Queens mixes elevator buildings with large areas of attached and detached houses and garden apartment complexes. A typical Queens Blink job covers more than one door: the front entry, a side door down a driveway, a garage or a back yard. That spreads cameras out, and Blink's 100-foot Sync Module range starts to matter. A camera on a detached garage at the end of a long driveway may sit at the edge of what one Sync Module can hear, especially if the signal has to pass through the brick or stucco of the house first. We measure before we mount and, where the far camera is weak, follow Blink's own advice and add a second Sync Module closer to it rather than hoping. Queens also bore a heavy share of the June 2025 outages NY1 reported, with 3,016 customers out at one point that evening, which is a reminder that cameras depending on a router and modem stop when the power does.

The Bronx: long corridors and house blocks

The Bronx has some of the largest apartment complexes in the country. Parkchester alone, built by Metropolitan Life between 1939 and 1942, was recorded with 171 brick buildings of eight or thirteen stories and 12,271 apartments. Co-op City, in the northeast Bronx, counts 15,372 units in 35 high-rise buildings and seven townhouse clusters. Apartment-door Blink installs here follow the same rules as Manhattan, with management policy and hallway privacy coming first. Long corridors and fire-rated stair doors can put the door camera a long way, in signal terms, from a router at the back of the apartment. The north and east Bronx also have blocks of one- and two-family houses with porches and driveways, where the job looks more like Queens or Staten Island.

Staten Island: the house with a chime

With 83 percent of its units in one- and two-family homes, Staten Island is where a wired Blink Video Doorbell most often has a real chime to connect to. That makes the chime transformer the first thing we check. Blink says the doorbell needs 16 to 24 volts AC and no more than 40 VA, and older houses sometimes have transformers below that range, which is what triggers the "Wired power not supported" message. When the voltage is wrong, the choices are a battery-only doorbell, a transformer upgrade by the right trade, or Blink's Wired Doorbell 2K+ running from the plug-in adapter where an indoor outlet sits close to the door. Staten Island jobs also tend to add Outdoor cameras on the driveway and the back yard, and those spread the system out far enough that Sync Module placement decides whether every camera stays connected.

Before You Book

Lease, Door, Router, Power: What to Check in a New York Home Before Your Blink Visit

Nearly every Blink job that runs long in New York runs long for one of a handful of reasons: the building says no to the spot we planned, the door is the wrong thickness for the bracket, the router is two walls of brick away, or the doorbell power is not what anyone assumed. A few minutes with your lease, a tape measure and your phone camera avoids almost all of it. Please do not drill holes, open the chime or move the router yourself before we come; just look, measure and send us what you find.

Read the lease for three words

You do not need a lawyer to spot the clauses that matter. Look for anything about "alterations," "common areas" and "doors." Leases and house rules often say you may not change the apartment door, its hardware or its finish without written consent, and that nothing may be attached to hallway walls. Those clauses do not usually forbid a camera on a shelf inside your apartment, which is where a Blink Mini can watch the door from behind. They may affect a doorbell bracket on the outside face of the door. If the clause is unclear, the simplest step is an email to the managing agent describing exactly what you want: a battery doorbell on a clamp mount that leaves no holes, with motion zones set to your own doorway. Keep the reply. If the answer is no, we will design around it rather than putting your deposit at risk.

Walk the signal path

Stand at your router and walk to the door where the camera will go. Count the walls you pass through and notice what they are made of. In a prewar apartment the walls between rooms are often plaster, but the wall between the apartment and the hallway, and the walls around stairs and elevator shafts, can be masonry. In a brownstone the front and rear walls and the party walls are brick. Each of those costs signal. Then open your phone's Wi-Fi settings at the door and see how many bars it shows. One bar or none at the door usually means a Sync Module close to the door, a Wi-Fi extender, or a wired access point, and it is far better to know that before the visit than to discover it with the doorbell already on.

Look at the power you really have

If you live in a house, find the doorbell chime inside, usually a small box high on a wall near the front door or in the hall. If there is one, there is probably a transformer somewhere, often near the electrical panel or in the basement, with its voltage printed on it. Do not open anything; just take a photo if you can see the label. If you live in an apartment and your only "doorbell" is the building buzzer, tell us. That tells us right away that the doorbell will run on batteries, or on Blink's plug-in adapter if you choose the Wired Doorbell 2K+ and have an outlet nearby, and that we should focus on battery life and signal.

Building Specialists

Blink Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

New York has more kinds of front door than any city we know. Each building type changes three things about a Blink job: what the device is fixed to, how the signal gets from the router to the camera, and who has to agree before anything goes up. These are the types the city's housing data describes, and how we handle each.

Prewar Walk-Ups

Four- to six-story tenements built under the early multiple-dwelling laws, with masonry bearing walls, apartment doors on narrow landings and routers deep inside railroad layouts. Battery doorbells and careful Sync Module placement.

Prewar Elevator Buildings

Eight- to twelve-story buildings from the 1910s and 1920s and larger doorman buildings from the 1920s and 1930s. Long corridors, heavy doors, staff and management rules about what goes on a door.

Co-ops and Condos

Boards control hallways and often require insurance papers and set work hours. Shareholders and owners decide inside their units, but a doorbell on the corridor face of a door may need approval.

Brownstones and Row Houses

Stoops, vestibules and areaways, often in LPC historic districts. Brick front, rear and party walls between the router and the door; the owner decides, subject to the Commission.

Two- and Three-Family Houses

An owner on one floor and tenants on the others, separate entries, sometimes one shared router. Each household's cameras need their own account and their own privacy settings.

Detached and Attached Houses

Real chimes, driveways, yards and garages, mostly in Staten Island, Queens and the outer Bronx. Wired doorbell options and several outdoor cameras spread across a lot.

Many New York apartment doors are hollow steel or metal-clad wood set in steel frames, because the city's rules ask a lot of them. HPD's fire safety guidance says buildings with three or more apartments must have self-closing doors, and Local Law 111 of 2018 made doors to interior corridors and stairs in residential buildings self-closing, with a deadline of July 31, 2021, and treats a failure to maintain them as an immediately hazardous violation. A heavy self-closing steel door is hard on a doorbell in two ways. It swings shut with force every time, which tests any clamp bracket, and it blocks radio signals, which shortens the Sync Module link and runs the battery down faster. On metal doors we choose clamp mounts that grip the top edge firmly without stopping the closer from latching the door, test the door's closing several times with the bracket on, and put the Sync Module where the signal can reach the doorbell through the smallest amount of steel, usually close to the hinge side inside the apartment. We never fit anything that stops a self-closing door from closing and latching on its own.

Brick and brownstone

On a brownstone or brick row house we treat the facade as something to protect. Where a camera must go outside, we fix into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick or stone, because a joint can be repointed later and a cracked brownstone lintel cannot be put back. Brownstone itself is a soft sandstone that spalls if it is drilled carelessly. Where the view allows, we keep the camera off the street face entirely and place it inside the vestibule looking out through the glass, beneath the stoop looking at the areaway gate, or on the side return of the stoop, which also tends to suit the LPC's preference for keeping equipment off primary facades. Inside, the brick front and party walls are the reason a Sync Module placed next to the router at the back of the parlor floor often loses the front camera, and the reason we often place it on a front-room shelf instead.

Frame houses and siding

On the wood-frame and sided houses common in Staten Island, Queens and parts of Brooklyn and the Bronx, mounting is the easy part: screws into the trim or through the siding into the framing behind, with the screw holes sealed against water. The work is in the power and the range. We check the chime transformer before promising a wired doorbell, and we measure the signal at the driveway, the back yard and the garage before deciding whether one Sync Module will do. Outdoor cameras go under eaves or overhangs where they can, out of direct rain, and pointed so that low winter sun does not blind them at the hour packages usually arrive.

For a business that needs continuous recording, many cameras, or footage that stays in the building, we install a wired system instead and say so in the quote. Rules and practical limits for business cameras in NYC

  • Licensed installer. The city's business portal says installing or maintaining security systems as a business requires a license from the New York State Department of State. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431. A business owner can mount their own Blink camera; a paid installer needs the license.
  • Audio is a separate question. New York's eavesdropping rules under Penal Law §250.00 turn on consent to recording conversations. In a shop, we leave Blink's audio off by default and only turn it on where the owner has thought it through.
  • Private spaces stay private. Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent. No cameras in restrooms or changing areas, ever.
  • Biometric signage. The City's rule under Local Law 3 of 2021 requires certain businesses that collect biometric identifier information from customers to post notices at entrances, but the disclosure provision generally does not apply to information collected through photographs or video recordings such as security cameras. Turning on face-recognition features is a different matter, and we do not set those up for businesses.
  • Sharing with the NYPD is voluntary. NYC 311 says businesses can register their security camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org. A 2019 state bill would have made registration mandatory, but it remained a bill. We can help you register if you choose to.
  • Storefront grilles and sightlines. A 2009 city law required roll-down security grilles to be at least 70 percent transparent by July 1, 2026. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the City Council voted to repeal that deadline, so a compliant gate would be required only when a gate is replaced for other reasons. Either way, we place cameras so a closed gate does not blind them.
  • Insurance papers. Many commercial landlords and managing agents want a certificate of insurance before a contractor works in the space. Tell us when booking and we send it before the visit.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a New York Front Door Before the Box Is Opened

We do not publish stories about a customer's home unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at a New York front door, whether it is a steel apartment door on a sixth-floor corridor or a wooden door at the top of a brownstone stoop.

We leave the Blink box closed. First we ask who owns the surface: the door, the frame, the wall beside it. Then we look at the door's swing and the closer, and measure its edge and the gap at the top. We open the Wi-Fi settings on a phone at the door and note the signal, then walk back toward the router counting walls and noting brick, plaster and steel. If there is a chime, we look for the transformer label. If the only bell is the building buzzer, we say so out loud, because it decides the power plan. We stand where the camera will be and look at what it will see: your doorway, or your neighbor's too, or the sidewalk. Only when that picture makes sense do we open the box, register the Sync Module and mount the first device. In a city of shared hallways and thick walls, that ten minutes is the difference between a doorbell that works for a year and one that is offline by the first cold snap.

Your Questions Answered

Blink Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Renters, landlords and the apartment door

Am I allowed to put a Blink doorbell on my own apartment door in New York City?

Usually yes, as far as state and city law go. Connextivity's review of New York apartment camera rules found no New York State or City law that prohibits a resident from putting a camera on their own apartment door or that dictates how it must be aimed. What can stop you is the lease and the building's policy, because the outside face of the door and the hallway around it are often treated as the building's. A no-drill bracket on the door is the setup least likely to run into a rule, while a camera screwed into the hallway wall is the one most likely to need written approval. We read the relevant clause with you before choosing the mount, and if the building says no, we set up a Blink Mini inside the apartment watching the door instead.

My landlord told me to take my Blink doorbell down. Can a landlord actually make me remove it?

It depends on what your lease and the building's rules say. A New York renter asked exactly this on JustAnswer after installing a Blink doorbell, and the answer given there was that a landlord generally cannot forcibly remove such a device without the tenant's consent unless it violates the lease or building policies. That came from a non-lawyer question-and-answer site and is not legal advice. A separate JustAnswer thread about a Brooklyn building said that if a tenant mounts a camera in a common area the landlord owns, without permission, the landlord has the right to remove it. So the location matters: a battery doorbell on your door with no holes is in a stronger position than a camera on the hallway wall. If you are in a dispute, talk to a tenant lawyer or a tenant organization. What we can do is move the setup to a spot your lease clearly allows.

Can my Blink camera's view of the hallway intrude on my neighbors' privacy?

It can, and that is the strongest reason a building will give for regulating it. One New York guide notes that management can regulate hallway-facing cameras that interfere with other tenants' privacy. A Justia answer to a co-op question in April 2026 put the line practically: video of a common area is generally lawful as long as the camera is not aimed into someone's apartment when their door is open. With Blink, we use the app's motion zones and the camera's angle to keep the view on your doorway and the stretch of hallway in front of it, not across into a neighbor's door. We also turn off audio in shared hallways, because whether passively recording hallway conversations counts as eavesdropping has never been cleanly settled, according to Connextivity. That combination is the one most buildings accept.

Is my landlord allowed to install cameras inside my apartment?

Not in private spaces without your consent. New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it a criminal offense to install a camera where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent, and the inside of your apartment is the clearest example of such a place. Landlords commonly put cameras in lobbies, hallways, laundry rooms and entrances, and New York tenants have pushed back hard when owners went further: in 2019, 6sqft reported tenants at the 700-unit Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville objecting to the owner's plan for facial-recognition entry, and City Council Member Carlina Rivera later sponsored a bill to restrict facial recognition in residential buildings. If you find a camera inside your unit that you did not agree to, that is a legal matter, not an installation one, and you should get advice. Your own Blink cameras inside your apartment are yours to place.

Doorbell power in New York buildings

My building only has a buzzer at each apartment. Can a wired Blink Video Doorbell take power from it?

No, and this catches many people. The Blink Video Doorbell needs a chime system supplying 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz, with a 40 VA maximum, according to Blink's support pages. A building intercom or buzzer is a different system, designed by the building's intercom maker, and wiring a Blink doorbell to it can stop the intercom working for you and possibly for others. Blink says that if the doorbell shows "Wired power not supported" during wiring, the chime wiring is not compatible and the doorbell will default to running in Event Response mode on batteries. In apartments we install the doorbell on batteries from the start, or use Blink's Wired Doorbell 2K+ on its plug-in adapter where an outlet is close enough, and leave the building's buzzer alone.

Our house has a doorbell chime but the Blink app says wired power is not supported. What is going on?

Usually the transformer feeding the chime is outside the range Blink needs, or the wiring between the button and the chime is not what the doorbell expects. Older houses often have transformers that supply less than 16 volts, which was plenty for a mechanical chime. We check the voltage at the button and at the transformer, look at the chime itself, and tell you the options. If the voltage is right, the problem is usually a connection we can fix. If it is not, the options are running the doorbell on batteries, having the transformer replaced by the appropriate trade, or switching to the Wired Doorbell 2K+ with its plug-in adapter. Blink notes that the adapter setup has no battery backup, so in an outage the doorbell goes dark until power returns.

Historic buildings and landmark blocks

My brownstone is in a historic district. Do I need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval for Blink cameras on the front?

Possibly, for anything on the exterior. The Commission's rules amendments, presented publicly in 2018, codified staff practice for installing objects and equipment on facades and areaway features and listed security cameras among them, and its proposed rules would bar many such objects from primary facades. Chapter 11 of the LPC Permit Guidebook includes a section on installing security cameras, which favors placement off the primary facade where possible and finishes that match the building, and notes that staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria. The Commission does pursue unpermitted work: a 2013 hearing calendar included an application to legalize cameras and an intercom installed without permits on a rowhouse at 52 West 76th Street. A Blink camera inside the vestibule looking out, or on the rear of the house, usually avoids the question. For anything on the street face, check with the Commission first, and we will place the camera to match what is approved.

Can a Blink Outdoor camera be mounted on brownstone or brick without damaging the facade?

Yes, if the fixings go in the right place. We drill into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick or stone, because mortar can be repointed and a cracked or spalled brownstone face cannot easily be repaired. Brownstone is a soft sandstone, so we drill slowly with a sharp masonry bit and use plastic or sleeve anchors sized for the light weight of a Blink Outdoor camera, never heavy wedge anchors. Where possible we use existing features, such as the underside of a stoop or a wooden door frame, so the stone is not touched at all. On a landmark block we confirm what the Commission allows before any hole goes in.

Signal, range and thick walls

Our prewar apartment has thick brick walls and the doorbell keeps disconnecting. Will Blink ever work reliably here?

Usually, once the Sync Module and the router are in the right places. Blink's guidance is to keep the Sync Module away from thick walls and materials like concrete, brick, stone or metal, and it notes that a second Sync Module can help cameras with weak signal. A doorbell vendor adds that walls, brick and metal doors both shorten range and drain batteries. In prewar buildings, where some walls are 12 to 18 inches of masonry, we often move the Sync Module to a spot within a few feet of the door on the inside, and if the Sync Module itself cannot hear the router from there, we add a Wi-Fi extender or a small wired access point near the front of the apartment. We test with the door closed, because a steel door shut in its frame is the worst case and the one that matters.

Does a battery doorbell on a heavy self-closing steel door wear out faster than one on a house?

It can, for two reasons. A steel door weakens the radio link, so the doorbell works harder and uses more power to stay connected, which a doorbell vendor notes increases battery drainage. And a self-closing door shuts with force every time, which tests the bracket and the battery contacts. We use clamp brackets that grip firmly, check that the door still closes and latches on its own with the bracket fitted, and set motion sensitivity and zones so the doorbell is not waking for every person who walks down the corridor. In a busy hallway, fewer false events is the single biggest battery saver.

The pieces of a Blink system

Which Blink devices need a Sync Module, and which work without one?

The Blink Mini 2K+ connects directly to Wi-Fi without a Sync Module. The Blink Outdoor 2K+ and the Blink Battery Doorbell 2K+ do need one, according to a renter's guide that summarizes Blink's product requirements. That shapes the plan. An apartment setup of one Mini watching the door from inside needs only Wi-Fi. A doorbell on the door plus an Outdoor camera on a balcony needs a Sync Module placed where both can reach it. Blink says its Sync Module (1st Gen), Sync Module 2 and Sync Module Core can communicate with cameras up to 100 feet away, though walls shorten that a great deal. We register the Sync Module first and place it before mounting any battery device.

Can Blink run on the 5G or cellular internet plan many New Yorkers use instead of cable?

Blink needs a home Wi-Fi network for setup and for the cameras to send clips, so the question is whether your plan gives you a home router. The NYC Council's internet access data found 12.8 percent of city households relying on a cellular data plan only and 24.5 percent without a cable, fiber or DSL subscription. If your service includes a home internet router, cellular-based or not, Blink can connect to its Wi-Fi like any other. If your only internet is the phone in your pocket, a camera system that depends on Wi-Fi is not a good fit, and we would rather tell you that before you buy than after. NYCHA residents may have access to the city's Big Apple Connect free home internet program, which the City says runs through 2028.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Blink Camera Installation in NYC

We could not capture Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask boxes or Bing and DuckDuckGo answers for this topic from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Blink and doorbell cameras generally, set against what Blink's own documents, New York City's rules and our field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers; they help with a rough idea, but they cannot tell you whether your apartment door has a chime or a buzzer.

"Any wired doorbell can be swapped for a Blink."

Quick answers often say you can replace any existing doorbell with a wired Blink in a few minutes. Blink's own support pages are narrower: the Video Doorbell needs a chime system providing 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz and no more than 40 VA.

In New York, the most common "doorbell" is a building buzzer, which HPD guidance on the Housing Maintenance Code describes as the minimum intercommunication system for post-1968 buildings with eight or more apartments. That is not a chime circuit, and connecting a Blink to it can interfere with the intercom.

When the wiring does not match, Blink shows "Wired power not supported" and the doorbell runs on batteries in Event Response mode. We measure first and choose battery, chime or plug-in adapter power from what we find.

"A Sync Module reaches 100 feet, so put it anywhere."

Blink does say its Sync Modules can communicate with cameras up to 100 feet away. The same support article tells owners to avoid thick walls and materials like concrete, brick, stone and metal, and suggests a second Sync Module for cameras with weak signal.

The 100-foot figure is an open-air figure. In a city where 54 percent of units are in buildings put up before 1947, and where prewar masonry walls can be over a foot thick, the effective range from a router at the back of a railroad apartment to a steel front door can be a fraction of that.

We place the Sync Module by measurement, not by the number on the box, and test every camera with doors closed before we leave.

"You can put a doorbell camera anywhere outside your door."

Many answers treat the space outside your door as yours. In a New York apartment building it usually is not. Connextivity notes that a camera on a hallway wall sits on a common area and typically needs building approval in a co-op or condo, and a JustAnswer reply in a Brooklyn case said a landlord can remove a camera installed in a common area without permission.

The same sources say no state or city law bars a camera on your own apartment door. The difference between the door and the wall beside it is the difference between a likely yes and a likely no.

So we default to no-drill brackets on the door itself, or a Mini inside, and only mount on hallway walls with written approval in hand.

"Professional Blink installation is available from Blink."

Some answers imply Blink or Amazon will send someone. NerdWallet's Blink review states it plainly: no professional install is available from Blink. The product is designed to be set up by the owner.

That works well in a house with good Wi-Fi and a modern chime. It works less well on a steel door in a prewar walk-up, a landmark brownstone stoop or a co-op with a strict board, which together describe a large share of New York homes.

Third-party installers fill the gap. In New York State, anyone installing security systems as a business needs a Department of State license, which is why our license number is on this page.

"Batteries last up to two years, so set it and forget it."

Battery claims in quick answers usually assume mild weather, modest activity and strong signal. A battery supplier's guidance lists cold weather, weak Wi-Fi and frequent alerts as the main things that cut battery life, and notes that alkaline cells lose significant capacity in cold weather while lithium cells hold steady across a wide temperature range.

A Blink doorbell on a north-facing stoop in a New York January, behind a steel door, in a corridor with dozens of neighbors walking past, is close to the worst case on all three counts.

We fit lithium cells in exposed devices, tighten motion zones so the camera does not wake for every passer-by, and improve signal where it is weak, which together make more difference than any single battery brand.

"Wired means it keeps working when the power goes out."

It is easy to assume a plugged-in camera is the safer choice. Blink says its Wired Doorbell 2K+ on the plug-in adapter has no battery backup and needs continuous, uninterrupted power.

Outages do happen here. NY1 reported 10,019 Con Edison customers without power on one evening during the June 2025 heat wave, with more than 12,000 out at one point, and noted that one customer can be a whole building. When the power drops, the router and modem usually go too, so even a battery camera cannot send clips.

Where that matters, we suggest a battery doorbell rather than a plug-in one, and a small battery backup unit for the router, so the system rides through short outages.

"Landmark rules don't apply to small cameras."

Quick answers rarely mention the Landmarks Preservation Commission. But its rules amendments specifically list security cameras among the equipment it regulates on facades and areaway features, and its Permit Guidebook has a section on installing them.

With 1,460 individual landmarks and many historic districts full of row houses, including Brooklyn Heights, the city's first, a camera on a stoop can be exactly the kind of facade change the Commission reviews. Its 2013 calendar included a case to legalize cameras already installed without permits on a West 76th Street rowhouse.

Small size does not exempt a device. We keep cameras off the primary facade where we can, and for anything on the street face of a landmark building we check with the Commission first.

FAQ

NYC Blink Camera Installation FAQ

Do I need my landlord's permission to put a Blink camera inside my New York apartment?
+

For a camera that sits inside your own apartment, on a shelf or a small mount, most leases do not require permission, and Blink's own renter guide notes that plug-in indoor cameras like the Mini are the easiest fit for rentals. What usually needs permission is anything that changes the door, the walls in a way the lease restricts, or the common areas outside. If your lease has an alterations clause, read it, and if it is unclear, a short email to the managing agent describing a no-drill setup usually settles it. Keep the reply on file.

Can Blink be installed in a rental without drilling a single hole?
+

Yes. A battery doorbell can sit on a no-drill clamp bracket that grips the top of the door, a Mini can stand on a shelf facing the door, and an Outdoor camera can go on a balcony railing clamp or adhesive mount. Some no-drill door brackets fit inward-opening doors only and have limits on door thickness and the gap at the top, so we measure your door before choosing one. We also check that a self-closing door still closes and latches on its own with the bracket fitted.

Which Blink camera is simplest for a studio or one-bedroom apartment?
+

The Blink Mini 2K+ is usually the simplest, because it plugs into an outlet and joins your Wi-Fi directly without a Sync Module. Set inside the apartment facing the door, it records whoever comes in and avoids most lease questions. If you want to see who is knocking before you open, a Battery Doorbell 2K+ on the door adds that, but it needs a Sync Module placed near the door, which matters in buildings with thick walls or a steel door.

How do I keep my security deposit safe when I install Blink cameras?
+

Use mounts that leave no marks, get written approval for anything that touches the door or walls, and photograph the area before and after installation. No-drill door brackets and shelf-mounted Minis leave nothing behind when removed. Where adhesive mounts are used on painted surfaces, we choose ones that release cleanly and show you how to remove them. When you move out, take the devices down carefully and keep the original brackets with the cameras.

When I move to a new apartment, can my Blink system come with me?
+

Yes. Blink devices are tied to your Blink account, not the building, so you take them down, pack them with their brackets and set them up again at the new address. The Sync Module needs to join the new Wi-Fi network, and every battery device placement has to be rethought for the new walls and door. We can take the system down at the old place and put it back up at the new one, and the written placement note we leave at hand-over makes that faster.

What does a Blink camera install cost in New York City?
+

It depends on the borough, how many devices you want, whether you already own them, the mounting surface and whether the Wi-Fi needs help. We publish every Blink line for each borough on our Blink price pages, with a calculator so you can add up your own job. Send your address, door photos and models and we will tell you which lines apply before you book.

How long does a typical Blink installation take?
+

A single Mini or one doorbell on a no-drill bracket with good Wi-Fi is a short visit. A brownstone with a doorbell, two outdoor cameras and a second Sync Module, or a house with a chime check and four outdoor cameras, takes considerably longer because of the measuring, masonry work and testing. Building rules can add time too, such as co-op work hours or a service elevator booking. We give you an expected visit length with the quote.

Is Blink a good choice for cameras across a whole apartment building?
+

Usually not for the building as a whole. Blink is designed for individual homes and low-traffic spots, recording clips on motion and relying on Wi-Fi and cloud storage. A lobby, mailroom or entrance that sees constant traffic is better served by a wired recorder system that records continuously and keeps footage in the building. Blink works well for a tenant's own door or a quiet storage room, and some owners combine the two.

What is the right way to set up a Blink camera outdoors on a New York house or stoop?
+

Start with the signal, not the bracket: confirm the Sync Module can reach the outdoor spot with good strength. Choose a spot sheltered from direct rain, under an eave or overhang where possible, at a height that shows faces rather than the tops of heads. On brick, fix into mortar joints; on a landmark block, check the Commission's rules first. Then set motion zones away from the sidewalk and neighbors' property and fit lithium cells for winter.

My Blink camera shows offline in the app. What should I check first?
+

Check that your router has power and internet, that the Sync Module's lights look normal, and that the camera's batteries are fresh. If you recently changed your router or Wi-Fi password, the Sync Module and any Mini cameras need to join the new network. If only one camera is offline, especially one behind a steel door or masonry wall, weak signal is the usual cause, and the fix is moving the Sync Module, adding a second one or improving the Wi-Fi near that camera.

Will Blink batteries hold up on a stoop through a New York winter?
+

They will last noticeably less than in mild weather. Cold, weak Wi-Fi and frequent motion alerts all shorten battery life, and alkaline cells lose a lot of capacity in the cold while lithium cells hold steady across a wide temperature range. For exposed outdoor devices we use lithium cells, keep motion zones tight so the camera wakes less often, and make sure the signal is strong so the camera is not straining to stay connected.

Can I register my Blink camera with the NYPD?
+

NYC 311 says you can register your security camera locations with the NYPD through NewYorkCityConnect.org, so police know a camera may have footage if something happens nearby. Registration is voluntary. A state bill once proposed making it mandatory for cameras facing public spaces, but it remained a bill. If you choose to register, we can show you how during the hand-over.
