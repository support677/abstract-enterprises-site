The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Condo Buildings Need Their Own Camera Plan

A Manhattan condominium is rarely a simple box with a front door. A typical tower has a lobby with a staffed desk, a mail and package room behind it, a service entrance on the side street where deliveries, contractors and building staff come and go, a freight elevator, a cellar full of storage cages and bike racks, a trash and compactor room, often a garage ramp, and one or more amenity floors with a gym, a lounge, a children's playroom or a roof terrace. Each of those spaces is a different camera problem, and each is shared by every owner. New York's Condominium Act lists halls, lobbies, entrances, basements, recreational facilities, parking areas and storage spaces among a condo's common elements (Real Property Law §339-e), so in almost every Manhattan building those spaces, and the cameras that watch them, are governed by the board of managers rather than by any one owner.

The people inside the building change constantly, too. The NYC Comptroller's March 2024 analysis of the city's homeowner market found that 21 percent of Manhattan condo units are owned by LLCs (NYC Comptroller, Spotlight: New York City's Homeowner Housing Market). An LLC-owned unit may be rented out, used a few weeks a year, or sit empty between sales, and the owner may live in another country. When something goes wrong in a building like that, the desk staff and the managing agent are often the only people who saw the lobby that day, and the camera recorder is the only reliable memory the condominium has. A board that treats its condo security camera system as building infrastructure, with a policy, a budget line and a named person responsible, gets far more use from it than a board that bought a kit years ago and forgot the password.

Packages are the most common reason Manhattan boards call. CBS New York reported on May 22, 2018 that a thief carrying a garbage bag had taken packages from the lobby of a Jones Street building in Greenwich Village, where deliveries were often left unattended; residents said parcels had been disappearing for years, and the building's super planned a secure delivery room (CBS New York). That story is a small walk-up, but the pattern scales up: in a doorman tower the risk shifts from the open lobby to the package room door, the shelf where a porter stages oversized boxes, and the moment a courier is waved through the service entrance. We design Manhattan condo camera layouts around those hand-off points, because a camera that only shows the front sidewalk rarely answers the question an owner asks the next morning.

Manhattan also adds rules that most of the city doesn't deal with as often. A large share of the borough's condos sit in landmarked buildings or historic districts, where the street facade is protected. Many towers spend months behind sidewalk sheds during facade work, which can blind an entrance camera. Newer condos came out of office conversions with camera cabling left over from the commercial tenants. And nearly every managing agent has vendor rules: an insurance certificate after award, set work hours, freight elevator bookings and a superintendent who has to sign off before anyone touches the ceiling. Our Manhattan condo camera work starts from those constraints instead of discovering them on installation day.

Manhattan facts that shape a condo camera project

  • Protected buildings: the Landmarks Preservation Commission had designated 38,550 buildings and sites citywide by the end of Fiscal 2026, including 1,473 individual landmarks and 159 historic districts and extensions (Mayor's Management Report, Fiscal 2026). Manhattan is where many of those districts are, from Tribeca and SoHo to the Upper West Side.
  • Facade camera limits: LPC staff can approve security cameras when, among other criteria, no more than two cameras are installed per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings and no more than three on large residential buildings (LPC Permit Guidebook, chapter 11).
  • Sidewalk sheds: the Mayor's Office said on October 19, 2026 that the city had reduced the number of sidewalk sheds by approximately 1,200 since January (NYC Mayor's Office), which still leaves many entrances working under a shed.
  • Conversions: of the homes created by turning offices, hotels and industrial space into housing, 317 were condos due on the market in 2026, according to Corcoran Sunshine figures (Bisnow, Dec. 17, 2025).
  • New development: about 3,000 new-development units were on the Manhattan market in the third quarter of 2026, and 240 went into contract versus 331 a year earlier (CRE Daily, Oct. 7, 2026), so many buildings will move from sponsor control to an owner-run board with a camera system they did not choose.
  • Absentee ownership: 21 percent of Manhattan condo units are LLC-owned, per the Comptroller analysis cited above.

This page covers cameras only. If your board is also weighing intercom, fob, elevator or amenity access work, those systems are on our Manhattan condo security systems page, and if the lobby panel itself is the problem, our condo intercom replacement guide walks a board through that decision. New condo camera systems

Building Specialists

Condo Security Cameras for Manhattan's Architecture

The same four cameras cost different amounts of labor in different Manhattan buildings because the walls, ceilings and rules are different. Before a board compares two proposals, it helps to know which of these building types it lives in, because the type predicts where cable can travel, how the cameras mount, and what the managing agent will ask for.

Glass and concrete towers

Post-2000 towers in Hudson Yards, Midtown, the Financial District and along the West Side usually have finished drywall ceilings below a concrete slab, a dedicated low-voltage riser and an equipment room. Cable travels well through the riser; the work is in each floor's finished ceiling and in getting a slot on the freight elevator.

Prewar masonry conversions

Condos carved out of older apartment houses on the Upper West Side, Upper East Side and in Harlem have plaster walls, thick masonry and crowded utility chases. We favor existing chases, the cellar and stair bulkheads, and we use surface raceway only where the board accepts it in writing.

Cast-iron and loft buildings

SoHo and Tribeca loft condos often sit inside historic districts with cast-iron fronts. LPC criteria forbid attaching equipment through cast iron or decorative surfaces, so street-side cameras move to mortar joints, plain masonry, a side wall or the inside of the vestibule.

Office-to-condo conversions

Converted office buildings around Water Street, Wall Street and Midtown South sometimes keep raised floors, drop ceilings and leftover Category cable from the commercial tenants. Leftover cable is tested before it is trusted; untested runs from a demolished office plan rarely go where a residential layout needs them.

Boutique walk-up condos

Small condos of five to twenty units, common in the Village, the Lower East Side and Chelsea, often have no doorman and no package room. A video doorbell at the street door, a vestibule camera and one camera on the mail area do most of the work.

Mixed-use condos

Buildings with a retail or office condo unit on the ground floor share a facade, sometimes a cellar, and sometimes a service corridor with the residences. The declaration decides who owns those spaces, and the camera layout follows it.

Mounting and wiring details by building type

In concrete towers the ceilings are usually suspended or furred drywall, so corridor cameras either drop through an access panel or mount on a low-profile box with the cable fed from above. Where a corridor has no access, a camera can sit at the elevator lobby looking down the hall, which covers more doors with less ceiling work. Equipment rooms in these buildings often already hold the intercom head end and the building network; we ask for a dedicated, labeled shelf or rack space and a battery backup rather than tucking a recorder on top of someone else's equipment.

Prewar buildings reward patience. The cellar is almost always the easiest highway, with risers going up through pipe chases, dumbwaiter shafts that were closed off decades ago, or the back of a stair. Plaster ceilings in public halls are often decorative, so a corridor camera may go on a wall bracket above the door frame instead of in the ceiling. When concealed paths are not available, we price exposed raceway painted to match as a separate line, so the board can see exactly what the concealed option costs before it chooses.

Loft buildings bring tall ceilings, exposed beams and wide vestibules. A camera at twelve or fourteen feet sees a lot of floor but very little face, so entrance cameras in lofts usually come down to door height on a wall mount, with a second, wider camera higher up for context. For office conversions, we usually find the old security riser and the old comms room first. Whether those spaces can be reused depends on who owns them under the condo declaration, which is a question for the managing agent before it is a question for us.

Commercial Condo Security Cameras

Business Condo Security Cameras in Manhattan

Commercial condo units are common in Manhattan: a restaurant or bank branch owning the ground floor of a residential tower, a medical practice owning a suite in a Midtown building, or an office condo in a converted loft. Commercial condo security cameras raise one question residential boards rarely face, which is whose system covers which space. The commercial owner usually controls cameras inside its own unit and storefront; the residential board controls the lobby and shared corridors; and the shared service corridor, trash room or loading dock belongs to whichever party the declaration says, or to both.

We see the same business corridors again and again. Along Broadway and lower Fifth Avenue, ground-floor retail condos sit under residential floors in loft buildings. Around Madison and Lexington Avenues on the Upper East Side, medical and professional office condos share entrances with residences. In the Financial District, conversions put residential condos above retail and fitness tenants. Along Eighth and Ninth Avenues in Chelsea and Hell's Kitchen, restaurant condos share cellars and trash rooms with the building. In Harlem, new condominiums along Frederick Douglass Boulevard and 125th Street often have a commercial unit at street level. Each of those arrangements changes where the residential system should stop.

Our approach for mixed-use condos is to give each owner its own recorder for its own space, and to share only what both sides agree to share in writing. A shared service corridor camera can be recorded on the residential system and made viewable to the commercial owner through a limited account, or the other way around. That keeps a restaurant's kitchen out of the residential board's footage and keeps residents' lobby comings and goings out of a tenant's hands, while still letting both parties see who propped open the shared back door.

Compliance points that come up in commercial condos

  • Staff areas: New York's Labor Law §203-c bars video recording of employees in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas, as summarized by FirstService Residential (FirstService Residential, Sept. 23, 2024). That applies to a condo's porters and doormen as much as to a restaurant's kitchen staff.
  • Facade equipment: commercial storefronts in historic districts fall under the same LPC criteria as the residential entrance next door, and both count toward the frontage limit on the building.
  • Insurance and access: commercial owners may have their own insurer's requirements for recorded areas; we follow whichever written requirements each owner gives us and do not substitute our own.
  • Licensing: the installer should hold a New York State license covering security and video surveillance installation. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Condo Security Cameras in Manhattan: Questions Answered

These are the questions Manhattan board members, resident managers and managing agents ask us most during walkthroughs. Each answer reflects how we actually handle the situation; where the answer depends on your declaration, bylaws or a lawyer's view, we say so.

The lobby, the desk and the street

Our concierge desk monitor shows sixteen tiny tiles. How should it be laid out?

Give the desk a dedicated layout with the four or five views staff act on, shown large: the street door, the vestibule, the package room door and the service entrance, plus the garage gate if there is one. Everything else stays on the recorder and on a second page the doorman can flip to. Sixteen thumbnails on a 24-inch screen make every face the size of a fingernail, and in practice staff stop looking. We set the main layout so it returns automatically after someone browses other cameras, and we label each tile with a plain name such as "Service door, W 54th" rather than "CH07."

Landmarked fronts: the frontage count comes first

Landmark status rarely rules cameras out, but it caps them. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no other option, and when it must, it should be in a discreet location, as small as possible and finished to blend in. LPC staff can approve security cameras when they meet the published criteria, including no more than three cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on large residential buildings and two on small ones, attachment at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and never through cast iron or decorative surfaces (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11). We start with positions inside the vestibule or on a return wall, then use facade positions only where nothing else reaches.

A sidewalk shed has blocked our entrance camera for over a year. What can we do?

Move the view under the shed rather than fighting it. A camera mounted on the underside of the shed's deck, or inside the vestibule looking out through the door glass, keeps the entrance covered while the facade work drags on. The shed belongs to the facade contractor, so the managing agent has to approve anything attached to it, and the camera has to come down when the shed does. Inside-the-vestibule positions survive the shed's removal, which is why we usually recommend them.

Should the service entrance be on the same recorder as the lobby?

Yes, in nearly every building. The service door is where package couriers, food deliveries, contractors and staff enter, and incidents often cross between the two doors: someone comes in through the service side and leaves through the lobby. Keeping both on one recorder means a single timeline and one export when the managing agent needs to show what happened. The exception is a mixed-use building where the service corridor belongs to a commercial unit; then the camera goes on whichever system the declaration assigns that space to.

Garages, cellars and the roof

Garages leased to a parking operator

The garage lease decides more than the camera plan does. When the condominium leases its garage to a parking operator, the lease usually decides who installs and maintains equipment inside it, and many operators already run their own cameras at the cashier booth and gate. The condo still has a strong interest in the pedestrian door from the garage into the building and the elevator lobby at garage level, because that door is a way into the residences. We normally put the condo's cameras on that door and lobby, coordinate with the operator for anything inside the drive aisles, and avoid two systems pointing at the same gate.

Cellar aisles and storage cages

The usual answer is a wide-angle turret at one end of the aisle, aimed down its length, with a second camera at the cellar door. Cage aisles are long, narrow and badly lit, so a camera that handles low light matters more than resolution. We avoid aiming into individual cages; the goal is to see who walked down the aisle and when, not to inventory an owner's belongings. If the cellar also holds the bike room, a camera on the bike room door usually answers more questions than one inside it.

Roof terraces and the windows across the street

We point roof terrace cameras down and inward, at the terrace door, the furniture area and the parapet line, and use the camera's privacy masking to black out any part of the frame that shows a neighboring building's windows. In Manhattan, a roof camera aimed level will see into a dozen apartments within half a block, which helps nobody and invites complaints. We confirm the final view with the resident manager on the day of installation and save a screenshot of the masked frame for the board's file.

Older systems, staff and outages

Our building was converted from offices. Can the condo reuse the old office camera cabling?

Only the runs that test good and go somewhere useful. Office tenants wired cameras for open floors and reception desks; a residential layout needs cameras at corridors, mail rooms and residential entrances, which rarely line up with the old drops. We trace and test each leftover run, keep the ones that land near a position on the new plan, and abandon the rest. Ownership matters too: if the old cable runs through space that now belongs to a commercial unit, the managing agent should confirm the condo can use it.

Do we need to involve our building staff's union before adding a camera at the desk?

That is a question for the board's labor counsel, not for a camera installer, and we would not try to answer it for you. What we can tell you is what the law says about places cameras cannot go: video recording of employees in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms is barred, per FirstService Residential's summary of Labor Law §203-c. Habitat reported a Midtown East condo with cameras above the doorman desk and in the elevators, and the questions that raised among owners and staff (Habitat, Aug. 28, 2023). We aim desk cameras at the counter and the people approaching it, not at a staff member's screen.

Power outages and the recorder

With a battery backup, the recorder and network switch keep running through a short outage and shut down cleanly in a longer one, so the drive isn't corrupted. Without one, everything stops, and a recorder that loses power in the middle of writing can lose recent footage or damage its drive. We size the backup to the recorder, switch and cameras it powers, and we put the recorder on the building's emergency circuit only if one exists and the building's electrician has already provided an outlet; we do not do electrical work.

How do you keep installation from clashing with move-ins and doorman shifts?

We book the freight elevator through the managing agent, work inside the building's permitted contractor hours, and stage cable and ladders in a space the super assigns. Lobby work is scheduled for the quietest part of the day the building suggests, and anything at the desk is done in short stretches so the doorman is never without a working door view. Move-ins and large deliveries take priority on the freight car; we plan around them, not the other way round.

Equipment

Types of Condo Security Cameras We Install in Manhattan

Equipment is the easiest part of the decision to over-think. For most Manhattan condos, the difference between a camera that works and one that doesn't is placement, lighting and cable, not a spec sheet. These are the categories we install, and where each one earns its place in a Manhattan building.

Turret cameras

Our default for lobbies, corridors and cellar aisles. No dome bubble to scratch or reflect light, an infrared or white-light option for dark spaces, and a mount small enough for a plaster ceiling.

Vandal-resistant domes

For garage ceilings, service corridors and anywhere a cart or ladder might strike the camera. Impact-rated housings take a knock and keep recording.

Bullet cameras

For long views: a garage ramp, a rear court, a side-street service door seen from across the sidewalk. Their shape is visible, which is part of the point at a back door.

Multi-lens and panoramic cameras

One housing, several sensors. Useful at the center of a large garage level or a wide plaza where three fixed cameras would otherwise be needed.

PTZ cameras

Pan-tilt-zoom units for plazas, courtyards and large roof decks, paired with fixed cameras at every door so a PTZ pointing the wrong way never leaves an entrance uncovered.

Elevator cab cameras

Compact corner cameras for the cab with a wireless link to the hoistway. Placement keeps the floor buttons and the door in view.

Video doorbells for street doors

For boutique condos without a doorman, a wired video doorbell at the street door records every visitor and lets owners answer from their phones.

Network video recorders

Sized by camera count, resolution and the board's retention period, rack-mounted in larger buildings and on a shelf in smaller ones, always on battery backup.

PoE switches and cabling

One network cable per camera carries power and video. Every run is labeled at both ends and recorded on the camera map we hand over.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Cameras in Manhattan

Board members increasingly start their research with a chatbot or an AI summary at the top of a search page. Those answers are written for an average home in an average town, and they tend to repeat the same handful of claims. Here are seven we hear repeated in Manhattan board meetings, and what actually happens in a Manhattan condominium.

"You can mount cameras wherever you need them on the outside of the building."

A generic answer treats the facade like a suburban garage wall. In Manhattan, a building that is an individual landmark or sits in a historic district is regulated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which designated 38,550 buildings and sites citywide by the end of Fiscal 2026, including 159 historic districts and extensions.

LPC's guidebook limits camera counts per 25 feet of frontage, asks that equipment be as small as possible and finished to blend in, requires concealed conduit where conduit is needed, and rules out attaching through cast iron or decorative surfaces. A permit application needs manufacturer cut sheets for the cameras.

What that means in practice: we plan facade cameras first, keep their count low, and move as much coverage as possible inside the vestibule. Boards in protected buildings should expect the exterior part of a proposal to be the slowest part to approve.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for a high-rise."

Wireless sounds simpler because no cable is pulled. In a Manhattan tower, concrete slabs, steel, elevator shafts and hundreds of residents' Wi-Fi networks all compete with a wireless camera's signal, and battery cameras need someone to recharge them.

A wired camera on a network cable gets steady power and a steady connection, and it records to the condominium's own recorder rather than to a phone app account. That is the reliability a board needs when a footage request comes in three weeks after an incident.

We use wireless only for short point-to-point links where cable cannot go: the elevator cab to the hoistway, or a bridge across a courtyard. The cameras themselves stay wired.

"Put a camera on every floor and you're covered."

A forty-story tower with a camera on every corridor sounds thorough and costs a great deal, but most corridor cameras in residential towers record almost nothing useful. Incidents cluster at the doors into the building, the package room, the garage and the amenity floors.

Corridor cameras also bring privacy questions every time a unit door opens. FirstService Residential's guidance is that hallway cameras must be placed to avoid capturing footage of the inside of any residential unit, which limits where they can point on a tight corridor.

We usually recommend full coverage of entrances, the desk, packages, service areas, garage and amenities first, then corridor cameras at elevator landings on floors with a specific history of problems.

"Cloud cameras mean you don't need a recorder."

Cloud recording works well for a single apartment. For a condominium, it means a monthly fee per camera, a heavy upload on the building's internet connection, and footage stored under an account that someone has to own and pass on when the board changes.

An on-site recorder keeps the condominium's footage in the building, under accounts registered to the association, with retention set by the board's own policy. Remote viewing still works for the managing agent and named board members.

If a board wants an off-site copy for one or two critical views, we can add that, but the recorder remains the system of record.

"Cameras will stop package theft."

Cameras help identify who took a package and often deter a casual thief. They do not stop a determined one. In the Jones Street case CBS New York reported, a resident recorded the thief, and the lasting fix the super planned was a secure delivery room, not another camera.

The best results come from pairing cameras with process: packages go into a locked room or locker, the desk logs who collects them, and the camera covers the door and the hand-off. That combination answers "who took it" in minutes instead of hours.

Package handling procedures belong to the managing agent and the desk staff. Our part is making sure the cameras show the room's door, the shelf and the person signing for a box, clearly enough to act on.

"Turn on the microphones; recording audio makes the system more useful."

Many cameras have built-in microphones, and some AI answers suggest enabling them. New York's eavesdropping law treats mechanical overhearing of a conversation without the consent of at least one party as a crime (Penal Law §250.00, §250.05).

A lobby microphone records residents, guests and staff who never agreed to it. Habitat's 2023 report on a Midtown East condo described exactly that worry among owners.

We install condo common-area cameras with audio recording switched off and turn it on only if the board's counsel approves it in writing. We are not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice.

"A camera system costs about the same wherever you are in New York City."

Cost guides often give a single national range. Our own published four-camera prices differ by borough: $950 in Queens and the Bronx, $999 on Staten Island and $1,099 in Manhattan, matching our borough price pages.

A camera that takes an hour to mount in a garage can take most of a morning in a finished lobby.

Lighting, glass and weather in Manhattan buildings

Manhattan lobbies are often lit for looks rather than for cameras: dark stone, low warm light and a wall of glass facing a bright street. A camera at the back of that lobby, aimed at the door, sees silhouettes against the daylight. Cameras with strong wide dynamic range help, but the real fix is placement: an entrance camera inside the vestibule, aimed across the door rather than straight out at the street, sees faces lit from the side. At night the problem reverses, and the glass reflects the lobby's own lights back into the lens, so we angle cameras slightly off the glass and avoid infrared pointing at it.

Outdoors, roof terraces and setbacks catch wind and driving rain, and garage ramps collect road salt in winter. Outdoor cameras get weather-rated housings and stainless or coated mounts, and garage cameras sit high enough on the wall to stay clear of truck mirrors. Where a camera faces west over the Hudson or east over the river, late-day sun can wash out the image for an hour; we check the compass direction of every outdoor view and choose positions that keep the low sun out of the frame where we can.

Privacy and the Law

Where Manhattan Condo Cameras May Point, and Where They Must Not

Privacy is the topic that stalls more condo camera votes than price. Owners who agree a lobby needs a camera still worry about a lens that sees into their foyer when the door opens, or a microphone that picks up a conversation at the mailboxes. The sources below are what New York law and New York building professionals actually say. We are installers, not attorneys, and the board's own counsel should review any policy before it is adopted; nothing here is legal advice.

Common areas are not private in the legal sense

An attorney answering a condo camera question for CooperatorNews wrote that "New York does not recognize a common law right of privacy in public or common areas," while also noting that a camera which "peers into a neighboring unit" may interfere with privacy, and that "the law recognizes the interior of a neighbor's unit as subject to considerable privacy safeguards" (CooperatorNews, Q&A: Camera Placement). That is the line we design to: lobbies, corridors, garages and amenity entrances can be watched; the inside of a home cannot.

Rooms where cameras never go

New York's unlawful surveillance statute makes it a felony to use a device to view or record a person, for no legitimate purpose and without their knowledge or consent, "in a bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" (Penal Law §250.45). Staff locker rooms and changing areas fall under Labor Law §203-c, as FirstService Residential summarizes it. In a Manhattan condo those rooms include the porters' locker room, the staff bathroom off the service corridor, the gym's changing rooms and the pool showers. We will not mount a camera in any of them, even when asked, and we check that no camera outside them can see in through a door.

Hallways and unit doors

FirstService Residential's guidance for New York condos and co-ops is that hallway cameras "must be carefully placed to avoid capturing footage that shows the inside of any residential unit." On a tight Manhattan corridor that usually means mounting at the elevator landing and looking along the hall, rather than across from a door, and applying a privacy mask over each doorway so an open door shows a black rectangle. We check every corridor view with a door propped open before the installer leaves the floor.

The board's authority has limits too

A board's power to install cameras comes from the declaration and bylaws. Andrew Lieb, the attorney quoted in Habitat's 2023 piece on a Midtown East condo's cameras, put it directly: "A house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable." Boards should have counsel confirm that the camera plan, and the footage policy that goes with it, sit within what their documents allow before the contract is signed.

Unit owners' own cameras

Owners increasingly want video doorbells or small cameras at their own doors. Habitat's February 2023 article on video doorbells in co-ops and condos quoted Manhattan attorney Debra Guzov: "you can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways" (Habitat, Feb. 20, 2023). The CooperatorNews Q&A likewise said condos "typically prohibit unit owners from unilaterally altering" common areas and that "a request for board consent is advisable." Many boards solve this with a written owner-camera policy: allowed models, mounting rules, no audio, and a view limited to the owner's own doorway.

Footage Policy

Writing a Footage Policy Your Desk Staff Can Follow

The recorder will do whatever it is told. The policy decides what it is told. A Manhattan condo with a staffed desk has more people near the system than most buildings, across three shifts, so the policy needs to be short enough that a relief doorman on a Sunday overnight can follow it. These are the decisions we ask boards to make before we configure the accounts.

  • Retention: how many days the recorder keeps before overwriting. FirstService Residential reports footage is "generally stored for 30 to 90 days depending on the building size." We size drives to whatever the board picks.
  • Live viewing: who may watch cameras live. Usually the desk, the super or resident manager and the managing agent; sometimes named board officers.
  • Playback and export: who may search recordings and save a clip. Usually fewer people than can view live, often just the managing agent and resident manager.
  • Request log: FirstService recommends that buildings "maintain a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage." We set up the recorder's own user log and can give the agent a simple request form.
  • Owner requests: the same source advises that "residents should not have access to footage or copies of footage without prior approval from the board and legal counsel." The policy should say who decides and how quickly.
  • Outside requests: who handles a request from police or an insurer. That is the managing agent's call under the policy, with counsel where needed.
  • Turnover: what happens to accounts when a board member, agent or staff member leaves. Accounts are named, so removing one person doesn't change everyone's password.

Once the board answers those questions, we build the accounts to match: a desk account that views live and plays back but cannot export, an agent account that can export and manage users, and board accounts with whatever rights the policy grants. We also turn on the recorder's audit log so the agent can see who exported what, and when. None of this costs extra; it is part of setting the system up properly.

Telling owners and residents

Owners hear about a camera project in one of two ways: from the board before installation, or from a neighbor after a camera appears outside their door. The first is better. We give the managing agent a plain-language summary for circulation: which spaces will have cameras, that audio is off, who can see recordings, how long they are kept, and when work will happen on each floor. Owners who rent their units can forward it to tenants. Signage at entrances is a common practice in New York buildings and is the board's choice; we can mount signs the board supplies.

After the Sponsor Leaves

Taking Over a Sponsor-Installed Camera System

Many Manhattan condos were built by sponsors who installed a camera system as part of the original build-out, chose the equipment, and in some cases kept the administrator credentials with their own property team. When the owners elect a board and the sponsor steps back, the new board can find it has cameras on the ceiling and no one in the building who can change a setting. With about 3,000 new-development units on the Manhattan market in the third quarter of 2026, according to CRE Daily, this is a situation many boards will face in the next few years.

Our takeover visit has three goals. The first is control: identify the recorder and every camera, and either obtain the administrator account through the managing agent or reset it where the manufacturer's procedure allows, then register new named accounts to the association. The second is inventory: a camera map with each position, model, cable path and what it currently sees, so the board knows what it inherited. The third is a gap list: spaces the sponsor never covered, cameras pointed at nothing useful, drives near capacity, and any remote-access setting left wide open.

From there the board can decide whether to keep, extend or replace. Many sponsor systems are perfectly serviceable and only need better aiming, a desk layout and a larger drive. Some were built around consumer cameras that do not suit a building of that size. We tell the board which situation it is in, with prices for each path, and the board decides when it is ready. If the sponsor still controls the board, we work for whoever signs the contract, and changes to a system the sponsor installed are the sponsor-controlled board's decision.

Board Checklist

Before the Board of Managers Votes on Cameras

A short list of things we would want settled if we sat on a Manhattan condo board. None of them is complicated, and each one prevents a common argument later.

  1. Read the declaration and bylaws with counsel to confirm the board can install cameras in each space on the plan and adopt a footage policy.
  2. Know who owns each space. Garages leased to operators, cellar areas assigned to commercial units and roof areas assigned to penthouse owners change where the condo's cameras can go.

Network Safety

Keeping the Condo's Camera Network Separate and Locked Down

A camera recorder is a computer on the building's network, and a careless one can be reached from the internet. In a Manhattan tower the building network often carries the intercom, access control, the gym equipment and the management office as well, which makes separation matter. Our standard setup keeps cameras on their own switch and their own network segment, with the recorder as the only device that talks to the outside world, and only through the manufacturer's secure remote-viewing service or a connection the building's IT provider manages.

We change every default password, disable features the building does not use, set the recorder's clock from a reliable time source so timestamps hold up when a clip is exported, and record firmware versions on the handover sheet. Residents' Wi-Fi never shares a network with the cameras. If the condo has an IT provider, we work to their network rules; if it doesn't, we leave a written record of how the camera network is set up so the next vendor is not starting from scratch.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check First in a Manhattan Condo

Walk into a Manhattan condo lobby with a camera installer and you'll notice we look up before we look at the cameras. The first question is where the cable can travel: is there an access panel above the desk, a soffit along the mailroom wall, a riser closet on every floor, or nothing but finished plaster? The answer to that question sets the price more than any camera choice. Next we look at the desk monitor and ask the doorman which view he actually uses. The answer is almost always the street door and the package room, and it is often the one view that is too small or badly lit.

Then we go downstairs. Cellars tell you how a building really runs: where deliveries are staged, which door the porters prop open for the trash, whether the bike room lock works. In towers with garages we check where the pedestrian door from the garage lands, because that door is frequently the least watched way into the residential floors. On the roof or amenity floors, we stand where a camera would go and look out. If we can see into a neighbor's windows, so can the camera, and the layout changes. Finally we open the recorder, if there is one, and check three things: whether anyone can log in, whether the drive is actually recording, and how many days of footage it really holds. More often than boards expect, one of the three fails.
