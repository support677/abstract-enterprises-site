The Neighborhood

Why Columbia Street Waterfront Properties Need Alarm Installation

The Columbia Street Waterfront District is small and it is easy to overlook. Wikipedia describes it as about twenty-two blocks on the Upper New York Bay, west of the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway, running from Atlantic Avenue down to the entrance of the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel, between Cobble Hill and Red Hook, and part of Brooklyn Community Board 6. It became a separate place in 1957, when the new expressway cut Columbia Street off from Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill. For an alarm, that history matters in one practical way: the district is a pocket, reached from the rest of Brooklyn across a handful of streets that bridge the trench, and it is quieter at night than the brownstone blocks on the other side. Quiet blocks are pleasant to live on and they are also blocks where a person working at a back door is unlikely to be noticed.

The building stock is unusually mixed for so few blocks. There are older brick row houses and walk-ups on streets like Union, Warren, Kane and Degraw, some with storefronts below. There are the townhouse condominiums of the urban renewal years; 6sqft records that Columbia Terrace opened its first phase on President Street in 1984 with 51 condo units, and that three later phases added 129 apartments on President and Carroll Streets in seventeen low-rise buildings. There are factory conversions: the former Tiffany factory on Tiffany Place became condos in 1986, and 25 Carroll Street, once a pasta factory, became seventeen loft condos in 2010. And there are newer buildings, from Columbia Commons, with 42 market-rate condos and 94 income-restricted rentals, to the small new apartment buildings on Summit Street. Each of these needs a different alarm, and the differences are what most of this page is about.

What decided the shape of this page, though, is the record of how burglars have actually got into homes here. When we went through the local news, the break-ins reported in the district were not the front-door kick-ins that alarm advertising is built around. In October 2014, DNAinfo reported, burglars forced a trapdoor that led into a bedroom from an alley off Tiffany Place between Kane and Degraw Streets, and police said the hinges had been unscrewed; the family had been out to dinner for less than two hours. In February 2014, a home on Union Street was entered by what police called a possible fire-escape burglary, sometime between noon and 7:30 p.m. In April 2014, on Warren Street between Hicks and Columbia, a resident came home after three days to find a bedroom door and a living room window open and no sign of forced entry. In March 2016, two men forced the front doors of a house under renovation on Woodhull Street at about six in the morning and were loading new appliances into a stolen minivan. And in February 2019, Gothamist reported, someone walked through the unlocked front door of 259 Columbia Street and carried off chairs, a grill, a blender and a box of toilet paper.

Those reports are several years old, and we use them for what they show about openings, not to claim anything about crime today. Burglary in the 76th Precinct, which covers the district along with Carroll Gardens, Red Hook, Cobble Hill and part of Gowanus, is far below where it was. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the week of November 2 to 8, 2026 shows 81 burglaries in the precinct so far this year against 92 at the same point in 2025, a fall of 12 percent, and 102 in all of 2025 against 735 in 1990. So we will not tell you the district is dangerous. We will tell you that the ways in here are mostly at the back, the side and above, that several of them involve something left open or unlocked, and that a well-planned alarm deals with exactly those situations: it reports a door lifted off its hinges, it refuses to arm with a window open, and it lets you know when a shared door has been left wide.

Six facts about the district that shape an alarm here

  • Burglary in the 76th Precinct is down, with short-term swings. 81 reported in 2026 through November 8, against 92 a year earlier (−12.0%); but 10 in the last 28 days against 7, and 4 in the report week against 2 (NYPD CompStat, week of November 2–8, 2026).
  • The long-run fall is steep. The same report counts 735 burglaries in the precinct in all of 1990, 203 in 2001 and 102 in 2025, a drop of 86.1 percent since 1990, and a 33-year change of −77.9 percent in the year-to-date figure.
  • It has spiked before. Patch reported 132 burglaries in the precinct in 2015, a ten-year high, up from 96 in 2014, before falling about 28 percent in the first five months of 2016. A low year is not a promise about the next one.
  • A small, cut-off district. About twenty-two blocks west of the expressway, separated from Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill since 1957, according to Wikipedia. After a 1975 sewer accident, thirty-three buildings were demolished, and urban renewal was finished by 1984, which is why so much of the housing is either very old or from the 1980s onward.
  • Hundreds of homes in a few complexes. Columbia Terrace alone accounts for 180 condos and apartments across its four phases, and Columbia Commons adds 136 homes, according to 6sqft. Townhouse condos and mid-size buildings mean boards, shared doors and common elements on many jobs.
  • Burglary is rare by national comparison. CrimeGrade gives the district an A for burglary, at about 1.1 reported burglaries per 1,000 residents a year against a US rate of 2.4, and notes from New York-wide NYPD data that burglaries most often happen between midnight and 6 a.m.

A Panel That Will Not Arm, a Rear Door That Always Reads Open or a Shop Alarm That Calls Nobody: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Columbia Street Waterfront

A lot of alarm calls from the district are not for new systems at all. The townhouse condos from the 1980s and early 1990s were often fitted with hardwired alarms when they were new, and many of those panels have been through two or three owners since. The common faults are predictable: a dialer that went silent when the owner dropped the landline, a back door contact that worked loose as the frame aged, a window zone that reads open so the panel refuses to arm, a keypad that chirps at three in the morning because a battery somewhere is low, and a store alarm whose monitoring stopped when the business changed hands. Others come from owners whose smart-home kit was discontinued by its maker.

Most of these are fixed on the first visit, often in one to two hours. A hardwired system with sound wiring can usually keep the wiring with a new panel and a cellular communicator, which is the cheapest upgrade on our list. If a siren is sounding and will not stop, we will talk you through silencing it by phone first, because in an attached building your neighbors hear it as clearly as you do. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Columbia Street Waterfront’s Architecture

The district was built in two waves with a gap between them. The first left the brick row houses, walk-ups and warehouses of the old waterfront; the expressway, the decline of the piers and the 1975 demolitions cleared a great deal of it; and the second, from the 1980s on, filled the gaps with townhouse condos, factory conversions and, more recently, small new apartment buildings. Six kinds of property cover nearly every alarm job we are asked to price here.

The older brick row house

Three- and four-story houses on the side streets, attached on both sides, with a street door, a rear door to a yard or alley, a cellar and, on many, a fire escape across the back. The openings that matter are almost all behind the house.

The walk-up with a shop below

A storefront at street level, a cellar under it, and two or three apartments above reached by a separate street door. Two or three different households, a business, and one or two doors they all share.

The 1980s townhouse condo

The Columbia Terrace phases and similar low-rise condos: framed construction, each home with its own entrance, many with a rear garden or patio, all inside a condominium whose board owns the common elements.

The factory loft

The former Tiffany factory on Tiffany Place, converted in 1986, and the old pasta factory at 25 Carroll Street, converted in 2010. Heavy masonry, high ceilings, long corridors, one entry door per unit and big windows.

The new small apartment building

Buildings such as those on Summit Street and Carroll Street, and larger complexes like Columbia Commons. Fob or keypad entry, building-owned smoke systems, and residents who want only their own door and windows on an alarm.

The port-edge and industrial building

Warehouses, depots and workshops along the western edge and near the Brooklyn Marine Terminal: roll-up doors, yards, few people at night, and owners who want to know who opened up and when.

The older brick row house: start at the back and look up

On a row house in the district, the survey begins where the 2014 break-ins began: at the back. The technician goes through the house to the yard or the alley and looks at the rear wall from outside. He notes the rear door and how it is hung, any door or hatch that opens from an alley or a passage, the cellar door and the windows at grade, and then he looks up. A fire escape across the back of the house turns every window it passes into a ground-floor window for practical purposes, and the Union Street burglary in February 2014 was, in the police’s words, a possible fire-escape burglary. The windows on the fire-escape landings get contacts. Other back windows get contacts only if something makes them reachable: an extension roof, a neighbor’s shed, a wall a person can climb.

Then he checks the hinges. This sounds fussy, and it is the single most useful habit we have taken from the Tiffany Place report. The burglars there did not break the trapdoor’s lock; according to police, they unscrewed its hinges. A door or hatch with its hinge pins or screws on the outside can be taken off without touching the latch, and a contact mounted only near the latch may not move until the door is already swinging free. So on any door or hatch that opens outward from an alley, we look at which side the hinges are on, recommend non-removable pins or security screws where they are exposed, and mount the contact where any movement of the door, from either edge, separates the magnet from the switch. Inside, a motion detector on the route from that door to the rest of the house catches anyone who gets past it anyway.

The cellar comes last. Many of these houses have a cellar with its own door to the yard or under the stoop, and that door often has the weakest lock in the building because nobody thinks about it. It goes on the alarm. Where the boiler and water heater are down there, a carbon monoxide detector and a water sensor on the floor go on the same panel, so a leak or a bad flue reaches your phone the same way a door does. Most row houses end up with a short, specific list that sits within our basic residential system or a wireless system in the $600 to $1,200 range.

The walk-up with a shop below: three sets of keys, one street door

The mixed buildings on and near Columbia Street are the hardest small jobs in the district, and the reason is not technical. A typical building has a storefront with its own door and often a gate, a cellar used by the shop, a separate street door to the stair, and two or three apartments. The shop owner, each tenant and the building owner all have different hours, different worries and different rights. The 2019 burglary at 259 Columbia Street, between President and Carroll Streets, began with an unlocked front door, and what was taken, eight chairs, a grill, a blender and a box of toilet paper, sounds like goods somebody had stored inside the building rather than inside a locked home.

Our approach is to give each party its own system or partition and to treat the shared door as a matter for agreement: the shop gets a commercial alarm, a tenant gets a small system inside the apartment door, and the building owner, if wanted, gets a contact on the street and cellar doors that notifies whoever the households choose when either is left open. Nobody is armed into anybody else’s space.

The 1980s townhouse condo: your doors, the board’s walls

The condos of the renewal years are where the district’s alarms are most often old rather than missing. Many were fitted with hardwired panels when they were new, and the wire is often still in the walls. Because these homes are framed rather than solid masonry, that wiring is sometimes in better shape than an old row house’s, and a panel replacement on existing wiring, at $800 to $1,500 on our list, can bring a 1980s system up to a cellular panel with an app. Where the old wire is broken or unknown, wireless sensors on the same doors and windows cost less than chasing it.

The thing to settle first is ownership. In a condominium, the exterior walls, the roof and usually the outside face of each door belong to the association, while the interior of each home belongs to its owner. An alarm that lives inside your home, on the inside of your doors and windows, is normally your own business. A siren mounted outside, a camera on the facade, a contact on a shared garden gate or anything drilled through an exterior wall usually is not, and most boards want to see a description first. We write that description for the board and send our certificate of insurance before anything is installed.

The factory loft: one door, big glass and open rooms

A loft in one of the converted factories is, from an alarm’s point of view, the opposite of a row house. There is usually one way in, the unit door from the building corridor, and the building itself controls the street entrance. The windows are large and often high, and most face the street, a courtyard or the water rather than anything a person could stand on. So a loft alarm is usually compact: a contact on the unit door, a motion detector placed to see the whole open floor from a corner, a glass-break detector only where a window is genuinely within reach of a roof, a fire escape or a neighboring terrace, and a keypad inside the door.

Original masonry and timber do not take adhesive well, so devices are fixed mechanically where the board allows, and motion detectors are mounted for the real ceiling height. Most loft systems fit inside the wireless range on our list.

The new small apartment building: inside the apartment door only

The buildings that went up in the last fifteen years, from Columbia Commons to the small developments on Summit Street, arrive with the building’s own systems: a fob or keypad at the street door, cameras in the lobby, and a fire alarm or interconnected smoke detectors installed by the developer. Openigloo’s list of new developments in the district shows open violation counts from 1 to 15 per building, a reminder that the building’s systems are the landlord’s responsibility and that a resident’s alarm should not be bolted onto them. An apartment alarm here is small and wireless: a cellular panel in a closet, a contact on the apartment door, contacts on any window or terrace door that can be reached from a setback, a courtyard or a neighboring terrace, one motion detector covering the entry hall, and phone alerts. It comes out without a mark when you move.

The port-edge building: long nights, wide doors

Industrial users along the western edge need heavy-duty contacts on roll-up doors, every roof hatch and skylight on the alarm, separate zones for stock and offices, and opening and closing reports. Brick Underground’s account of Columbia Street, beside a live poultry market and a sanitation depot where trucks start around 4 a.m., is a reminder that some buildings are busy when others are empty, so the arming schedule follows the building’s own day.

How They Got In

Five Break-Ins, Five Different Ways In: What the District’s Own Burglary Reports Teach an Alarm Installer

Most alarm advice is written as if every burglary were the same: someone forces the front door, and the alarm goes off. The reports from this district describe five different events, and each one asks a different thing of an alarm. We take them one at a time, because between them they explain almost every decision we make on a survey here. None of them is recent, and we are not suggesting these are typical of 2026. They are simply the best local evidence there is about how homes in these particular buildings have been entered.

Tiffany Place, October 2014: the hinges, not the lock

The family went out to dinner at about half past five and came home at ten past seven. In that window, according to DNAinfo’s account of the police report, someone opened a trapdoor that led from an alley off Tiffany Place, between Kane and Degraw Streets, into a bedroom, and took a laptop, a Kindle and an iPad worth about $800. The hinges had been unscrewed. What this tells an installer is that the strongest lock in the world is irrelevant on a door whose hinges can be removed from the outside, and that a door that opens from a private alley gives a burglar time and cover to work. On a survey, every alley door, hatch and trapdoor gets three things: a check of its hinges, a contact placed so that movement at either edge is detected, and a motion detector inside on the route beyond it. We also set that zone as an instant zone with no entry delay when nobody uses the door, because no member of the household should ever be coming in that way.

The timing matters too: the house was empty for under two hours in the early evening, when a bedtime-only habit leaves the alarm off. If everyone is out, the alarm is on, and the app can remind you if the last phone leaves and nobody has armed.

Union Street, February 2014: the fire escape in daylight

A 44-year-old resident was out between noon and 7:30 p.m. When she got home, her bedroom had been ransacked, and police described the case as a possible fire-escape burglary. About $6,310 was taken, including a $2,000 engagement ring, a $1,000 vintage pocket watch, a laptop and $500 in cash. Two lessons follow. The first is that fire-escape windows are entry points no matter what floor they are on, and they get contacts, which do not stop you climbing out in a fire, rather than anything that blocks the window. The second is that this happened in the middle of a weekday afternoon, when the street was presumably busy and the building presumably quiet. Burglary is not only a night problem, and a system that is only armed at night is half a system.

Warren Street, April 2014: nothing was forced

A resident of Warren Street between Hicks and Columbia Streets left at about a quarter to six one evening and came back three days later, at about half past six. A bedroom door and a living room window were open, there were no signs of forced entry, and about $1,400 in jewelry and cash was gone. We do not know from the report whether the window had been left open, and we will not guess. What we can say is that this is the kind of burglary an alarm handles better than any lock, provided the alarm is set up properly. A panel with a contact on that window will not arm while the window is open; it tells you which window it is, by name, on the keypad and on the phone. The household then has a choice: close it, or bypass it deliberately, which the panel records. And if the window was closed and someone opened it during those three days, the alert would have reached the owner within seconds, not seventy-two hours later.

That is why bypass rules are part of every handover: zones named in plain words, a list of which ones may ever be bypassed, and a notification whenever a bypass is used.

Woodhull Street, March 2016: the house nobody was living in

At about six in the morning, police found two men loading a brand-new refrigerator into a stolen minivan, double-parked, with its seats taken out to make room; a new range and a washer had already been moved out and staged. The house was under renovation, on Woodhull Street between Hicks and Henry Streets, and the burglars had forced the front doors. One man was arrested at Woodhull and Hicks; all the appliances were recovered. A house under renovation is one of the most attractive targets on any block: it is empty at night, it is full of new and resaleable goods, and the doors and locks are often temporary. A temporary wireless alarm on the doors and on the room where appliances and materials wait, with a code for each trade that is removed when they finish, costs a fraction of one appliance. When the work is done, the same sensors usually move into the finished house.

259 Columbia Street, February 2019: the street door left open

The burglar at 259 Columbia Street, between President and Carroll Streets, “slipped through an unlocked front door,” as Gothamist put it, and walked off with eight chairs, a grill, a blender and a box of toilet paper that the NYPD valued at $650. It reads like a comic item, and it is also the most common security failure in small mixed buildings anywhere: a shared door that somebody did not lock. No single household’s alarm can fix that, and we do not pretend it can. What can help is a contact on the shared door that tells the people who agreed to receive it when the door has been open or unlatched for more than a few minutes, and, if the shop or the building owner keeps goods in the hall or the cellar, a separate zone on that storage space so that what is kept there is actually protected.

What the five have in common

None of the five describes a front door kicked in on an occupied home. Three came from the back, the side or above; two involved something open or unlocked; one was a house nobody lived in yet; all happened while nobody was home. So our district quotes usually list fewer sensors at the front and more at the back than an off-the-shelf kit would.

Columbia Street is the district’s only real commercial street, and it has changed several times in twenty years. Wikipedia describes the empty storefronts of the decades after the expressway and the bakeries and restaurants that opened through the 2000s; BK Magazine noted that the corner of Kane Street had been quiet since Pok Pok closed in 2018, until Laurel Bakery opened there in April 2024. The businesses that call us fall into six settings.

The Kane Street corner

Laurel Bakery at 115 Columbia Street, open Wednesday to Sunday from 8:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. with no indoor seating, and Krok next door at 117. Daytime businesses that sit dark for more than half of every day.

Sackett to Union

The Longshoreman at 215 Columbia Street, between Sackett and Union, and the storefronts around it under apartments. Late hours, staff leaving by the back, and stock in the cellar.

South along Columbia Street

Popina at 327 Columbia Street, open since 2017, and the smaller shops toward the tunnel. Rear yards and gardens enclosed by the backs of neighboring buildings.

Industrial neighbors

The live poultry market and the sanitation truck depot that Brick Underground described on Columbia Street, and the workshops along the western blocks. Early starts, wide doors and yards.

The port edge

Businesses near the Brooklyn Marine Terminal’s northern piers, where the city’s Vision Plan, approved in September 2025, proposes housing, a park and new commercial space over the next decade or more.

Empty storefronts between tenants

Ground floors that sit vacant for months or years, as the Kane Street corner did. Owners still have fixtures, plumbing and a cellar to protect, and nobody inside to notice a forced door.

What the precinct has said about commercial burglaries

In March 2019, the Red Hook Star-Revue reported from a 76th Precinct community council meeting that the commanding officer, Deputy Inspector Megan O’Malley, was most concerned about “the recent increase in commercial burglaries, especially at night,” and urged businesses on Court and Smith Streets to keep their cameras working. Court and Smith Streets are across the expressway, but the advice applies just as well to Columbia Street, and it lines up with what we see on commercial surveys: night is when shops are empty, and footage is only useful if the recorder was running and somebody can find the right minute. A shop alarm here therefore does two jobs. It reports the first opening, at the gate, the door or the glass, to the owner’s phone and, if the owner chooses, to a monitoring station within seconds. And it marks the moment on the camera system so that the clip from that minute is waiting when the owner or the precinct asks for it.

Bakeries, cafés and daytime shops

A shop that closes at four in the afternoon and opens at half past eight the next morning is empty for sixteen and a half hours a day, and for two whole days a week if it closes early in the week as Laurel Bakery does. That is a different problem from a bar that is empty for six hours. The alarm needs to arm reliably every afternoon, which the app can check and nudge, and it needs to report to someone who will act during a long, quiet night. Ovens, proofers and refrigeration move warm air, so motion detectors in the back are aimed along the floor and away from them, and we walk-test them with the equipment running. The front is usually mostly glass, which gets a glass-break detector, and the door gets a contact, with the keypad placed so the first person in at dawn reaches it without crossing the whole shop.

Restaurants and bars under apartments

The restaurants between Sackett and Union and farther south are mostly in buildings with homes above. That shapes three settings. The siren is indoors and its cutoff is short, well inside the fifteen-minute limit that the city’s Administrative Code sets for audible burglar alarms, because the people upstairs hear it too. The rear door, which staff use for deliveries and garbage, is a full alarm zone after closing and a chime-only door during service. And the cellar, which usually holds the wine, the liquor and the dry stock, is a separate zone, so an alert at 3 a.m. says which part of the premises was entered. Closing staff each get their own code, and the owner gets a notification when the last person arms and a reminder if nobody has by a set time.

Industrial buildings and the port edge

The industrial users on the western blocks start early and keep their own hours. Roll-up doors get heavy-duty contacts, every roof hatch and skylight goes on the alarm, and stock rooms and offices are separate zones. Yards are protected with outdoor beams along the inside of the fence rather than ordinary motion detectors, which wind, rain, birds and headlights would set off constantly. For businesses near the Brooklyn Marine Terminal, the honest advice is to buy for the building as it stands. Time Out reported that the environmental review and permitting could take about a decade, and Borough President Antonio Reynoso called it “more of a proposal than it is an actual plan.” An alarm is a few years’ purchase, not a bet on the waterfront’s future.

Compliance notes for district businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. In mixed buildings we set the cutoff well inside that.
  • Installer licensing. Alarm installation businesses in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431. Ask any installer for theirs.
  • Burglar alarms are not fire alarm systems. Monitored smoke detectors on a burglar alarm are useful, but where the building code or the FDNY requires a fire alarm system, they do not replace it. The FDNY’s rules make it unlawful to transmit two or more unnecessary fire alarms in a three-month period, so smoke devices are kept well away from kitchens and ovens.
  • Detectors in rental buildings. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and sets carbon monoxide rules for owners and tenants. Adding monitored detectors to an alarm does not take over the landlord’s or the tenant’s duties.
  • No citywide burglar alarm permit that we found. Unlike many suburban towns, New York City has no general permit for a home or shop burglar alarm that we could find. Avoiding false alarms still matters, because each unnecessary dispatch costs police time and costs the owner credibility.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Columbia Street Waterfront: Questions Answered

These questions come from the district’s break-in reports, buildings and businesses; none is repeated in the FAQ below.

Alleys, hinges and windows left open

The 2014 Tiffany Place burglars got in by unscrewing a trapdoor’s hinges. If someone lifts our alley door off its hinges instead of opening the latch, will a contact still report it?

It will if the contact is in the right place, and that is exactly what we check. A door contact is a magnet on the door and a switch on the frame; it reports the moment the two separate. When a door is opened normally, it swings away from the latch side first, so contacts are usually mounted near the latch. When a door is removed by its hinges, the hinge side may move first, and a door that is only lifted a little at the latch edge could, in a badly placed installation, stay within the switch’s range for a moment. So on alley doors and hatches we mount the contact at the top corner on the latch side, where any real movement of the door separates it, and on doors that are a known target we add a second contact on the hinge side. We also look at the hinges themselves: exposed pins or screws on the outside get non-removable pins or security screws, which is carpentry rather than alarm work but costs very little. And behind every alley door there is a motion detector on the route into the house, so even if the door somehow comes off without a report, the first step inside does not.

On Warren Street a resident came home after three days to an open living room window and no forced entry. When our panel refuses to arm because one window reads open, is it safe to bypass that window just this once?

Sometimes, and only if you know why it reads open. The panel is doing its job: it is telling you that one opening is not protected. If the window is open because you forgot, close it and arm normally. If it is closed but still reads open, the sensor has shifted or its battery is failing, and bypassing it means leaving that window unprotected until it is fixed. If it is open on purpose, for a cat or for air on a hot day, a bypass is a reasonable choice for an evening at home and a poor one for three days away. We set the panel so a bypass lasts only until the next disarm, never permanently, and so the app sends a notification whenever one is used, naming the window. That way a bypass is a decision someone made and can see, not a habit that slowly leaves half the house unarmed. If you find yourself bypassing the same window every week, tell us; it usually means the sensor needs moving or the window needs a second sensor that allows it to stay open a few inches.

Shared doors and the things kept behind them

The 259 Columbia Street burglar took chairs and a grill after walking through an unlocked street door. Our restaurant keeps its sidewalk tables, chairs and heaters in the building’s ground-floor hall overnight. Can that storage be on our alarm?

Yes, with the building owner’s agreement, and it is worth doing because furniture stacked in a shared hall is exactly what that case shows going missing. The hall is not part of your leased premises, so nothing goes in without the landlord saying so in writing. Once that is settled, the storage can be its own zone on the restaurant’s panel: a contact on the door to the hall or the storage area, or a motion detector covering only the corner where the furniture sits, armed when you close and disarmed when you open. Residents passing through the hall to the stair should never trip it, so we choose between a contact and a motion detector by looking at how the hall is used. If the stair runs past the furniture, we use a contact on a lockable storage door or a cage instead of a detector in the open hall. A camera covering the furniture, tied to the same alarm, gives you a clip if anything is moved after closing.

Our storefront on Columbia Street has been empty since the last restaurant moved out, and the next lease may be months away. What does an empty shop need from an alarm?

Less than a working shop, but more than nothing: it has fixtures, plumbing and often a cellar, and nobody inside to notice a forced door or a burst pipe. The minimum we would fit is a contact on the street and rear doors, a motion detector on the main floor, a contact on the cellar door or hatch, and water and low-temperature sensors in the cellar for the winter, all wireless and reporting to your phone. When a tenant signs, the sensors can move to another property or stay for the new tenant’s alarm company.

Shops, bakeries and the precinct’s advice

The precinct commander once urged local businesses to keep their cameras working because of night burglaries. When our shop alarm trips at night, can it mark the right minute on our cameras so we are not scrolling through hours of footage?

Yes, if the alarm and the cameras speak to each other, and on the systems we install they do. With cameras on the same platform as the alarm, an alarm event starts a recorded clip from the camera assigned to that zone, the rear door camera for the rear door, the floor camera for the motion detector, and that clip is attached to the event in the app. With a separate recorder, we can usually set the recorder to bookmark the moment the alarm trips, or at least make sure its clock matches the alarm panel’s to the second, so the time on the alarm history takes you straight to the footage. What we will not do is promise that footage catches a face; that depends on light, angle and luck. But a clip from the right thirty seconds, delivered with the alert, is far more useful to you and to the 76th Precinct than a hard drive somebody has to search.

Our bakery closes at four in the afternoon and opens at half past eight, and is closed two days a week. Is a monitoring station worth paying for, or are phone alerts enough?

For a shop that is empty for sixteen hours a day and whole days at a stretch, we would usually recommend monitoring. Phone alerts depend on someone seeing them and acting at three in the morning on a day off. A monitoring station’s staff exist to answer the alert, try your call list and, if nobody confirms a false alarm, request a police response. For a home where someone is usually around, phone alerts are often enough. Monitoring through our partners is optional, month to month, from $30 a month, and can be added later without changing equipment.

Condos, renovations and the street at dawn

Our Columbia Terrace townhouse was built in the 1980s with framed walls. Can a new alarm be wired through them rather than stuck on, and is that better?

Often it can, and sometimes it is already there. Framed walls, unlike the solid brick of the older row houses, have cavities that a technician can fish wire through from a basement or an attic, and many renewal-era condos were wired for an alarm when they were built. If the old wire is intact, we test each run and reuse it with a new panel, which is the cheapest route. If it is not, new wire through framed walls is practical but means small openings at each device and some patching that we do not do ourselves; a painter or the board’s contractor handles that. Wired is not more secure than a modern wireless sensor in a home this size, and wireless is quicker and leaves no patching. Owners tend to choose wired when the old wire is already there, or when they are opening walls anyway for a renovation, and wireless otherwise.

The Woodhull Street burglars came at six in the morning with a stolen minivan double-parked outside. Can our system warn us about a vehicle stopped outside the house for a long time before dawn?

An alarm cannot, but a camera tied to it can, within limits. A curb camera can flag a vehicle that stops in a drawn area for longer than a set time during chosen hours, such as midnight to 6 a.m. On these streets, though, delivery vans and early trucks stop at odd hours for innocent reasons, so a vehicle alert alone is mostly noise. For a house under renovation we rely on the alarm for the decision, a contact on the front doors that reports the instant they are forced, and on the camera for the evidence: the clip from the same minute shows the van, and often its plate.

Reading the numbers

The precinct’s burglaries are down for the year but up over the last 28 days, ten against seven. Should a household read anything into a short-term rise like that?

Not much on its own. Ten burglaries in 28 days across a precinct covering five neighborhoods is a small number, and small numbers swing: the same report shows 81 for the year against 92 a year earlier, and Patch’s account of 2015 shows a ten-year high of 132 followed by a fall of about 28 percent the next spring. A short rise is a good prompt to check that your alarm arms, every sensor reports and the call list is current. The decision to install should rest on your own building’s openings and how often it is empty.

Nest Secure stopped working in April 2024, and the Marine Terminal plan could take a decade. How do we avoid buying a system that its maker can switch off while we still live here?

By choosing equipment whose life does not depend on one consumer brand’s app. Google ended Nest Secure on April 8, 2024, and the devices stopped working in the Nest app; it offered owners an ADT system with a year of monitoring as a replacement. That is the risk with any kit that only works with its maker’s cloud. The panels we usually install are 2GIG units on the Alarm.com service, which is used by many independent dealers rather than one retail brand, and many of its sensors can be learned by other panels that listen on the same radio frequency. If the service you use changes, the panel can usually be moved to another dealer or monitoring station, and if the panel itself has to be replaced, most of the sensors can stay. We also install other brands when an owner asks for one, and we will tell you plainly which ones we would trust to last a decade.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Columbia Street Waterfront

These claims turn up again and again in generic online advice and machine-written answers about home alarms. They are paraphrased, not quoted, and each is set beside what the district’s own records and our surveys show.

“Burglaries happen at night, so arm the alarm when you go to bed.”

New York-wide figures that CrimeGrade cites do put the most common burglary hours between midnight and 6 a.m., and night arming matters. But the district’s own reports describe a Union Street home entered between noon and 7:30 p.m. and a Tiffany Place bedroom entered while the family was at dinner.

Both happened while the homes were empty in daylight or early evening, the hours a bedtime-only habit leaves uncovered.

The rule we teach is simpler: whenever the home is empty, the alarm is on, whether you are gone for three days or for one dinner. The app makes that a single tap from the sidewalk, and it can remind you when the last phone leaves and nobody has armed.

“A neighborhood with an A crime grade doesn’t need an alarm.”

CrimeGrade’s A for burglary here reflects about 1.1 reported burglaries per 1,000 residents a year. That is genuinely low, and we say so to every customer.

A low average says little about one house with an alley door, a fire escape or an empty storefront below. And the precinct’s figures swing: Patch counted a ten-year high of 132 burglaries in 2015 after 96 the year before.

A low rate is a reason to buy a smaller, well-planned system, not a reason to leave the back of the house unwatched.

“Wireless alarms run on your home Wi-Fi.”

Many consumer kits do lean on the home network. The panels we install use their own cellular connection, with battery backup, and use Wi-Fi only as an optional second path.

As an alarm company sales manager told Brick Underground, “you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.”

In older brick buildings where Wi-Fi struggles from the cellar to the top floor, that independence matters more than any feature list.

“An alarm can’t help if you leave a door or window unlocked.”

The Warren Street and 259 Columbia Street cases both involved openings that were unlocked or open, and generic advice treats that as a lock problem only.

A panel with a contact on every opening will not arm while one is open, names it on the keypad and the phone, and records any deliberate bypass. A contact on a shared street door can tell residents when it has been left open.

An alarm cannot lock a door for you, but it makes forgetting visible, which is most of the battle. In a small building with a shop below, that visibility is often the only thing that tells anyone the street door has been standing open since the last delivery.

“A smart-home security kit is a permanent purchase.”

Owners of Google’s Nest Secure learned otherwise on April 8, 2024, when the system was shut down and stopped working in the Nest app. Brownstoner readers were still asking what to do with the equipment on their walls.

Any system tied to one maker’s cloud carries that risk. Panels on a platform used by many independent dealers can usually move between them.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Hinges and Name the Street Twice

This describes how our technicians survey a district property, not a story about one job. After a quick look at the street side, the technician goes straight to the back. On every door or hatch that opens from an alley, a yard or a passage, he looks first at the hinges: which side they are on, whether the pins or screws are reachable from outside, and how the door sits in its frame. Only then does he decide where the contact goes and whether it needs a partner on the hinge side. Then he looks up at the fire escape, the extension roofs and the neighbors’ walls, and down at the cellar door.

The second habit is about the address. Brick Underground’s Columbia Street resident recalls a cab driver asking, “Are you quite sure this is where you live?”, and the district is easy to misplace from the other side of the expressway. So the dispatch notes on every monitoring account name the cross streets, say that the building is west of the trench, and describe which door responders should use. It takes two minutes, and it is the part of the job customers never see.

Is an alarm for a condo apartment in one of the newer buildings simpler than one for an old row house?
+

Usually much simpler. A condo apartment typically has one entry door and a few reachable windows, so a basic system covers it. A row house may have a street door, a rear door, an alley door, a cellar, a fire escape and a roof hatch, each of which needs its own sensor.

If a burglar comes in and smashes the keypad before the entry delay runs out, does the alarm still report?
+

Yes, on the panels we install. The keypad and the panel are separate, and the panel is hidden elsewhere. With Alarm.com’s service, an entry that is not followed by a proper disarm is reported even if the keypad is destroyed during the delay, so breaking the keypad does not silence the system.

Can the written price change once the technician is installing?
+

Not without your agreement. The written price lists every sensor and opening. If the technician finds something on the day that the survey could not show, such as a hidden hatch or a broken old wire, he tells you, gives you the price for it and waits for your yes before doing that extra work.

The siren is loud in a small apartment. Can its volume be turned down, or can it be set to chime only?
+

Yes, within limits. Door chimes and keypad beeps can be turned down or off, and an indoor siren can be set to a lower level in an apartment. We keep it loud enough to be heard throughout the home, and its cutoff stays well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit.

Do we have to be home for the whole installation in a Tiffany Place loft?
+

Someone needs to let the technician in, show him the doors and windows, and be there at the end to choose codes, set up the app and watch every sensor being tested. In a loft the middle of the visit can run without you, and the building’s elevator or loading rules are arranged beforehand.
