AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in the Bronx

Ask a chatbot what TV mounting costs in the Bronx and you'll usually get a national average and a picture of a suburban house with drywall over wood studs. That isn't what a typical Bronx week looks like. Our technicians drill into Co-op City concrete, Concourse plaster over clay tile, Parkchester masonry and the brick fireplaces of Throgs Neck houses. Here are seven claims we see repeated, and what actually happens on Bronx walls.

1. AI says: ““TV mounting in the Bronx costs about $45 an hour.””

That number comes from marketplace listings, where TV mounting in the Bronx is advertised from $45 an hour, and some posted jobs on task sites offer $40 to $60 to mount a 65″ or 75″ TV. Those are opening offers, not finished jobs.

An hourly rate says nothing about what's in the wall. A concrete wall in Co-op City needs a hammer drill and masonry anchors, and Concourse plaster needs a test hole first. Before you compare, ask whether the hourly figure includes insurance, a COI for your building, the right anchors and who covers the damage if the mount fails.

Our Bronx price is flat: $209.99 for a 32″–55″ on drywall over studs, with each wall type listed separately. Plaster is $45, brick $65 and concrete $75. The calculator shows your total up front, and a slow hole doesn't make the bill grow.

2. AI says: ““You can't mount a TV on a concrete wall.””

This one turns up in answers about high-rise living, and Co-op City residents often take it as a no. It's wrong. Concrete is one of the strongest walls there is for a TV. It just needs different tools from drywall.

What goes wrong is the method, not the wall. Plastic plugs and a cordless drill won't work in poured concrete, and a hole drilled straight into reinforcing bar has to be moved. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors rated for the load, and a bracket with slotted rails so a hole can move an inch if steel is in the way.

Concrete adds $75 to the Bronx base. A 55″ comes to $284.99 and a 65″ to $314.99. What you give up is in-wall wiring, because there's no hollow cavity. The tidy alternative is a $59 raceway you can paint, which nearly vanishes once it matches the wall.

3. AI says: ““Just find the studs with a stud finder and use lag bolts.””

That's good advice for a house built in the last few decades. In a Grand Concourse building it can lead you astray. Prewar plaster is thick and often sits on metal lath, clay tile or brick, so a stud finder beeps at pipes, lath and nails and misses the actual structure.

Following that advice is how people end up with a row of holes and a lag bolt spinning in hollow tile. A mount that pulls loose a few months later usually started this way. A small test hole, a short look behind the plaster and the right anchor for tile or brick solves the problem.

We do this on every plaster wall, and the add-on is $45 regardless of what we find. A 65″ on Concourse plaster is $284.99. If the test hole turns up solid brick instead, the wall is priced at $65, and we tell you before we change anything.

4. AI says: ““A big-box store install covers any wall at one price.””

Big-box packages are usually priced for drywall. Plaster, brick, concrete, stone and ceiling mounts often cost extra or get turned down on site, which is a common complaint in Reddit threads about installers who arrive, look at the wall and leave.

In the Bronx, that matters, because most apartments aren't drywall over wood studs. A Parkchester apartment, a Co-op City tower unit and a Riverdale prewar co-op could each end up with a different add-on, and you want to know which one before the day of the appointment.

Our chart prices every wall before you book: tile or marble +$95, concrete or block +$75, brick or stone +$65, plaster +$45, steel studs +$30. Choose the closest match in the calculator. If we arrive and the wall is different, you'll see the price change before work starts. There are no surprise charges.

5. AI says: ““Run the TV's power cord through the wall to hide it.””

Plenty of how-to answers suggest this, but a regular TV power cord isn't rated for use inside a wall. Hiding one there is a fire-code problem, not just a matter of looks. The right way uses a listed in-wall power kit that feeds a recessed receptacle behind the TV.

In the Bronx there's a second problem: many walls have no cavity to work with. Co-op City concrete and Parkchester masonry are solid. Cutting a channel into them is demolition that buildings won't permit for a TV, and patching and painting aren't part of what we do.

So we match the method to the wall. A hollow wall gets our code-compliant in-wall kit for $169, fed from an existing outlet. A solid wall gets a $59 paintable raceway. A brand-new outlet is an electrician's job and must be in place before we arrive.

6. AI says: ““Bronx homes are all apartments, so fireplaces and outdoor TVs don't come up.””

About one in five Bronx households owns its home, and that's still a lot of houses. Fieldston alone has 257, and Throgs Neck, Country Club, Pelham Bay and City Island are full of detached homes with chimneys, decks and finished basements.

That's where fireplace questions come up. A 65″ over brick is $434.99 all in. A pull-down mount brings the screen down from a tall mantel, and a heat check comes before any drilling. Outdoor mounts on decks near the Sound need weather-rated brackets, because salt air rusts cheap steel quickly.

Our Bronx price list includes all of it: above-fireplace placement $130, MantelMount pull-down $160, outdoor $90, high mount above about 8 feet $75, and a full home theater hookup for a basement room at $350. These jobs are booked from the same calculator as an apartment mount.

7. AI says: ““Any handyman can walk into a co-op and mount a TV.””

Not in most Riverdale co-ops, management-run Concourse buildings or big complexes. Boards and managing agents often require a certificate of insurance listing the corporation and agent as additional insureds before a contractor is let in, and some want it days ahead.

If the paperwork is missing, the technician gets sent away and you lose the day. Under our policies, a return trip caused by missing building approvals costs $150, so it's worth getting the paperwork settled when you book.

We're a licensed, insured NYS low-voltage contractor, and the COI is free. Send us your building's sample certificate when you book, and we'll issue a matching one.
