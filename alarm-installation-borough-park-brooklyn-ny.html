In a large part of Borough Park that habit stops for about twenty-five hours every week and for two or three days at a time on Yom Tov. An alarm here has to be planned so that it does its job by itself from Friday afternoon to Saturday night, in whatever way your household and your rav are comfortable with. That is what this page is about.

The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need Alarm Installation

Borough Park sits in southwestern Brooklyn between Sunset Park to the west, Kensington and Green-Wood Cemetery to the north, Flatbush to the east, Mapleton to the southeast and Bensonhurst and Dyker Heights to the south. Wikipedia’s account of its history begins in 1887 with the hamlet of Blythebourne, laid out by Electus Litchfield, and continues in 1902, when State Senator William H. Reynolds bought land and named the area Borough Park. Jewish immigrants began arriving around 1904 and 1905. From the 1980s the neighborhood’s population shifted toward Hasidic families, and today it is one of the largest Orthodox and Hasidic Jewish communities in the United States; Wikipedia’s infobox lists Yiddish as spoken by 56.8% of residents.

That is the fact an alarm installer cannot ignore here. Shabbos runs from before sunset on Friday until after nightfall on Saturday, and Yom Tov adds days of its own; on all of them many households avoid operating electrical devices. An alarm that depends on a person entering a code every time the front door opens does not fit that week. Some families have dealt with it by leaving the system off for the whole of Shabbos. Others arm it on Friday afternoon and live with a keypad that beeps at every door and a motion sensor that blinks whenever someone walks past. Neither is a good answer, and both come up when people call us.

Burglars know the rhythm too. In February 2023 Yeshiva World News reported the arrest of a man whom the Boro Park Shomrim had tracked for more than a year; officers from the 66th Precinct detained him at 16th Avenue and 51st Street on a Friday evening, carrying stolen property and burglary tools. The report said he targeted homes and apartments on Friday nights because he knew that “many Jewish residents let their guard down on Shabbos, and that many go away, as well.” In August 2025 the Shomrim reported an arrest for packages stolen from homes in the 38th Street area over Shabbos, after its volunteers reviewed camera footage.

The houses themselves shape where a burglar goes in. In August 2014 CBS New York and Yeshiva World News reported that one man had broken into eight Borough Park homes in under two months, entering every time through a rear window, and had taken silverware and jewelry worth over $200,000. The police time windows for those burglaries ran from a single day to about a month, which suggests houses where nobody was home to notice. Attached and semi-attached houses on the numbered streets put almost all of their vulnerable openings at the back, out of sight of the street and of the Shomrim patrols, facing yards and the backs of other houses.

The buildings keep changing as well. Wikipedia notes that a 1992 special zoning law let Borough Park owners build on 65% of a lot with smaller setbacks and backyards, and that since 1990 the neighborhood has had more private construction permits than any other residential neighborhood in Brooklyn. In 2016 the Brooklyn Eagle described a two-family, two-story house at 1311 44th Street being enlarged to 5,633 square feet as a four-family, five-story building, according to Buildings Department filings. Every enlargement adds windows, doors, a roof or an extension that the old alarm never knew about. A system installed in the 1990s and never extended can leave a whole new floor unprotected.

And the avenues close together. Wikipedia’s article on Thirteenth Avenue says that on the roughly one-mile Borough Park stretch “all stores and businesses are shuttered on the Jewish Sabbath and Jewish holidays,” chain stores included. The same article counts fourteen banks between 45th and 55th Streets in 2009 and describes stores selling silver Judaica, electronics, books and wigs. A store that locks up before candle lighting on Friday and reopens on Saturday night or Sunday morning needs an alarm that arms itself if the last person forgets, and an alert that reaches someone who will deal with it, whether or not the owner is answering the phone that day. When you add cameras for the yard and the store floor, the recordings also give the precinct and the Shomrim something to work from.

Shabbos and Yom Tov

An Alarm That Looks After Itself From Candle Lighting to Havdalah

Before anything else, a word on what we do and do not do. We are alarm installers. We do not decide what is permitted on Shabbos and Yom Tov, and the arrangements below are not rulings. Different households follow different poskim, and the halachic literature on alarm systems describes a range of views. What we can do is explain what each type of equipment is technically able to do, set it up the way your rav has approved, and make sure it behaves exactly as planned when nobody is going to touch it. To show the kinds of questions involved, we refer below to two published overviews: Peninei Halakha by Rav Eliezer Melamed, chapter 17 of its Shabbat volume, and a 2010 summary by Rabbi Yosef Bitton on Halakha of the Day. Ask your own rav before relying on anything here.

Start with the timetable, not the keypad

Peninei Halakha’s section on security systems describes a timer as the preferred approach: the system is armed during the hours when people are asleep or away and disarmed when they are up and moving about. It adds that changing the schedule electrically on Shabbos is forbidden, and that some arrangements let the current state of an external timer be extended. In practice that means the work happens on a weekday. We sit with you and write down the household’s week: when the house is empty on Friday afternoon, when people come back from shul on Friday night, when they sleep, when they leave on Saturday morning, when guests come for the meal, and when Shabbos ends. That table becomes the schedule in the panel.

Modern platforms make this easier than older panels did. Alarm.com’s Smart Arming, as Alarm Grid described it in 2022, can arm the system to Stay mode at a fixed time and disarm it at a fixed time, on supported panels such as the DSC PowerSeries Neo, Qolsys IQ Panel 2 and 4, 2GIG GC2, GC3 and Edge and Interlogix Concord and Simon XT, for residential customers on an interactive plan. It can also arm when no activity is seen for a period, or disarm when certain motion detectors see movement. We point out the difference to every family: a time-based rule does what it does whatever happens in the house, while an activity-based rule reacts to people walking past sensors. Which of those your rav permits is a question for him; we set up whichever he approves and leave the other switched off.

The door the family uses

Rabbi Bitton’s summary describes arming the system before Shabbos and, where possible, bypassing one door the family uses while the other doors and windows stay armed. That arrangement is common in houses we plan, because it fits the way people actually live on Shabbos: everyone comes and goes through the front door or the side door, while the rear windows, the basement door and the extension are never opened. The front door zone is left out of the schedule entirely, or put in its own group that is never armed on Shabbos, and every other opening is protected the whole time. If the front door has a glass panel, a glass-break detector in the hall still protects it against a break-in without reacting to the door being opened normally.

Motion detectors, lights and the beeps in between

Motion detectors are where households differ most. Peninei Halakha’s section on motion detectors records that some authorities forbid walking in a house where active sensors are working unless the system is fully disabled or the sensors are covered, while others permit it, a view the book calls the primary position; it still recommends disabling them where possible. It also says that a light which switches on as you approach your own entrance must be disabled. Rabbi Bitton writes that some people cover or disable sensors before Shabbos but that walking past them is allowed. We do not choose between those views. We make each option possible and clean: interior motion detectors on their own partition or relay that can be switched off for Shabbos; outdoor motion lights and camera floodlights on a separate circuit or schedule so they do not come on at your own door; and indicator LEDs turned off at the sensor so nothing blinks when someone walks by.

The small signals are easy to miss and they matter. On many panels the keypad chimes or speaks every time a door opens, even when the system is disarmed, and wireless door contacts flash a light when they send a signal. In a 2021 thread on the Surety support forum, a homeowner with a Qolsys IQ Panel and PowerG door sensors asked how to stop exactly that for Shabbos; the support reply showed how to turn the sensor’s LED off in its programming and explained that the panel cannot schedule changes to its own sound settings. So the chime has to be turned off as a standing setting, or set per zone, rather than switched off each Friday. We set those options on the day of installation and show you where they are, so a visiting technician cannot quietly turn them back on.

Older wired systems have their own answers. NAPCO’s technical FAQ, answering a question titled “Does my security system have ‘Sabbath Mode’,” describes powering the motion sensors through a relay module and toggling that relay from the keypad, and suggests labeling the relay group “Sabbath Mode.” The Surety thread mentioned above adds that the “Sabbath Mode” section of the PowerG manual refers to a wired contact on a PG9945 transmitter, with a manual switch wired in parallel with the door contact so the zone can be taken out of detection, and that it does not apply to a wireless sensor’s built-in reed switch. Wherever a household wants the motion sensors physically off for Shabbos, a relay or a switched power supply is cleaner than taping over lenses, and it can be put on a timer so nobody has to touch it on Friday at all.

The siren on a Friday night

Sooner or later a system goes off on Shabbos: a cat, a balloon drifting past a sensor, a window that was not quite closed. New York City’s noise code answers part of the problem. Section 24-238 of the Administrative Code says no owner of a building may operate an audible burglar alarm unless it is capable of terminating, and does automatically terminate, its operation within fifteen minutes of being activated. Every panel we install has its bell cutoff set inside that limit and the setting written into the handover sheet. Rabbi Bitton’s summary discusses what a household may do if an alarm will not stop, beginning with asking a non-Jew to turn it off; that is a question for your rav, and a system that stops itself on time keeps it from becoming an emergency for the block.

Who the monitoring station calls, and what it does next

Monitoring is the part most often set up wrong for a Borough Park household. A standard call list starts with the homeowner’s cell phone; on Shabbos that phone is off or in a drawer, and the station works down a list of people who will not answer either, then dispatches or gives up depending on its rules. Peninei Halakha discusses monitoring services and notes differing opinions about the staff who handle calls on Shabbos; it also says that if the service calls to ask whether a dispatch is needed, one should answer to prevent an unnecessary trip. Your rav can tell you what applies in your house. Our job is to make the call list match the answer.

In practice we set up two lists with the monitoring provider. The weekday list is the usual one. The Shabbos and Yom Tov list names the people who will actually pick up — a neighbor who is not observant, a relative in another neighborhood, an adult child who lives elsewhere — and tells the station whether to send the police on a confirmed alarm without reaching anyone, or only on a second zone or a glass-break. Where the provider’s software can switch call lists by day, we use that; where it cannot, the Shabbos list becomes a standing written instruction on the account. We write the instruction down, send it, and check with a test signal that it was entered correctly.

When the house is empty for a week

The 2023 arrest report quoted above made the point that many families go away. Pesach is the obvious case, but summer, simchas out of town and Yom Tov with relatives all leave houses empty for days. For those weeks the schedule is not the right tool; the house should simply be armed Away from the moment you leave until you are back, with every zone including motion active, the call list switched to people who will answer, and a camera or two that you or a relative can look at. If you will be using the phone app during Chol Hamoed or on weekdays while away, we make sure it shows each zone by name — “rear bedroom window,” not “zone 14” — so you can tell at a glance what tripped.

Two- and three-day closures

The calendar regularly joins Yom Tov to Shabbos. Rosh Hashanah 2026, for example, began just before sundown on Friday, September 11 and ended after nightfall on Sunday, September 13, according to Chabad.org: two nights and two full days in which an observant family or business would not touch the panel. A weekly schedule handles that only if the Yom Tov days were entered in advance as exceptions. We set those up for the coming year when we hand the system over, and we will update them each fall if you want us to; the update is a weekday phone call or a short visit, never something that has to be done on the day itself.

For stores the effect is larger. A 13th Avenue shop that closes early on Friday may stay shut through Sunday night when the dates line up, and a delivery entrance or a back door left unlatched on Friday is exposed for the whole stretch. Closing reports, sent to the owner and a manager when the system arms, and a “failed to arm” alert if it has not armed by a set time, catch that before candle lighting rather than after.

Building Specialists

Alarm Systems for Borough Park’s Architecture

The houses on Borough Park’s numbered streets were built in waves from the turn of the twentieth century onward, and many have been rebuilt, raised or extended since the 1992 zoning change. That mix decides where sensors go, how cable gets there and how much of the work can be wireless.

Attached and semi-attached houses

Party walls on one or both sides, a stoop or porch in front and every other opening at the back. Front windows face a busy street; rear windows, yard doors and basement entrances face yards that only neighbors can see. The rear gets the most protection.

Two-family houses

An owner’s duplex over or under a rented floor, often with a shared front door and separate inner doors. Two partitions, or two systems, so each household arms its own space on its own schedule.

Enlarged and rebuilt houses

Houses raised by a floor or more, pushed back into the yard, or replaced outright. New openings, new stairs, new roofs and often a new basement layout that the original alarm never covered.

Apartment buildings and co-ops

Walk-ups and elevator buildings on and off the avenues. Unit alarms on wireless sensors, kept off common areas unless the board approves, with no holes in shared walls.

Stores with homes above

Ground-floor shops on 13th, 16th and 18th Avenues with apartments upstairs. The store and the apartments are separate systems with separate call lists, never one keypad for both.

Shuls, yeshivas and halls

Buildings used in shifts — early minyanim, school days, evening shiurim, simchas — with offices and storerooms that need protecting while the main spaces are in use.

How the work changes from one Borough Park building to the next

Attached houses. Party walls are usually solid masonry, and two or three courses of brick are a lot for a wireless signal to pass through, but the signal rarely has to: the panel goes in the middle of the house, near the stair, and the farthest sensors are at the back. We test signal strength from every sensor position before fixing anything, and where a basement or rear extension is too far for a reliable signal, we add a wired zone or a repeater rather than accept a weak link. On the rear wall we put contacts on every opening sash, glass-break detectors on large fixed panes and sliding doors, and a contact on the basement door, which is the opening owners most often forget.

Porches and stoops. Many houses have an enclosed front porch or a vestibule in front of the main door. If the porch is part of the house, its outer door becomes the perimeter and the inner door a second line; if it is open, the main door is the perimeter and the porch is outside. On Shabbos the porch is also where packages sit — the 38th Street thefts reported by the Shomrim in 2025 were packages — so a camera covering the porch is often more useful there than another sensor.

Enlargements. When a house has been enlarged since the alarm went in, we walk it with the zone list in hand. New rooms are usually finished, so we use wireless sensors where opening plaster would be a waste and pull wire only where the walls are open or the run is short and hidden. The zone list is rewritten to match the house as it is now. If the enlargement is still under construction, we can pre-wire the openings before the walls close; it is the cheapest point in a house’s life to put an alarm in.

For an alarm that means long, predictable closures, a closing routine squeezed into a busy Friday, and owners who will not be checking their phones while the store is shut.

Under the elevated West End Line, with stations at Fort Hamilton Parkway, 50th Street and 55th Street; vibration from the trains is something we account for when choosing glass-break and shock sensors.

McDonald Avenue

The eastern edge of the neighborhood, with the F train of the IND Culver Line running along it. Businesses here that do not close for Shabbos need ordinary nightly schedules instead.

For a store, the questions are practical. Who closes on Friday, and how do they arm the system? Most of our commercial panels give every employee their own code, so the closing report says who armed it and when. If nobody has armed it by a time you choose — say an hour before candle lighting — the system can arm itself or send a “failed to close” alert to the owner and a manager while there is still time to send someone back. Delivery doors and roof hatches get contacts, because a back door left on the latch is the classic way a store is found open on Saturday night. Where money is counted, a holdup button goes under the counter and reports silently.

For electronics, jewelry and silver, we add glass-break detection on display windows, a motion detector covering the stockroom, and a shock sensor or contact on the safe. Stores under the West End elevated get sensors tested with a train going past during the test, so a passing train does not set off a glass-break detector at 2 a.m. For an office upstairs, the alarm usually only needs the entrance and the files room; for a yeshiva office, add the room where tuition payments and records are kept.

Rules that apply to Borough Park alarms

  • Licensed installers. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Its licensing page lists 81 hours of qualifying education in five modules and fingerprinting for employees. Ask any installer for the license before they start.
  • Siren time limit. NYC Administrative Code §24-238: a building’s audible burglar alarm must stop by itself within fifteen minutes.
  • Detectors in rented homes. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors there and in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning equipment, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room.
  • Exits stay exits. An intrusion alarm never locks a required exit. Store and shul exits keep their panic hardware; we monitor them with contacts and, where wanted, a local exit alarm.
  • Fire alarm work is separate. A commercial fire alarm system is designed, filed and inspected under FDNY and Buildings rules. Through fire alarm installation in Borough Park we handle that separately from the burglar alarm.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Borough Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Borough Park callers bring to us, grouped by subject and answered in full. Where a question touches on what is permitted on Shabbos or Yom Tov, the answer describes what the equipment can do; the decision about using it belongs to you and your rav.

Shabbos and Yom Tov

We keep the alarm on all of Shabbos. How do we come in from shul without setting it off?

Take the door you use out of the Shabbos arming. The usual arrangement is that the front or side door your family walks through is on its own zone, and that zone is either bypassed for the whole of Shabbos by the schedule or placed in a partition that is never armed from Friday afternoon to Saturday night. Everything else — the rear windows, the yard door, the basement door, the extension — stays armed throughout. Rabbi Bitton’s overview describes bypassing one family door in just this way while the rest of the house stays armed. If the door you use has glass in it, a glass-break detector in the hall still covers someone smashing the pane, without reacting when the door opens normally. If interior motion detectors are part of your Shabbos plan, they are set either off or on a schedule that keeps them off while people are up and about. We set this up on a weekday, test it with you by walking in and out of every door, and leave a card by the panel saying exactly which openings are protected on Shabbos.

Our keypad beeps and the door sensor light flashes every time someone walks in. Can that be stopped for Shabbos and Yom Tov?

Usually yes, but on most panels it is a standing setting rather than something that switches on Friday. Door chime is a per-zone or per-keypad option: we can turn it off for the family door, or for all doors, permanently. Voice announcements can be turned off the same way. The flashing light on a wireless door sensor is set in that sensor’s programming; in the Surety forum thread about a Qolsys panel with PowerG sensors, turning the LED off at sensor level is what worked, and the support reply said the panel cannot schedule changes to its own sound settings. So the practical approach is to decide once which beeps and lights you never want, turn them off, and keep the useful ones — such as the entry-delay warning on armed doors — only where they matter. Older keypads that cannot silence chime per zone are often the reason families leave a system off; replacing just the keypad can solve it.

If the siren goes off on Friday night and nobody in the house will touch the keypad, how long will it ring?

Not more than fifteen minutes if the system is set up legally. New York City’s Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of stopping, and to stop, by itself within fifteen minutes of activation. We set the bell cutoff inside that limit on every panel and write the setting on the handover sheet. What happens next depends on the panel: most will go on reporting to the monitoring station and will sound again only if another zone trips. That is the other half of the answer — the monitoring station should already know, from your Shabbos call list, whom to call and whether to send the police without reaching anyone. Rabbi Bitton’s overview discusses what a household may do about an alarm that will not stop; ask your rav, but a correctly set cutoff means the question rarely arises.

Who should the central station call when an alarm trips on Shabbos, and what happens if nobody answers?

Write a separate Shabbos and Yom Tov call list with people who will actually answer: a neighbor or relative who is not keeping Shabbos, or someone you trust in another neighborhood, plus clear instructions on what the station should do if nobody picks up. The choice is usually between dispatching the police on any alarm, dispatching only when two different zones trip or a glass-break follows a door, and calling only. Peninei Halakha mentions differing views on the monitoring staff who handle calls on Shabbos and says that if the service calls to ask whether a dispatch is needed, one should answer to prevent an unnecessary trip; whether that applies to you is a question for your rav. We put the agreed instructions in writing with the monitoring provider, then send a test signal on a weekday and listen to the station read the instructions back.

We go away for Pesach. How do we leave the house armed for a week and still know it is all right?

Arm it fully — Away mode, every zone including interior motion — when you leave, and switch the call list to the people who will answer while you are gone. A schedule is not needed for those days, because nobody is coming in. Before you go, check the backup battery and make sure the communicator is cellular rather than relying on a router that may reboot, and put a light timer on in a front room so the house does not look dark for a week. If you will look at the app on Chol Hamoed, check before you leave that the zones are named by room so a notification reading “rear kitchen window” means something. Burglaries like the 2014 rear-window series happened in time windows of days and weeks; an alarm with a working siren and a station that knows whom to call takes those windows away.

Rosh Hashanah ran from Friday to Sunday this year. Can one schedule cover a three-day closure without anybody touching the panel?

Yes, if the Yom Tov days are entered in advance as exceptions to the weekly schedule. A weekly rule knows only Friday and Saturday; Rosh Hashanah 2026, which began before sundown on Friday, September 11 and ended after nightfall on Sunday, September 13, needed the Sunday to be treated like Shabbos as well. Some panels and platforms accept holiday dates or a separate schedule that overrides the weekly one; where yours does not, the days are covered by a second schedule we set in advance. When we hand a system over we enter the coming year’s Yom Tov dates, which you confirm against your own calendar, and we can update them every fall. For stores the same exceptions keep the alarm armed across a closure that runs into a third day, so a manager does not have to come in to re-arm it.

The house itself

The 2014 burglar came in through rear windows. Which openings at the back of an attached Borough Park house should be on the alarm?

All of them, but in order of risk. First, every ground-floor and basement window and door at the back, including small bathroom and laundry windows; then any window reachable from a yard structure, an extension roof, a fire escape or a neighbor’s wall; then upper-floor rear windows. Large fixed panes and sliding doors get a glass-break detector as well as contacts, because a contact only knows when a sash opens. Basement doors to the yard are the opening owners most often leave off. In the eight burglaries CBS New York and Yeshiva World News reported in August 2014, entry was through a rear window each time, and silverware and jewelry were the main losses; the back of an attached house is where nobody on the street can see what is happening. A camera covering the yard does not replace any of those sensors, but it shows what happened and helps the Shomrim and the precinct.

We enlarged the house and added a floor and a rear extension. Does the old alarm cover the new rooms?

Almost certainly not, unless someone added zones when the work finished. An alarm knows only the sensors programmed into it, and building work often removes old contacts with old window frames. We walk the house with the panel’s zone list, mark every opening that exists now, and add sensors for the ones that are missing — usually wireless in finished rooms, wired where walls or ceilings are open. Older panels may run out of zones on a big enlarged house; then we either add an expander or replace the panel. Wikipedia notes that Borough Park has had more private construction permits than any other residential neighborhood in Brooklyn since 1990, and the Brooklyn Eagle described a two-family house on 44th Street being taken to five stories; houses like that are the ones where an old system is most likely to have gaps.

Our sukkah balcony door is open for a week every fall. How do we keep it on the alarm the rest of the year?

Give that door its own zone and its own name, and bypass it on purpose for Sukkos rather than letting it fault the whole system. A guide from brooklynjewish.org notes that sukkahs go up on sidewalks and balconies across the neighborhood, so balcony and rear-deck doors that are used constantly for eight or nine days and rarely the rest of the year are common. Before Sukkos, the bypass is set for those dates; after Sukkos, the zone returns to normal automatically if the panel supports dated bypasses, or on a weekday visit or phone call if it does not. Remember the sukkah itself: a structure on a balcony or flat roof can give someone a step up to a window. While it stands, a glass-break detector in the room behind that window is worth having.

We rent the ground floor of our two-family. Can the tenant have an alarm separate from ours?

Yes, and in many cases it is better. If you want one system, we partition the panel so the tenant’s floor and the tenant’s door arm and disarm with the tenant’s code, on the tenant’s schedule, and your Shabbos arrangement never touches their door. If the tenant keeps a different practice on Shabbos, or you do not want to manage their codes, two separate systems with separate monitoring accounts are simpler. Shared spaces — the front hall, the basement, the yard door — need a decision about whose system covers them. As the owner of a non-owner-occupied two-family, you also have HPD duties for carbon monoxide detectors, and gas detectors where there is gas piping, which we check during the visit; those are separate from the burglar alarm.

Packages keep disappearing from our porch over Shabbos. Can the alarm do anything about it?

Not much on its own. An intrusion alarm protects openings into the house; a package on the porch is outside them. What helps is a camera that sees the porch and the walk, recording continuously, so there is footage to give the Shomrim or the precinct afterward — the 2025 arrest for packages taken over Shabbos in the 38th Street area came after Shomrim volunteers reviewed camera footage. If your porch is enclosed and part of the house, a contact on its outer door can be zoned so it alerts the monitoring station when opened during set hours. Our Borough Park camera installations usually cover the porch and the yard together.

Stores and institutions

Our store on 13th Avenue closes before candle lighting on Friday. What should arm it, and who gets the alert?

Ideally the closing manager arms it with their own code on the way out, and the system confirms it by sending a closing report to you and a second person. Behind that sits a safety net: a scheduled auto-arm at a fixed time after your latest possible close, or a “failed to close” alert if it has not been armed by then, early enough on Friday that someone can still go back. For the closure itself, the monitoring call list should name someone who will answer on Shabbos, and the dispatch rule should say plainly what to do if nobody does. Stores on 13th Avenue that sell silver, electronics or jewelry should add glass-break detection on display windows and a contact or shock sensor on the safe. A back or delivery door left on the latch is the commonest way a closed store is found open; a contact on it means the panel will not arm until it is shut, and you will know before candle lighting rather than after Havdalah.

Our shul has a small office, a sefarim room and a basement simcha hall. How do you alarm a building that is open at different hours every day?

Split it into areas and arm each on the hours it is empty. The office, where records and money are kept, can stay armed outside office hours, including on Shabbos, because nobody goes in. The sefarim room may be open for learning at night and closed by day. The hall is armed except during simchas and events, which a gabbai or office manager schedules from the app on a weekday. The main sanctuary’s doors are usually left to the shul’s own opening routine. Peninei Halakha mentions an aron kodesh connected to an alarm and says that connecting and disconnecting it for the Torah reading is done through grama, with a key that takes effect a few minutes later; that is a question for your rav, and where an ark is to be protected we wire it as a separate zone so it can be arranged exactly as he directs. Exits keep their panic hardware throughout.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Borough Park

We could not open Google’s AI Overview or other AI answer boxes from our build environment, so the claims below are not quotations. They are the generic statements that appear again and again in automated answers and national advice pages about home alarms, and each one meets a different reality on Borough Park’s streets.

“Arm your alarm every time you leave and disarm it when you come home.”

This is the basic instruction in every alarm manual, and for six days a week it is right. It assumes the person coming home will tap a code, press a fob or open an app. On Shabbos and Yom Tov many Borough Park households will do none of those, and the instruction has nothing to say about what happens then.

The result we see is one of two habits: the system is left off from Friday to Saturday night, or it is left on and the family lives with alarms they cannot answer. Both defeat the purpose. The Yeshiva World News report of the 2023 arrest quoted a burglar’s understanding that residents “let their guard down on Shabbos”; leaving the alarm off is exactly that.

The fix is a plan for the day itself: perimeter armed by schedule, the family door out of the arming, interior sensors handled as your rav permits, and the call list written for who will answer. Set up once on a weekday, it runs every week without anyone touching the keypad.

“Motion detectors are the most important sensor in the house.”

National guides often build a starter kit around one or two motion sensors because they cover a room cheaply. In a house that is full of people for twenty-five hours, a motion sensor that is armed is useless, and the halachic overviews we cite show that households treat even disarmed sensors differently.

In an attached Borough Park house the more important sensors are on the openings: contacts on every rear window and door, glass-break detectors on large panes, and a contact on the basement door. Those can stay armed all of Shabbos while the family moves freely inside, because nobody opens a rear bedroom window at 2 a.m. on Friday night.

Motion detectors still have a place, for the weeks the house is empty and for weekday nights. We put them on their own partition or relay so they can be off whenever the household wants, without weakening the perimeter.

“Professional monitoring means police are dispatched within minutes.”

Most monitoring stations try to reach someone on the call list before dispatching, to cut false alarms. If every person on the list is an observant family member whose phone is off for Shabbos, the station works through the list, reaches nobody, and then follows whatever default its rules set.

Response time is therefore partly a question of the instructions, not only the station. A Shabbos list that names a neighbor or relative who will answer, and a written instruction such as “dispatch without verification if a glass-break and a door trip together,” changes what happens at 11 p.m. on Friday.

We set that up with the provider, have the instructions read back, and send a test signal. Peninei Halakha’s note about answering a service’s call to prevent an unnecessary trip is one more reason to decide in advance, with your rav, who should be on that list.

“Wireless DIY systems install in an hour and work in any home.”

A plug-in hub and stick-on sensors do install quickly, and in a small apartment they can be fine. In an attached house with brick party walls, a basement, a rear extension and a raised top floor, the farthest sensors can be at the edge of a hub’s range, and nothing tells you a sensor is marginal until it misses a signal.

The bigger gap is coverage. A kit sized for “an average home” rarely includes enough contacts for every rear and basement opening of an enlarged Borough Park house, and its default settings leave chimes and LEDs on.

If you already own a DIY system, we can install it properly for a labor-only fee, test signal strength from each position, and add what it is missing. If it cannot do what your Shabbos plan needs, we will tell you before we start.

“Alarm systems cost a set monthly fee.”

Advertised prices usually bundle equipment into a multi-year monitoring contract, so the monthly figure hides the hardware cost. Reddit threads in r/homesecurity are full of people asking whether $40 to $50 a month on a three- or five-year contract is fair, and the honest answer is that it depends on what was financed.

On our Brooklyn list the equipment and installation are priced up front — a basic system from $600, complete home protection from $2,500 — and monitoring starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract. Borough Park pays the Brooklyn base rate, with no surcharge.

The extra time a Shabbos plan takes, mostly programming and testing schedules, partitions and call lists, is part of the installation price. It is not a monthly fee and does not need a service plan to keep it working.

“A loud outdoor siren scares burglars away.”

Sirens do drive intruders out, but the advice usually ignores the neighbors. On an attached block, a front siren that rings for a long time on a Friday night alarms the whole street, and nobody in the house may be willing to stop it.

New York City already limits the problem: Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building’s burglar alarm to stop by itself within fifteen minutes. We set the cutoff well inside that and favor an indoor siren near the bedrooms, with an outdoor siren at the back if wanted.

The siren is the last line. What actually protects the house is the monitoring station knowing whom to call and the rear openings being covered, so that the alarm is real when it sounds.

“Newer houses come with alarms already installed.”

Some new buildings are pre-wired, but in Borough Park much of the “new” housing is enlarged or rebuilt older houses. The original alarm, if there was one, covered the house as it was, and construction often removes contacts with old windows.

Wikipedia notes the neighborhood’s unusually high number of private construction permits since 1990, and the Brooklyn Eagle’s 2016 example of a two-family on 44th Street being enlarged to five stories shows the scale. A five-story house protected by a panel programmed for two stories has gaps by definition.

Ask for a zone list and walk the house against it. If openings are missing, or the zone names refer to rooms that no longer exist, the system needs extending before it can be trusted.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Write Down the Household’s Week Before Choosing a Sensor

This describes how we plan alarms for observant households in general, not any particular customer. At the first visit we ask for ten minutes with whoever runs the house and fill in a simple table: Friday afternoon, Friday night, Shabbos morning, Shabbos afternoon, Motzei Shabbos, and a line for Yom Tov. For each, who is home, which door they use, whether anyone sleeps on the ground floor, and whether guests come. Then we ask which devices the household is comfortable with on those days — none at all, or a schedule set in advance — and whose guidance they follow, so that if a question comes up we know to say “ask your rav” rather than guess.

Only after that table exists do we mark the openings on a sketch of the house and assign them: armed all of Shabbos, never armed on Shabbos, armed only while sleeping, armed only when away. The hardware choice follows almost automatically. It is the step that keeps a motion sensor from being put where nobody will ever be able to arm it, and it makes the plan easy to explain to the rest of the household, because it reads like their week rather than like an alarm manual.

Can you take over the alarm a previous owner left in a house we just bought on one of the numbered streets?
+

Will installing the alarm in an attached house mean opening walls we just renovated?
+

Not if you do not want it to. In finished rooms we use wireless contacts, glass-break and motion detectors, which mount on the frame or wall with small screws or adhesive, and we only run wire where it can go through a basement ceiling, a closet stacked above another closet, or the space beside the stair. Before fixing anything, we test the wireless signal from the farthest positions, usually the rear extension and the top floor, because brick party walls and long attached houses can weaken it. Where a signal is marginal we add a repeater or a short wired run rather than leave a weak sensor. If walls are still open from the renovation, tell us early: pre-wiring then is the neatest option.

Do you set the system up in English only, or can the app and the keypad prompts suit a household that mostly speaks Yiddish?
+

Keypad prompts and app menus come in whatever languages the manufacturer supports, so we cannot promise a Yiddish interface. What we can control is everything we type in ourselves. Zone names, user names and the printed card by the panel can be written the way your household speaks about the house, in English letters or however you prefer, so that a notification reads like the room you call it. On many keypads the voice prompts can be turned off entirely, which some families prefer anyway. If a grandparent or a helper will use the system, we train them separately on the few buttons they need.

Can our old system’s motion detectors be taken off a schedule without replacing the panel?
+

Often, yes, depending on the panel. If it supports partitions, the motion detectors can be moved to their own area that is simply never armed on Shabbos or Yom Tov while the doors and windows stay armed. If it does not, a relay can cut power to the motion detectors on a timer or from the keypad on a weekday; NAPCO’s technical FAQ describes exactly that approach with a relay module labeled “Sabbath Mode.” Either way the change is made once and nobody has to touch anything on Friday. If the panel is too old to partition and has no relay output, a new keypad or panel is usually cheaper than a workaround. Whether motion detectors should be off on Shabbos at all is a question for your rav.

Is a wireless system reliable in a house with thick brick party walls on both sides?
+

Mostly, because the signal rarely has to go through a party wall: the panel sits in the middle of the house and the sensors are in the same house, front to back and floor to floor. The difficult paths are long ones, from a central panel to a rear extension, a deep basement or a raised top floor, through several interior masonry walls and floors. We test the signal strength from every sensor position before mounting it, and the panel can show it afterward. Where a reading is weak, a repeater halfway, a second wireless receiver, or a short wired zone fixes it. A neighbor’s system on the other side of the party wall does not interfere with yours.

Do we need a separate fire alarm, or do the smoke detectors connect to the burglar alarm?
+

In a one- or two-family house, monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors can connect to the same panel as the burglar alarm, so a fire on Shabbos is reported to the monitoring station even if nobody touches anything. That is in addition to, not instead of, the detectors the law requires: HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors, and gas detectors where there is gas piping, in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied, and smoke detectors in every unit of a multiple dwelling. Stores, shuls, halls and larger apartment buildings may need a commercial fire alarm system designed and filed separately, which we handle through our fire alarm service rather than by adding detectors to an intrusion panel.

How long does an install take, and can the work avoid Friday afternoons and Erev Yom Tov?
+

A basic system in an apartment or small house is usually one day; a complete system in a large semi-attached or enlarged house can take two days, mostly because of the number of rear openings and the time spent testing schedules. We schedule Sunday to Thursday and Friday mornings, and we do not book work for Friday afternoons or Erev Yom Tov unless you ask us to. The house is left fully working at the end of each day, never half-installed over Shabbos. If something cannot be finished before Shabbos, the unfinished zone is clearly bypassed and the rest of the system is armed on its schedule.

What should we ask any alarm installer before letting them in?
+

Ask for the New York State license number; the Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, and its page lists the training required. Ask for a certificate of insurance. Ask who will hold the installer code, and get it in writing that you can have it if you change companies. Ask whether monitoring requires a multi-year contract and what happens to the equipment if you cancel. And ask them to show you, before installation, how the system will behave from Friday afternoon to Saturday night: which doors are armed, what beeps, who the station calls. An installer who has not thought about that is planning a weekday system. Our license number is #12000287431.
