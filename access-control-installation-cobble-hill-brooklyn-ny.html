The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Properties Need Access Control

Cobble Hill is small. The Encyclopedia of New York City counts about forty blocks, and the usual boundaries are Atlantic Avenue to the north, Court Street to the east, Degraw Street to the south and the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway to the west, with Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill, Carroll Gardens and the Columbia Street Waterfront on each side. Most of it was built between the 1830s and 1880; the name itself was revived in 1959 from an old map. Roughly twenty-two of those blocks sit inside the Cobble Hill Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in December 1969 and extended in June 1988.

What makes this neighbourhood different for entry control is not the brownstone count, though there are plenty. It is how many of its doors are shared by design. In the late 1870s the philanthropist Alfred Tredway White built two experiments in decent working-class housing here: the Home and Tower Buildings on Hicks Street, organised around large courtyards and reached by open-air stairs and balconies instead of dark interior halls, and the Workingmen’s Cottages of Warren Place, a single gated mews running from Warren Street to Baltic Street with 34 small brick houses facing a private courtyard. The towers were restored between 1978 and 1986 and began converting to condominiums in 2008. A century and a half later those buildings still put a shared gate or a shared courtyard between the street and every front door.

The second pattern is reuse. St. Peter’s Church on Hicks Street and its school became condominiums; the former Strong Place Baptist Church at Degraw Street became apartments in 2010; and the designation report notes low-rise apartment buildings put up before the 1920s among the houses. Each conversion created a new association or landlord who inherited entrances designed for a congregation, a classroom or a single family. And in the north of the neighbourhood, around Henry, Hicks, Amity and Pacific Streets, the Long Island College Hospital campus closed in August 2014. Fortis Property Group’s River Park plan turned parts of it into condominiums, starting with the Polhemus buildings on Amity Street and 5 River Park on Henry Street, and the site at 354 Hicks Street is now being built by Madison Realty Capital as a 20-storey tower with 34 condominiums above five floors of medical space and an attended garage, with completion anticipated in 2027.

Those three patterns — shared gates, converted institutions and mixed-use towers — raise the same practical questions again and again. Who is the administrator when a sponsor still controls the board? How do thirty-odd households agree on one gate code policy? How does a garage attendant reach a lobby without reaching the apartments above? How long may the association keep a departed renter’s name in the system? The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) answers part of the last question for class A multiple dwellings: authentication data generally goes within 90 days, and a departed tenant’s reference data no later than 90 days after they permanently leave.

The commercial edges add a fourth set of doors. Court Street and Smith Street are the two main shopping streets, where generations-old family businesses such as Italian meat markets and barber shops sit beside new restaurants and cafés; northern Smith Street has been called Brooklyn’s Restaurant Row since the late 1990s and became a weekend nightlife strip in the late 2000s. Atlantic Avenue along the northern edge has one of the city’s largest collections of Middle Eastern shops, some open for decades. Late closing times, early deliveries and staff who change each season make credential turnover the main job there. Package theft also turns up in the 76th Precinct blotter: one Brooklyn Paper roundup listed a parcel taken from a building’s vestibule on Amity Street near Henry Street in the afternoon.

Five things a Cobble Hill building should settle before choosing hardware

  1. Who is the administrator of record? A named person or role — association secretary, managing agent, building owner — written into the minutes or the management agreement. Without one, credentials accumulate and nobody removes them.
  2. How many groups share the door? Households, renters, staff, a commercial tenant, contractors, visitors. Each group becomes a set of credentials with its own hours; the count decides whether a keypad lock is enough or a controller is needed.
  3. What happens when someone leaves? A sale, a lease ending, a cleaner replaced, a contractor finished. Decide who tells the administrator and how quickly, and put expiry dates on anyone whose end date is already known.
  4. What will be recorded, and for how long? If the building is a class A multiple dwelling, the Tenant Data Privacy Law sets the outer limits. Even where it does not apply, a short written policy avoids arguments later.
  5. Who pays for which door? In condos and mixed-use buildings, gates, lobbies and garages can belong to different budgets. Agreeing this before the quote saves rewriting it afterwards.

We ask these five questions at the first call. None of them needs a technical answer, and boards that settle them before the walkthrough usually get a simpler, cheaper system, because the hardware no longer has to make up for decisions nobody has made.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Cobble Hill’s Architecture

Cobble Hill’s building stock is older and more varied than its size suggests. The district designation report lists Greek Revival and Italianate rowhouses alongside Romanesque Revival, Gothic Revival, Queen Anne, Second Empire and neo-Grec work. For entry control, though, the useful categories are about who shares the entrance, not the style of the cornice.

Gated mews and cottage rows

Warren Place is the clearest example: one gate, a long private courtyard, dozens of separate owners. The gate is the only door every household shares, so it carries the whole system. We size it for heavy use, put the controller where the association can reach it, and give each cottage its own group of credentials.

Courtyard complexes with open stairs

The Home and Tower Buildings were built without enclosed lobbies; residents climb outdoor stairs and walk along balconies. Control therefore sits at the courtyard gates and street passages rather than at a vestibule door, and readers have to cope with weather on every side.

Converted churches and schools

Former sanctuaries and classrooms have big, heavy doors, odd thresholds and entrances placed for crowds rather than residents. These conversions often rely on a side or rear door as the everyday entrance, with the grand front door kept for exit only.

New towers on the hospital campus

The River Park buildings and 354 Hicks Street bring attended lobbies, package rooms, storage, bike rooms, amenity floors and, at 354 Hicks, medical floors and a garage. Here the question is zoning many user groups on one system, not finding wall space for a reader.

Split rowhouses and small walk-ups

Houses divided into a few flats and pre-1920s low-rise apartment buildings: a street door, a vestibule door and sometimes a garden gate, with an owner who may also live there. Small numbers of users, but the privacy and lock rules still apply once there are three or more units.

Ground-floor shops with homes above

Along Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue, a storefront door, a back or cellar door and a separate street door to the apartments upstairs, often with a different person responsible for each.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue

Commercial Cobble Hill is three streets and a corner. Each has its own rhythm, and the rhythm sets the credential schedule.

  • Court Street — the eastern edge and the older shopping street, with family-run food shops and barbers next to newer boutiques and cafés. Typical doors: a glass storefront door in an aluminium frame, a rear or cellar door for stock, and a separate street door to apartments above. The landmark bank building at 130 Court Street, on the Atlantic Avenue corner, shows how much of this street is historic fabric.
  • Smith Street — the northern blocks are the “Restaurant Row” that grew from the late 1990s, with bars added in the late 2000s. Typical doors: kitchen back doors, garden doors for outdoor seating, and staff who close after midnight.
  • Atlantic Avenue — the northern edge, home to long-established Middle Eastern groceries and shops and covered by the Atlantic Avenue Business Improvement District, which the City Planning Commission approved in January 2011 for the stretch from Fourth Avenue to the expressway. Typical doors: deep shops with a loading door at the back and storage below.
  • The hospital corner — NYU Cobble Hill’s emergency department and the medical floors planned at 354 Hicks Street put clinical staff, patients and residents in the same few blocks. Clinics there run their own access policies; our role is to make sure the residential side and the medical side do not share credentials by accident.
  • Offices above shops — second-floor offices, studios and practices over Court Street and Atlantic Avenue storefronts, sharing a narrow street door with flats or with each other.

Across all of them, the most common request is not “stop break-ins” but “stop handing out keys”. A restaurant with a dozen part-time staff cannot rekey every time one leaves; a shop that has been open for decades rarely knows how many copies of its back-door key exist. A reader or keypad turns that into a list someone can read and edit.

Rules that shape commercial and residential doors here

  • Egress first. NYC Building Code §1010.1.9.8 lets a sensor-released electric lock go on an egress door only if a sensor on the inside releases it, loss of power releases it, a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” device 40 to 48 inches above the floor and within 5 feet of the door cuts power directly and keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and a fire alarm or sprinkler activation releases it. §1010.1.9.9 allows a magnetic lock released by hardware on the door itself that works with one hand. Both call for locking devices listed to UL 294.
  • Self-locking entrances. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, and a two-way intercom with remote release where there are eight or more apartments. A reader does not replace the intercom requirement.
  • Apartment doors. Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 requires a key lock and at least one key for each dwelling-unit entrance door, and in class A multiple dwellings a heavy-duty latch set, a deadbolt with a key outside and a thumb-turn inside, and a chain door guard.
  • Tenant data. Local Law 63 of 2021 covers class A multiple dwellings that use a smart access system: a written policy, express consent, minimum data, purging on a schedule, no sale of data, and no requirement that a tenant use the system to enter their own apartment.
  • Landmarks. Inside the historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s permit guidebook lets staff approve intercoms and similar devices in or beside a door opening, attached through mortar joints or plain surfaces rather than ornament, with conduit concealed. Interior work generally needs no LPC permit.

This is a plain-language summary to help you plan, not legal advice. For a condo or co-op, the declaration, bylaws and any alteration agreement also apply; we work to whatever your board’s counsel and managing agent require.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up most often in Cobble Hill, grouped by the kind of door they are about. Each answer is written for this neighbourhood’s buildings; where a rule or fact comes from an outside source, the source is listed at the bottom of the page.

Gates, Mews and Courtyards Shared by Many Households

A shared gate is the hardest door in the neighbourhood to manage, because nobody owns it outright and everybody uses it.

More than thirty households share the single gate into our mews. Who should be allowed to add and delete credentials on it?

Fewer people than you think: two administrators, chosen by the association and named in its minutes, with a third login held in reserve by whoever keeps the association’s records. Every additional person who can add a credential is another way for a fob to appear that nobody voted on. On a gate like the one at Warren Place, where 34 houses face one courtyard, we set up one administrator account per role rather than per house, and give each household its own group so a household can ask for a change but cannot make one.

Each house then gets a fixed allowance — for example four fobs or phone credentials and two codes — recorded against the house number in the system rather than against a person’s name alone. When a house is sold or a tenant leaves, one instruction to the administrators disables that whole group and new ones are issued. Nobody else’s credential changes.

The software choice follows from this. A volunteer committee changes every year or two, so we favour systems whose admin rights can be transferred by email invitation and whose logs export to a spreadsheet, and we leave a one-page note in the association’s files saying exactly how to hand the role over.

Our courtyard can be entered from two different streets. Should both gates keep the same hours?

Usually not. In a through-block layout one gate tends to be the everyday entrance, near the subway or the shops, and the other is used mostly for deliveries, bins or a short cut. Giving both the same hours means the quieter gate is unlocked for hours when nobody is watching it. We normally set the busier gate to credential-only at all hours and the quieter one to credential-only too, but with a short daytime window for scheduled services if the association wants one.

If both gates must stay in step — because residents want to walk straight through — we wire them to the same controller and the same schedule, and add a door-position contact on each so the system reports whichever one is left open. A gate that is propped is the most common gap in a shared courtyard, far more common than a forced one.

Running cable between the two gates is the main cost driver. Where the courtyard can be crossed underground or along an existing wall, a single controller is simplest; where it cannot, each gate gets its own networked controller and both report to one account.

Our buildings use outdoor stairs and balconies instead of interior hallways. Where does a reader go when there is no lobby?

At the edge of the complex rather than at each stair. In a courtyard plan like the Home and Tower Buildings on Hicks Street, the natural control points are the street passages and gates that lead into the courtyard. Once someone is through those, every stair is reachable, so a reader on each stair adds cost without changing who can get in.

Readers at those points live outdoors on every side, so we use weather-rated readers or keypads, mount them under any overhang the masonry offers, and keep electronics such as the controller and power supply indoors in a cellar or service room. Cable runs inside existing conduit or along the line of the building rather than across open courtyard paving.

If the board later wants to separate the stairs — for example to keep a commercial tenant out of a residential stair — a reader can be added at the foot of that stair and placed on the same controller. We plan cable spare capacity for that at the first install so the courtyard does not have to be opened twice.

Condo Conversions and New Boards

Churches, a school, worker housing and now hospital buildings have all become condominiums here. Each handover raises the same access questions.

Our building used to be a church. The developer left a door panel nobody can program. What should the board ask for at handover?

Four things, in writing: the administrator login for the access software with the right to change its owner; the name and serial number of the controller and the licence it runs under; the list of credentials currently active, with who they were issued to; and the installer’s wiring notes showing where each door’s cable runs and where the power supply sits. Without the first two, you do not own the system in any practical sense.

Conversions of large old buildings often end up with the everyday entrance at a side or rear door, while the original front doors are kept mainly for exit. That means the panel you can see may not be the only device. We walk every door, find each lock’s power source and check what the fire alarm does to it, because a converted sanctuary or school usually has more exits than a purpose-built condo.

If the developer cannot or will not hand over credentials, the controller can usually be reset and re-enrolled, or replaced, but that is a cost the board should raise before the sponsor leaves. We can write a short list of what is missing for the board’s counsel to put to the sponsor.

The sponsor still controls our condo board for now. Who actually owns the access control system?

The hardware attached to common doors normally belongs to the condominium as part of its common elements, but the software account may sit in the sponsor’s or the managing agent’s name, and the account is what controls who gets in. Your declaration and offering plan govern the ownership question; your counsel can read them. Our part is practical: we show the board exactly whose name is on the account today.

Where the sponsor will run the board for a while yet, a sensible interim step is to add a unit-owner administrator alongside the sponsor’s login and to switch on the audit trail, so every credential added or removed is recorded with who did it. When control passes, the sponsor’s login is removed and the audit trail stays.

We do not take sides between a sponsor and owners. We install, document and hand over to whoever the board’s resolution names, and we keep that resolution with our job file.

Several units in our condo are rented out. Does the smart-access privacy law treat renters differently from owners?

Local Law 63 of 2021 applies to a class A multiple dwelling that uses a smart access system, and it protects tenants and the users they authorise. In a condo with rented units, the safest course is to apply the law’s rules to everyone: one written privacy policy, express consent from every user, the same minimum data and the same purge schedule. Running two policies for owners and renters creates mistakes that only the renters can sue over.

Two parts matter most in practice. Renters cannot be required to use the smart system to get into their own apartment, so unit doors keep a working key. And when a renter permanently moves out, the law requires their reference data — the credential and the details it is checked against — to be removed no later than 90 days afterwards, so the move-out checklist needs a line for the access system.

We set the system so a unit owner can request credentials for their tenant through the administrators, and so the tenant’s group carries the lease dates. When the lease ends, the group expires automatically and the purge timer starts. This is a summary to help planning, not legal advice; the HPD page and the law’s text are listed in our sources.

Clinics, Garages and Residents Under One Roof

The rebuilt hospital corner brings something Cobble Hill did not have before: towers where medical tenants, parking and homes share lobbies, lifts and service doors.

Our new building on the old hospital campus has medical offices below the apartments and a garage underneath. How do you keep three groups apart on one system?

By zones and roles, not by separate systems. Each door belongs to a zone — residential lobby, clinic entrance, garage, shared service corridor — and each credential belongs to a group that opens a defined set of zones at defined times. A clinic employee’s card opens the clinic entrance and the staff corridor during clinic hours; a resident’s fob opens the residential lobby, the garage door and the amenity floor; a garage attendant’s card opens the garage and the route to the lobby desk.

The administrative side is split the same way. The residential board, through its managing agent, administers residential groups and sees residential logs; the medical tenant administers its own staff; building engineering holds a master role for maintenance. Nobody needs to see another group’s comings and goings to do their job, and on the residential side the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what should be collected anyway.

Shared doors — usually a service entrance or a loading area — are where most of the planning time goes, because they need a written owner. At 354 Hicks Street, which CityRealty describes as 34 condominiums above five floors of medical space with an attended garage serving both, that list of shared doors is the first thing we would ask the developer’s team for.

The garage attendant needs to reach the residents’ lobby but not their floors. How is that set up?

The attendant’s credential is given the garage door, the door from the garage into the building, and the lobby, and nothing above it. If the elevators are on the access system, the attendant’s group gets the lobby floor only; residents’ groups get the lobby, the garage level and their own floor. Lift restriction is set up with the elevator contractor, because the reader in the car has to talk to the elevator controller.

Where residents hand over their car keys, the bigger risk is the key box in the attendant’s booth rather than the doors. We can put that cabinet or room on a reader too, with its own short log, so the board can see when it was opened without tracking residents at all.

For valet hours and overnight cover, the attendant group follows the garage’s staffed hours. Outside those hours the garage door works for residents only, and any attendant credential tried after hours shows up on the log as a refused attempt.

Construction is still going on next door to our finished building. How do we keep contractor credentials from outliving the job?

Give every contractor credential an end date on the day it is issued. Modern access software lets an administrator set a start and expiry date on each credential or group; when the date passes, the credential stops working without anyone remembering to delete it. We set default expiry on the contractor group so an administrator cannot accidentally issue an open-ended one.

Issue credentials to named people rather than to a company, even if the company asks for “ten cards for the crew”. A card that moves between workers cannot tell you who used it. A short sign-out sheet at the desk, matching credential numbers to names, is enough for a small crew.

When a phase finishes, the general contractor’s group is disabled in one step and the log for that group is kept only as long as the board’s policy allows. If the work touches the residential side, any non-tenant user details also fall under Local Law 63, which requires reference data for non-tenant users to be removed no later than 90 days after their access expires.

Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue Shops

Shops and restaurants here change staff often and keep long, uneven hours. That, more than crime, is what an access system fixes for them.

Our Smith Street restaurant has a back garden for outdoor seating. Can the garden door stay free during service and lock itself at closing?

Yes. The garden door is set to an unlock schedule that matches service hours, so guests and staff pass freely while the kitchen is open, and it relocks automatically at the time you choose. Staff credentials still open it after hours for clearing up, and any credential use is logged. If service runs late one night, a manager can extend the unlock from a phone.

Because diners must always be able to leave the garden, the door keeps free exit from the garden side at all times. If the door is an exit route for the dining room, egress rules apply: the lock cannot trap anyone, and any electric lock that controls exit has to release on power loss and on fire alarm. We check which way the door swings and what the occupancy is before choosing between an electric strike and an electrified lever.

A door-position contact tells the system whether the door actually closed at relock time. A garden door that is left ajar after the last table often closes against its frame without latching; the system can send a message to the closing manager if that happens.

Our second-floor office over a Court Street storefront shares the street door with a dental practice. Who should control that door?

The building owner, ideally, with each tenant administering only its own people. A shared street door works best on one controller held by the landlord or managing agent, with two tenant groups: your staff and the practice’s staff. Each tenant can be given a limited login that adds and removes its own people but cannot see or change the other tenant’s group or schedule.

The schedules can differ. A dental practice usually needs patients to reach the stairs during appointment hours, which can mean a door station that its front desk answers or a timed unlock; your office may want the door credential-only all day. One door can do both if the practice answers its own visitors and the door relocks after each release.

If the landlord will not take the role, the two tenants can share one system by written agreement, but someone has to be the administrator of record and pay for the licence. We write that into the quote so the arrangement does not depend on a handshake.

Our Court Street fitness studio runs early classes before any staff arrive. Can members let themselves in with their phones?

Yes, with a phone credential tied to the membership. The studio’s booking or membership system can be linked to some access platforms, or a staff member can issue and revoke phone credentials by hand; either way, a member with a booked early class can open the street door and the studio door during a window before the class starts.

Unstaffed entry raises two practical points. First, the door must relock behind members and report if it is held open, so the studio is not left unlocked between arrivals. Second, the space inside needs its own rules — a camera covering the entrance and a clear policy about who may be on the premises before staff arrive. We can fit the entrance; the policy is yours.

If the studio shares its street door with apartments above, members should get the studio door and only the time-limited street-door window, not the residents’ credential group. That is the same zone logic we use in larger mixed buildings, scaled down to two doors.

Records, Roles and the Ninety-Day Rules

Most buildings that call us already have readers. What they lack is a clear answer about records and who can see them.

A resident gave her mother a code for six months while she recovered from surgery. When that permission ends, what has to happen to the mother’s details?

In a building covered by Local Law 63, the mother is a non-tenant user whom a tenant asked to be given access. When her access expires, the law requires her reference data — the code and the details the system checks it against — to be removed no later than 90 days after the access ends, unless the resident asks for it to be kept. Setting an end date on the code when it is issued makes that automatic.

In practice we set temporary codes up as a group with a start and end date entered at the time of issue, and we switch on the platform’s automatic deletion where it has one. Where it does not, the administrator gets a monthly reminder listing credentials that expired more than 60 days ago, so they can be cleared well within the limit.

The resident does not need to share anything beyond what the policy asks for. A name and the dates are enough; there is no reason to record the mother’s health or relationship, and the law forbids using the system to track the relationship status of tenants and their guests.

Our managing agent wants to see our building’s entry log from their Manhattan office. Who else should have that access?

As few people as the job needs. Remote access for the managing agent is reasonable — it is how most agents handle lost fobs and move-outs — but the agent’s login should be an administrator role, not a shared password, so every action it takes is recorded under the agent’s name. Board members generally need to see reports, not to issue credentials.

Logs themselves should be readable by a small, named group: the agent, the board president or a designated officer, and building staff only if their duties need it. Local Law 63 lets owners use access data to monitor common areas for security and to grant access, but not to harass or evict tenants or to sell the information, and it requires a written policy telling residents what is kept and for how long.

When the agent changes, the departing firm’s login is removed on the handover date and the new firm gets its own. We can schedule that change for you so it happens on the right day rather than whenever someone remembers.

Equipment

Types of Access Control We Install

Every device below is something we fit regularly in Brooklyn. The right one for a Cobble Hill door depends less on the door than on how many people share it and who administers them.

Encrypted fob and card readers

The workhorse for shared gates and condo lobbies. We specify encrypted credentials rather than the older unencrypted 125 kHz type, which can be copied with cheap handheld cloners. One reader per controlled door; one credential per person.

Keypad readers and keypad locks

Codes suit visitors, cleaners and contractors who should never carry a fob. On a single shop door or a small house a battery keypad lock can be enough; on a shared gate a wired keypad reader feeds the same controller as the fob readers.

Phone credentials

Residents tap a phone instead of a fob, and administrators issue and revoke them remotely. Useful for gates where a household changes often, and for studios and offices whose members already use an app. Every phone door still needs a fob or key route for people without one.

Video door stations

A camera and call button at the gate or vestibule, answered on an indoor monitor or by phone. Many stations also contain a reader and keypad, so one device covers residents and visitors at the same opening.

Electric strikes and electrified levers

The part that actually unlocks. Strikes replace the keeper in the frame; electrified levers sit in the door. We choose fail-secure or fail-safe hardware door by door according to whether the door is an exit route and what the fire alarm must do to it.

Magnetic locks with code-compliant release

Used on glass and aluminium doors where a strike will not fit, and only with the release devices the Building Code requires: a sensor on the inside, a push-to-exit button and fire-alarm release, or a release switch built into the door hardware.

Controllers and power supplies

The cabinet that holds the door logic, schedules and battery backup. In a converted church or courtyard complex we place it in a cellar or service room the association controls, labelled and on a circuit an electrician has confirmed.

Door-position contacts and request-to-exit sensors

Small parts that make the log meaningful: a contact reports whether the door is really closed; a request-to-exit sensor tells the system a person walked out, so it does not report a forced door every time someone leaves.

Elevator and amenity-room control

Readers in lift cars and at package rooms, bike rooms, storage, gyms and roofs, each on its own schedule. In newer towers this is often the bigger part of the job than the street door.

National alarm and doorbell brands compared with a local access installer

People often ask whether a subscription brand covers the same ground. Names such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe are best known for alarm monitoring, video doorbells and do-it-yourself security kits. Those products are designed mainly around one household and one front door. A shared Cobble Hill gate or a mixed-use lobby needs something different: many independent users, several administrators, schedules, zones and records that follow the city’s privacy rules.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Cobble Hill

We could not read Google’s AI Overview or its question boxes from our research environment this time, so this section does not quote them. It corrects claims we hear repeated by customers and see in generic cost guides and articles, set against how doors actually work in Cobble Hill.

“Access control is for office buildings, not homes”

The idea comes from the way the industry markets itself: card readers on office turnstiles, server rooms and corporate lobbies. It is accurate that a single family behind its own front door rarely needs a controller and a credential database.

Cobble Hill is full of homes that are not behind their own front door. Thirty-four cottages share the Warren Place gate. The Home and Tower Buildings share courtyards. Converted churches and new towers share lobbies, package rooms and garages. Each of those is a multi-user door with the same problem an office has: people arrive and leave, and someone needs to remove them when they do.

So the better test is not “house or office” but “how many independent people use this door, and who removes them”. If the answer is more than one household and nobody in particular, a small access system usually costs less over a few years than repeated rekeying.

“A keypad on the gate is enough for a whole mews”

A single shared code is the simplest thing to install and the hardest to keep secure. Generic advice for homeowners often stops at a keypad lock with a code, because for one house that is fine.

On a gate shared by dozens of households, a single code becomes everyone’s code: it is given to cleaners, delivery drivers, guests and former tenants, and it cannot be withdrawn from one of them without changing it for all. Within a few months nobody can say who knows it.

A keypad reader on a controller solves this by giving each household or person its own code or fob. The hardware cost difference is modest; the real difference is that removing one person no longer means telling thirty households a new number.

“Installation takes an afternoon”

Some cost guides describe access control as a quick install, and for a battery keypad lock on a sound wooden door that can be true.

On a heavy church door, a gate that has sagged on its hinges, or a lobby where the cable must cross a landmarked facade, the electronics are the short part of the job. Closers, hinges and latches often need work first, cable routes have to be found without opening finished walls, and fire-alarm release has to be tested with whoever services the alarm.

We give a realistic time in the written plan, door by door. A single door on a sound frame is often done in a day; a converted building with several doors, a controller and an elevator interface takes longer and usually needs the elevator contractor on site.

“All the entry data is the building’s to keep”

Software makes it easy to keep every swipe forever, and many systems do so by default. Articles written for a national audience rarely mention city rules.

In New York City, Local Law 63 of 2021 changes that for class A multiple dwellings with smart access. Authentication data generally has to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymised; a departed tenant’s reference data and a guest’s reference data have to go within 90 days of the tenant leaving or the guest’s access ending; and the data cannot be sold or used to harass or evict.

We set retention and purge rules at installation and write them into the handover notes, so the building’s privacy policy and its software agree. That matters more in Cobble Hill than most places because so many buildings here are condos or rentals rather than single homes.

“Magnetic locks are the easy option for any door”

Magnetic locks are popular in generic guides because they need no work inside the door or frame and suit glass doors where a strike will not fit.

They are also the lock type the NYC Building Code is most specific about. On an egress door a maglock needs either a sensor-release package — inside sensor, push-to-exit button within reach, release on power loss and on fire alarm — or a release switch in the door hardware itself, and the locking device has to be listed to UL 294.

In converted churches and schools, where many doors are exits, and in shops with glass storefronts, we often use one but never without those release devices. On ordinary wooden entrance doors an electric strike is usually simpler, cheaper to run and safer in a power cut.

“Expect to pay a few hundred dollars”

National guides quote a wide range of numbers, and they are often for different things. Fixr’s smart lock guide put an installed smart lock at roughly $250 to $400, while installer and supplier guides commonly put a wired commercial door at $1,500 to $3,500 or more.

Both can be right. A battery smart lock on one house door is a few hundred dollars; a wired reader with a strike, a controller, cabling and credentials is a different product. Our published Brooklyn price for a single wired door is $1,500 to $2,500 including the reader, strike, wiring and ten programmed fobs.

The cost driver people miss in Cobble Hill is cable distance and route. A gate at the far end of a courtyard, or a lobby whose controller must live two floors down, costs more than a door with the controller behind it.

“Phone apps have made fobs obsolete”

Phone credentials are convenient and we install them often. Some articles go further and treat physical credentials as a thing of the past.

In a shared building that is not workable. Some residents do not carry smartphones or do not want building software on them; phones run out of battery; and the Tenant Data Privacy Law says a tenant cannot be required to use the smart access system to enter their own apartment. A building that goes phone-only at the street door has to think hard about who is left outside.

We set up phones as one option among several: phone, fob, code and, at unit doors, a key. Each person chooses, and the administrator manages them all in one list.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count Every Way the Door Opens

This is a note on how we start, not a story about a particular customer. On any shared door, before choosing a reader, we do a credential census. We ask the administrator, or whoever holds the keys, to tell us every way the door can be opened today: keys, fobs, cards, codes, phone credentials, a button at the concierge desk, a master key held by a contractor. Then we count each one and try to put a current person beside it.

In a building that has changed hands, the count rarely matches. Fobs turn up that were issued to a sales office, codes that belonged to a cleaning company two contracts ago, a programming card left in a drawer. None of it means anyone has done anything wrong; it is what happens when a door outlives its first owner. In a neighbourhood where so many buildings have been churches, schools, a hospital or a philanthropist’s housing experiment, that history is normal.

The census decides the plan. If most credentials can be matched to people, we tidy the list and carry on with the existing system. If they cannot, we re-enrol everyone on new credentials in one step and switch the old type off, because auditing an unknown list costs more than replacing it. Either way, the administrators finish with a list they can read and defend, which is what access control is supposed to give them.

FAQ

Cobble Hill Access Control FAQ

How many administrators should a small condo association have on its access software?
+

Two named administrators and one reserve login is enough for most small associations. More logins mean more people who can add credentials nobody approved. Each administrator should have their own login rather than a shared password, so the audit trail shows who made every change.

Can the system tell our association when a shared gate is forced, not just left open?
+

Yes, if the gate has a door-position contact and the system knows when it unlocked. A gate that opens without a valid credential or exit signal is reported as forced; one that unlocks normally but stays open is reported as held. Both alerts can go to the administrators by email or app notification.

Will a reader and strike work on the heavy original doors of a converted church?
+

Usually, once the door itself is sound. Heavy doors need a closer sized for their weight and hinges that keep them square, otherwise they will not close far enough for the strike to hold. We check weight, swing and existing exit hardware first and choose a heavy-duty strike or an electrified exit device to suit.

Can a resident let a visitor in from their phone without giving them a fob?
+

Yes. With a video door station or an app-connected reader, the resident sees or hears the visitor and releases the door remotely, and the release is logged against the resident. The visitor never receives a credential, so there is nothing to collect or delete afterwards.

How do you label cables and controllers so another installer can work on the system later?
+

Every cable is labelled at both ends with the door it serves, the controller is labelled with each door and zone, and the power supply is marked with its circuit. The handover notes include a simple riser diagram and the controller model and serial number, so any competent installer can pick it up.

Do the readers accept phones held up like a contactless payment card?
+

Many current readers do. Depending on the platform, residents can use an app with Bluetooth or NFC, and some systems also support credentials stored in a phone wallet. We tell you in the quote which phone options the chosen reader supports and which phones they work with.

Can a reader be added to a door that already has a panic bar?
+

Yes. The usual approach is to keep the panic bar for exit and add electrified hardware for entry: an electric strike that suits the bar, an electrified trim on the outside, or an exit device with a built-in electric release. Free exit through the bar is never changed.

Can the same fob open the building and its bike room and storage cages?
+

Yes. Each room is a separate door in the system with its own schedule and its own list of groups. A resident’s fob can open the lobby and bike room but only the storage cage assigned to their unit, and the association can see which rooms are actually being used.

Will you provide a certificate of insurance naming our condo association or managing agent before work starts?
+

Yes. Send us the certificate requirements from the board or managing agent, including the exact names to be listed as certificate holder and additional insured, and we arrange the certificate before the work date. Many buildings will not schedule the job until it is on file.

How long do readers and controllers usually last before they need replacing?
+

Hardware normally lasts many years; what ages first is usually the software support and credential technology. We recommend reviewing the system when the maker stops issuing firmware updates or when the building still relies on older unencrypted credentials, rather than on a fixed number of years.

Can a reader be mounted on a glass storefront door with a narrow aluminium frame?
+

Yes, with the right hardware. Narrow-stile readers fit the frame beside the door, and the lock is often a slim electric strike in the frame or a magnetic lock with the release devices the Building Code requires. Cable runs inside the frame or through the transom so nothing is surface-mounted on the glass.
