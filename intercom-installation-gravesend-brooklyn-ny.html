The Neighborhood

Why Gravesend Properties Need Intercom Installation

Gravesend is one of Brooklyn's oldest places and one of its most mixed. The town was chartered to Lady Deborah Moody in the 1640s, and its original square still shows in the street grid around Gravesend Neck Road and McDonald Avenue. About 30,600 people lived there in 2020. What matters to an intercom installer is how many different kinds of door that population answers. Along the Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue stand co-op towers of seventeen stories that went up between 1959 and 1967, each with hundreds of apartments on one lobby panel. Along Ocean Parkway and the side streets near Avenue Z are six- and seven-story elevator buildings from the 1950s and 1960s. On the lettered avenues are four-story walk-ups from the early 1930s. Between them are blocks of 1920s houses, many of them turned into two-family homes during the Depression, and in the northeast corner, between McDonald Avenue, Kings Highway, Ocean Parkway and Avenue U, the large rebuilt houses of the Sephardic community. A single intercom approach cannot serve all of those.

The first reason Gravesend buildings call us is age. Most of the apartment stock was built with an intercom, or had one added, decades ago, and the original equipment is reaching the end of what spare parts can support. When a lobby panel installed in the 1980s or 1990s fails, the board or owner often learns that the replacement part is discontinued and that the handsets upstairs will not work with anything current. Sometimes the failure is total. A tenant reviewing a twelve-apartment 1931 building on Avenue S wrote in May 2022 that "The buzzer/intercom does not seem to work for any of the units." When every apartment goes dead at once, the cause is almost always in the shared parts: the power supply, the control box in the cellar, the lobby panel or the release on the door. That is fixable, but it has to be found.

The second reason is who lives upstairs. Gravesend's elevator buildings house many older residents, and for them the intercom is not a convenience. CBS New York reported in July 2026 that the elevator at 2470 West 1st Street, a seven-story Gravesend building, had been out since the end of June after the Department of Buildings issued a cease-use order. Tenants described being unable to carry groceries or laundry up and down the stairs; a 70-year-old on the sixth floor said she could not do laundry at all, and the management company's notice told tenants to call the super for help with groceries and packages. In a building like that, the apartment station is how a resident sees who is downstairs, talks to a courier, and opens the door without making the trip. A station that is hard to hear, hard to reach, or dead on the top floors leaves people stuck behind their own front door.

The third reason is the law and the boards that live with it. Under the state's Multiple Dwelling Law, buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom between the front door and each apartment, and the city's housing maintenance code requires the intercom to work. Habitat magazine's February 2024 report, "A Working Intercom Isn't an Amenity. It's Required by Law," quoted Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." Gravesend has a large share of co-ops, from the Shore Parkway towers to six-story buildings on Ocean Parkway, and their boards are the ones who decide when an aging system gets replaced. The last reason is the northeast blocks, where many households keep Shabbat and where houses rebuilt since the 1990s cover most of their lots behind fences and hedges. There, a door station has to suit a family's practice as well as its floor plan, and a house of three or four levels needs a way to answer from every floor.

Seven Gravesend facts that shape an intercom plan

  • The towers are big and from one era. 2015 Shore Parkway, home of the Contello Towers #2 co-op, was built in 1962 with 17 floors and 160 apartments. Contello Towers at 2740 Cropsey Avenue lists 321 units on 17 stories from 1962, and Contello Towers III at 2652 Cropsey Avenue, a 17-story building from 1967, is described as being in Gravesend. Listing sites file the 1935 Shore Parkway co-op towers under Gravesend too. Their risers were planned in the same decade, and they age together.
  • The towers carry violation histories. The openigloo record for 2015 Shore Parkway showed 47 open HPD violations over ten years, 9 of them class C and 30 class B, which it rated at 0.29 per unit against a city average of 0.81. A board planning an intercom upgrade in a building like that is doing it alongside everything else on the list.
  • Mid-century rentals are common near the water. 2661 Shore Parkway, built in 1951, has six floors and 60 rent-stabilized apartments. Buildings of that era usually have one lobby panel, an elevator, and a riser of multi-pair cable that may or may not still be sound.
  • Ocean Parkway has elevator co-ops. 2610 Ocean Parkway, a six-story building with 56 apartments from 1964, is reviewed by a resident as a "Great coop near Coney Island Hospital." Buildings of this size are far past the eight-apartment line at which the law requires a working intercom.
  • The walk-ups are older than the towers. 106 Avenue S, a four-story building with twelve apartments, dates from 1931. Walk-ups of that age had their intercoms added later, often run up the stair or the outside wall, and that cable is where most of their faults start.
  • Public housing runs on its own system. The Marlboro Houses, completed in 1958, are 28 NYCHA buildings, three of them sixteen stories and the rest seven, home to nearly 4,000 residents according to News 12. NYCHA maintains its own entry systems there, and residents report intercom problems to NYCHA rather than to a private installer.
  • The northeast houses are large and private. 6sqft described the rebuilt houses around Avenues S, T and U as covering most of their lots and often hidden behind fences, tall trees or hedges, in the largest Sephardic Jewish community in the United States. A station at the street, a release on a gate or door, and answering on every floor are the usual ask.

Repair Calls in Gravesend

Need Repair in Gravesend? Top Floors That Stopped Ringing, a Handset That Only Hums, or a Whole Building Gone Quiet

Most of the Gravesend intercom calls we get are not for new systems. They start with something that stopped working, and in this neighborhood's buildings they fall into a few patterns that each point somewhere different. A whole building that went quiet at once, like the Avenue S walk-up in that 2022 review, points to the shared equipment: the transformer or power supply, the amplifier or control box, the lobby panel's own circuit board, or the strike on the door. A single line that went dead, say every apartment ending in C in a tower, points to the riser pair or junction that serves that line, often where it passes a closet or a renovated apartment. The top few floors failing while the lower floors work points to voltage drop or a corroded splice partway up a long run. A handset that rings but carries only hum or a whisper points to a failing power supply, a bad ground or water in a junction box. And a door that never releases, even though residents can hear the visitor perfectly, is the release, its power or the wire between them.

Each of those has a different first check, so the visit starts with questions rather than tools. What changed recently: an apartment renovation, a new tenant, a power cut, heavy rain into the lobby, a new phone provider? Which apartments are affected, and on which floors? Does the panel light up? Does anyone hear the visitor at all? Sometimes the answers point straight at the fault. When they do not, the technician starts at the power and the control equipment and follows the riser toward the affected apartments, testing as he goes. If the panel or the control box is so old that parts are gone, we say so and tell you whether a repair is still worth the money or whether it should go toward a replacement. A repair is quoted before work starts, and the Brooklyn hub lists repairs and upgrades of existing systems at $150 to $1,200, with a $250 service call when a technician is sent to diagnose.

Repairs matter more in Gravesend's elevator buildings than almost anywhere else, because the residents who depend on the station most are the ones least able to come down when it fails. When the elevator is working, a dead handset is an annoyance. When the elevator is out, as it was for weeks at the West 1st Street building in 2026, a dead handset on the sixth floor means a resident who cannot find out who is ringing, cannot talk to the courier, and cannot let a relative in. When we are called to a building in that situation, the stations on the upper floors are the first ones we bring back.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Gravesend

Every Gravesend intercom job turns on three questions: which door visitors really use, who in each household answers and from where, and whether the wire already in the building can carry what a new system needs. These are the services we plan most often for the neighborhood's towers, elevator buildings, walk-ups and houses.

Lobby panels for large co-ops

Video or audio street panels with an electronic directory or keypad for buildings of one hundred to three hundred apartments, with a release on the lobby door and a plan for any second entrance.

Apartment station replacement

New wall screens, handsets or hands-free stations in every apartment, mounted where the resident can reach and hear them, including lower mounts and visual signals where a household needs them.

Phone answering for residents

Calls from the street panel to residents' phones through an app or to an ordinary phone number, alongside a wall station or instead of one, household by household.

Riser testing and line repair

Every pair tested from the control box to each line and floor, good runs reused, failed runs replaced, and the riser labeled and mapped so the next repair is quick.

Second-entrance stations

Stations at a parking-lot door, a side entrance or a rear courtyard gate that residents use, labeled so the person answering knows which door is calling.

Walk-up and two-family systems

Small systems for four to twenty apartments or two to three households: a slim street panel, a station or phone per household, and a release on the street door.

House door and floor-to-floor systems

A station at the street door or gate, answering on every level of a large house, and calls between floors, kitchen and basement on the same equipment.

Takeovers of existing systems

Identifying a system another company installed, recovering or resetting its administrator account, and putting it in the board's or owner's name.

Phased replacements

A new lobby panel and control equipment now, with apartment stations changed line by line or year by year on a system chosen so the later steps do not mean starting over.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Gravesend's Architecture

Gravesend's housing went up in waves: the 1920s houses, the early-1930s walk-ups, the mid-century rentals and NYCHA blocks of the 1950s, the elevator co-ops and towers of the late 1950s and 1960s, and the rebuilt houses of the northeast from the 1990s on. Each wave left a different kind of wall at the street door, a different path for the riser and a different answer to who picks up when the panel rings. These are the six kinds of building we plan for most; the notes below the cards explain the method for each.

Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue co-op towers

Seventeen-story co-ops from 1959 to 1967 with one hundred to three hundred and more apartments, a main lobby, often a parking-lot entrance, and risers that run beside the elevator and stair cores.

Six- and seven-story elevator buildings

Co-ops and rentals from the 1950s and 1960s on Ocean Parkway, West 1st and the blocks near Avenue Z, with one lobby, one elevator and fifty to eighty apartments.

Early-1930s walk-ups

Four-story brick buildings of eight to twenty apartments on the lettered avenues and numbered streets, with a vestibule, a stair and an intercom added long after they were built.

1920s two-family houses

Attached and semi-detached houses split into two households during the Depression, some still two-family and some converted back, with one street door or two.

Rebuilt houses in the northeast

Large houses from the 1990s on, around Avenues S, T and U between Ocean Parkway and McDonald Avenue, built to the edges of their lots behind fences and hedges, on three or four levels.

Stores with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on Avenue U, Kings Highway, McDonald Avenue and Avenue X, with a shop at the sidewalk and a separate residential door beside it.

Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue co-op towers: one panel, hundreds of apartments, one board

In a seventeen-story tower, the lobby panel is the visible part of a much larger system. Behind it are a control box and power supply in the cellar or a ground-floor closet, a riser of multi-pair cable that climbs beside the elevator or stair core with a junction box on each floor or every few floors, and a branch from each junction to every apartment on that floor. With 160 apartments at 2015 Shore Parkway or 321 listed at Contello Towers on Cropsey Avenue, that is a great deal of cable, almost all of it installed in the early 1960s or replaced in a later upgrade, and almost none of it documented. Before any equipment is chosen, we find where the riser runs, how it is organized, and how much of it still tests sound.

The directory is the second design question. A panel with a push button for each of three hundred apartments is not realistic, so tower panels use an electronic directory, a keypad for apartment codes, or both, and the choice affects every visitor and every courier. Older residents and visiting relatives usually find a scrolling directory with large names easier than a code; drivers who come every day prefer a code. Most tower boards end up with both on one panel. The third question is the second entrance. Many towers have residents who come and go by a parking-lot or side door more than by the front lobby, and that door needs either its own call station or a reader for residents only, decided before the panel is ordered.

Because these buildings are co-ops, the board decides, and it usually decides over more than one meeting. We give the board a written design with the test results, the directory options, the cost of reusing the riser compared with rewiring the worst lines, and a phased plan if the budget calls for one. A tower can replace the lobby panel and control equipment first, keep the existing apartment handsets working on a compatible system, and change apartments line by line over later budget years.

Six- and seven-story elevator buildings: the station is the resident's front door

The six- and seven-story buildings on Ocean Parkway, West 1st Street and the blocks near Avenue Z are the backbone of Gravesend's apartment stock. 2610 Ocean Parkway, from 1964, has 56 apartments on six floors; 2661 Shore Parkway, from 1951, has 60 on six. Their risers are usually a single bundle of multi-pair cable up a shaft or the stair, and a fault on one floor is easier to find than in a tower. What makes these buildings different is who lives in them. Many residents are older, many have lived there for decades, and many depend on the elevator to reach the lobby at all.

That changes the design. The apartment station has to be heard from the bedroom and the kitchen, not only beside the door. It has to be reachable by someone who uses a walker or a wheelchair, which may mean a lower mount than the original handset or a second station. Video helps residents who do not hear well, because a face on a screen and a light that flashes are easier to trust than a voice through an old speaker. And phone answering helps households where a son or daughter manages deliveries and visits for a parent, as long as the parent still has a station of their own. We ask about every apartment's needs during the survey and write them on the quote.

Early-1930s walk-ups: cable added after the fact

The four-story walk-ups from the early 1930s were built with doorbells or speaking tubes, not modern intercoms, and their systems were added later. The cable for those systems usually runs up the stairwell in a corner or under a molding, sometimes along the outside of the building, and branches into each apartment through the wall beside its door. It has been painted over, nailed, rerouted by renovations and occasionally cut. In a twelve-apartment building like 106 Avenue S, that means a total failure is often a single fault in the cellar or the vestibule, while a single dead apartment is a break in its branch.

For a building of this size, the right system is usually modest: a slim street panel with a button per apartment, a station or phone answering for each, and a release on the street door, all running over the existing pairs if they test good. Two-wire systems that carry video over one pair are built for exactly this kind of building. If the old cable is beyond saving, new cable is run in the stairwell in a painted raceway rather than through apartments, so a future renovation cannot cut a neighbor off.

1920s two-family houses: two households, sometimes one again

Gravesend's 1920s houses were mostly built for one family, and many were converted into two-family homes during the Depression; Wikipedia's account of the neighborhood also notes that some have since been converted back. That history shows up at the front door. A house split decades ago may have two bells, one wired to each floor, an old talk-back that died years ago, and a release that only the downstairs apartment can operate. A house converted back may still have two bells and a second station upstairs that nobody uses.

For a two-family house, the usual design is a small video or audio station at the street door with one button for each household, a station or phone answering on each floor, and a release both households can use. For a house that has become one family again, the second button can be retired or turned into a second call point that rings the whole house, and the upstairs station can stay as an extra answering point. We test the old bell wire first, because in many of these houses it can carry a new two-wire system without opening walls.

Rebuilt houses in the northeast: many floors, a gate and a household's practice

The houses rebuilt around Avenues S, T and U since the 1990s are a different job altogether. 6sqft described them as covering most of their lots, in Mediterranean and Spanish styles with terra-cotta roofs, often hidden behind fences, tall trees or hedges. A visitor may not be able to see the front door from the sidewalk, so the call station belongs at the gate or the walk, with a release on the gate and the door. The house itself is often three or four levels, including a finished basement, and the household wants to answer from the kitchen, the bedrooms and the lowest floor, and to call between those floors.

A single system can do all of that: a station at the gate, indoor screens on each level, phone answering for the adults, and room-to-room calling on the same equipment. In many of these homes the system also has to fit the family's Shabbat practice, which affects where the camera points, what lights up and when, and how guests arriving on foot are let in. We cover that in its own section below. Because these houses are often built with structured cabling in the walls, the indoor stations can usually run on network cable that is already there, which we test before relying on it.

Stores with apartments above: keep the shop's callers out of the hallway

On Avenue U, Kings Highway, McDonald Avenue under the F and Avenue X, many buildings have a store at the sidewalk and two or more apartments upstairs, reached through a narrow door beside the shop. The residential station on that door should be clearly separate from anything the store uses, so customers never ring an apartment and couriers for the store are not sent up the stairs. A slim panel with a button per apartment, labeled by apartment rather than name if the tenants prefer, and a release on the street door usually does it. The cable for the residents' system should stay out of the shop's space unless the owner and the tenant have agreed to access, because a locked store at night is a locked route to the riser.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Before the Panel, Ask Who Cannot Come Downstairs

The question that shapes a Gravesend intercom more than any other is one we ask on every survey in an elevator building, before anyone looks at a catalog: which residents cannot easily come down to the lobby? In a tower or a six-story co-op, the answer is rarely zero. There are residents who use walkers or wheelchairs, residents recovering from surgery, residents who are hard of hearing, and residents whose children handle their deliveries from another borough. In a walk-up, the residents on the top floor are the ones who climb the most stairs for every visitor. The answer tells us where the stations go, which apartments get video, which households need phone answering as well as a wall station, and how long the door should stay released after someone buzzes.

The second habit is testing before quoting. Every pair from the control box to each line or floor is checked for continuity, for shorts to the next pair and for leakage to ground. In buildings of the 1950s and 1960s, results usually split three ways: most pairs are sound, a handful are open where an apartment was renovated, and a few show faults only when they are warm or damp, which is what produces the hum that comes and goes. That test, written up and attached to the quote, is what lets a board choose between reusing the riser and replacing part of it on numbers rather than guesses.

Answering From Upstairs

The Lobby Is Six Floors Down: Planning a Gravesend Intercom Around Residents Who Cannot Easily Reach the Door

In a small walk-up, an intercom saves a resident a trip down the stairs. In a Gravesend elevator building, it can be the only way some residents deal with the front door at all. The CBS New York report on 2470 West 1st Street in July 2026 made that plain. With the building's only elevator out since the end of June, one tenant just home from cancer treatment could not carry groceries upstairs, and a 70-year-old on the sixth floor could not take her laundry down. The management company's notice told tenants to call the super for help with groceries and packages. Every one of those deliveries began with someone ringing the lobby panel, and every one depended on the resident upstairs being able to answer.

The same problem shows up at the bottom of the building. News 12 reported in April 2026 that only five of the 28 buildings at the Marlboro Houses have accessible ramps, and that residents with mobility problems often wait outside for help to get up the steps. Annie Batts told the reporter, "I have to sit out and wait for somebody to come by and help me put my walk up." The Marlboro Houses are NYCHA buildings and their entry systems are NYCHA's to fix, but the situation she describes is not unique to public housing. Many of Gravesend's private six-story buildings and walk-ups also have a short flight of steps between the sidewalk and the street door, and their intercoms were designed for people who walk up those steps without a second thought.

An intercom cannot build a ramp or fix an elevator. What it can do is make sure the resident upstairs is never cut off from the door, and that the visitor at the bottom of the steps is never stuck without a way to call. These are the design points we work through in Gravesend elevator buildings and walk-ups.

At the street door

  • Panel height and reach. The Department of Buildings rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must sit between four and five feet. We place the panel inside those limits and as low within them as the door allows, so a visitor in a wheelchair can reach the buttons.
  • A call point where people actually wait. Where the street door is up a flight of steps, a second call point at the foot of the steps, on a post or the stair wall, lets a visitor or a returning resident who cannot manage the steps call an apartment or the super. It rings the same directory as the main panel.
  • A clear sign that the call connected. The panel should show a light and play a tone when a call connects and when the door is released, so a visitor who does not hear well or cannot hear over traffic on the Shore Parkway knows to push.
  • Longer release where needed. The release time can be set long enough for someone who walks slowly to get from the panel to the door, and the door closer has to be adjusted so the door still shuts and latches behind them.

Inside the apartment

  • A station where the resident spends the day. In a six-story building, the original handset usually hangs by the apartment door. A resident who spends most of the day in a back bedroom may need a second station there, or a hands-free station that can be answered without picking anything up.
  • Video and a visual ring. A screen that lights up and shows the visitor's face helps residents who do not hear the chime or do not trust a voice they cannot place. It is also how a resident who will not open for strangers decides whether to let a courier in.
  • A phone for the person who helps. Where a relative or aide handles a resident's deliveries from elsewhere, the street panel can ring that person's phone too, so a courier at the lobby can be let in even when the resident is asleep or not well. The resident's own station keeps working either way.
  • Stations that do not depend on the internet. In a building where many residents do not use smartphones, wired stations in every apartment are the backbone, and phone answering is added on top for those who want it.

None of this replaces what a landlord, a board or NYCHA owes residents when an elevator or a ramp is missing. It is what we can do, as the intercom installer, to keep the door from being one more barrier. When we survey a Gravesend elevator building, we ask the super or managing agent which residents need these changes and write each one on the quote by apartment.

Shabbat and the Door

Shabbat, Guests on Foot and a Camera at the Gate: Entry Planning for Observant Households in Northeast Gravesend

The northeast blocks of Gravesend, between Ocean Parkway and McDonald Avenue around Avenues S, T and U, are home to what 6sqft, citing the Wall Street Journal, called the largest Sephardic Jewish community in the United States, about 30,000 people, mostly of Syrian descent. 6sqft placed the priciest blocks between the synagogues on Ocean Parkway and a yeshiva on McDonald Avenue. Many families there keep Shabbat, and for a door station that is a design requirement, not a footnote. Between Friday evening and Saturday night, an observant household does not operate electrical devices, and guests often walk over from synagogue for meals. An intercom that lights up, records or expects someone to press a button at the wrong moment works against the house's own rules.

The practical questions have been written about at length. Rav Eliezer Melamed's Peninei Halakha, in its chapter on electricity on Shabbat, discusses intercoms, monitors and doorbells at building entrances. It advises that a person should not press an intercom to ask a guard to open the door, "since using electricity is prohibited," and suggests placing the guard's station near the entrance so a resident can knock from outside the camera's range and be let in by hand. On cameras it says "a Jew may not stand where his image will be captured on the monitor," and on doorbells it notes that ringing one is disputed and should not become a habit, "as doing so regularly belittles Shabbat." Opinions differ between authorities, and every family follows its own rabbi. Our job is not to rule on any of it. Our job is to give the household a system that can be set up the way its rabbi says.

What a door system can do for a Shabbat household

  • Camera placement with a clear approach. If the family wants guests and residents to be able to reach the door without walking into the camera's view, the station can be placed and aimed so its view covers the gate or the walk while leaving a clear path to the door or a knocker outside it. We mark the camera's field of view on the survey so the family can see exactly what it covers.
  • Screens and lights that stay dark. Many indoor monitors can be set so they do not wake on motion, and many outdoor stations can turn off their button lights and motion recording on a schedule. Where a model cannot do that, we say so before it is chosen.
  • Schedules set before Shabbat. Release permissions, call routing and lighting can follow a weekly schedule or a holiday calendar entered in advance, so nothing needs to be changed by hand on Friday afternoon.
  • A non-electric way in. Some families keep a mechanical lock, a key box or a knocker as the Shabbat entrance and use the intercom only during the week. The station can sit beside a door that still opens by key without depending on the release.
  • Staff or a gentile neighbor at the door. In larger homes and community buildings, the answering station can be placed where a staff member on duty sees the door directly and opens it by hand, which is the arrangement Peninei Halakha prefers for guarded entrances.

The same considerations reach beyond the houses. Synagogues, yeshivas and community buildings in the northeast receive large numbers of people on foot on Shabbat and holidays, and many keep a side or rear door locked on weekdays with a buzz-in for staff and visitors. For those buildings, the plan usually separates the weekday system, a video station that rings the office and the staff's phones, from the Shabbat arrangement, a staffed or unlocked door at set hours, and writes both into the schedule. Where a door is also part of the fire alarm or must stay free to exit, that comes first, as it does everywhere.

Boards and the Law

When the Co-op Board Owns the Intercom: What Gravesend's Tower and Elevator Co-ops Are Expected to Keep Working

Much of Gravesend's apartment stock is co-op housing: the towers on the Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue, many of the six-story buildings on Ocean Parkway, and smaller co-ops on the side streets. In a co-op, the intercom belongs to the corporation, and the board decides when it is repaired, upgraded or replaced. That decision is not entirely a matter of preference. Habitat magazine's February 2024 report set out the legal frame. Under the state Multiple Dwelling Law, buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom linking the front door to each apartment, and the city's housing maintenance code also requires a functioning intercom. Courts have treated intercoms as an essential service, so a broken one can breach the warranty of habitability.

For older buildings the city's own rule reaches the same place. The Department of Buildings rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, provides that buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments shall be equipped with self-closing and self-locking doors and with an intercommunication system, and it sets the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. Almost every co-op tower and elevator building in Gravesend has well over eight apartments, whatever its construction year, so the question in these buildings is never whether an intercom is required. It is how well the one they have works.

Habitat's report also described what residents can do when a board does not act: a written letter to the board demanding repair, a 311 complaint, an HP proceeding in Housing Court, which the article notes can be started without a lawyer, and in some cases a defense if the co-op sues for unpaid maintenance. The case the article followed involved a sixth-floor shareholder in a 54-unit co-op who had been without intercom service for about five years after a downstairs neighbor's renovation. Andrew Freedland of Herrick Feinstein explained that whether the neighbor is liable depends on the proprietary lease or the alteration agreement. In Gravesend's towers, where the riser branches run through apartment walls and closets, renovations are one of the most common causes of a dead line.

How we help a Gravesend board plan

  • Test results first. Before the board debates equipment, it gets a written test of the riser: which lines and floors are sound, which are failing, and where renovations have cut branches.
  • Options, not one number. The quote compares keeping the riser with a compatible new lobby system, rewiring only the failed lines, and a full rewire, with the disruption each means for shareholders.
  • A phased schedule. If the budget is split across years, the plan says which part comes first and how old and new apartment stations keep working together in between.
  • Alteration agreement language. We can describe, in plain terms, where the intercom wire runs inside a typical apartment, so the board and its attorney can decide what a renovating shareholder's contractor must protect.
  • A record for the board. At handover the board receives a riser map, the test sheet for every apartment, the administrator logins in the corporation's name and the warranty terms.

We do not give legal advice, and boards should have their attorney confirm how the law applies to their building. What we can do is make sure the board's decision is based on the actual condition of its system and a clear picture of what each option costs and involves.

Power and Equipment

Cellars Below Avenue X, Dial-Out Panels and Battery Backup: Where a Gravesend Intercom's Equipment Should Live

Every intercom has a control box, a power supply and, on newer systems, a network switch somewhere out of sight. In Gravesend's apartment buildings that equipment usually sits in the cellar, near the boiler or the electrical room, because that is where the building's services arrive. In the southern part of the neighborhood, that location carries a known risk. The National Weather Service's summary of Hurricane Sandy's coastal flooding noted that inundation extended inland to Avenue X, into Bensonhurst, Gravesend and Sheepshead Bay, shutting down the Belt Parkway. We have no block-by-block flood records of our own, but a cellar that took water once can take it again, and an intercom power supply on a low shelf there is the first part of the system to fail.

When we install or replace intercom equipment in a building south of Avenue X or near Coney Island Creek, it goes as high as the space allows: high on the cellar wall, in a ground-floor closet, or in a riser closet on an upper floor if the building has one. The cable from the street panel to that location is routed so it does not dip into a flood-prone space on the way. If the cellar is the only place, the design notes which parts must be checked after any water event.

The second question is the telephone line. Some lobby panels in Gravesend buildings do not ring apartment handsets at all; they dial residents' phones over a copper line the building pays for every month. THE CITY reported in 2022 that Verizon had listed 65 switching centers and thousands of addresses in 2017 where it would stop offering and maintaining copper service, and that saltwater from Sandy damaged copper lines across the city. Fittingly, one of Gravesend's own landmarks is the Art Deco telephone building at the Meucci Triangle, where 86th Street meets Avenue U. A panel that depends on copper can stop calling anyone for reasons the board does not control. When we find one, we tell the board what the line does and costs, and whether a cellular or internet path for the existing panel or a replacement makes more sense.

The third question is power during an outage. The lobby door's release, the panel, the apartment stations and any switch all draw power, and when the building loses it, the door's fail-safe or fail-secure setting decides whether it unlocks or stays locked. A battery backup sized to the panel, the release and the control equipment keeps the street door and the intercom working through a short outage, which matters most in exactly the buildings where residents cannot come down to open the door by hand. We size the backup on the quote and say how long it should last.

The last question is the network, for systems that use one. A networked intercom belongs on its own segment of the building's network, so a panel on the sidewalk is not a doorway into anyone's computer, and the administrator account should be in the board's or owner's name, not the installer's. If the building changes its internet provider or replaces its router, the intercom must be on the checklist, because that is the most common reason a working system suddenly stops ringing phones.

The third is the controlled entrance of a program or community building, where every person at the door should be seen and the release should stay with staff.

Rules a Gravesend business door has to respect

  • Free exit at all times. A door with an electric release must still open from inside without a key, special knowledge or effort. Strikes are matched to the door and frame, and every job is tested with the power off.
  • Visual and audible signals. Where a two-way communication system is provided for entry, the ADA Standards call for both visual and audible signals. We choose stations that provide both and mount them within reach of a seated visitor.
  • Fire alarm interfaces. If a door's lock is tied to a building fire alarm, that connection belongs to the fire alarm side and its approvals. We find any such door on the survey and coordinate rather than rewire it.
  • Rated doors. Doors off public corridors and stairs are often fire-rated, and release hardware for them must be listed for rated openings.
  • Shared entrances. In a mixed building, anything on the common street door is the building owner's decision, and the residents' system must keep working whatever the commercial tenant changes.
  • Cameras and privacy. A video station records whoever stands in front of it. Decide who can see the footage and how long it is kept, and let staff, parents and regular visitors know the station has a camera.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Gravesend: Questions Answered

These are the situations Gravesend boards, owners, residents and business owners are most likely to bring us, grouped by topic. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Buying into and running a tower co-op

The listing for a Cropsey Avenue co-op tower we are looking at advertises "Controlled Access" and "24 Hour Access." Before we buy, how can we tell what that actually means at the lobby door?

Those phrases describe a feature list, not a working system. "Controlled access" can mean anything from a fob reader at the lobby door to a staffed desk, and "24 hour access" usually means residents can come and go at any hour. Ask the managing agent what the lobby panel is, how old it is, whether it rings apartment stations or phones, and whether any apartments are known not to ring. Then try it: have someone ring your prospective apartment from the street panel during a viewing.

Some members of our board say everyone has a cellphone now, so a new intercom can wait. Is the intercom really something a co-op can put off?

Not in a building of eight or more apartments. As Habitat reported in 2024, state law requires a two-way voice intercom between the front door and each apartment in buildings built or converted after 1968, the city's code requires it to work, and courts have treated it as an essential service. Cellphones help visitors reach residents, but they do not open the lobby door for a courier or a home attendant. The board's real choice is how to replace the system, not whether.

Our tower is one of a group of neighboring co-ops built in the same years, each with its own corporation and board. Can the boards share one design and bid while keeping separate systems and separate bills?

Yes, and it often makes sense. Towers of the same era usually have similar risers and similar failures, so one survey method, one equipment family and one set of drawings can serve all of them, which simplifies spare parts and training for staff who move between buildings. Each corporation still gets its own quote, its own contract, its own administrator account and its own invoice, and no building's system depends on another's.

Cost and scope for apartment buildings

Our building has the main lobby on the parkway side and a second entrance from the parking lot that most residents use. Does that second door add much to the price, or is it the number of apartments that matters?

Both matter, and the doors matter more than most boards expect. Swiftlane's 2026 cost guide puts it simply: "Cost scales with door and entrance count, not just unit count." A second entrance needs its own station or reader, its own release and power, and a cable run to the control equipment. The apartment count drives the stations and the riser work. Your quote will show the second door as its own line, so the board can decide whether it gets a full call station or a resident-only reader.

Video costs more than audio. For a twelve-apartment walk-up where many tenants are older, is the difference worth paying?

Usually, yes. Swiftlane estimates the video premium at roughly 15 to 40 percent over audio for a small building. For older tenants, being able to see who is ringing matters more than for most, because a face is easier to trust than a voice through a speaker, and a screen that lights up helps those who do not hear the chime. If the existing pairs test good, a two-wire video system keeps the extra cost to equipment rather than new cable.

The buzzer in our Avenue S walk-up does not work for any apartment at all. Where does a fault like that usually sit?

In the parts every apartment shares. When no apartment can hear the visitor or release the door, the likely causes are the transformer or power supply, the amplifier or control box, the lobby panel itself, or the door release and its wiring. A failure in one apartment's branch cannot silence the others. That is good news, because shared parts are found and fixed in one place, and a repair is often far cheaper than a new system.

We manage a 60-apartment elevator building near Avenue Z. How many days should we tell tenants to expect a crew working in the halls and apartments?

It depends on whether the riser is reused. HomeAdvisor's guide estimates one to two hours per station. When the existing pairs test good, a building that size usually means a day or two at the lobby and cellar, then apartment visits floor by floor over several more days. Rewiring lines adds time in the stair and halls. Your schedule lists each floor's day, and tenants get a two-hour arrival window for their apartment.

Our building's street door is up a short flight of steps with no ramp, and a neighbor who uses a walker waits outside for someone to help her. Can the intercom give her a way to call for help?

Yes. A second call point at the bottom of the steps, on a post or the stair wall, can ring her own apartment, a family member's phone or the super, using the same directory as the main panel. It does not replace a ramp, which is the owner's responsibility, but it means nobody has to wait outside hoping someone walks by. We set the door's release time long enough for the climb.

Houses on the side streets and in the northeast

Our 1920s house was split into two apartments decades ago, and we are converting it back into one family home. What happens to the second bell and the station upstairs?

They can stay useful. The second button can be removed and the station plate replaced with a single-button model, or kept as a second call point that rings the whole house. The upstairs station becomes an extra answering point, so the family can answer from either floor. If the old bell wire tests good, a new two-wire video station can reuse it, which avoids opening the walls you may have just finished.

Our rebuilt house already has a home automation system with touch panels and TVs in several rooms. Can the door station show up on those screens?

Often, but it depends on the door station's model and the automation platform. Some video door stations are made to send their video and calls to Control4, Crestron or Savant touch panels and to TVs through the integrator's system; others only work with their own monitors and app. We check that the station you choose is supported by your platform before ordering, and coordinate the programming with whoever maintains the automation.

Records and violations

Our buyer's attorney asked whether the co-op has open HPD violations for the intercom. Where would that show up, and what should the board do about it?

HPD violations are public and searchable by address, and the inspector's description usually names the intercom, the apartment and the floor. Sites such as openigloo also summarize a building's open violations; 2015 Shore Parkway, for example, showed 47 open across all categories. If an intercom violation is open, the board should repair the cause, keep the technician's test sheet for every affected apartment, and have its agent certify the correction to HPD.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Gravesend

Quick answers about intercoms are mostly written for single houses or for new buildings with modern wiring. Here are claims that come up often, checked against the sources on this page and what our surveys find in Gravesend's buildings.

"An intercom installation costs about $2,500"

Search summaries often repeat HomeAdvisor's national average: "Installing an intercom system costs an average of $2,500, including intercom materials and labor," in a normal range of $1,200 to $8,500.

Our Brooklyn list, from $400 for audio on a small building to $4,500 for a walk-up of up to twenty apartments, is a better guide for small Gravesend jobs, and larger buildings are quoted after a wire test.

"Everyone answers on their phone now, so apartment stations are obsolete"

App-based entry is often presented as the modern default for every building.

In Gravesend's elevator buildings, many residents are older and some do not use smartphones at all. CBS New York's report from 2470 West 1st Street described tenants in their seventies coping with weeks without an elevator. For residents like them, a wall station that works without the internet is the part of the system they depend on, and state law requires two-way voice and a release in each apartment, not on each resident's phone.

The designs we plan here mix wall stations for everyone with phone answering for those who want it.

"Wireless intercoms avoid running cable in old buildings"

Many summaries suggest Wi-Fi or cellular systems to skip wiring entirely.

A Wi-Fi station inside one apartment, a few feet from the resident's own router, can work. A wireless link from a lobby panel to 160 apartments through seventeen floors of concrete and block cannot be relied on, and a cellular panel needs signal where its equipment lives, which in many Gravesend buildings is a cellar.

The cheapest way to avoid new cable is usually to test and reuse the riser already in the walls.

"Old intercom wiring has to be replaced"

The opposite claim appears too: that any building over a certain age needs a complete rewire before a new system goes in.

In mid-century buildings the multi-pair riser cable itself is often still sound. The failures we find are mostly at terminals, junction boxes and the branches that pass through renovated apartments. A whole-building failure, like the one a tenant described at the Avenue S walk-up in 2022, is usually a single shared part.

Our test of every pair shows how much of the riser is good before a board pays to replace any of it.

"Any electrician or handyman can install an intercom"

Some answers treat intercoms as simple low-voltage wiring that anyone handy can do.

An intercom touches the building's main door, its locking hardware and, in many buildings, its fire safety. The release has to suit the door and frame, the door must still open freely from inside, the panel must sit at the heights the city's entrance-door rule sets, and a door tied to the fire alarm cannot be rewired casually.

AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431, and we do not do the electrical work that some jobs need; a licensed electrician does that.

"A smart doorbell is the same thing as an intercom"

Product round-ups often list video doorbells alongside building intercoms as if they were interchangeable.

A video doorbell shows a visitor on a phone. It does not open the door, it does not ring separate households, and it does not work without the internet. In a two-family house or a building of any size, the intercom's job is to let the right household talk to the visitor and release the door, which a doorbell was never designed to do.

Many Gravesend houses have both: a doorbell camera for the porch and a wired door station that opens the gate.

"Intercoms are a convenience, not a requirement"

Some summaries describe intercoms as an amenity that boards and owners can add when the budget allows.

Habitat's 2024 report was titled "A Working Intercom Isn't an Amenity. It's Required by Law," and it described courts treating intercoms as an essential service. The Department of Buildings rule at 1 RCNY 42-01 also requires an intercommunication system in buildings erected or converted before 1968 with eight or more apartments.

FAQ

Gravesend Intercom Installation FAQ

When the old lobby panel and apartment handsets come off the wall, who repairs and paints the wall behind them?
+

AESS does not patch or paint walls. We size new stations and panels to cover old openings where we can, and we fill only the mounting holes our own equipment needs. Where an old handset leaves marks or a larger opening, the quote says so before work starts, so the building's super, painter or contractor can plan to finish it afterward.

Can the screen of an apartment station stay dark at night so it does not light up a bedroom?
+

On most current indoor screens, yes. The display can be set to stay off until a call comes in, the brightness of the standby light can be lowered, and on some models the screen can be scheduled to stay off overnight while the chime still sounds. We check the chosen model's settings and set them for each household that asks.

Do you take the old panel, handsets and power supply away when the new system is in?
+

Yes. Removed equipment leaves with the crew unless the board or owner asks to keep it, for example as spares for a system that will be phased out over time. Old cable inside walls is usually left in place and labeled as abandoned, because pulling it out would mean opening walls.

Once we approve the quote, how soon is the equipment ordered and the installation date set?
+

Equipment is ordered after the deposit is received. Stock items usually arrive quickly, while special-order panels can take longer and shipping times are outside our control. The installation dates are set once the equipment is in hand, so nobody is given a date that depends on a delivery that has not arrived.

If our building or house switches internet provider, will the intercom stop ringing our phones?
+

It can, if the system uses the internet for phone answering and the new router is not set up for it. Wall stations wired to the building keep working regardless. Before the switch, let us know, and we can reconnect the intercom to the new router the same day so phone calls from the door resume.

Can an apartment station sit on a table or shelf instead of being fixed to the wall?
+

Some models offer a desk stand, and they suit residents who want the station beside a favorite chair or bed. A desk station still needs its cable to reach that spot, usually through a small wall plate nearby. We confirm the chosen model has a stand option and plan the cable outlet where the household wants it.

Can neighbors in our building call each other's apartments on the new system?
+

Many current systems allow apartment-to-apartment calls, and some allow calls from an apartment to the super or the lobby desk. The board decides whether to turn that on, since some residents prefer not to be called by neighbors. We set it up as the board chooses and note it on the handover sheet.

How can we tell whether our building's intercom is an older analog system or a newer digital one?
+

Look at the lobby panel and the equipment in the cellar. A panel with a speaker grille, a button per apartment and a transformer and amplifier box downstairs is usually analog. A panel with a screen, a keypad or a scrolling directory and a network switch or controller is usually digital. A photo of each lets us identify it before the visit.

Do you work with the building's super during the installation?
+

Yes, and in Gravesend's elevator buildings the super is often the person who knows which apartments are hard to reach and where the cellar keys are. We agree with the board or agent beforehand what the super will help with, such as unlocking the cellar and letting the crew into vacant apartments, and keep the super informed each day.

Can the street panel's camera be used to check who rang after a delivery goes missing?
+

If the chosen system saves a snapshot or clip with each call, yes, the board or manager can review who rang and when. How long those images are kept and who may view them should be decided by the board and written down. We set the retention period and the viewing permissions at handover.

Yes. Intercom work is performed under NYS license #12000287431, and AESS carries general liability insurance. If your board or managing agent requires a certificate of insurance naming the corporation, send us the requirements and the certificate is issued before work begins.
