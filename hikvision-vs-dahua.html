The two-minute version. Hikvision and Dahua are the two Hangzhou companies whose cameras and recorders fill more New York ceilings, storefront corners and basement racks than any other professional brands we service. On paper they are near twins. Both sell 4K and 8MP network cameras that see in color at night, both sell recorders that power the cameras over one network cable each, both classify people and vehicles with on-camera AI, both speak ONVIF so other brands can share a recorder, and both record locally with no monthly fee. Hikvision says it was founded in 2001; Dahua's own site gives its legal name as Zhejiang Dahua Technology Co., Ltd. If you read the specifications line by line, the honest result is a near tie with small wins on each side.

What breaks the tie in 2026 is not a spec sheet. It is who stands behind the product in the United States. Hikvision USA is still here: in its September 2026 notice about the latest Federal Communications Commission rules, the company told dealers that sales, technical support and warranty service continue in the U.S. for uses outside the restricted categories. Dahua's American arm is gone. An FCC order released January 20, 2026 records that Dahua USA was formally dissolved as of December 31, 2025, that its Certificate of Dissolution was filed with the California Secretary of State on December 17, 2025, and that Luminys, the Taiwan-backed company that bought the business in 2024, confirmed it would not fill any orders for Dahua-branded products after that date. So when a New York homeowner, landlord or shop owner asks us which to buy new, our answer is Hikvision. When someone already owns a Dahua system that works, our answer is to keep it, harden it and extend it carefully, because the cameras do not stop working because a U.S. subsidiary closed.

Two more facts belong at the top. First, both companies sit on the FCC's Covered List, with entries dated March 12, 2021 that apply when the equipment is used for public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, or other national security purposes. That makes neither brand the right pick for a government-linked building, a federal contractor, or a nonprofit spending a federal security grant. Second, for a private home, a walk-up apartment building or a store, the FCC has said in writing that its 2026 import and marketing prohibition does not affect the continued use of equipment you already bought legally. The rest of this page explains each of those points with the source, then goes through cameras, recorders, apps, fees, security history, warranties and our installed prices, so you can make the call yourself.

The Verdict by Buyer

Who Should Pick Hikvision and Who Should Stay With Dahua

Hikvision is the better fit if

  • You are putting in a new wired system at a private house, a two- or three-family, a walk-up apartment building, a store or an office that has no government, grant or federal-contract tie.
  • You want a U.S. company you can name on the warranty paperwork. Hikvision USA publishes its repair-warranty table, from 36 to 60 months depending on the product line, and says warranty service continues for non-restricted uses.
  • You want color video at night on a stoop, a driveway or an alley, and you are fine with a soft white light coming on, or you want an infrared model where light would bother neighbors.
  • You want a camera that can take a hit. The dome we compared is rated IK10, which matters for a vestibule, a laundry room or a hallway ceiling that a tenant can reach with a broom handle.
  • You care about long-term parts. A brand with a working U.S. distribution channel is simply easier to match in three years, when a camera dies and you want the replacement to look and behave like the other seven.

Dahua is the better fit if

  • You already own a Dahua recorder and cameras that work. Keeping a sound system running is almost always cheaper than replacing it, and Dahua hardware does not stop recording because Dahua USA closed.
  • Your existing Dahua recorder has open channels and you want to add a camera or two. We can often add another brand's ONVIF camera to it, or a Dahua camera when matching stock is available, and keep one app.
  • Active deterrence is the thing you care about most, for example a closed shop's back door where a siren and red-and-blue lights tell people to keep moving. Dahua's TiOC models are built around that, and if you already run DMSS, staying with it keeps alerts in one place.

Notice what is missing from the Dahua list: a brand-new Dahua system for a property that has none. That is not a judgment of the cameras. It is that we cannot honestly sell a new owner a product line whose U.S. company no longer exists and whose U.S. warranty path we could not confirm, when a near-identical Hikvision system comes with a published U.S. warranty table.

When we would steer you to neither

If the building houses a government office or a public-safety agency, if you are a federal contractor, if the cameras will be paid for with a federal security grant, or if the property could fall under the FCC's critical-infrastructure definition, pick a brand that is not on the Covered List. Our best security camera brands hub lists the ones we install, including Hanwha, Axis and Luminys, and explains where each fits. If you simply would rather not own either Chinese brand, that is a reasonable preference too, and we will quote an alternative alongside.

What Changed in 2026

Dahua USA Is Gone: What the FCC Order Says and What It Means for You

Most Hikvision vs Dahua comparisons that rank today were written by resellers in Australia, Britain or the Gulf, and almost none of them mention the change that matters most for a buyer in Brooklyn or the Bronx. Here is the sequence, with the source for each step.

2024: the sale to Luminys

In January 2024, SecurityInfoWatch reported a letter from Dahua Technology USA to dealers and distributors saying the company was selling its North American business to a Taiwan-based manufacturer, Luminys Systems, which the report described as believed to be a subsidiary of Foxlink. The letter promised no impact on dealers and continued technical support at the time. Luminys went on to sell cameras under its own name, which is a separate product line and not covered by this comparison.

December 31, 2025: dissolution and the last orders

The FCC's Office of Engineering and Technology and its Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau released an order, DA 26-66, on January 20, 2026. It quotes a filing from Luminys stating that Dahua USA "has been formally dissolved as a legal entity" as of December 31, 2025, with its Certificate of Dissolution filed in California on December 17, 2025, and that Luminys "will not, after December 31, 2025, fulfill any orders for 'Dahua'-branded products." On that basis the FCC withdrew its earlier action against Luminys's own equipment authorizations and closed the proceeding. SecurityInfoWatch reported the same facts on January 23, 2026.

What it means if you already own Dahua

Nothing switches off. A Dahua recorder records to its own drives, and Dahua cameras keep streaming to it. The DMSS app was still listed and updated on the U.S. App Store when we checked in November 2026. What changes is the paperwork behind the hardware. We could not confirm who, if anyone, now honors a Dahua-branded warranty claim in the United States, or what firmware and app support U.S. owners can count on. Our practical advice is simple: keep your invoices, write down the recorder's model and firmware build, make sure the recorder is not exposed directly to the internet, and plan replacements one camera at a time as they fail rather than all at once.

What it means if you are buying new

Dahua's global site still lists new models, and its older eyeball camera from the same series, the IPC-HDW3849H-AS-PV, is now tagged "Discontinued" there. Product lines turn over quickly in this industry, and a system bought today is one you will want to match in five years. With no U.S. company behind the Dahua name, that matching gets harder every year. That is the main reason our recommendation for a new system is Hikvision, or a brand off the Covered List if your building needs one.

Is Luminys just Dahua with a new label?

The FCC's January 2026 order concluded that Luminys is not an entity identified on the Covered List and that its equipment is not currently barred from equipment authorization. That is the regulator's finding, and it is why our hub lists Luminys among the brands we install for buildings that need to stay off the Covered List. Luminys products have their own names, apps and spec sheets, so we treat them as their own brand rather than as a Dahua substitute, and we do not compare them on this page.

Federal Rules

The FCC Covered List, the 2022 Authorization Ban and NDAA Section 889 in Plain English

Both brands carry the same federal footnote, and it is the single most misunderstood part of the Hikvision vs Dahua question. Here is what each rule actually says, in the order it happened, and what it means for a private New York property. We are installers, not lawyers. If a contract, a lease or a grant depends on this, ask whoever wrote the requirement and keep their answer with your system records.

March 12, 2021: both companies added to the Covered List

The FCC keeps a list of equipment it has found to pose an unacceptable national security risk. On March 12, 2021 it added video surveillance and telecommunications equipment produced or provided by Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company and by Dahua Technology Company, in each case "to the extent it is used for the purpose of public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes." Read the last half of that sentence twice. The listing is about the use, not about every camera in every building.

November 2022: no new FCC authorizations

In November 2022 the FCC barred new equipment authorizations for Covered List equipment, Hikvision and Dahua included. Its news release said the rules applied to future authorizations. In practice that froze both brands' U.S. product lines at whatever models had already been authorized, which is why each brand's U.S. lineup is limited to models that were authorized before then.

Section 889 of the 2019 defense law

Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal year 2019 names Hikvision and Dahua. Part B, in effect since August 13, 2020, bars federal agencies from contracting with companies that use covered equipment, which is why a federal contractor should not have either brand anywhere in its own offices. Federal grant rules at 2 CFR 200.216 separately bar recipients from spending grant or loan money on Hikvision or Dahua video surveillance equipment for the listed purposes. A house of worship or a nonprofit using a Nonprofit Security Grant should treat both brands as off the table for that project.

June 2026: older models, too, for the restricted uses

On June 26, 2026, in Public Notice DA 26-635, the FCC prohibited the continued importation and marketing of previously authorized equipment from the companies on its 2021 list. For use-based entries like Hikvision and Dahua, the prohibition applies to importing and marketing for the restricted uses. The Federal Register notice set the effective date at July 16, 2026, and it states plainly that "consumers may continue to use any device or equipment that they currently possess, if the equipment was legally purchased and maintains an existing equipment authorization." The FCC's own notice says the prohibition "will not affect the continued use or operation of already-purchased communications equipment."

October 13, 2026: a narrower definition of critical infrastructure

The Covered List entries mention "physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure," and in April 2024 a federal appeals court told the FCC its earlier definition of that phrase was too broad. The FCC's answer took effect on October 13, 2026 (91 FR 57798). It keeps the long-standing federal definition of systems and assets so vital that their loss would have a debilitating impact on security, the economy or public health and safety, ties it to the 16 critical infrastructure sectors and 55 National Critical Functions published by the Department of Homeland Security, and drops the "connected to" language the court objected to. If your property is a utility site, a water or transit facility, a hospital or anything else that could fit those sectors, ask before you buy either brand.

What Hikvision says, and what it is doing about it

Hikvision USA's September 2026 notice calls the rules "not a blanket ban," says there is no requirement to remove installed products, tells dealers to apply a use-restriction label to equipment, and says its Hik-Connect app is a software service that is not itself subject to equipment authorization. Hikvision also told partners in April 2026 that it filed a petition for judicial review in December 2025 challenging related FCC actions, and it acknowledged that limits on importation could affect access to replacement parts, warranty service and technical support. Those are the company's own statements, and we repeat them so you can weigh them, not as an endorsement.

So what does a New York homeowner or landlord do?

For a private house, a co-op apartment, a walk-up or a store with no government or federal-money tie, the rules above do not make it illegal to own or to keep using either brand, and they do not require you to tear anything out. Where they bite is supply and support: no new models since 2022, older ones now restricted for certain buyers, and in Dahua's case no U.S. company at all. Where the rules apply directly, for government sites, contractors, grant-funded projects and critical infrastructure, we do not install either brand, and we will quote one that is off the list.

Night Video

ColorVu vs WizColor and TiOC: Color at Night on a New York Block

Night is where most of our customers' footage matters, because night is when the package walks off the stoop, when the car door gets tried and when someone sleeps in the vestibule. Both brands have spent years selling "color at night," and both get there the same basic way: a lens that lets in a lot of light, a sensitive sensor, and a small light on the camera that comes on when the scene is too dark.

Hikvision ColorVu

The DS-2CD2187G2-LSU dome we compared has an F1.0 lens and Hikvision lists color video down to 0.0008 lux, and 0 lux with its light on. Its supplement light is white light, reaching up to 30 m,, and Hikvision lists smart supplement light on the model. Hikvision describes ColorVu as full-color imaging in a variety of lighting conditions, including darkness. On a typical brownstone stoop or a driveway beside a two-family house, 30 m is more reach than you need.

Dahua WizColor and TiOC

The IPC-HFW3849T1-ZAS-PV-PRO bullet has an F1.2 motorized zoom lens from 2.7 to 12 mm on a larger 1/1.8-inch sensor, and Dahua lists color down to 0.0006 lux. Its lights are multi-core infrared plus warm light, each rated up to 60 m, and Dahua lists Smart Dual Light, automatic and manual illuminator control, and a zoom-priority mode. On the earlier eyeball camera in the same series, Dahua describes built-in infrared and LED lights with three illumination modes. Dahua's camera page also promotes a newer WizColor 2.0 generation with an F0.8 aperture that it says captures 1.6 times more light than F1.0; we did not identify which models carry it, so we have not compared it here.

Which one we would put on your house

The numbers are close enough that mounting height and the street light across the road will matter more. Two practical points decide it for most New York jobs. The first is the neighbors. A white or warm light that comes on all night across a narrow back yard in Bay Ridge or a shared alley in Astoria will irritate the people whose bedroom windows face it, and a camera that can run in infrared after dark is kinder to them than one whose light stays on all night. Hikvision also sells infrared models for spots like that. The second is reach. A Dahua varifocal bullet with 60 m of light is a better tool for a long parking lot or a warehouse yard; a fixed Hikvision dome is a better tool for a stoop, a lobby or a laundry room. We pick the camera for the spot, and on a new system that means a Hikvision model chosen for that spot.

Active deterrence: lights and sirens

Dahua's TiOC models combine the camera with red and blue warning lights and a speaker; the bullet we compared lists a built-in speaker with a maximum sound level of 110 dB at 10 cm in Dahua's lab tests, plus dual microphones for two-way talk. Hikvision's TandemVu PTZ cameras carry a strobe and an audio alarm speaker, which Hikvision pitches for parking lots, crosswalks and industrial parks, though the dome we compared has a microphone and no speaker. Deterrence works best at a closed business: a back door, a loading area, a roll-down gate. At a home it needs careful tuning, because a siren that goes off for the mail carrier is the fastest way to get a feature switched off for good.

Smart Detection

AcuSense vs WizSense: Fewer False Alerts From Cars, Cats and Headlights

The single biggest complaint we hear about older camera systems is alert fatigue. A phone that buzzes forty times a night for headlights, rain, a cat on the fence and a plastic bag soon gets ignored, and then the one alert that matters is missed. Both brands answer that with deep-learning detection that sorts people and vehicles from everything else.

Hikvision AcuSense

Hikvision describes AcuSense as deep-learning algorithms built into selected cameras and recorders that automatically detect humans and vehicles and push notifications only for them. Its U.S. page says the configuration-free function is on by default. Hikvision's own marketing says AcuSense reduces false alarms by up to 90 percent; that is the company's claim, not a measurement of ours. On the recorder we compared, the DS-7608NXI-K2/8P, the device itself can run perimeter protection on 2 channels at 4MP and Motion Detection 2.0 with human and vehicle recognition on all channels, and it adds face picture comparison on 4 channels. When the camera has AcuSense of its own, all 8 channels can use the camera's detection.

Dahua WizSense

Dahua describes WizSense as a series of AI products based on deep learning that focus on humans and vehicles. On the bullet we compared that means tripwire and intrusion rules that classify people and vehicles, stay and loitering detection, Smart Motion Detection 4.0, face detection, and AcuPick, which works with a Dahua recorder to search live and recorded video for a particular person or vehicle. The NVR4208-8P-EI recorder adds 2 channels of perimeter protection, 4 channels of SMD and 1 channel of face detection and recognition on its own.

What actually cuts false alerts

The AI on both brands works well when the camera is aimed and set up properly and badly when it is not. What makes the difference on our jobs is ordinary: a detection line drawn across your walk rather than the public sidewalk, a minimum target size so a cat does not count as a person, a schedule that mutes the porch camera while you are home, and a camera mounted high enough that headlights do not sweep straight across the lens. We set those up on every handover. On paper Dahua's recorder offers more built-in AI channels and AcuPick search is useful on a busy storefront. Hikvision's on-by-default classification is simpler for a homeowner who never wants to open a settings menu. Both are good enough that the setup matters more than the brand.

Recording

Recorders and Storage: Where Hikvision and Dahua Keep Your Video

Unlike Ring or Google Nest, neither brand needs the cloud to keep your footage. The recorder in your closet or basement is the system. Every camera plugs into it with one network cable that carries power and video, it writes everything to hard drives inside it, and the app connects to it when you want to watch from your phone. That makes the recorder the most important box in a Hikvision vs Dahua decision, and the two we compared are close cousins.

Hikvision DS-7608NXI-K2/8P

Hikvision calls it an 8-channel PoE 1U K Series AcuSense 4K NVR. It has 8 PoE ports with a total PoE power budget of up to 80 W, 2 SATA drive bays that each take a drive of up to 16 TB, 80 Mbps of incoming bandwidth, a 4K HDMI output and a separate VGA output, and it records cameras up to 12MP. It runs from a 48 V supply and is rated for 14 °F to 131 °F. The firmware listed on Hikvision's U.S. page in November 2026 was dated June 2026, which tells you the model is still being maintained.

Dahua NVR4208-8P-EI

Dahua calls it an 8-channel 1U 8PoE WizSense recorder with 2 drive bays. It has 8 PoE ports with a 130 W total budget and up to 25.5 W on a single port, 2 bays that each take a drive of up to 20 TB, 256 Mbps of incoming bandwidth with AI switched off and 180 Mbps with it on, a 4K HDMI output and VGA, and it records cameras up to 16MP. It runs on ordinary 100 to 240 V mains and has the same 14 °F to 131 °F rating.

What those numbers mean in practice

On paper the Dahua recorder has more headroom: more PoE power, larger drives and more bandwidth. That headroom matters when you plan eight high-resolution cameras with heaters, lights and sirens, because a power-hungry camera like the Dahua bullet can draw up to 18.3 W on PoE at full tilt. On the Hikvision recorder we would budget the cameras against the 80 W total, or power a long-range camera from a separate PoE switch. Neither brand gives a simple "days of recording" number, because retention depends on resolution, frame rate, compression and how busy the scene is, so we size the drives on every quote and set retention in writing. For most homes and small buildings, two weeks to a month of continuous recording is the target we design to, with motion-event recording added where a longer history is needed.

Mixing brands on one recorder

Both cameras list ONVIF profiles, the industry standard that lets one brand's camera stream to another brand's recorder. Dahua's recorder page goes further and lists access for ONVIF, RTSP and named brands including Axis, Hanwha, Sony and Panasonic. In practice, a mixed system usually loses some of the clever features, such as the brand's own AI events and search, because those travel over each brand's own protocol. It is still the cheapest way to keep a working Dahua recorder in service while you replace failed cameras with something you can buy and warranty, and it is how we usually handle a partial upgrade.

Power cuts and the recorder

Every camera on a PoE recorder goes dark when the recorder loses power. On our installs, either brand, the recorder and the network gear go on a battery backup sized to ride out a short outage and shut the recorder down cleanly in a long one. A recorder that crashes mid-write is the most common reason we find a corrupted drive and a gap in the footage, and it is cheap to prevent.

Security Record

Hikvision and Dahua Security Flaws: What Was Found and How We Lock Systems Down

Both brands have had serious, publicly listed flaws, and the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency keeps some of them on its catalog of vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild. That catalog is the most useful single fact here, because it separates theoretical weaknesses from ones that attackers actually use.

Hikvision

CVE-2021-36260 was a command-injection flaw in the web server of many Hikvision cameras and some recorders that let an attacker run commands without logging in; it was scored 9.8 out of 10 and CISA set a January 24, 2022 deadline for federal agencies to patch it. An older Hikvision authentication flaw, CVE-2017-7921, was added to the same CISA catalog on March 5, 2026, which tells you that cameras running firmware from years ago are still being targeted. The fix in both cases is Hikvision's firmware update and keeping the camera off the open internet.

Dahua

CVE-2021-33044 was an authentication bypass during login on Dahua devices, also scored 9.8, and CISA added it to its exploited-vulnerability catalog on August 21, 2024 with the instruction to apply the vendor's fix or stop using the product if no fix is available. In July 2025 Dahua published an advisory for two more flaws, CVE-2025-31700 and CVE-2025-31701, scored 8.1, affecting several IP camera and PTZ series, and said firmware builds dated after April 16, 2025 were not affected. For a U.S. owner of a Dahua system, the question now is how firmware updates will reach you without a U.S. company, which we could not confirm.

Where that leaves the comparison

Neither brand has a clean record, and neither is unusual among camera makers for having flaws. What we see on service calls is that the damage almost always comes from the same three habits: a recorder port-forwarded straight onto the internet, a default or shared password, and firmware that has not been touched since the day it was installed. Those are fixable on both brands. The difference in 2026 is that Hikvision still publishes and maintains firmware through a U.S. company, while Dahua owners are relying on a global site with no U.S. presence.

Settings we apply on every system, either brand

  • Latest firmware on the recorder and every camera on install day, with the build dates written on your handover sheet.
  • Cameras on the recorder's own PoE network, so they never sit directly on your home or office network.
  • No port forwarding and no universal plug-and-play; remote viewing through the app only.
  • Unique admin password stored with you, separate user accounts for everyone else, and default accounts disabled.
  • Time set from the network so every clip carries the right timestamp when the police or an insurer asks.

Existing Systems

Keep, Extend or Replace: A Plan for an Existing Dahua or Hikvision System

Many of the Hikvision and Dahua calls we take are not new installs. They come from someone who bought a house, took over a building or inherited a store and found a recorder in a closet, a box of unlabeled cables and an app login nobody remembers. Here is how we work through it.

Step one: find out what you have

We read the model numbers and firmware dates off the recorder and every camera, check the drives' health and how many days of video are actually on them, and test each cable run. If you want more coverage, the recorder's open channels come first. On a Hikvision recorder, we add Hikvision cameras. When the recorder itself has to go, the cable usually stays. Network cable that was run well ten years ago will power and carry today's cameras. Myths

Hikvision vs Dahua Claims That Need a Second Look

"Hikvision and Dahua are banned in the U.S., so you have to take them down"

No. The FCC's Covered List entries for both companies are scoped to public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and national security uses, and the FCC's 2026 notice says its import and marketing prohibition does not affect the continued use of equipment you already bought legally. The Federal Register notice says consumers may keep using devices they own. Federal contractors and grant-funded projects are the exception, because separate federal rules apply to them.

"Dahua is gone, so my Dahua cameras will stop working"

No. What ended on December 31, 2025 was Dahua USA, the American company, and orders for Dahua-branded products through Luminys. Your recorder keeps recording and your cameras keep streaming. The real risks are warranty claims, firmware updates and finding matching replacements, which is why we plan Dahua systems camera by camera from here on.

"Dahua is just a cheaper Hikvision"

Not on the evidence of the two systems we compared. Dahua's bullet has more light reach and built-in deterrence, and its recorder has more PoE power and takes larger drives. Hikvision's dome has the faster lens and a vandal rating. Feature for feature, they are peers. The gap in 2026 is support, not quality.

"Color night vision means there is no light on the camera"

Not on these models. Hikvision lists a white supplement light on the ColorVu dome, and Dahua lists infrared and warm light on the WizColor bullet. Both rely on a very open lens and a sensitive sensor first, and both turn on a light when the scene is too dark for color. Plan where that light will shine before you mount the camera.

"More megapixels always means a better picture"

Both cameras we compared are 8MP, 3840 by 2160. Whether you can read a face at the gate depends far more on the lens angle, the mounting height, the light and the recorder's bitrate than on the number on the box. An 8MP camera aimed at the sky over a driveway records a beautiful sky.

"These brands send your video to China"

We cannot verify claims about any company's data flows either way, and we do not make them. What we can control is the setup: on our installs, video records to the recorder in your building, the recorder sits on its own camera network with no forwarded ports, and remote viewing goes through the app with accounts in your name. Hikvision says its optional cloud storage package uses Amazon Web Services storage. If you want no remote access at all, we can leave the recorder offline and you can view it on a monitor on site.
