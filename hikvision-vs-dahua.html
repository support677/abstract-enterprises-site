The two-minute version. Hikvision and Dahua are the two large Hangzhou camera makers whose equipment fills a remarkable share of New York's basements, lobbies and storefront ceilings. Their professional cameras are close cousins on paper. Hikvision's DS-2CD2387G2-LSU/SL is an 8MP turret with an F1.0 lens, white light out to 30 meters, a strobe and a speaker, a microSD slot up to 512 GB and an IP67 metal housing. Dahua's IPC-HDW3849H-AS-PV-PRO is an 8MP eyeball with an F1.0 lens, infrared and warm light out to 30 meters on the 2.8 mm lens, red and blue warning lights and a speaker, a microSD slot up to 512 GB and an IP67 housing. Put one of each on the same alley wall and most owners would struggle to say which picture came from which brand. That is why the honest comparison in 2026 is not really about pixels.

It is about three things that sit outside the camera. The first is federal status. Both companies have been on the FCC's Covered List since March 12, 2021, for equipment used for public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and other national security purposes. In June 2026 the FCC went further and prohibited the continued importation and marketing of already-authorized covered equipment for those purposes, while saying plainly that people may keep using equipment they already own. The second is the company behind the box in the United States. Hikvision still publishes a US warranty table with up to 60 months on its professional cameras and recorders. Dahua's American operations were transferred to Luminys Systems Corporation, which says it honors Dahua Technology USA's existing warranties and service agreements. The third is the security history of the equipment on your wall, because each brand has a flaw in the federal catalog of vulnerabilities known to be exploited, and an unpatched recorder is a real risk whatever logo is on it.

So the rule we give New York owners is short. If you have a working Hikvision or Dahua system in a private home, shop or apartment building with no government tenant, you can keep using it, and repairing or extending it is often the cheapest sound choice, as long as the firmware is current and the recorder is not exposed to the internet. If you are starting from nothing, we would rather quote a brand that is not on the Covered List, and we will show you that price next to what a Hikvision or Dahua extension would cost. If the building has any federal, state or city tie, a public safety function, or a lender or insurer asking about the Covered List, we do not install either brand there. Between the two, Hikvision is the easier one to support for the long haul in the United States today, and Dahua remains a sound camera for owners who already have it. The rest of this page explains each edge of that rule.

The Federal Picture

The Covered List, Section 889 and the FCC's June 2026 Import Rule, in Plain English

No part of the Hikvision vs Dahua question causes more confusion in New York than the federal rules, and no part is more often explained badly. Forum posts swing between "nothing has changed" and "both brands are illegal now." Neither is right. There are three separate federal layers, each with its own reach, and the brands are treated identically under all three. Here they are in the order they arrived.

Layer one: Section 889 and federal buying

Section 889 of the John S. McCain National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2019 is carried into federal contracts by clause 52.204-25 of the Federal Acquisition Regulation. That clause defines covered equipment to include, "for the purpose of public safety, security of Government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes, video surveillance and telecommunications equipment produced by Hytera Communications Corporation, Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company, or Dahua Technology Company (or any subsidiary or affiliate of such entities)." Two prohibitions follow. A contractor may not provide the government any equipment, system or service that uses covered equipment as a substantial or essential component. And under the second part, the prohibition "applies to the use of covered telecommunications equipment or services, regardless of whether that use is in performance of work under a Federal contract." The clause also flows down into subcontracts.

In everyday terms, Section 889 is aimed at the federal government's own buying and at the companies that sell to it. A private homeowner in Bay Ridge is not a federal contractor. A medical practice in Midtown that bills federal programs, a construction company with a federal job in the pipeline, or a nonprofit with a federal grant may well be bound by contract terms that reach the cameras in their own offices, and those owners should read their agreements or ask their contracting officer before buying either brand.

Layer two: the FCC Covered List

The FCC keeps a list of communications equipment and services that a national security agency or Congress has found to pose an unacceptable risk. On March 12, 2021, it added "video surveillance and telecommunications equipment produced by Dahua Technology Company, to the extent it is used for the purpose of public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes, including telecommunications or video surveillance services provided by such entity or using such equipment." The Hikvision entry, for Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company, has the same date and the same words. A footnote on the FCC page adds that the listing should be read to include subsidiaries and affiliates. The list we read was dated November 6, 2026, and both entries were unchanged.

The phrase that matters is "to the extent it is used for the purpose of." These are use-based entries. Other entries on the same list, such as the ones for Huawei and ZTE telecommunications equipment, are not limited that way. For Hikvision and Dahua, the FCC tied the listing to what the equipment is used for. That is why we keep telling private owners that the list is not, by itself, an instruction to rip out a working system.

Layer three: equipment authorization and the 2026 import and marketing rule

Almost every electronic device sold in the United States needs an FCC equipment authorization. In its 2022 rules, the FCC stopped new authorizations for covered equipment. For the use-based entries, the FCC's June 2026 notice explains how that played out: new authorization applications from those manufacturers "are generally prohibited until those manufacturers have submitted, and received Commission approval for, compliance plans," and "the Commission has yet to approve any such compliance plans," partly because a federal appeals court in 2024 upheld the FCC's order but sent back its definition of "critical infrastructure" to be rewritten.

That left a gap: equipment authorized before the 2022 rules could still be imported and marketed. On June 26, 2026, the FCC's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau and Office of Engineering and Technology closed most of it in Public Notice DA 26-635. The notice prohibits "the continued importation and marketing of certain previously authorized covered equipment added to the Covered List in 2024 or earlier," effective ten days after Federal Register publication. The summary appeared in the Federal Register on July 6, 2026, so the rule took effect in mid-July. For use-based listings like Hikvision's and Dahua's, the FCC wrote that importation and marketing "will only be prohibited if imported or marketed for the purpose of" public safety, government facilities, "other national security purposes," and physical surveillance of critical infrastructure, with that last purpose suspended until the FCC adopts a new definition of critical infrastructure.

The same notice is equally plain about people who already own the equipment: the prohibition "will not affect the continued use or operation of already-purchased communications equipment." The Federal Register summary puts it in consumer terms: people "may continue to use any device or equipment that they currently possess, if the equipment was legally purchased and maintains an existing equipment authorization." It also explains why replacements are caught: "Allowing imports and marketing replacements for existing covered equipment would defeat the entire purpose of this prohibition." Hikvision argued in the proceeding that the FCC lacked authority to do this; the FCC noted its "continued disagreement" and went ahead.

What the three layers mean on a New York block

For a family in a semi-detached house in Marine Park with a Hikvision recorder in the den, nothing in the three layers stops them from using it, and nothing requires them to remove it. For a deli in Jackson Heights with a Dahua kit over the register, the same is true. For a building that houses a city agency office, a public school program, a police-related tenant, a utility facility or a federal contractor, the picture changes quickly, because those are exactly the uses the listings name. Those owners should plan to move to a brand that is not on the list, and we will not install new Hikvision or Dahua equipment there.

The part that does affect ordinary owners is supply. When the authorization door closed in 2022 and the import and marketing door narrowed in 2026, the practical availability of new Hikvision and Dahua cameras and recorders through ordinary US channels became less predictable. We do not claim to know which individual models any distributor still stocks on a given day, and we will not guess on this page. What we can say is that when a camera on a Hikvision or Dahua system fails, we check what is available legally and in stock for that purpose before quoting a like-for-like swap, and if the answer is unclear, we quote an ONVIF camera from another maker that the existing recorder can still record.

Who Stands Behind It

What Happened to Dahua in America, and Why It Changes This Comparison

For most of the last decade, a New York installer buying Dahua equipment was buying through Dahua Technology USA, the company's American arm, with its own sales, support and warranty desk. That arm no longer operates under the Dahua name. In a letter that installers shared online, Luminys Systems Corporation announced that it had "completed the transition of Dahua Technology USA's operations to Luminys Systems Corporation," and promised that it "will honor all existing Dahua Technology USA commitments, including warranties and service agreements." Trade press reported Luminys as a subsidiary of Foxlink, a company based in Taiwan, and later reported that Luminys was cleared to market its equipment in the United States again once Dahua USA had been dissolved, after an FCC review. Our own Luminys camera installation page covers that history in more detail.

This is not the first time Dahua has stepped back from an American brand. Dahua previously owned Lorex, the home camera brand sold in big-box stores, and announced its sale to Skywatch, a privately held cloud company headquartered in Taiwan, for around 72 million US dollars. We cover what that means for Lorex owners on our Lorex vs Reolink comparison.

Put together, the practical result for a New York owner is this. If you have a Dahua-branded recorder and cameras, the equipment keeps working, the DMSS app and SmartPSS software still exist on Dahua's site, and the company that took over the American business says it is honoring the warranties and service agreements it inherited. What you no longer have is a Dahua-branded US company growing the Dahua line for American buyers. Hikvision, by contrast, still publishes a US-specific warranty and return policy on its US site, with terms by product category and a named returns process through its authorized distributors. That difference in who stands behind the product is the main reason we lean toward Hikvision when an owner asks us which of the two is easier to keep supported, even though neither is our first choice for a brand-new system.

Is Luminys just Dahua with a new label?

Owners ask this constantly, and it deserves a careful answer rather than a slogan. Luminys took over the people and operations of Dahua's American business and honors its warranties, so the history is shared. Luminys also sells its own camera and recorder lines, with names like LumiLuxSmart, LumiNightKing and LumiColor on our Luminys price list for NYC, and it is under Taiwanese rather than Chinese ownership. Whether that is enough for a particular board, lender or insurer is their call, not ours. What we can tell you is that a Luminys system does not carry the Covered List entry that Dahua Technology Company's equipment carries, which is why it is one of the brands we quote when a Dahua owner wants to replace rather than repair.

Who Should Pick Which

Who Should Stay With Hikvision, Who Should Stay With Dahua, and Who Should Pick Neither

Hikvision is the better fit if

  • You already have a Hikvision recorder that works and you want to add, replace or re-aim cameras without starting over.
  • You want a published US warranty term on professional equipment you already own: Hikvision's US table lists 60 months for professional IP cameras and 9 Series professional recorders.
  • Bright, color video at night on a dark side yard or back lot matters most, and a steady white light on motion is acceptable to the neighbors.
  • Several people in the family or the building staff already use Hik-Connect and you would rather not retrain them.
  • The building is private and has no government tenant, public safety use or federal contract tied to it.

Dahua is the better fit if

  • You already have a Dahua recorder and cameras that work, and the DMSS app is how you check the store or the house.
  • Your storefront relies on the red and blue warning lights and the siren to move people along from the gate after closing, and you want the same behavior on a new camera at the back door.
  • You want a camera rated down to −40°F for an exposed roof or a rear yard with no shelter, which is lower than the Hikvision turret we compared.
  • You have an existing Dahua Technology USA warranty claim and want it handled through Luminys, which says it honors those commitments.
  • Again, the building is private, with no government tie.

When we would steer you to neither

If you are buying a brand-new system from nothing, we start with brands that are not on the FCC Covered List: our own non-branded recorder systems, Luminys, Uniview, Hanwha or UniFi, depending on the building. The cameras are comparable for most New York jobs, the prices on our lists are in the same range as a Hikvision or Dahua build, and you avoid every future conversation with a board, a buyer's lawyer or an insurer about the list. If your building has any government, public safety or critical-infrastructure connection, the answer is neither, full stop. And if your Hikvision or Dahua recorder is old enough that its firmware can no longer be updated against the flaws in the federal catalog of exploited vulnerabilities, we will recommend replacing the recorder even if the cameras stay.

After Dark

Color at Night: Hikvision ColorVu 3.0 vs Dahua WizColor and TiOC

Both companies built their reputations in the last few years on one promise: color video in the dark, instead of the gray infrared picture older cameras gave you. In New York that promise matters more than the spec sheets suggest, because a police detective, an insurance adjuster or a landlord's lawyer wants to know the color of a jacket, a car or a bag, and a black-and-white clip cannot tell them. Each brand gets there with a very wide lens opening, a sensitive sensor and a light of its own, but they light the scene differently.

How Hikvision does it

Hikvision calls its approach ColorVu, and the current generation ColorVu 3.0. Its technology page lists an F1.0 "super large aperture," what it calls active alignment, AI wide dynamic range that switches itself on according to the brightness of the scene, 3D LUT color correction for accurate color, and what it calls Super Confocal Technology, so the same lens stays sharp under visible and infrared light. Hikvision says that with ColorVu 3.0, cameras rely "less on supplemental light in darkness." On the DS-2CD2387G2-LSU/SL we compared, that adds up to a color sensitivity of 0.0005 lux at F1.0, zero lux with its white light on, and a white light that reaches up to 30 meters. The sensor is a large 1/1.2-inch chip, which helps it gather light. Hikvision describes the result simply as "24/7 colorful imaging."

The cost of that approach is the light itself. A camera that leans on white light glows when it fires, and on a narrow Brooklyn side yard or a Queens driveway that faces a neighbor's bedroom window, a white light that comes on every time a cat walks past is a fast way to start a feud. Hikvision's Smart Hybrid Light technology, listed in its ColorVu family, lets a camera run infrared until something worth seeing happens and then switch to white light for color. On the models that offer it, we set exactly that, and we aim the light so it covers your property and not the neighbor's.

How Dahua does it

Dahua calls its color-at-night approach WizColor, and its three-in-one cameras TiOC. The IPC-HDW3849H-AS-PV-PRO we compared also uses an F1.0 lens, with a color sensitivity of 0.0004 lux and zero lux with its illuminator on. Instead of one white light, it carries two multi-core illuminators that combine infrared and warm light, each reaching up to 30 meters on the 2.8 mm lens and up to 40 meters on the 3.6 mm version. The sensor is a 1/1.8-inch chip, smaller than the Hikvision turret's. On its camera range page, Dahua also promotes a newer WizColor 2.0 generation with an F0.8 aperture that, in Dahua's words, "captures 1.6 times more light than F1.0." We have not tested that generation on a New York job, so we pass it on as Dahua's claim rather than ours.

The warm light is the part neighbors notice. A warmer tone is less harsh than a cold white floodlight, which can make Dahua's approach easier to live with on a residential block. The trade is that warm light changes how colors read on camera, so we spend a few minutes at handover walking a family member through the yard so they can see how a red jacket and a blue jacket actually look on the recorder at midnight.

Which picture we would rather have

On paper the Hikvision turret has the larger sensor and the Dahua eyeball has the slightly better listed sensitivity and the wider field of view, 109 degrees against 102 degrees on the 2.8 mm lens. On the wall, the bigger differences come from things neither spec sheet controls: a streetlamp shining straight into the lens, a dirty dome, a camera mounted too high to see faces, or a recorder set to a low bit rate to save drive space. We have pulled clear, usable color footage at night from both brands, and we have pulled useless footage from both brands where the camera was badly placed. If color at night is your main concern, the most important decision is where the camera goes and how the light is aimed, not which logo is on it.

Fewer False Alarms

Smart Detection: Hikvision AcuSense vs Dahua WizSense

The single biggest complaint we hear from owners of older camera systems of any brand is the phone that buzzes forty times a night because a tree moved, headlights swept across the wall or rain hit the lens. Both companies answered that complaint the same way, by putting a person-and-vehicle classifier in the camera. Hikvision brands it AcuSense; Dahua brands it WizSense. The names are different, the goal is the same.

Hikvision's detection tools

The Hikvision turret we compared lists line crossing, intrusion, region entrance and region exit as smart events, each of which can be set to trigger only on "specified target types (human and vehicle)," along with ordinary motion detection with the same filter and face capture. On its Pro Series page, Hikvision says its newest version, AcuSense 3.0, "reduces typical false alarms—such as lights reflecting on stationary vehicles—by up to 90%," and pairs with back-end tools it calls AcuSeek and AcuSearch for faster searches by text and image. That 90 percent figure is Hikvision's claim, not a measurement of ours. The recorder we compared, the DS-7608NXI-K2/8P, adds its own analysis: perimeter protection with human and vehicle recognition on two channels at 4MP, and what Hikvision calls Motion Detection 2.0 on all channels.

Dahua's detection tools

The Dahua eyeball we compared lists tripwire, intrusion, stay detection, loitering detection, face detection, audio detection, scene changing and what it calls SMD, Dahua's smart motion detection, among its alarm events. Loitering and stay detection are useful extras in New York, where the problem at a store gate or a building vestibule is often not someone passing through but someone who settles in for an hour. Dahua's WizSense recorders, like the NVR4108HS-8P-EI, add face recognition at the recorder level.

How we set either one up so it stays quiet

The classifier only helps if the zones are drawn well. On a typical brownstone stoop we draw the line across the top step, not the sidewalk, so a passing dog walker never triggers an alert but a person climbing to the door always does. On a storefront we set the intrusion zone to the space between the gate and the glass after closing hours only, so customers do not set it off during the day. In a lobby we use line crossing on the inner door so the camera alerts on people entering, not people leaving. On either brand, the tuning takes longer than the mounting, and it is the part most do-it-yourself installs skip. It is also the part that decides whether you keep checking the alerts or switch them off in the first month.

Lights and Sound

Strobes, Sirens and Talk-Back: Deterrence on Each Brand

The newest selling point for both brands is a camera that does something when it sees a person: flashes a light, plays a warning or lets you speak through it. For shop owners on commercial strips, it is often the feature they ask about first.

Hikvision's strobe and audible warning

The DS-2CD2387G2-LSU/SL carries the words "Strobe Light and Audible Warning" in its name. Hikvision's ColorVu page pairs ColorVu with a feature it calls Live Guard, where cameras "deter trespassers with visual and auditory warnings while sending notifications to users remotely."

Dahua lists its speaker at up to 110 decibels at 10 centimeters, noting that this is a laboratory figure. Its TiOC 2.0 page describes customized sounds and a light alarm to warn a target in the area, and names fire exits and emergency exits among the typical places for it. The red and blue flash is the feature that gives Dahua's TiOC cameras their recognizable look, and on a dark gate it reads to a passer-by as "this place is watched" in a way a plain dome does not.

How we set deterrence up in the city

Deterrence is the easiest feature to get wrong in New York. A siren or a recorded warning that fires at 2 a.m. on a block of attached houses is a noise complaint waiting to happen, and a red and blue light can look uncomfortably like a police car to a neighbor across the street. Our rule on either brand is to schedule it: lights and sound only during closed hours for a store, only on a zone that is clearly private property, and at a volume that a person standing at the gate hears but the apartment upstairs does not. We leave sound off entirely on cameras that face a shared yard or a neighbor's door. And we test it with the owner standing at the curb, so they know exactly what a passer-by experiences before we leave.

The Hikvision recorder we compared

The Dahua recorder we compared

How long a recorder actually keeps video

The number that matters more than either spec sheet is how many days of video the drive holds before it starts writing over the oldest footage. That depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, the compression, whether they record all the time or only on events, and the size of the drive. When we take over a building's existing system, the first thing we do is open the recording settings and work out the real number of days, because we regularly find recorders set up to keep three or four days when the owner believed it was a month. Each camera we compared also takes a microSD card up to 512 GB. It is a small extra cost per camera, and on a building where someone might pull the recorder's network switch, it is worth it on either brand.

Security Record

Patches and Hacks: Each Brand's Entry in the Federal Exploited-Flaws Catalog

Every network device has security flaws discovered over its life. What matters is whether the maker fixes them and whether owners install the fixes. The federal Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency keeps a catalog of flaws it knows are being exploited in the real world, and each of these two brands has at least one well-known entry in it. We name them here because they are the reason an unmaintained recorder in a basement is a genuine risk, not because either brand is uniquely careless.

Hikvision: CVE-2021-36260

The National Vulnerability Database describes CVE-2021-36260 as a command injection flaw in the web server of some Hikvision products. CISA added it to its catalog on January 10, 2022, under the name "Hikvision Improper Input Validation," with the required action "Apply updates per vendor instructions." In plain terms, Hikvision released firmware that fixes it, and a camera or recorder that has never been updated may still be open to it.

Dahua: CVE-2021-33044

The National Vulnerability Database describes CVE-2021-33044 as "the identity authentication bypass vulnerability found in some Dahua products during the login process," where attackers "can bypass device identity authentication by constructing malicious data packets." CISA added it to its catalog on August 21, 2024, as the "Dahua IP Camera Authentication Bypass Vulnerability," with the required action "Apply mitigations per vendor instructions or discontinue use of the product if mitigations are unavailable." That wording, discontinue use if no fix is available, is stronger than the Hikvision entry's, and it matters for owners of older Dahua equipment whose firmware may no longer be updated.

What we do on every Hikvision or Dahua system we touch

  • Check the firmware version on the recorder and each camera, and update to the maker's current release where one exists for that model.
  • Close any port forwarding on the building's router that exposes the recorder's web page to the internet, and use the brand's app connection instead.
  • Replace default and shared passwords with strong, unique ones, and set up separate user accounts instead of one login passed around the building.
  • Put the cameras on the recorder's own PoE ports or a separate network segment, so they cannot be reached from the building's guest Wi-Fi.
  • Turn off services the building does not use, such as unencrypted web access or old remote-access features.
  • Write down what we changed and hand it to the owner, so the next technician does not undo it.

If a recorder is too old to receive a fix for the flaw that affects it, we say so in writing and quote a replacement recorder. On a Dahua unit affected by CVE-2021-33044 with no available mitigation, CISA's own wording is the reason we will not leave it running on a network as-is.

Mixing Brands

Can Hikvision and Dahua Work Together, or With Another Brand?

Yes, within limits, and this is one of the most useful facts on the page for owners with a working recorder. Both cameras we compared list ONVIF Profile S, Profile G and Profile T. ONVIF is an industry standard that lets a camera from one maker send video to a recorder from another, Profile S covers live video, Profile G covers recording and playback on the device, and Profile T covers newer video formats and features. In practice, an ONVIF camera from almost any professional maker can be added to a Hikvision or Dahua recorder, and a Hikvision or Dahua camera can be added to most other recorders.

The limits are the smart features. Basic live video and recording cross over cleanly. Person-and-vehicle alerts, deterrence lights, two-way talk and some of the newer search features often work fully only when the camera and recorder come from the same maker, because each company uses its own protocols for them, Hikvision's ISAPI and Dahua's own interfaces, alongside the open standard. When we add a third-party camera to an existing Hikvision or Dahua recorder, we test each feature the owner cares about before calling the job done, and we tell them in advance which ones may not carry over.

This is why we can often save an owner money. If a building has a sound eight-channel Hikvision recorder and three failed cameras, we can replace those cameras with ONVIF cameras from a maker that is not on the Covered List, keep the recorder and the cable, and push the bigger replacement decision a few years down the road. The same works for a Dahua recorder. When the recorder itself eventually needs replacing, the newer cameras move to the new recorder with it.

Reality Check

What Forums and AI Answers Get Wrong About Hikvision and Dahua

"They are the same company"

They are not. Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company and Dahua Technology Company are separate businesses that happen to be based in the same Chinese city and compete in the same market. Their cameras look alike because the whole industry has converged on the same shapes and the same features, not because one makes the other's products.

"Both brands are illegal to own in the US"

The FCC says the opposite about equipment people already have: its June 2026 notice says the import and marketing prohibition "will not affect the continued use or operation of already-purchased communications equipment." The Covered List entries are tied to public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and national security uses. What is restricted is new authorization, and import and marketing for those purposes.

"Nothing has changed since 2021"

A great deal has. The FCC's 2022 rules stopped new equipment authorizations for covered equipment, a 2024 appeals court ruling sent the FCC back to redefine critical infrastructure, and the June 2026 notice prohibited continued import and marketing of previously authorized covered equipment for the listed purposes. Dahua's American operations moved to Luminys. Anyone repeating a 2021 summary is out of date.

"Dahua is always cheaper"

Several installer blogs say Dahua runs cheaper than Hikvision on equivalent models. That may have been true in some markets at some times, but in New York in 2026 the installed cost is dominated by labor, cable and the recorder, and our own NYC pages give the same installed range for both brands. Availability, not list price, is now what moves a quote.

"Switching to an ONVIF camera means losing everything"

You keep live video and recording. You may lose some brand-specific smart alerts, deterrence controls and two-way talk if the camera and recorder come from different makers. For a building with a sound recorder and a few dead cameras, that trade is often well worth it.

"A camera with more megapixels sees farther at night"

Night performance depends far more on the lens opening, the sensor size and the light than on pixel count. The two 8MP cameras we compared have the same F1.0 lens opening and both reach about 30 meters with their own light. A well-aimed 4MP camera with a good light will often beat a badly placed 8MP one.
