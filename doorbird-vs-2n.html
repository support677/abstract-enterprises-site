DoorBird vs 2N:
Which Video Intercom Is Better?

DoorBird vs 2N, model by model: DoorBird D22x and A1121 · 2N IP Verso 2.0, IP Style, IP One and IP Solo · Updated November 2026

DoorBird vs 2N is a split decision, and the building decides it: for one-, two- and three-family houses, brownstones and small walk-ups we would usually put in DoorBird, and for apartment buildings, co-ops, condos and commercial entrances we would usually put in 2N.

Quick answer: For a house, brownstone or small building with up to 18 doorbells, pick DoorBird: a 5 MP, 180-degree camera, card and Bluetooth access, a free app with no fee to answer on your phone, and over 50 finishes. For an apartment building, co-op, condo or office entrance, pick 2N: modular IP Verso 2.0 or touchscreen IP Style stations, secured card readers, cloud or on-site management, and a 5-year warranty. We install both in New York City. Our NYC intercom page lists 2N IP video intercom systems from $900 installed, DoorBird stations are quoted per building after we see the door, and we book requests within 2 hours.

Both are true IP video intercoms that run on a network cable, answer on your phone and release a door strike or magnetic lock, so neither is a bad choice. The difference is scale: DoorBird is built around a beautiful single door and the owner's app, and 2N, part of the Axis group since 2016, is built around a lobby full of residents, cards and a manager who has to keep it all straight.

The short version. A DoorBird station and a 2N station do the same basic job. Each one sits beside the street door, rings one or more people when a visitor presses a button, shows them live video and two-way audio on a phone or an indoor screen, and closes a relay that releases the lock. Both connect to the building's network over a single cable that also carries power, both speak SIP so they can ring an ordinary office phone system, and both work with the big smart-home controllers. If the door were all that mattered, you could flip a coin.

The door is not all that matters in New York. A brownstone owner wants the station to look right against a 120-year-old doorway, wants it to ring three phones, and does not want a yearly fee. A co-op board wants forty apartments in a directory, fobs that cannot be copied at the corner kiosk, a super who can delete a lost card from his phone, and a contractor who will still be able to get parts in ten years. Those are two different jobs, and the two brands were built for them in that order: DoorBird started with the single-family front door and has grown upward, and 2N started with the intercom for the lobby and the office and has grown downward.

Who Should Buy Which

Who Should Pick DoorBird, and Who Should Pick 2N

Pick DoorBird if…

  • You own a one-, two- or three-family house, a brownstone or a townhouse, and the intercom is for your household and maybe one or two tenants.
  • You care how the door looks. DoorBird lists more than 50 materials and colors for its front panels, including brushed stainless, bronze and brass finishes and dozens of RAL powder coats, and it sells a 3D configurator to see the result.
  • You want to answer on your phone with no subscription. DoorBird calls its basic cloud service, with live video and image history of the last 50 visitors, permanently free.
  • You run a smart home on Crestron, Control4, Loxone, KNX or Home Assistant, and you want the doorbell inside that system. DoorBird lists all of them as integrations and says its stations support Crestron's RAVA intercom protocol natively, with no gateway.
  • The building has up to 18 doorbells and one main entrance, and a resident or owner, not a management company, will look after it.

Pick 2N if…

  • The building has more apartments than buttons make sense for. The IP Style's 10.1-inch touchscreen directory scrolls through names instead of rows of buttons.
  • A board or management company wants to control access centrally: add a new tenant, cancel a lost fob, give the dog walker a code that works on Tuesdays. 2N's My2N platform does that from a browser, and 2N calls it free apart from premium services.
  • You want card readers that are hard to copy. 2N offers secured 13.56 MHz readers that read HID Seos, iClass SE and MIFARE DESFire credentials, and its own PICard secured cards.
  • The entrance takes abuse. 2N rates the IP Verso 2.0, IP One and IP Solo for −40 °C, the IP One to IP66, and it gives a 5-year warranty on its whole range.
  • It is an office, medical suite or mixed-use building where the intercom must ring a reception desk on the phone system, show up in a video management system, or tie into a commercial access control platform.

If you fall in the middle, a three- to eight-unit walk-up with one owner who lives on site, both brands work. In that case we usually look at two things: whether the owner wants to manage residents' credentials from a dashboard or is happy doing it on the station, and whether the old buzzer wiring can carry a network signal. We go through both below.

One more honest point: a lot of New York buildings do not need either. If the building is large, turnover is high and the board wants residents to buzz in from their phones with no handsets at all, the systems on our ButterflyMX installation page or an Akuvox setup may fit better, and if the budget is tight and the wiring is good, repairing the existing buzzer through our NYC intercom repair service is sometimes the right first step.

Who Makes Them

Who Makes DoorBird and 2N in 2026

Both brands now belong to large Swedish security groups, which matters more to a building than it sounds: it decides who will be making parts, firmware and replacement stations when the intercom you buy this year needs service in the 2030s.

DoorBird: a Berlin maker, now inside ASSA ABLOY

DoorBird is the brand of Bird Home Automation GmbH. Its About page says the company develops and manufactures IP video door intercoms, access control devices, indoor stations and accessories, that its headquarters and production plant are in Berlin, with sales offices in San Francisco and Jacksonville, Florida, and that DoorBird has been part of the ASSA ABLOY Group since October 2022. ASSA ABLOY is the lock company behind brands many New York supers already know from door hardware, and DoorBird now lists ASSA ABLOY and Yale locks among its integrations.

The first DoorBird series product shipped in 2015. The company says almost 40 people work in its own Berlin production facility and more than 130 across the business, that it has been audited by TÜV Saarland to the ISO/IEC 27001:2024 information security standard, and that its D21x series has been certified to the UL 62368-1 safety standard with Intertek, which it describes as suitable for commercial and multi-family residential applications. That last point answers a question we hear from building owners who assume DoorBird is only a doorbell for houses.

2N: the Prague company that made the first IP intercom

2N was set up in Prague in 1991 by three friends and started with telephone exchanges. Its About page says it invented the first IP intercom in 2008, the 2N Helios IP Vario, which it sold for ten more years, and that it became part of the Axis group in 2016. Axis is one of the world's largest network camera makers, which is why 2N stations share Axis processors, video codecs and the ONVIF camera standard. 2N says all of its technology is still developed, tested and manufactured in Prague.

The family tie shows on the product pages: the IP One runs on an Axis ARTPEC-7 processor, the IP Verso 2.0 and IP Style stream H.265 and Axis Zipstream video, and Axis accessories such as the AXIS T8120 PoE midspan and the AXIS A9210 network relay are sold alongside 2N stations. For a building that already records on Axis or other ONVIF cameras, a 2N station drops into the same recorder as another camera.

What the ownership means for you

Neither brand is a start-up that might vanish. In practice the bigger difference is the sales channel: DoorBird sells an online shop with listed prices as well as through installers, while 2N sells through distributors and installers and does not list prices on its product pages. That is one reason homeowners find DoorBird first, and property managers usually meet 2N through their integrator. On our side, both are ordered through the trade and installed the same way.

Models Compared

Which DoorBird and 2N Models This Page Compares

Both catalogs are large, so here are the models that matter for New York entrances, and the ones this page compares.

DoorBird: the D22x series and the A1121

DoorBird's current outdoor station line is the D22x series. Its shop lists the D2201V to the D2218V, with one to eighteen call buttons, each in a surface-mount or flush-mount version; keypad versions (D2201KV, D2202KV and the horizontal D2201KH); fingerprint versions with DoorBird's Fingerprint 50 or an ekey sLine reader; the D22DKV and D22DKH with a display and keypad for a name directory; and the D2200E and D2200EA, the second an accessibility version. We use the D2203V data sheet, a three-button station for houses and buildings with up to three units, as the reference for D22x specifications below.

A lot of DoorBirds already on New York doors are the older D21x series, such as the D2101V and the D2104V to D2118V, and the D21DKH we installed in October. DoorBird's D2203V data sheet says existing D21x installations can be upgraded by replacing the main electrical unit, which keeps the front panel and the hole in the wall. The DoorBird A1121 is a separate keypad-and-reader unit, with no camera, for back doors, garages, storage rooms and elevators.

2N: IP Verso 2.0, IP Style, IP One and IP Solo

The 2N IP Verso 2.0 is the modular workhorse: a main unit with a camera, in nickel or black, that can be combined with 20 different modules, from button rows and a touch display to keypads, card readers, a fingerprint reader and an induction loop for hearing aids. The 2N IP Style is the flagship for lobbies, a glass-fronted station with a 10.1-inch touchscreen. The 2N IP One is a slim single-button aluminium station that 2N pitches at family homes, and the 2N IP Solo is its compact, older single-button model, with or without a camera.

2N also makes the very rugged IP Force 2.0 for gates, garages and industrial doors. Its first-generation IP Force is being discontinued, with a final order date of June 30, 2026, according to 2N. We mention it because many parking-garage and gate intercoms in the city are IP Forces, and an owner replacing one today will be offered the 2.0.

Indoor stations

Both brands sell indoor touchscreens for residents who want a screen on the wall rather than, or as well as, a phone. 2N's range includes the Indoor View, Indoor Compact, Indoor Talk and Clip, plus the IP Phone D7A desk phone with a 7-inch touchscreen. DoorBird sells its A1101 indoor video station with a touch display. On a 2N residential job the indoor station also changes the phone-app licensing, which we explain in the apps section.

Camera and Video

Camera and Night View: 5 MP and 180° Against 2N's Four Cameras

Every review site leads with megapixels. On an intercom, the field of view and how the camera copes with a bright street behind a dark face matter more, because the job is to see who is standing at the door, often with a hood up, at 7 a.m. in January.

DoorBird D22x: 5 MP behind a 180-degree lens

DoorBird's D2203V data sheet lists an HD 5-megapixel camera with HDR behind what it calls a high-end ultra-wide-angle hemispheric lens: 180 degrees diagonally, 140 degrees horizontally and 100 degrees vertically, with the image straightened. Night vision uses a light sensor, an automatic IR-cut filter and two 850 nm infrared LEDs. The older D21x stations, which we still service, have a 720p camera behind the same 180-degree lens and twelve infrared LEDs.

The DoorBird also carries what it calls a 4D motion sensor, built on a 24 GHz radar module, with a range you set from 1 to 10 meters in one-meter steps. It can tell whether someone is coming or leaving and trigger an action, such as switching on an outside light between 9 p.m. and 6 a.m. On a stoop facing a busy sidewalk, we turn the range down so every passer-by does not ping the owner's phone.

2N: four cameras for four jobs

2N publishes more camera detail. The IP Style has a 5-megapixel-class chip, streams up to 2560 × 1920, covers 138 degrees horizontally and 114 vertically, and adds forensic wide dynamic range. 2N also lists adaptive face zooming, where the station detects a face in the frame and zooms in for the person answering, and bi-directional video, so a visitor can see the receptionist, which 2N notes lets hearing-impaired visitors sign.

The IP Verso 2.0 uses a 1/2.7-inch color CMOS sensor at up to 1920 × 1440 and 30 frames per second, a 125 × 105 degree view or a 138-degree peephole mode, wide dynamic range and infrared, rated to 0.8 lux without the infrared on. The IP One has the widest 2N view, 156 by 116 degrees, with WDR and a 0.3 lux rating. The IP Solo camera is the modest one: 1280 × 960 stills and 640 × 480 video calls across 128 by 95 degrees.

What we see on real doors

On a brownstone stoop or a narrow walk-up vestibule, the DoorBird's 180-degree lens is a real advantage: it shows the person at the door and the package on the step, even when the station has to sit to one side of the door frame. In a lobby, where the visitor stands square in front of a wall-mounted station, the 2N IP Style's higher resolution and face zoom give a sharper, closer face, and the 2N recording stream is easy to send to the building's camera recorder over ONVIF.

Glass vestibules are the hardest light in the city. A visitor in front of a sunny street turns into a silhouette on any camera without good wide dynamic range, so on a south-facing glass entrance we lean toward the IP Style or IP Verso 2.0, or we move the station a few feet to put a wall, not the street, behind the visitor. DoorBird lists HDR on the D22x but does not publish a lux rating or a WDR figure, so we judge it on site.

Installation height matters more than any spec line. DoorBird gives a minimum lens height of 145 cm, about 57 inches, and asks installers to check the best height before cutting the wall. On stoops, where the visitor stands one or two steps below the door, we measure from where people actually stand, not from the landing.

Audio

Audio on a Loud New York Street

Audio is where cheap intercoms fail first in New York, because traffic, buses and wind on an avenue drown out a small speaker. Both brands use full-duplex audio with echo cancellation, which means both sides can talk at once without the conversation cutting in and out.

DoorBird

DoorBird's data sheets list a speaker and microphone with noise reduction and echo cancellation (ANR and AEC) and two-way, full-duplex audio streaming. It does not publish a speaker wattage or a sound-pressure figure, so on a loud street we test the volume with the owner on the phone before we close up the wall.

2N

2N publishes the numbers. The IP Style has two built-in microphones and two 4-watt speakers and rates its maximum sound pressure at 85 dB at one meter. The IP Verso 2.0 has a 5-watt Class D amplifier driving a 2-watt speaker, rated 78 dB at one meter, and a line output. The IP One has a 5-watt amplifier and a 3-watt speaker. All of them have automatic adaptive volume and full duplex with echo cancellation, and they support wideband G.722 audio as well as standard telephone codecs.

Our call on audio

For a house on a side street, either brand is clear enough. For a building on a truck route, a bus stop or under an elevated train, the IP Style's two microphones and higher output are the safer choice, and the IP Verso 2.0 can add an induction-loop module for visitors with hearing aids, which helps an accessible entrance. DoorBird lists an optional hearing loop with PMR symbols as a separate accessory for the D22x.

Apartment Scale

Call Buttons, Directories and How Many Apartments Each Can Handle

This is the section that settles most building jobs, because it decides whether a visitor can find the right apartment and whether the station still makes sense when the building has 30, 60 or 120 units.

DoorBird: up to 18 buttons, or a display and keypad

DoorBird's D22x stations come with one to eighteen illuminated call buttons, each with a nameplate, so every apartment gets its own button. Above that, or where the owner does not want names on the facade, the D22DKV and D22DKH versions combine a display with a keypad, and the visitor scrolls or types a code. Our D21DKH job used that layout: a scrolling list of names with apartment numbers on the screen, arrow keys, and a bell key.

For a three-family house or a 12-unit walk-up, DoorBird's button rows are simple and hard to get wrong. Past about 18 apartments, the visitor experience on a button station gets crowded, and the display versions become the DoorBird answer.

2N: modules, a touchscreen and a big directory

2N's IP Verso 2.0 builds its front panel from modules held in frames, which 2N lists in one-, two- and three-module sizes for surface or flush mounting. A building can have rows of five-button modules, a keypad, a card reader and a touch display that scrolls a directory, all in one surface or flush-mounted panel. The IP Style replaces all of that with its 10.1-inch, 1280 × 800 touchscreen, an on-screen keypad and a directory that 2N lists as handling 10,000 users in an office or residential mode.

On the My2N side, 2N lists a maximum of 500 devices in one installation and up to five devices called from one speed-dial button, which is more than enough for any building we see in the five boroughs.

Which layout we use

Up to about a dozen apartments, we like buttons: visitors understand them, couriers find them fast, and a dead button is easy to spot. Between 12 and 30, either a DoorBird display version or a 2N IP Verso 2.0 with a touch display works, and the choice comes down to management, below. Over 30 apartments, we nearly always quote the 2N IP Style or a Verso 2.0 with a display, because the directory, the resident list and the access cards all live in the same platform and the management company can keep them current without a service call.

Access Control

Cards, Codes and Phone Access: DoorBird Readers Against 2N WaveKey

A modern intercom is also the building's front-door access control: residents get in with a fob, a code or a phone, and visitors get buzzed. Both brands do this, but the depth is different.

DoorBird readers and the A1121

The D22x has two RFID readers built in, 125 kHz and 13.56 MHz, each managing up to 3,000 tags, plus Bluetooth Low Energy that works with DoorBird's Bluetooth key fob remote. The 13.56 MHz reader reads the card serial number of MIFARE Classic, MIFARE DESFire EV1 and EV2 and other ISO cards; DoorBird notes that encrypted reading of those cards requires a DoorBird Cloud Pro or Cloud Enterprise license. Schedules can be set per tag, for example a cleaner's fob that only works on Wednesday mornings.

The DoorBird A1121 is the piece we use on back doors and service entrances. Its data sheet describes a compact keypad with up to 500 PIN codes, 125 kHz and 13.56 MHz readers (one or the other, chosen in setup) for up to 500 transponders, Bluetooth, a tamper sensor, two relays, a Wiegand output for an existing access control or alarm panel, and Wi-Fi or a network cable with PoE. DoorBird says all functions keep working on the local network if the internet drops, and that pairing it with the A1081 I/O door controller adds up to three more doors, gates or elevators.

2N readers, WaveKey and secured cards

2N treats access as a core product line. The IP Verso 2.0 takes reader modules for 125 kHz cards (EM4xxx and HID Prox on the right versions), 13.56 MHz cards (HID iClass, FeliCa, MIFARE and NFC from the My2N app) and secured versions that read HID Seos, iClass SE, MIFARE SE and MIFARE DESFire credentials. There are keypad and touch-keypad modules, combined touch keypad and reader modules, and a fingerprint module that 2N says is certified to an FBI standard with fake-finger detection. The IP Style has the readers built in and adds 2N's PICard secured DESFire EV2 and EV3 cards.

Phone access runs on 2N's WaveKey Bluetooth technology, with four modes: touch, tap in the app, card mode and motion. On its IP Style page, 2N says its developers opened a door 15,000 times with 100 different phones and the door opened in under one second in 98 percent of cases. 2N stations also accept QR codes, which suit one-time visitors and deliveries.

Wiegand and OSDP

If the building already has an access control panel, the intercom has to talk to it. 2N sells Wiegand and OSDP modules for the IP Verso 2.0 and a Wiegand module for the IP Style. DoorBird's D22x data sheet lists Wiegand input and output, OSDP and RS-485 as hardware ready, to be enabled later with a software update; the A1121 has a working Wiegand output today.

For a co-op that already runs card readers on several doors, that difference is real: with 2N we can wire the front station into the existing controller now. With DoorBird we would either use the A1121's Wiegand output, run the front station on its own, or wait for the software update DoorBird describes.

Our call on access

For a family house or a small building where the owner hands out a few fobs, DoorBird's built-in readers and the A1121 for the back door are plenty. For any building that cares about cards being copied, that has more than one controlled door, or that wants residents to open doors with their phones without fumbling, 2N is the stronger system. Our NYC access control installation page covers readers, mag locks and controllers in more depth, and our elevator access control page covers lift readers.

Locks and Power

Relays, Locks and Power at the Door

The intercom only lets someone in if its relay can drive the lock on that door, so this is where we spend time on every survey.

DoorBird

The D22x has two bistable relays, each switching up to 24 volts AC or DC at 1 amp, with normally open, common and normally closed contacts, so it can control two doors or a door and a gate from the app. It also has two external inputs, for example for a request-to-exit button, and a 12 V DC output at 0.48 amps that can power a small electric strike directly. It is powered by PoE to the 802.3at standard. DoorBird's I/O Door Controller can add relays or move them inside the building, where a thief cannot reach the wires by prying off the station. The older D21x took 802.3af PoE or a 15-volt supply.

2N

The IP Verso 2.0 has one passive switch contact, normally open or normally closed, rated 30 volts at 1 amp, one input, and an active output of 8 to 12 volts at up to 400 mA; it takes standard 802.3af PoE or a 12-volt, 3-amp supply. The IP Style needs PoE+ (802.3at) or a 12-volt, 4-amp supply, and has a 30-volt, 1-amp switch output, a 12-volt, 600 mA active output and three inputs. The IP One has a normally open contact, a 12-volt, 600 mA output and one input on 802.3af PoE. For more doors, or to keep the lock wiring inside, 2N sells a Security Relay and external IP relays with one or four outputs, plus the AXIS A9210 network I/O relay module.

Mag locks, strikes and the release time

Most New York street doors we work on have either an electric strike in the frame or a magnetic lock on the header. Neither brand's relay should drive a large mag lock directly; we put a separate lock power supply in, with the intercom relay switching it, and add the exit button and any fire-alarm release the door needs. On our two-door DoorBird job, each station switches a mag lock and the owner asked for a 30-second release, which both brands let us set.

Whichever brand goes up, we install the lock side the same way: a supply sized for the lock, the relay wired so that a pried-off station cannot open the door, and every release tested from the app, the indoor station and the exit button before we leave.

Old Buildings

Wiring Old New York Buildings: New Cable, Bell Wire and the Old Panel Hole

Here is the part no spec sheet mentions. Most New York walk-ups and brownstones still have an audio buzzer system from decades ago: a panel at the street door, handsets in each apartment, and thin bell wire running up a riser shaft that was closed in long ago. Both DoorBird and 2N are network devices. So the first question on any job is not which brand, but how a network signal gets from the street door to a switch, and from the switch to the apartments.

Running new cable

Where we can, we run a new Cat6 cable from the street door to a PoE switch in the cellar or a closet, which is what both makers expect. 2N recommends Cat-5e or better for the IP Verso 2.0. In a brownstone that often means a neat run through the vestibule ceiling and down to the cellar; in an older walk-up it can mean fishing through a plaster wall or running surface raceway in a stairwell, which the owner needs to see before agreeing to it. Our NYC network installation page explains how we run and test that cable.

Using the old buzzer wire

When a new cable is impossible, both brands have an answer. DoorBird says its stations can connect through a network cable or, with a two-wire converter, over existing bell wire to an RJ45 socket that carries PoE and data. 2N sells the 2N 2Wire, a set of two converter units that carry audio, video and power over existing analogue wiring between the intercom and a switch, and lists NVT PoLRE switches and PhyLink adapters among the accessories for its stations.

Old wire is a gamble, and we treat it as one. Before we promise video over a 60-year-old pair, we trace it, check it for splices and shorts, and test the converter link on the real run. If the wire fails, the honest quote includes new cable, and we would rather tell you that at the survey than after the station is on the wall.

Filling the old hole

Replacing an old panel almost always leaves a cut-out that does not match the new station. 2N sells Retrofit Intercom Cover Plates in small, medium and large sizes to cover the old opening under a new IP Solo, IP Style or Verso. DoorBird sells surface-mount and flush-mount housings and a protective hood, and its 50-plus finishes make it easier to match a painted or stone facade. On a landmark-district brownstone, we photograph the old panel and the opening before ordering, so the cover and the finish are right the first time.

Inside the apartments

Residents do not need to keep the old handsets. With either brand most people answer on a phone, and the wiring question inside the building only comes back if they want an indoor screen. A 2N or DoorBird indoor station needs its own network connection, so for a building where every apartment wants a wall screen, we price the riser cabling, not just the stations.

Apps and Fees

Apps and Phone Answering: What Each One Costs to Keep Running

This is where the two brands differ most in money over the years, so read it before you compare quotes.

DoorBird app and Cloud Pro

The DoorBird app is free on iOS and Android. When someone rings, every phone set up for that button gets a push notification with live video and two-way audio, and the owner can open the door or gate from the app. DoorBird calls its basic service Cloud Free and describes it as permanently free: live video, event history with images, download to the phone, and storage of the last 50 visitors and motion events.

Cloud Pro is the paid upgrade. DoorBird lists HD video recordings of doorbell events and motion events with no limit on the number, kept for 7 days, with search filters and a flexible response function. It needs an extra 0.5 megabits per second of upload bandwidth per station. DoorBird also says you can record to a local NAS or network video recorder instead, with Synology among its examples. The app adds geofencing, opening a door or gate automatically as you come home, which we set up carefully, and only where the owner understands what it does.

My2N app and 2N's licensing

2N's resident app is My2N, which replaced the older 2N Mobile Video app; 2N publishes a transition FAQ for buildings still on Mobile Video. My2N handles video calls from the intercom, phone access with WaveKey, time-limited guest QR codes and PINs, delivery codes, an apartment-admin role so one resident can manage the rest of the household, a call log with a snapshot of each caller, and, with the right elevator integration, calling or sending the lift. It also works on an Apple Watch. 2N lists iOS 17 or Android 10 and later as requirements.

Calls from the intercom to a phone run through 2N's cloud, and 2N publishes when those are free. In residential projects, it gives one free apartment license per installation, plus one more free apartment license for every 2N indoor station, and one apartment license covers up to four devices. Beyond that, a subscription is needed, and in non-residential projects such as offices and mixed-use buildings, cloud calling to phones is always paid. 2N also says the installation type is chosen when the site is created and cannot be changed later, so a mixed-use building has to be set up correctly on day one.

2N lists its own subscription prices for when the free licenses run out: 44 US dollars a year per apartment, or 270 dollars for ten years through distributors, and 31 dollars a year per device in commercial projects. Calls between 2N and Axis devices, such as from the street station to a 2N indoor screen, are free, as are site and user management, firmware updates and remote diagnostics.

What that means for a real building

A homeowner with one 2N IP One and no indoor station gets one free apartment license, which covers four phones. A 20-unit building where every apartment answers on phones and nobody buys indoor screens will need subscriptions for most apartments after the first, whereas a building that puts a 2N indoor station in each apartment gets free phone calling for those apartments for the life of the indoor stations, according to 2N. A DoorBird building does not have that line item at all for phone answering, but it does not have My2N's guest codes, delivery codes or central resident management either.

We put the expected app fees in writing on every 2N quote, so a board sees the ten-year cost, not just the install. When a building wants phone-only answering at large scale and no indoor screens, we also compare 2N with the app-first systems on our apartment intercom systems page.

Management

Who Manages Residents and Cards: DoorBird's App Against My2N and Access Commander

Who keeps the intercom up to date after we leave is the question boards forget to ask. A building with 40 apartments has move-ins, move-outs, lost fobs and a new porter every year.

DoorBird: managed per station

DoorBird stations are configured in the DoorBird app or in DoorBird's web administration, where the owner or installer sets which phones ring for which button, adds and removes RFID tags and PIN codes, and sets schedules and actions for each. For a house or a small building with one owner, that is simple and enough. For a larger building, each station is its own island, and there is no central dashboard in DoorBird's materials that compares to 2N's platforms; DoorBird does mention a Cloud Enterprise license in its D22x data sheet, but its public pages we read did not describe what it includes, so we do not assume.

2N: My2N in the cloud, or Access Commander on site

2N publishes a comparison of its two management platforms. The My2N Management Platform is cloud-hosted, intended for apartment buildings, and free apart from premium services such as cloud calling under certain conditions. It handles resident credentials (RFID, PIN, mobile access), visitor PINs and QR codes, access groups and logs, CSV import of residents, firmware management, multi-factor login, remote administration, Dormakaba lock integration and KONE elevator destination control.

2N Access Commander is the on-site option for commercial buildings. It runs on a virtual machine or 2N's Access Commander Box 2.0 mini PC and is licensed. 2N lists credentials including RFID, PIN, mobile, QR code, fingerprint and licence plate, time-and-attendance as an optional add-on, lift access with the AXIS A9210 relay module, 2N Fortis electronic cylinders and handles, and remote administration listed as coming in 2027.

Which one we set up

For a co-op, condo or rental building, we set up My2N and give the managing agent or super an account, so a lost fob is cancelled in a minute and a new resident gets a phone credential without a site visit. For an office or medical building that needs time-based rules, fingerprint or licence-plate entry, or that cannot depend on a cloud service, we quote Access Commander. For a DoorBird house, we hand the owner admin rights in the DoorBird app and a printed list of what is programmed.

DoorBird Connect

Built to Last

Weather, Finishes and Warranty

Weather and vandal ratings

New York doors see freezing rain, road salt, summer sun on dark metal and the occasional kick. DoorBird rates its D22x stations IP65 against dust and water and IK08 against impact, for −25 to +55 °C. 2N rates the IP Verso 2.0 and IP Solo IP54 and IK08, the IP Style IP65 and IK08, and the IP One IP66 and IK08, and gives −40 to +60 °C for the Verso, One and Solo and −30 to +60 °C for the Style. All of these cope with a New York winter. On an exposed stoop or a gate facing a parking lot, we add a hood or pick the higher-rated station.

Materials and finishes

DoorBird's front panels are 3 mm brushed stainless steel as standard, and its shop lists V4A stainless for salt water and grinding dust, PVD bronze, titanium, gold and brass finishes, real burnished brass and architectural bronze on some models, and dozens of RAL powder coats. For a landmark-district brownstone with a painted iron door or a bronze-trimmed entry, nothing else we install comes close.

2N's choices are fewer and more architectural: the IP Verso 2.0 in a cast zinc frame in nickel or black, the IP One in grey, bronze or black aluminium with a button backlight that can be set to any RGB color to show idle, ringing, in-call and door-open states, and the IP Style behind hardened glass. They look right on a modern lobby or a renovated rental; they look out of place on a 19th-century stoop unless the finish is chosen carefully.

Warranty, firmware and repairs

DoorBird gives a 24-month warranty on all its products, more than the statutory minimum according to its warranty page, and publishes an out-of-warranty repair service with pricing. 2N offers a 5-year warranty on its entire portfolio and says it provides a minimum of 8 years of firmware support, including feature updates and security patches.

That five-against-two gap is one of the strongest arguments for 2N on a building, where the board wants to know the front door is covered until the next capital plan. It matters less on a house, where a DoorBird out of warranty can be sent for repair or, on a D21x, upgraded by swapping the electronics. Reality Check

What Reviews and Forums Get Wrong About DoorBird vs 2N

"DoorBird is just a consumer doorbell"

It started at the single-family front door, but DoorBird sells stations with up to 18 buttons, display-and-keypad directory stations, and the A1121 for side doors and elevators, and it says its UL 62368-1 certification makes the D21x suitable for commercial and multi-family residential buildings. It is a serious intercom; it is just strongest in smaller buildings.

"2N always needs a subscription"

Not in most homes. 2N's published rules give every residential installation one free apartment license, covering up to four phones, and one more for each 2N indoor station. Subscriptions start when a residential building answers on phones beyond those free licenses, and on all non-residential sites. It is a real cost on a large phone-only building, and none at all on a house with one station.

"More megapixels means the better intercom"

The DoorBird D22x and the 2N IP Style are both 5-megapixel-class stations, and the IP Verso 2.0 sits around 2.8 megapixels at 1920 × 1440. Field of view, wide dynamic range against a bright street, mounting height and the speaker matter more on a real door than the last megapixel.

"DoorBird is slow to ring the phone"

Some owners do report delays; one Home Assistant forum post we found complains of waits of up to 30 seconds. In our experience, ring delays on any IP intercom almost always come from the network: a weak Wi-Fi bridge to the station, a congested router, phone battery-saving settings or a slow upload. DoorBird asks for a wired broadband connection with no proxy, and its Cloud Pro needs extra upload bandwidth. Give either brand a wired, PoE-powered connection and a decent router, and the ring arrives in a couple of seconds.

"2N is only for big commercial buildings"

2N sells the IP One and IP Solo for family homes and gives one free phone license for a residential installation. It is more often the building brand because of its directory, readers and management, but a 2N IP One on a house door is a perfectly good choice, especially if the owner values the 5-year warranty over finishes.

"You can install either one yourself"

DoorBird sells online and many owners buy a station first and call us second. Both data sheets assume a professional: DoorBird's says assembly requires professional skills or a technician. The station is the easy part; the PoE switch, the riser cable, the lock power supply, the fail-safe or fail-secure setting on the lock, and the exit and fire-alarm releases are what a licensed installer is there for.

Can DoorBird or 2N work over my building's old buzzer wiring?
+

Often, with converters. DoorBird can connect over existing bell wire with a two-wire converter, and 2N sells the 2N 2Wire converter set that carries audio, video and power over existing analogue wiring. We test the old wire first, because some runs are too damaged to use.
