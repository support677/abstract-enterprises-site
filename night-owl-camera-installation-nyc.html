Why New York City Properties Need Night Owl Camera Installation

A Night Owl system is built for people who will put it up themselves, and New York City buildings are some of the hardest places in the country to do that. When a shopper asked on Best Buy's product page how much installation would cost for a Night Owl 16-channel DVR kit, the company's representative answered that installation is not included with the product and is not offered through Night Owl, and pointed the buyer to Best Buy's Geek Squad, saying they would need to contact Geek Squad for its pricing. Night Owl's own homepage leans into the self-install idea: it calls the company the "#1 Wired Security Brand*" in the United States and stresses that its systems do not need a mandatory monthly subscription or a long-term contract, with optional cloud backup sold separately. That is an attractive offer for a homeowner. It also means that the moment the box is opened in a Bed-Stuy brownstone, a Jackson Heights garden apartment or a storefront on Fordham Road, the owner is the installer, unless they hire one. Local installers know this pattern well; one Long Island and New York City company advertises specifically for people who recently bought a camera system from Costco, BJ's, PC Richards or another retailer and need it put in.

The difficulty starts with the age of the buildings. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council's housing stock visualization says more than 80 percent of New York City's housing was built more than 50 years ago, and that over half of all units, about 2 million, were built before 1947. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report puts it the same way: 54 percent of units sit in buildings constructed before 1947. One real estate analysis of the city's residential properties gives a median year built of 1931. Buildings from that era were put together with masonry, often very thick masonry; one pre-war apartment guide describes walls of 12 to 18 inches. A Night Owl wired camera is only as good as the cable that reaches it, and in a building like that, every cable has to get from the outside of the wall to the inside, from the street side to the yard side, and from the floor with the camera to the floor with the recorder. The kit's instructions assume a suburban house with an attic and a garage. Most New York buildings have neither.

That masonry is also why a wired brand makes sense here in the first place. A local installer that specializes in brownstones says their masonry walls are dense enough to significantly degrade Wi-Fi signals, especially between floors or between the rear yard and the front of the building. Our own notes from wireless camera jobs in Brooklyn record back yards and basement units routinely getting a Wi-Fi signal of around -85 dBm, which is too weak for any wireless camera to stream reliably. A Night Owl DVR or NVR kit sidesteps that problem because the video travels down a cable, not through the air. But it swaps a signal problem for a routing problem. The same brownstone installer, and another that covers townhouses across the city, describe the coverage most rowhouses need as the stoop and front door, the garden-level entrance, the rear yard, and any garage or side passage, and they plan cable through basements, closets and existing chases so no wire is left running down the facade. That is exactly the work most Night Owl owners do not want to do on a ladder over a busy sidewalk.

Then there is the question of whose wall it is. Rental units make up 68.1 percent of New York City's housing stock, according to the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 report, which draws on the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey. The Council's housing data and the construction press count about 3.7 million units in total. In practice, most people who want cameras in this city are renters, co-op shareholders or condo owners who control the inside of one apartment and nothing else. An installer blog on camera law in the city notes that tenants generally cannot put cameras in common hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's permission. A legal explainer on apartment camera rules adds that in a co-op or condo, a camera on a hallway wall sits on a common area and typically needs building approval, and that drilling or running cable through the building tends to fall under the alteration provisions of a lease or proprietary lease, which vary from building to building. A Night Owl kit that comes with long runs of cable and a bag of screws assumes the buyer owns every surface. We sort out who owns which surface before we quote.

The reasons people buy cameras in the first place are not hard to find. A New York State Attorney General consumer alert in 2024 told New Yorkers to use a doorbell camera or video surveillance system to help deter package thieves or help police identify them. CBS New York reported a lobby package thief in Astoria, at 36th Avenue and 31st Street, who police said used a screwdriver to disable a lock on the door, and separately reported thieves in Park Slope cutting open packages and taking things out of them while still inside the lobby. One Brooklyn resident, profiled by a delivery company's blog, logged more than 50 porch pirate and trespassing cases in two years. In the wider region, prosecutors charged 14 people in a ring that allegedly used shipment tracking data to target high-value packages, with the stolen goods allegedly sold in New York City and abroad. Security.org's annual package theft report found apartment residents more than three times as likely to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent, and noted that apartment dwellers often are not allowed to install doorbell or security cameras outside their units. A wired Night Owl camera aimed at a vestibule, a stoop or a mail area is a common answer; getting it installed legally and neatly is the part that needs a professional.

Owners of rowhouses and smaller buildings face one more layer: the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Wikipedia's summary of the commission counts 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, on top of the city's historic districts, and a building must be at least thirty years old before it can be designated. The LPC's 2018 rules amendments wrote down how staff handle objects people attach to facades, and security cameras are named among them. The commission's Permit Guidebook has a whole section called "Installing Security Cameras," in its chapter on health, safety and utility equipment, with the criteria staff use to approve them, and its examples show dome cameras finished to match the facade. A box of white bullet cameras with long brackets is not what that guidebook pictures for the front of a designated brownstone. In a historic district, where and how a Night Owl camera goes on the building is a design question as much as a technical one.

New York City facts that shape a Night Owl install

  • Old buildings, mostly: over 80 percent of housing is more than 50 years old and about 2 million units predate 1947, per the Citizens Housing and Planning Council; the Rent Guidelines Board puts the pre-1947 share at 54 percent.
  • Mostly renters: 68.1 percent of the housing stock is rental, according to the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report, so landlord and board permission shapes most jobs.
  • Housing concentrated in two boroughs: half of the city's housing is in Brooklyn and Queens; Manhattan has 7.5 percent of the land but a quarter of the units; Staten Island has about five percent, per CHPC.
  • Little new construction: only about 5 percent of the stock is in buildings built after 2010, according to a construction industry analysis, so most walls a camera cable must cross are decades old.
  • Protected buildings: 1,460 individual landmarks plus historic districts, with LPC rules that keep many security cameras off primary facades unless there is no feasible alternative.
  • Uneven home internet: the NYC Council Data Team found 24.5 percent of households had no broadband subscription and 12.8 percent relied on a cellular data plan only, which matters for Night Owl's remote viewing, though not for recording.

What owners often add next. In apartment buildings, the camera system is rarely the only entry problem. Once a Night Owl camera is watching the vestibule, owners and boards frequently ask about the intercom, the buzzer or a fob reader on the same door, because the city's legal requirements for entrances focus on those devices rather than cameras. If you want that looked at during the same visit, mention it when you book so the technician brings the right tools.

BNC DVR or PoE NVR: The Night Owl Box You Bought Decides How Your Building Gets Wired

The single most important fact about a Night Owl install in New York City is not on the front of the box. It is whether the kit is a DVR system that runs on BNC coaxial cable or an NVR system that runs on Ethernet with Power over Ethernet. Night Owl sells both, and the two are not interchangeable. When a buyer asked on Best Buy whether the cables from an older system would work with Night Owl's newer PoE recorder, the company's representative answered that they would only if they were Ethernet, Cat5e or Cat6, because a PoE NVR uses Ethernet to carry both power and video, and older BNC or coax cables will not work with it. For older DVR kits, a Night Owl representative said the opposite: you can keep the existing BNC cabling and simply swap the cameras and the DVR. That one distinction changes the cable we pull, the holes we drill, the power we need at each camera and the length of each run. We ask for the model number before we quote for exactly this reason.

When an old coax system is already in the building

Plenty of New York storefronts, walk-ups and two-family houses already have camera cable in the walls from a system that came before. If that cable is coax and it is in good shape, a Night Owl BNC DVR kit can be the least disruptive upgrade there is, because Night Owl's own guidance is that the existing BNC cabling can stay while the cameras and recorder are replaced. In a building where the old runs were fished through plaster years ago, keeping them saves drilling through a wall nobody wants opened again. The catch is that old coax is not always what it looks like. Connectors corrode at the camera end where weather gets in, cables get nicked where they pass through a window frame, and runs that were spliced in the ceiling are a common source of flickering pictures. We test each run before we promise it can be reused, and if one fails we replace that run rather than the whole system.

When the cable has to go in fresh

If there is no usable cable in the building, or you have bought a PoE NVR kit, every camera needs its own run of Ethernet back to the recorder or to a PoE switch. In a brownstone that usually means one run up from the cellar to the stoop, one out to the rear yard, and one or two to the garden-level door. In a pre-war walk-up it may mean runs from a basement recorder up through a dumbwaiter shaft, a pipe chase or a closet stack to the vestibule and the roof door, if the owner allows it. The advantage of PoE is that the same cable carries power, so there is no need for a separate power supply hanging beside a camera on a brick wall. The trade-off is that every run has to be good Ethernet, properly terminated and tested, because a single poor connector can stop a PoE camera from powering up at all.

Why cable length matters more here than in the suburbs

Night Owl's BNC kits ship with a 60 ft cable per camera, and the company says extra cable can take a run to 160 ft. SafeWise's review of a Night Owl system noted that long cable runs are best avoided because they add installation time. In a New York building, the route is rarely a straight line. A cable from a camera over a stoop to a cellar recorder may go up the facade, in through a window frame or a drilled hole, along a basement ceiling, around a boiler and across to the router, and the distance doubles quickly. We measure the route, not the distance as the crow flies, before we decide which camera can use the kit's cable and which needs a longer one.

Mixing generations is where DIY jobs stall

Night Owl sells several product series, and not all of them work together. One deal listing for a Night Owl 16-channel kit noted that its cameras and DVR were specific to the DP2 series and not backward compatible with other Night Owl wired products. Owners who add a newer camera to an older recorder, or try to put old cameras on a new recorder, often find the picture never appears. Before we add or swap anything, we check the series and the model numbers on both the cameras and the recorder, and we tell you plainly if a camera you already own cannot join the new system.

Decide what each camera has to prove

The most useful thing you can tell us is not where you want cameras but what you want them to show. "I want to see who takes packages from the vestibule" leads to a different position than "I want to see the street." A camera that has to identify a face needs to be closer and lower than one that only has to show that someone came through a gate. In a narrow brownstone vestibule, that may mean a small camera inside the outer doors rather than one high on the facade. In a store, it may mean one camera on the register and another on the door at head height. Write a line for each camera and we will plan the positions around those lines.

Think about where the recorder will live

The recorder holds every minute of footage, so it should not sit where a burglar can unplug it and carry it out. It also needs to be near the router, as SafeWise noted for the Night Owl DVR it tested, and it needs steady power. In rowhouses that often points to a basement utility area or a closet near the router; in stores, a locked back office or a cabinet. Tell us where your router is and where you would like the recorder, and we will tell you whether a cable can realistically reach both.

Check the lighting, not just the angles

A security vendor's guide to building compliance in the city makes a point that applies to every Night Owl camera: a camera pointed at an unlit corridor provides no usable footage. City rules require lighting in many common areas, but a vestibule bulb that has burned out or a rear yard with no fixture will undo a well-placed camera. Before the visit, take a photo of each camera position at night. If a spot is dark, we will tell you whether the camera's night vision will be enough or whether a light needs fixing first.

Night Owl Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

New York City has an unusually wide range of buildings packed close together. A historian describing the city's housing points out that the rowhouse first appeared in significant numbers in Manhattan in the 1820s and 1830s, that rowhouse neighborhoods spread between the Civil War and the First World War, and that most of the city's tenements went up quickly between 1860 and 1910 in Manhattan, Brooklyn and later the Bronx. After them came elevator apartment houses, garden apartments, mid-century co-ops, public housing and glass towers. Each type changes three things about a Night Owl job: what the camera is fixed into, how the cable gets back to the recorder, and who has to agree before anyone drills.

Brownstones and Rowhouses

Stoop, garden-level door, rear yard and any side passage. Masonry fronts, often in a historic district, with cable routed through the cellar and interior chases rather than down the facade.

Pre-War Walk-Ups and Tenements

Four- to six-story walk-ups with thick masonry walls. Vestibule, mail area, roof door and rear yard, with the recorder in a basement or the owner's unit and runs through shafts or closets.

Elevator Buildings and Doorman Co-ops

Lobbies, package rooms, service entrances and garages. Board approval, insurance papers and work hours come first; cameras cover common areas without facing any apartment door.

Two- and Three-Family Houses

Common in Queens, the Bronx and southern Brooklyn. Separate entrances, a shared yard and a basement that often makes the cleanest home for the recorder.

Detached and Semi-Detached Houses

More common in Staten Island and eastern Queens. Driveways, side gates and garages, with framing and siding that take cable more easily than brick but longer distances to cover.

Mixed-Use Buildings Over Stores

A shop on the ground floor and apartments above. The store's cameras and the building's cameras may need separate recorders, separate owners and separate permission.

Brownstones: one hole through the front, everything else inside

On a brownstone or brick rowhouse, the camera people most want is the one over the stoop, and it is the hardest one to wire neatly. The facade is masonry, often several bricks thick, sometimes faced with brownstone that chips easily. Our method is to make the smallest possible penetration in the least visible spot, usually tucked under a cornice, beside a door frame or inside the vestibule, then get the cable inside the building immediately and do the long part of the run in the cellar or a closet. Night Owl's BNC cameras need separate power as well as video, so on a DVR kit the cable to the stoop carries both; on a PoE kit, a single Ethernet run does the job. If the house is in a historic district, the camera position is chosen with the LPC's guidance in mind, which we cover below.

Walk-ups: the recorder belongs to the owner, not the hallway

In a pre-war walk-up, the useful cameras are usually in the vestibule, at the mailboxes, at the roof door and in the rear yard. The recorder should be in a locked space the owner controls, such as a basement room or the owner's own apartment, never on a shelf in a public hallway. The walls are thick masonry, so we look for existing vertical paths, an old dumbwaiter shaft, a pipe chase or a stack of closets, before drilling anything new. Where a cable has to cross a common hallway on the surface, we use a neat raceway that can be painted to match. A tenant cannot authorize any of this; the owner or managing agent has to, which is one more reason we ask who we are working for when you book.

Co-ops and condos: approval before anchors

In a co-op or condo, a shareholder or owner can usually put a camera inside their own unit, but a camera on a hallway wall sits on common property and, as one legal explainer on apartment camera rules puts it, typically needs building approval. Running cable through the building tends to fall under the alteration provisions of a proprietary lease, and a security vendor's compliance guide notes that alteration agreements may require licensed, insured contractors. When a board is installing a building-wide system, the same guide notes that camera system replacements may themselves need board approval. We send insurance certificates and work details in advance and follow the building's hours. Inside, mid-century buildings can mix masonry outer walls with block or metal-stud partitions, so we test the wall before choosing between masonry anchors and fixings for hollow walls.

Houses in Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island

Detached, semi-detached and two-family houses are easier to wire in one way and harder in another. Siding over wood framing takes a cable more easily than brick, and basements and attics give us places to run it. But the distances are longer: a driveway camera, a side gate, a garage and a back door can be spread around a house so that the kit's 60 ft leads fall short. On Night Owl BNC kits, Night Owl says extra cable can extend a run to 160 ft, and on PoE kits we cut Ethernet to the real length. We plan the recorder's position so the longest run is as short as it can be.

Garden apartments and planned communities

Some of the city's best-known housing was built as planned communities: the garden apartments of Jackson Heights from 1917, Forest Hills Gardens with its precast concrete panels, Parkchester's 171 brick buildings in the Bronx and Co-op City's towers and townhouse clusters. A home camera is mostly watched after something happens. A store camera is often watched live from the counter and then pulled for a specific minute when there is an incident. City programs and rules that touch business cameras

  • Voluntary NYPD registration. NYC 311 says any business with a security camera can share live video feeds with the NYPD during emergencies, and that camera locations can be registered at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registration is voluntary. A state bill to create a mandatory "safe streets" registry of private cameras facing public spaces has been introduced repeatedly; we found no evidence that it passed.
  • Connect New York. amNY reported on May 16, 2024 that a program called Connect New York lets businesses link their cameras to their local precinct for real-time viewing, using technology called Fusus, developed by Axon, in a pilot in nine precincts across the five boroughs, with a demonstration that started in Flushing and College Point, Queens. amNY reported that businesses can choose how and when their footage is accessible.   • Proposed help for small retailers. Council records show Int. 623-2026, introduced on February 12, 2026, would create a pilot installing cameras and plexiglass in some small businesses in ZIP codes with high retail-theft crime; it was laid over in committee on February 26, 2026.   • Roll-down gates and camera sight lines. A 2009 local law set a July 1, 2026 deadline for storefront security gates to be at least 70 percent transparent.   • Fire-rated penetrations. Field Notes: The First Look at a Night Owl Box on a New York Job

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow when they arrive to install a Night Owl kit the customer has already bought.

Night Owl won't install the kit I bought. Who in New York City can put it up for me?

Is installing a kit I already own cheaper than buying one from you, and how do you quote four cameras?

Installing your own kit is billed as labor only, so you are not paying for equipment twice. Four cameras is the most common Night Owl starting kit, and a four-camera quote turns on four things: whether it is a BNC DVR or PoE NVR kit, how far each camera is from the recorder by the real cable route, what the walls are made of, and whether any position needs board, landlord or Landmarks approval. The figures for each borough are on our published price pages. Send the model number and photos of the four spots, and we will tell you which lines apply before you book.

If the internet in our building goes down, does a Night Owl system keep recording?

Yes, according to Night Owl's own setup guide, which says the DVR does not need internet for basic operation; the internet connection is what lets you view the cameras remotely on a phone or computer. That matters in a city where the NYC Council Data Team found nearly a quarter of households without a broadband subscription. As long as the recorder and cameras have power, they record to the recorder's hard drive. When the internet returns, the app reconnects and you can review what was recorded while it was down. What stops recording is a power cut, which is why we look at where the recorder plugs in.

Cables, power and recorders

Our walk-up still has coax from the old camera system. Can a new Night Owl kit run on it?

If the new kit is a Night Owl BNC DVR system, very possibly. A Night Owl representative has said that with older DVR kits you can use the existing BNC cabling and simply swap out the cameras and the DVR. If the new kit is a PoE NVR system, no: Night Owl says PoE recorders need Ethernet, Cat5e or Cat6, and that older BNC or coax cables will not work with them. In both cases we test each old run before relying on it, because corroded connectors and damaged sections are common on cable that has been on a building for years.

The cables in the box won't reach from the cellar to the rear yard of our rowhouse. What are the options?

On a Night Owl BNC kit, longer cable. Night Owl says each camera comes with a 60 ft BNC cable and that extra cabling can take a run up to 160 ft per camera. On a PoE kit, we run Ethernet cut to the real length. Sometimes the better answer is to move the recorder: if the router can sit nearer the middle of the house, or a small PoE switch can be placed partway along, the longest run shrinks. We measure the route a cable would actually take, which in a rowhouse is usually much longer than the distance between the two points.

Can the recorder go in a closet upstairs instead of beside the router in the basement?

It can, if a cable can connect it to the router. SafeWise found that the Night Owl DVR it reviewed had no built-in Wi-Fi and had to be connected to the router by Ethernet. So the recorder can sit in a closet two floors up as long as we can run one network cable between it and the router, and run every camera cable to the closet instead. The question is which is easier in your building: bringing the camera cables to the router's location, or bringing one network cable to a safer room. In masonry buildings, we usually choose whichever route already has a chase or a closet stack.

Rowhouses, rentals and boards

Our brownstone sits in a historic district. Do the Landmarks Preservation Commission rules affect where Night Owl cameras can go?

They can. The LPC's 2018 rules amendments cover objects attached to facades, security cameras among them, and the rules would bar many such objects from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative or another agency requires the location; installations must avoid damaging significant architectural features and be as small as possible. The commission's Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras, and its examples show dome cameras finished to match the facade. In practice that often means a small camera inside the vestibule or on a side or rear wall instead of a large bullet camera above the front door. We check the designation and plan with those rules in mind; the permit itself is the owner's application.

I rent in a walk-up. May I put a Night Owl camera in the hall outside my apartment?

Not without the landlord's permission. An installer's guide to camera law in the city notes that tenants generally cannot install cameras in common hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's agreement, and a legal Q&A answer about a Brooklyn apartment building adds that a camera put in a common area without consent can be removed by the landlord. Inside your own apartment the rules are different, though you still must not record anywhere other people have an expectation of privacy. Ask the landlord in writing, describe exactly where the camera and cable would go, and we will install only what they approve.

The co-op board wants an alteration agreement before we run camera cable. Is that normal?

Yes, and it is worth doing properly. Drilling or running cable through a building usually falls under the alteration provisions of a proprietary lease, which differ from building to building, and boards often require licensed, insured contractors for the work. We provide our insurance certificate and the details of what we will drill and where the cable will travel, so the managing agent can approve it. If the board says no to any common-area run, we can usually keep the whole system inside the unit with the cameras facing out of windows or into the apartment's own entry.

Footage, police and privacy

Our shop runs Night Owl. How do we get footage to the local precinct after an incident?

The usual way is to export the clip from the recorder to a USB drive or download it through the app, then hand it to the detective. We show owners how to do that at handover, and we make sure the recorder's clock is correct, because a clip stamped with the wrong time causes confusion. Separately, NYC 311 says businesses can voluntarily register camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org so police know footage may exist. amNY has also reported on Connect New York, a pilot letting businesses share live views with their precinct, where businesses choose how and when footage is accessible.

The Night Owl cameras have microphones. Should audio be switched on in our store?

We usually suggest leaving it off unless you have taken advice. A legal guide to New York recording law notes that cameras capturing sound must meet the one-party consent rule in the state's eavesdropping law, and a camera recording conversations at a counter between staff and customers is not the same as recording pictures of a doorway. Video alone does what most store owners need. If you want audio for a specific reason, talk to your lawyer first, and we will set the cameras up to match what they advise.

Only if the two are compatible, and with Night Owl that is not guaranteed. "Night Owl will send someone to install it."

"Any old camera cable will work with a new system."

Quick answers often say you can reuse the wiring from a previous camera system. That is only half true. Night Owl has said its older DVR kits can use existing BNC coaxial cabling, with the cameras and recorder swapped out.

But Night Owl has also said that its PoE NVR systems use Ethernet to carry power and video, that older cables only work with them if they are Cat5e or Cat6, and that BNC or coax will not work with a PoE NVR. Our own notes from Brooklyn wireless jobs record back yards and basement units routinely getting a signal around -85 dBm, too weak for any wireless camera to stream reliably. A camera that drops off the network at 2 a.m. is not protecting anything.

That is the main reason a wired Night Owl kit suits so many city buildings. The cable is harder to install, but once it is in, the thickness of the walls stops mattering.

"The cameras need the internet to record."

Many answers blur recording and remote viewing. Night Owl's own initial setup guide says the DVR does not need internet for basic operation; the internet connection is what lets you view remotely on a computer or phone.

That distinction matters here. The NYC Council Data Team found that 24.5 percent of households had no broadband subscription, and 12.8 percent relied on a cellular data plan only. A household without home internet can still record footage on a Night Owl recorder; it just cannot watch live from a phone at work.

Where remote viewing is wanted, we connect the recorder to the router by cable and test the app from outside the building's network, so you know it works before we leave rather than when something happens.

"Put the camera wherever you like on your own building."

Owners of a landmarked building, or a house in a historic district, cannot always do that. If your building is designated, the camera position, size and finish are part of the plan from the start. A small camera in the vestibule or on a rear wall is often easier to approve than a large bullet camera above the front door.

"New York requires cameras in apartment buildings."

Some answers assume landlords must install cameras. A security vendor's guide to building compliance says there is no general New York City law that mandates cameras in apartment buildings; what the law does require is intercoms and lighting. "It's a four-hour job."

The box may say so. A buyer's review on eBay said a kit claiming a 4-hour install took more like 10 hours. SafeWise's review of a Night Owl system noted that long cable runs add installation time.

Our Installation Process

Every Night Owl job we do in New York follows the same eight steps, whether it is two cameras on a Staten Island driveway or sixteen across a Bronx apartment building. The order matters most in old masonry buildings, where a cable route chosen too quickly is the usual reason a job runs long.

You send the model number, the number of cameras and the building type. We confirm whether it is a BNC DVR or Ethernet PoE NVR kit and match the job to your borough's price list.

Every entrance, stoop, yard, hallway and storefront walked with you, each camera held at its spot on the live view, and what it must see agreed.

The recorder placed somewhere protected and wired to the router, clock, recording and motion zones set, and the Night Owl app linked on each phone.

Every channel checked for picture, recording and playback, a clip exported to prove footage can be handed over, cables labelled and a written layout left with you.

FAQ

NYC Night Owl Camera Installation FAQ

Will I have to pay a monthly fee once my Night Owl system is installed?
+

Not to Night Owl for basic recording. Night Owl says its systems do not require a mandatory monthly subscription or long-term contract, and footage records to the recorder's own hard drive. Night Owl does sell optional cloud backup for owners who want copies stored off site. We do not add any monthly charge of our own for a system we install.

Can a camera go on our brick facade without leaving the front of the house scarred?
+

Usually yes. We drill the brick face rather than the mortar joint wherever the camera base allows, use anchors suited to old masonry, make one small cable penetration in the least visible spot, and seal it against water. If the house is landmarked or in a historic district, we also plan the position around the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules, which often favor a small camera in the vestibule or on a side or rear wall.

Where should the Night Owl recorder go so a burglar can't just take it?
+

Somewhere out of sight, locked if possible, and near the router, since the recorder connects to it by cable for remote viewing. In rowhouses that is often a basement utility area or a closet; in stores, a locked back office or cabinet. We avoid shelves in shared hallways and spots under a shop counter, and we suggest battery backup so a short power cut does not stop recording.

Can everyone in the family, or every manager in the store, see the cameras on their phone?
+

Yes. At handover we link the Night Owl app on each phone that needs it and test it from outside the building's network, so it works away from home. Where the system allows separate users, we suggest giving staff or family members their own access rather than sharing the main password, and we show the owner how to change or remove access later.

Our home internet is a 5G box, and the app won't connect from outside. Is that fixable?
+

Often it is, but it depends on how the provider runs its network. Some 5G home internet services share addresses among many customers, which blocks the kind of inbound connection that older remote-viewing setups rely on. The recorder still records either way. We check how your service and your Night Owl model connect, and tell you plainly what will and will not work before you rely on remote viewing.

I own a building. How should the hallway cameras be aimed so tenants can't call it harassment?
+

Aim them to cover the hallway, the stairs and the entrances as a whole, not one apartment door. A security firm writing on tenant objections warns that a camera aimed only at a single tenant's door can look like harassment in a dispute, and a Justia answer on a co-op question noted surveillance is legal as long as the camera is not aimed into an apartment when the door is open. Writing down your security reasons and telling tenants in advance also helps.

Can a NYCHA resident put a Night Owl camera inside their own apartment?
+

Inside your own unit, a camera that covers only your own space is generally a personal decision, as long as it does not record anywhere other people expect privacy. Hallways, elevators and grounds belong to NYCHA, and residents have asked the city for cameras there, such as a participatory budgeting proposal at Gowanus Houses for cameras in elevators and near apartment doors. We do not install anything in NYCHA common areas without the authority's written permission.

Is it worth registering our cameras with the NYPD?
+

For many businesses it is. NYC 311 says camera locations can be registered at NewYorkCityConnect.org, and that businesses can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies. Registration is voluntary, and a bill to make a private camera registry mandatory has been introduced repeatedly without, as far as we found, passing. Registering simply tells detectives that footage may exist at your address.

If a camera from my store-bought kit fails after you install it, who covers it?
+

Equipment you bought yourself is covered by its manufacturer under Night Owl's own warranty terms, not by ours, because our warranty applies to products we supply. If a camera you supplied fails, we can help you confirm the fault so you can claim it from Night Owl or the store, and reinstall the replacement at our listed labor rate. Reviews of Night Owl on sites such as SmartCustomer include complaints about cameras failing, so keep your receipt.

Is the price the same in every borough?
+

No. Each borough has its own published Night Owl price list, and your property is priced from the list for the borough it is in. Manhattan and Staten Island lists run higher than Brooklyn and the Bronx because of access, parking and travel, and Queens sits slightly above Brooklyn. Long Island and Hudson Valley counties have their own lists too.

We are moving out next year. Can the Night Owl system come with us?
+

The cameras and recorder can, but the cable in the walls usually stays, since pulling it back out of masonry would do more damage than leaving it. When you move, we can take the cameras down, patch the mounting holes neatly, pack the recorder and reinstall it at your new address, priced from that borough's list. If you rent, check whether your lease says anything about removing installed equipment.

What do you leave behind so the next person can work on the system?
+

A written note of every camera, which recorder channel it uses, which cable runs to it and where that cable travels through the building, plus labels on each cable at the recorder end. In an old city building, where cables pass through cellars, closets and chases out of sight, that note saves the next technician hours of tracing, whether it is us or someone else.
