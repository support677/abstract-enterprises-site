The Desk Opens the Door · Strangers In by Day, Staff Out Safely by Night · Free Exit Always

Westchester Square was the seat of a town before it was part of the Bronx, and it still works like the middle of one. The 6 train stops above the square, eight bus routes meet around it, a Montefiore campus with an emergency department open around the clock stands a few blocks off, and the business improvement district’s corridors are lined with storefronts, upstairs offices and practices. Around the square, the person at the door is usually someone the staff have never met: a new patient, a walk-in client, a courier, a student. So the lock that matters is the one a receptionist opens with a button, many times a day, and the moment that matters most is the last staff member walking out after dark. We plan both: a release that only opens for someone the desk has seen, a second door between the public room and the private ones, and a closing routine that does not leave anyone alone at an unlit door.

The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Square Properties Need Access Control

Start with what the square used to be. Wikipedia traces Westchester Square to a settlement founded about 1654 by English colonists from New Haven on land Thomas Pell bought that year, a village the Dutch called Oostdorp and the English called Westchester. It was the seat of the Town of Westchester until 1895, when the town joined New York City, and the article notes that the square “is still laid out like a village,” with Owen Dolen Plaza, once the village green, again at its center. The neighborhood’s limits in the same article run along East Tremont Avenue and Silver Street to the north, Blondell Avenue and Westchester Creek to the east, Waterbury Avenue to the south and Castle Hill Avenue to the west, with East Tremont Avenue, Westchester Avenue and Williamsbridge Road as its main roads. It sits in Community District 10, in ZIP codes 10461 and 10462, and the 45th Precinct patrols it.

A village center collects the things a district needs, and this one still does. The elevated Pelham line opened a stop at Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue in 1920; Wikipedia’s station article gives the date as October 24 and says the station became accessible in January 2025. Buses meet it on Westchester Avenue, East Tremont Avenue, Williamsbridge Road and Morris Park Avenue. The private Huntington Free Library has stood on the west side of the square since the 1890s. St. Peter’s Episcopal Church, its chapel and its cemetery hold the corner of Westchester Avenue and St. Peter’s Avenue. A branch of the public library is on Glebe Avenue, the Bronx Council on the Arts has its headquarters on East Tremont Avenue, and when the Bronx Times described a new building on Blondell Avenue in 2026 it listed six schools nearby, Herbert H. Lehman High School among them.

Then there is the hospital. Westchester Square Hospital opened in 1930, according to Wikipedia, survived state attempts to close it in 2006 and 2009, and closed as a hospital in 2013, when Montefiore took over the building and turned it into an ambulatory care center with an emergency room, operating rooms and offices. The Norwood News, a paper published by a nonprofit affiliate of Montefiore, reported in October 2013 that Montefiore had bought the assets for $15.3 million with state grant money, that the emergency department stayed open around the clock and saw about 4,500 patients in its first three months, and that ambulatory surgery and gastrointestinal procedures had moved into two of the seven floors. Two years later the Bronx Times quoted the business improvement district’s chairman naming Montefiore and the Hutchinson Metro Center office towers as the area’s anchors, and reported that Montefiore ran several facilities on Williamsbridge Road near the district’s edge.

Put those together and you have a neighborhood whose front doors are opened mostly to strangers. A dentist on East Tremont Avenue, a physical therapist above a shop on Williamsbridge Road, a tax preparer under the el, a lawyer’s office in a converted Victorian house, a school’s side entrance and a church office all share one habit: somebody inside presses a button to let in a person they have not met before. That button is the real lock, and in most offices it is wired the way it was decades ago, to a buzzer that opens the door for anyone who rings while the receptionist happens to be looking the other way.

The neighborhood has also told the city plainly how it feels after dark. In the needs assessment the Westchester Square BID presented to Community Board 10, 77 percent of the consumers surveyed said they felt safe or very safe in the district during the day and only 38 percent at night; the same presentation noted that some areas are dimly lit in the evening despite streetlights, and that the elevated train blocks natural light along Westchester Avenue. When the city’s Department of Transportation held its second plaza workshop in March 2018, residents had asked for police presence after school hours and pointed out that lighting under the elevated 6 was lacking. In August 2026 News 12 reported that a streetlight outside a Tratman Avenue home in Westchester Square had been dark since December 2024 and was relit only after the station called the utility; the homeowner described people up to no good using the shadows.

None of that is about a lock that failed. It is about the minute a nurse, a hygienist or a bookkeeper steps out of a lit office onto a dark sidewalk under steel tracks, and about the minute before, when someone outside asked to be let in. Access control cannot light a street, and we say so throughout this page. What it can do is decide who gets through the door, give the desk a clear look at them first, make the staff exit the best-lit and best-watched door in the building, and leave a record of who came and went.

The square is also adding doors. In July 2026 the Bronx Times reported that construction had finished on a seven-story building at 1340 Blondell Avenue with 182 rental apartments, 15,304 square feet of commercial space, a small community facility and a 225-space garage, with ground-floor retail on the street. In August the paper reported that the Westchester Square BID was one of three Bronx groups sharing $325,000 in multiyear city grants that pay for program managers and new commercial district needs assessments. New commercial space and a fresh survey of merchants mean new tenants fitting out new doors, which is the best time to get them right.

Release Button Dead or a Street Door That Won’t Latch Behind Patients in Westchester Square: We Come the Same Day

Two kinds of failure jump the queue. The first is a door that will not lock: a street door whose strike no longer catches, so it drifts open behind every visitor, or a staff door that stays released after the desk lets go of the button. The second is any door that will not let people out. Ring (646) 490-0629 and tell us which door, what it does when someone pushes it from inside, and whether the building is open to the public right now. You get an arrival window on the call.

Until we get there, do not tape a latch, wedge a staff door or disconnect a release button that patients depend on. Put a person at the door if you can, keep the inner door closed and use the front desk as a checkpoint. If the fault is in a lock that keeps people in, prop nothing shut: keep the door free and staffed. We would rather find a door standing open with a person beside it than a door that someone has fastened in a way that stops people leaving.

Access Control for Westchester Square’s Architecture

The square’s buildings are a record of its history: a village with Victorian houses, a 1920 elevated line that brought shops underneath it, a hospital campus that grew from 1930, and in 2026 a seven-story building on its eastern edge. Each type takes door hardware differently.

Victorian houses turned into offices

Wikipedia notes many nineteenth-century homes and several Victorian mansions around the square. Where one now holds a practice or an office, the front door is usually wood, often with glass, set in a wood frame that needs checking before it carries an electric strike.

Storefronts under the el

Along Westchester Avenue, shops sit under the elevated structure where the BID’s own survey found natural light is blocked. Aluminum and glass doors with narrow stiles, and roll-down gates in front of them, limit where a reader and a lock can go.

Mixed-use buildings on East Tremont Avenue

Two- and three-story buildings with a shop at street level and offices or apartments above, reached through a separate street door and a stair. That stair door is shared, and so is the decision about it.

Practice and clinic buildings near the campus

Offices on Williamsbridge Road and the streets around St. Raymond Avenue, with waiting rooms at the front, exam or treatment rooms behind, and staff doors at the side or back.

Institutional buildings

The church and its chapel, the Huntington Free Library, the public library branch and the schools around the square have their own owners, their own security plans and, in two cases, landmark status. We describe them here as neighbors, not as our clients.

New construction on the edges

How the square changes the method

Around the square the question is how a stranger gets in, once, without going further than they should. That changes three things in our method.

First, we design from the desk outward. We sit where the receptionist sits and ask what they can see: the street, the vestibule, the waiting room, the inner door. If they cannot see the street door from the chair, the call station’s camera has to show it to them, and its angle is set for the person standing at it, not for the sidewalk.

Second, we count two doors, not one. In a practice the street door decides who reaches the waiting room and the inner door decides who reaches the staff side. They need different hardware, different schedules and different people able to open them.

Third, we plan the exit before the entry. Every door we touch opens freely from inside without a key, a card or special knowledge, and the door staff use to leave at night is chosen for its light and its view, not for its convenience.

The Desk Is the Lock

Letting Strangers In One at a Time: The Front-Desk Release Around the Square

Most offices near the square already have something that lets the desk open the street door: a buzzer button under the counter, an old intercom handset, sometimes a doorbell camera stuck to the frame by a previous tenant. These systems share one weakness. The button opens the door for whoever happens to be pulling on it at that moment, and nothing records who that was. The receptionist hears a buzz, glances at a small screen or at nothing at all, presses, and goes back to the phone. On a quiet afternoon that is harmless. On a busy morning, with a line at the counter, a ringing phone and a patient asking about a bill, the button gets pressed without anyone looking.

A front-desk release worth installing changes four things about that moment. The caller is seen before the door opens, by a camera placed for the person at the door rather than for the street. The release only works while that call is open, so a button pressed by habit a minute later does nothing. The door opens for a few seconds and then locks again whether or not anyone walked through. And each release is written down: the time, the station that called, and, if the system has a camera, a still of the caller. None of that slows the receptionist down. It removes the moments when the button is pressed for nobody in particular.

The camera matters more than the screen. A desk station shows only what the camera at the door sees, and the most common mistake we find is a camera aimed at the sidewalk from high on the frame, so that the desk sees the top of a hat and the street behind it. At a practice door the camera goes at a height and angle that frame a face from a step back, the hands, and the space beside the caller where a second person might stand. Under the el, where the BID’s own survey found that the elevated tracks block daylight along Westchester Avenue, that also means a camera that handles a dark doorway against a bright street, and a light over the door that the camera can rely on.

Then the two-door question. Many practices around the square have a vestibule or a short hall between the street door and the waiting room, and a second door between the waiting room and the treatment rooms or offices. The street door decides who reaches the public room. The inner door decides who reaches everything else. We put the desk release on the first and a staff credential on the second, with a closer strong enough to pull it shut behind a cart and a lever on the staff side that always opens it. A visitor who was let in from the street waits where they were let in. A staff member coming from the back can always get out.

Schedules finish the picture. During published hours the street door may open on a schedule for the first hour, when the desk is busiest and the waiting room is full of people with appointments, and return to desk release afterwards. Before opening and after closing it stays locked and answers only to staff credentials. The inner door never opens on a schedule. Holidays, half days and the evenings a doctor stays late are entered once, in advance, rather than remembered at the time.

Next to a 24-Hour Campus

Practices Beside an Emergency Department That Never Closes: Hours, Couriers and the Walk to the El

A few blocks from the square, the Montefiore campus on St. Raymond Avenue keeps its emergency department open around the clock, as the Norwood News reported when Montefiore took over in 2013. The offices around it keep office hours. That difference shapes the neighborhood’s evenings in ways that matter for doors. Patients, relatives, ambulette drivers, couriers and staff are moving around the area long after most practices have closed, and some of them knock on the wrong door.

We do not install inside the hospital campus, and nothing on this page describes its security. Montefiore runs its own buildings. Our work is for the private practices, offices, shops and owners around it, and for them the campus is a neighbor with a timetable different from theirs. Four parts of a practice’s day are affected.

Opening: the first hour

The first appointments of the day draw people to the door before the desk is staffed. In winter, and under the el in any season, that means patients waiting in the dark on the sidewalk. A practice can let the street door open on a schedule a few minutes before the first appointment while the inner door stays locked, so the waiting room fills and the staff side stays private. The desk takes over the street door once someone is sitting there. If the waiting room is unattended in that window, a camera over the inner door and a contact on it tell the first staff member to arrive what happened before they did.

Daytime: couriers and lab pickups

Specimen couriers, pharmacy deliveries, medical supply drivers and office suppliers all come during the day, and most of them do not need to pass the inner door. A courier credential that opens only the street door into the vestibule, paired with a lockable pass-through or drop box there, lets a pickup happen without anyone leaving the desk. Where a lab courier collects after closing, the same vestibule arrangement keeps the practice locked while the box is emptied, and the system records the time the courier came. What it should not be is a shared door code taped inside a supply closet.

Evening: cleaners and the last appointment

Cleaning crews arrive as the last patients leave. The common arrangement around the square is a key handed to the cleaning company years ago, copied to whoever works that night. We replace it with a credential that belongs to the company, works only on cleaning nights between set hours, opens only the doors the contract covers and ends when the contract ends. The practice decides whether a cleaner’s arrival should trigger a message to the office manager, which most owners find useful for the first month and then switch off.

Closing: the walk to the el

This is the moment the BID’s night-time survey figure is about. The last staff member out of a practice on Westchester Avenue steps into the shadow of the elevated tracks; one on a side street like Tratman Avenue may walk past a streetlight that, as News 12 reported in 2026, can stay dark for months. Access control cannot fix a streetlight, and 311 and the utility remain the route for that. It can make the exit itself better. We choose the staff exit for its light and its view rather than its convenience, put a fixture over it that comes on as the door opens, aim a camera at the few steps outside, and set the system to send one message when the building is armed and empty. If staff prefer to leave in pairs, a two-person closing routine on the panel makes the last step easy to follow.

Under the El

Storefronts and Stair Doors Under the Elevated Line and Around the Plaza

Westchester Avenue runs beneath the Pelham line’s steel, and its storefronts have lived with that since 1920. The BID’s needs assessment singled the avenue out: sidewalks on East Tremont Avenue, Williamsbridge Road and Zerega Avenue felt more inviting, it said, than Westchester Avenue, where the elevated train blocks natural light, and it listed the stretch of Westchester Avenue near St. Peter’s among the problem areas for debris and graffiti. That does not make the avenue unsafe, and the survey did not claim it did. It does make the doors there harder to see, harder to light and easier to ignore after closing.

Three kinds of door share the avenue. The shop door itself is usually glass in a narrow aluminum frame behind a roll-down gate, and it is open to the public all day. The back or side door, to an alley, a yard or a stair, is used by staff and deliveries. And in many buildings there is a separate street door, often steel with a small window, that leads up a stair to offices or apartments over the shops. Each needs something different.

On the shop door, the useful work is usually modest: a lock and reader that staff use to open and close, set inside the gate line so the gate still comes down over it, with the schedule following opening hours. The back door is where a reader, a door contact and a camera earn their keep, because that is the door used at the edges of the day, and the door a delivery driver leaves open. The upstairs street door is shared. When offices sit over a shop, each suite should be able to buzz in its own visitors without opening the others’ suites, and no one tenant should hold the only administrator account for a door everyone uses. Where the upper floors include three or more apartments, state law on locking entrance doors and intercoms and the city’s tenant data rules for residential buildings also come into it, and we write the plan so the owner can see which rules apply.

The plaza adds its own detail. Community Board 10 approved the Department of Transportation’s plan for a plaza at the south-west corner of Westchester and East Tremont Avenues in June 2018, according to the Bronx Times, replacing a triangular island and a slip lane, shortening the walk to the 6 station and putting tables and chairs outside the shops on that corner. The same report said the BID would handle the plaza’s maintenance, security and programming, and that opponents had worried about loitering. For the shops facing it, more people sitting outside means more eyes on the sidewalk in the evening and more people near the door at closing. Neither changes the hardware much. Both are good reasons to keep the staff door well lit and to close with the gate down before the till is counted.

Rooms Behind the Desk

Medication, Records and Supply Rooms: What the Rules Ask For and What a Reader Adds

Behind the inner door of most practices there are rooms that need more than the inner door. Two federal rules are often mentioned in the same breath as access control, and both are frequently misread.

The first is the Drug Enforcement Administration’s storage rule for practitioners, 21 CFR 1301.75. It says controlled substances in Schedules II through V are to be stored in a securely locked, substantially constructed cabinet, and allows pharmacies and institutional practitioners to disperse them through other stock instead. It is a rule about the cabinet. A card reader on the door of the room the cabinet stands in is a good addition, because it limits who reaches the cabinet and records when the room was opened, but it is not a substitute for the locked cabinet itself. If a practice holds controlled substances, we ask to see where they are stored and build the room’s access around that, and we leave the question of what the DEA requires to the practice’s registrant and its advisers.

The second is the HIPAA security rule’s physical safeguards, 45 CFR 164.310, which asks covered practices to have policies limiting physical access to their electronic information systems and the places they are kept, while letting properly authorized people in. Several parts of that standard are written as addressable rather than required, which means each practice decides what is reasonable for its own risks and records why. Readers on a server closet or records room, a door contact that reports when it is left open, and a list of who holds which credential are the kinds of measures practices commonly point to. They are tools for the practice’s own policy, not a compliance certificate, and we do not issue one.

Supply rooms and sample closets are simpler: a battery lever lock with a keypad or reader, on a door with a closer, and a short list of staff who can open it.

Every one of these doors must still open from inside without a key. A supply room that someone can be shut into is a hazard, and the city’s Building Code sets out how electrically locked doors in the path of exit have to release. We fit the free-exit hardware first and the lock second.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Westchester Square

The BID’s needs assessment inventoried every property along four stretches: East Tremont Avenue, the square itself, Williamsbridge Road, and Zerega Avenue with Westchester Avenue. It counted 318 storefronts, found 94 percent of them occupied and most in good condition, and heard from 105 merchants, about half of whom ran businesses with five or fewer staff and nearly half of whom had been in business more than ten years. Who signs, who pays, who keeps the password

In a rented office or shop, three parties have a say in a door. Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Westchester Square: Questions Answered

Questions practices, offices and shop owners around the square ask before a door goes in; the FAQ further down covers different ground.

The Desk That Opens the Door

Our receptionist buzzes in thirty or forty walk-ins a day. Is pressing a release button for each one really security, or just a doorbell with extra steps?

It is security only if three things are true: the desk sees the person before pressing, the button works only while that person’s call is open, and every press is recorded. Without those, a release button is a doorbell that unlocks, and on a busy morning it gets pressed for whoever is pulling on the door.

With them, thirty or forty releases a day become thirty or forty decisions the desk can stand behind, each with a time and a picture. The workload does not grow. What disappears are the presses made out of habit and the doors that open for someone the desk never saw.

Patients arrive before our first appointment and wait on the sidewalk under the el. Can the street door open on a schedule while the desk still controls everything beyond the waiting room?

Yes. The street door and the inner door are separate locks on separate schedules. The street door can unlock a few minutes before the first appointment so patients wait inside, while the inner door stays locked to anyone without a staff credential until someone is at the desk.

Once the receptionist arrives, one tap returns the street door to desk release for the rest of the day. A camera over the inner door and a contact on it tell the first staff member whether anyone tried it before they came in.

When the person at the front desk steps away, who answers the door?

Whoever you choose, in the order you choose. A call from the street station can ring the desk first, then a second station in the back office, then a manager’s phone through the app if nobody inside has answered after a set number of seconds.

The rule we suggest is that the call never goes unanswered and never opens itself. If no one picks up, the caller hears that the office will be with them shortly, the door stays locked, and the missed call shows in the log so the practice can see how often the desk is left empty.

Beside a Campus That Never Closes

At night people looking for the emergency department sometimes ring our practice door instead. What should our door station do and say after hours?

After closing, the station should not ring an empty office. It can play a short recorded message, in the languages your patients use, saying the office is closed and giving the way to the emergency department and your after-hours number, and the door stays locked.

If your practice runs evening hours, the station can still route calls to the desk until closing and switch to the message at a set time. Signage on the door that names the practice and its hours, posted by you, does as much as the hardware to stop the wrong rings.

Ambulette drivers drop our patients at the curb and need the door held while they bring a wheelchair in. Should the drivers get their own credential?

Usually not. A transport company changes drivers often, and a credential tied to a company that serves many offices is hard to keep track of. The better arrangement is a longer release from the desk for those arrivals, or a hold-open the desk can trigger and cancel, with an automatic return to locked.

If one company brings the same patients on fixed days, a dated credential for that company on those days can work. It should open the street door only, never the inner door, and it should end on the date the arrangement ends.

Our last staff member leaves after dark. What should the door, the light and the alarm do in the minute she walks out?

In that minute the light over the staff exit should come on as the door opens, the camera aimed at the steps outside should be recording, the door should relock and report closed as soon as it shuts, and one message should confirm that the building is locked and empty.

Nothing should require her to stand at the door fumbling with a key or a code while facing inward. If the alarm is set from a keypad, the keypad belongs inside, set before she opens the door, so the last action outside is simply walking away.

A lab courier collects specimens after we close. Does the courier need a code to our office, or can a drop box in the vestibule do the job?

A drop box or pass-through in the vestibule is the safer choice. The courier gets a credential that opens the street door into the vestibule only, during the pickup window, and the box itself is locked with a key or code the lab holds.

Your office stays locked, nothing inside is exposed, and the system records the time of each pickup. If the lab changes couriers, the company credential is reissued; nobody needs to be told your office code.

Rooms Behind the Desk

Does a card reader on our medication room door count as the securely locked cabinet the DEA asks for?

No. The DEA storage rule for practitioners speaks of a securely locked, substantially constructed cabinet for controlled substances, and a reader on the room door is not that cabinet. It is a useful layer on top: it limits who reaches the cabinet and records when the room was opened.

We build the room’s access around wherever the controlled substances are actually kept, and we leave the judgment about what the DEA requires to your registrant and advisers. We do not certify compliance with drug storage rules.

Our cleaning company changes its crew without telling us. How do we tie their access to the company rather than to whoever turns up that night?

Issue the access to the company, not to people. The company gets a credential, or a small number of them, that works only on cleaning nights, only between set hours and only on the doors in the contract, with an end date that matches the contract.

If you want to know who came, the system can send a message when the cleaning credential is first used each night. When the contract ends or the company loses a card, you cancel it in one step and nobody’s keys need changing.

Offices on the Corridors

Our tax-preparation office triples its walk-ins from February to April. Can the door run differently during the busy season?

Yes. The season can be entered once as a date range with its own settings: a longer release time, a second call station or phone that rings when the desk is busy, the street door open on a schedule during peak hours, and later closing times for staff credentials.

On the first day after the season the ordinary settings return by themselves. Seasonal staff get credentials that end on the last day of the season, which is the step most offices forget when they rely on keys.

We are signing a lease for one of the new ground-floor commercial spaces on Blondell Avenue. What should the lease say about who installs and owns the door system?

The lease should say who may install hardware on the storefront door and frame, whether the landlord’s own building system has to be used for any shared doors, who owns readers and controllers paid for by the tenant, and whether they stay or can be removed at the end of the term.

It should also say who holds the administrator account for any shared door and how the landlord gets in if the tenant cannot be reached. Settling those points before the fit-out is cheaper than unpicking them later, and we can price both options so the numbers are in front of you when you negotiate.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Westchester Square

We could not read an AI search summary for this neighborhood from where this page was written, so we do not quote one. These are claims we hear repeated by owners who have read or been told them, set against what we find on doors around the square.

“A video doorbell from the hardware store is all an office door needs.”

A video doorbell tells you someone is at the door. It does not, on its own, decide who gets through, keep a record of releases, stop a button being pressed after the call has ended, or separate the waiting room from the rest of the office. In a home that is often enough. In a practice where the desk opens the door for strangers dozens of times a day, it covers one step of six.

The doorbells we find on office doors around the square were often installed by a previous tenant and registered to a personal account nobody can reach. The practice sees an image on someone’s phone, but the door is still opened by a buzzer that works for anyone.

Keep the doorbell if you like it, as a second view. The street door still needs a station whose release is tied to the call, and the inner door still needs its own lock.

“HIPAA requires card readers on every door.”

The physical safeguards in the HIPAA security rule ask covered practices to limit physical access to their electronic information systems and the places they are kept, while letting authorized people in, and several parts of the standard are addressable rather than required. They do not name card readers, and they do not require one on every door.

What a practice needs is a policy it can explain and measures that carry it out. A reader on a server closet or records room is a common measure because it limits entry and keeps a record. A reader on the waiting-room restroom is not.

We install the measures a practice chooses and document them. The decision about what the practice’s risk analysis calls for belongs to the practice and its compliance adviser.

“A reader on the medication room satisfies the drug storage rules.”

The DEA’s storage rule for practitioners speaks of a securely locked, substantially constructed cabinet. A reader on the room door is a second layer, not the cabinet.

We see the confusion often enough to raise it on every survey of a practice that holds controlled substances. The reader is worth having: it narrows who can reach the cabinet and records when the room was opened. It just does not change what the cabinet has to be.

Ask your registrant or adviser what your registration requires before relying on any door hardware for it.

“Expect to pay a national per-door average.”

National averages blend residential smart locks, suburban office parks and high-rise lobbies. They say little about a two-door practice entry in an older Bronx building, where the frame may need work and the cable may have to travel under a waiting room floor.

Our prices are published on this page at the Bronx rate, which is the same multiplier as our Brooklyn list. Single doors start at $1,500, and the range for each line is set by how much the door, the frame and the cable path need, not by a national figure.

Directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish ranges for comparison, and they are a reasonable sanity check. A written door-by-door price after seeing the doors is the number to plan on.

“Magnetic locks are the strongest and the simplest choice for a street door.”

Magnetic locks hold well when powered and release when power goes. On a street door that means the door is open in an outage, and it needs extra devices to meet the city’s release rules for electrically locked exit doors.

For most practice and office street doors around the square, an electric strike with mechanical free exit is the better fit: locked from outside in an outage, always free from inside, and simpler to bring into line with the code.

There are doors where a magnetic lock is right, usually glass doors with no room for a strike, and we will say so when we see one. It should be a choice made for that door, not a default.

“A busy office does not need a lock on the street door during the day.”

Plenty of offices leave the street door open in business hours and rely on the desk to notice who comes in. That works until the desk is busy, and around the square the desk is busy most mornings.

A door that opens on a schedule for the first hour and goes to desk release afterwards keeps the convenience where it matters and closes the gap where it does not. The inner door stays locked throughout.

Which doors stay open during the day is a decision for each office. The point is that it should be a decision written into the schedule, not something that happened because the strike stopped catching.

“A national alarm company handles door access the same way anywhere.”

National alarm brands such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe are built around alerts and monitoring, and they do that well. Door access in a practice is a different job: who may open which door, at what hours, released from which desk, with what record.

Some of those companies offer access products, and some office owners are happy with them. Ask the same questions you would ask us: who owns the administrator account, can the inner door and the restricted rooms live in the same system, and what happens when you cancel the contract.

If you already have an alarm system you like, we work alongside it. The door system and the alarm can share a contact or an arming step without either company owning the other’s equipment.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading the Release Log on Day Thirty

This is a note on method, not a story about any one office. Thirty days after a desk release goes in, we ask the practice to open the log with us. It is the most useful half hour of the whole job, because the log shows how the door is really used rather than how anyone remembers it.

Three patterns turn up again and again. A cluster of releases in the first minutes after opening, often more than the appointments booked, which usually means the street door should open on a schedule for that window. Missed calls at the same time each day, which is lunch or a staff meeting, and means the call order needs a second station. And releases after the last appointment, which are usually couriers or cleaners who should have their own company credentials instead of being buzzed in.

Changes are made in the settings; nothing new is installed.
