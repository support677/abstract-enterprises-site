The Neighborhood

Why Weeksville Properties Need Access Control

Weeksville is the name of the free Black village James Weeks and other landowners built in central Brooklyn from 1838, and today it is the name residents, listings and the Heritage Center use for the blocks around Bergen Street, St. Johns Place, Dean Street and Prospect Place between roughly Troy and Ralph Avenues, north toward Atlantic Avenue. Maps disagree about whether those blocks belong to Crown Heights or Bedford-Stuyvesant. For a door job it matters less than one plain fact: in Brooklyn Community District 8, which takes in Weeksville’s Crown Heights side, the Furman Center puts homeownership at 19.3 percent against 32.7 percent citywide. Four out of five households rent. The person standing at the street door with a key is usually not the person who decides what lock is on it.

That shapes every access control conversation we have here. A landlord wants to know who came in, a tenant wants to know who is being watched, a church board wants volunteers to open the pantry without handing out a key to the sanctuary, and a shop owner who leases the ground floor wants a code on the back door without asking a landlord for every change. The equipment is the easy part. The work is deciding who controls the list of people, what the system is allowed to record, and what happens to the door when the power or the internet goes out.

Weeksville’s housing gives those questions an unusual mix of answers. On St. Johns Place and Schenectady Avenue there are small prewar buildings like the one openigloo lists at 235 Schenectady Avenue, also known as 1347 to 1351 St. Johns Place: built in 1909, four floors, six apartments, with rent-stabilized units and good cause eviction coverage. Off Bergen Street is NYCHA’s Kingsborough Houses, sixteen six-story buildings with 1,148 apartments, finished in 1941. On long-vacant lots stand new affordable buildings such as Weeksville Place on Prospect Place between Ralph and Buffalo Avenues, eight stories and 45 apartments, and the 199-unit lottery building at 1921 Atlantic Avenue. Between them are row houses, a few surviving wood-frame houses, churches that grew building by building, and auto shops and stores along Atlantic Avenue and Utica Avenue.

Each of those owners lives under different rules for the same piece of hardware. A fob reader on a six-family rental is governed by the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law and, where apartments are rent-stabilized, by what the state housing agency has expected in rulings on fob conversions. The same reader at Kingsborough is NYCHA’s own system, installed and run by NYCHA. A reader on a church side door answers to the trustees and, increasingly, to the terms of a state security grant. A keypad on a tire shop’s door answers to the shop’s lease. We start every Weeksville job by finding out which of these applies, because the answer changes what we install and what we hand over afterward.

The other reason access control matters here is that a broken door teaches people to prop it. When the NYC Comptroller’s auditors walked 262 NYCHA developments in the late summer of 2022, they found 40.1 percent of entrance door locks broken and 57.9 percent of residential entrance doors open or unlocked, up from 23.5 percent in 2018, and residents told them that intercoms, keys and release buttons that do not work are why doors get propped. Comptroller Brad Lander put it simply: “Broken doors are a symbolic reminder that home safety starts at the entrance.” Those were public housing doors, not private ones. The lesson carries across Ralph Avenue anyway: a system people cannot rely on is worse than a good mechanical lock, because it ends up wedged open with a brick.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Weeksville’s Architecture

Weeksville’s buildings come from very different decades, and the door in front of us usually says which. These are the six kinds of property we plan for here and what each tends to need from an entry system.

Prewar walk-ups

Three- to six-story apartment buildings from the early twentieth century on St. Johns Place, Schenectady Avenue and the streets around them, usually with a street door, a vestibule, a cellar door and a yard door. Many have rent-stabilized apartments, which brings state rulings on fob conversions into the plan.

Masonry row houses and two-families

Brick and brownstone rows that, as Brownstoner’s history of the area notes, replaced earlier wood-frame houses. A stoop door, a door under the stoop and a yard door, often with an owner downstairs and a rental floor upstairs who need separate lists.

Surviving wood-frame houses

A handful of nineteenth-century frame houses, such as the two-story fish-scale-shingled house at 1718 Dean Street that Brownstoner profiled in 2018. Wood frames and doors move with the seasons, and their old siding is not something we drill through without asking what it is.

New affordable buildings

Mid-rise and high-rise buildings on formerly vacant lots, such as Weeksville Place and the lottery building at 1921 Atlantic Avenue, with lobbies, laundries, bike rooms and roof terraces already wired for fobs by a developer, and residents who arrive in waves.

Churches and congregations

Congregations that grew over a century and own a sanctuary, a hall, a parsonage or an office, sometimes in buildings decades apart, like Berean Missionary Baptist Church’s 1894 church at 1635 Bergen Street and its 1961 church next door.

Auto shops and storefronts

One- and two-story buildings along Atlantic Avenue beneath the Long Island Rail Road viaduct, where Brownstoner’s 2025 history describes tire and repair shops and garages, and storefronts under apartments on Utica and Ralph Avenues. Overhead doors, steel personnel doors and roll-down gates.

What the materials mean for the install

Prewar walk-ups usually still have a heavy street door in a wood or hollow-metal frame set into brick. The brick takes a reader or panel easily at a mortar joint, and the frame takes a fail-secure strike if it is square and solid. Where the door has been replaced but the original wood frame kept, we check how much sound wood is left around the latch, because a new strike needs something to bite into. The vestibule door behind it is often lighter and glass-paneled; it gets a contact so the controller knows when both doors stand open at once, and sometimes a strike of its own.

Row houses bring a stoop door, an under-stoop door that opens straight onto the sidewalk and a yard door at the back. Cable for all three usually runs through the cellar ceiling to one controller, which keeps every wire inside the house. Wood-frame houses are different. The walls are hollow, which makes cable easier to hide, but the frames and doors swell in August and shrink by February, so we set strikes with enough tolerance to catch the latch in both seasons. On frame houses with old gray cement shingles, we ask before the visit what the siding is; New York State’s Department of Labor warns that material containing asbestos should often be left alone if it is in good condition, and anyone disturbing it must be certified and licensed. We plan mounting and cable routes that avoid that siding entirely.

New buildings are mostly steel and glass at the lobby, with storefront-style aluminum doors that take a listed magnetic lock or an electrified exit device rather than a strike. Their readers and controllers already exist; the question is whether they were installed well and who can manage them. Churches and storefronts tend to have steel doors in steel frames, which are strong but need the right strike body and sometimes a welded or bolted reinforcement. Overhead doors at auto shops are not usually put on readers at all; the person door beside them is.

What a Public Housing Fob Rollout Teaches a Private Weeksville Building

Weeksville has a large public housing neighbor, and NYCHA has spent the last several years moving its campuses from keys to electronic entry. Its fact sheet on the program describes layered access control as key fob access points, intercom systems, magnetic or electronic locks on doors and gates, new IT lines to what it calls the Smart Building Network, a Security Operations Center, and low-voltage centers with power backup. When NYCHA announced in November 2023 that it had finished over $24 million of safety and security work at 23 campuses, Kingsborough was one of the four Brooklyn campuses on the list. The announcement did not say which items went to which campus, so we do not claim to know what is on any Kingsborough door. What we can say is that the scale and the problems of those rollouts are public, and a private landlord on St. Johns Place can learn from both without spending a fraction of the money.

The first lesson is that fobs do not fix doors. The Comptroller’s 2022 audit found more than half of NYCHA’s residential entrance doors open or unlocked, and side and rear doors were not much better, with 28.5 percent of their locks broken. A reader on a door whose closer is worn out, whose strike is misaligned or whose frame is split will release correctly and still fail to lock, and the first tenant who gets tired of it will tape the latch. On a Weeksville walk-up we put the closer, hinges and latch right before we mount anything electronic, and we test the door from fully open, half open and a few inches open, because each tells us something different.

The second lesson is that the switch day decides whether people trust the system. In April 2025, CBS New York reported that at Queensbridge Houses in Queens, keys stopped working before the new fobs reliably did; residents, seniors and children among them, were locked out, some neighbors taped doors open, and the resident association president said 78 door complaints had been logged in one month on the north side alone, with one to three days to resolve each. NYCHA’s answer was that “Layered access fobs and door systems are objectively safer than traditional keys and locks.” Both things can be true. An electronic door is only safer if it works on day one and is fixed the same week it fails.

How we stage a switch in a small building so nobody is left on the sidewalk

In a six- or eight-apartment building the whole rollout fits in a day, and it can be done without anyone being stranded. The old cylinder keeps working while the reader, strike and controller go in and are tested. Fobs are enrolled in advance from the owner’s list of lawful occupants and handed out door to door, with a signed receipt where the owner wants one. The old key path stays live for a set number of days after the fobs are out, and only then is the cylinder changed to a restricted keyway held by the owner and the super for emergencies. Anyone who was not home on the day gets their fob from the super or from us on the return visit.

The owner also gets a phone number for the door, not just for the company. If a fob fails or the strike stops releasing, the call comes to us, and a visit is booked within two hours of it. That is the difference between a building that keeps its door shut and a building whose tenants learn to defeat it.

What a private building should not copy

A NYCHA campus has a security operations center and an IT department. A Weeksville walk-up has an owner who may live upstairs or in another borough, and a super who may look after several buildings. The parts of the public housing model that make sense at that scale are encrypted fobs, door contacts, a battery that carries the door through an outage and a record of entries the owner can consult when something goes wrong. The parts that do not are layers of cloud software with monthly fees, cameras watching every hallway and data kept longer than anyone needs. For rented buildings the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law pushes in the same direction: collect the minimum, keep it only as long as needed, and never use it to track tenants.

Switching a Rented Weeksville Building From Keys to Fobs

Most of the small buildings in Weeksville are rentals, and many of the older ones have rent-stabilized apartments. That does not stop an owner from putting a reader on the street door, but it does mean the switch has to be done in a way tenants and the state housing agency will accept. We are not lawyers and nothing here is legal advice; what follows is what published rulings and city guidance have said, and what we put in front of owners before a job starts so they can take it to their own attorney if they need to.

In one administrative review that has been widely reported, docket DR430034RT from July 29, 2016, the state’s Division of Housing and Community Renewal allowed a landlord to replace building entry keys with fobs or cards with no change to legal rents, on conditions: every occupant, children included, received a free fob; tenants could have up to four more free fobs for guests or employees; replacement fobs could cost no more than $25; the building still had to be accessible with metal keys in an emergency; and, in the ruling’s words, “the system could record entrances, but not departures.” Brick Underground’s 2018 summary of DHCR’s approach described the same pattern of free fobs, capped replacement fees and no new identification demanded from tenants.

The city adds its own layer. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, says owners of multiple dwellings that use keyless entry must get tenants’ express consent, collect only a limited list of data, give tenants a written privacy and retention policy, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, never track tenants outside the building, and never require smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment. The law also lists biometric data among the kinds of authentication it covers.

How those rules change what we install in a Weeksville rental

  • Entry-only readers. Unless the owner has a specific, documented reason, a rental street door gets a reader outside and a plain request-to-exit inside, so the system logs who came in and nothing about who left.
  • Short data retention. We set the controller or the cloud account to keep entry records no longer than the owner’s written policy allows, and the policy is drafted before the system goes live, not after a tenant asks.
  • A key path that stays. A restricted override cylinder on the street door, with keys held by the owner and the super, so the building can be entered in an emergency even if the controller and its battery are both down.
  • Fobs, not faces. We do not recommend fingerprint or face readers for residential street doors in rented buildings. Biometric data is on the city’s list, and when a Brownsville landlord applied in 2018 to put a facial recognition system on the doors of Atlantic Plaza Towers, more than 130 rent-stabilized tenants filed an opposition with the state.
  • A credential count the owner can defend. Enough fobs for every lawful occupant from the start, extras for aides and family, and a replacement charge that stays inside what DHCR has allowed.
  • Apartment doors untouched. The system covers common doors: street, vestibule, cellar, yard, laundry. A tenant’s own apartment door keeps its key.

The Atlantic Plaza Towers filing is worth reading for its tone as much as its law. The tenants’ attorney, Samar Katnani of Brooklyn Legal Services, said: “The ability to enter your home should not be conditioned on the surrender of your biometric data.” A small Weeksville owner is not going to install a facial recognition system. But the same instinct shows up when a landlord replaces a key with anything that records, and an owner who hands out free fobs, a clear one-page policy and a promise that exits are not logged usually finds the switch goes quietly.

Churches and Cultural Nonprofits: Doors on Schedules, and the State Security Grant

Weeksville’s congregations and community organizations are part of how the neighborhood came to exist: Berean Missionary Baptist Church was founded in 1850, and Brownstoner’s history of its 1894 building at 1635 Bergen Street notes it was built entirely by African Americans and still runs programs including food pantries and crime prevention. On the Ocean Hill–Weeksville corridors that NYC Small Business Services studied, houses of worship were the single largest category of storefront use, 79 of 560. Many of those congregations meet in buildings that were never designed for the way they are used now: a sanctuary open on Sunday, a hall used by outside groups on weeknights, a pantry door that opens to a line on the sidewalk, and an office with records that should not be open to anyone with a key.

The access problem in a church is usually keys that walked away. Keys go to trustees, ushers, musicians, the cleaning volunteer and the youth leader, and a few years later nobody knows how many are out. A small controller with codes or fobs per person, schedules for each door, and a list the pastor or the trustee chair can change from a phone solves most of it. The sanctuary doors can unlock themselves before a service and relock after; the pantry door can open on pantry days for the hours volunteers are present; the office door can stay on a short list. Exits always open from inside without a key or a code, which matters even more in a room full of people.

There is also money for this now. In April 2026 the Governor announced up to $70 million for the state’s Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes program, with organizations able to request up to $250,000 each and up to 280 projects expected to be funded. The announcement lists eligible organizations including community and civic centers, cultural museums and daycare centers at risk of hate crimes or attack because of their ideology, beliefs or mission, and lists eligible costs including lighting, locks, alarms, panic buttons, fencing, barriers and access controls. Applicants submit a vulnerability assessment showing their risk and how the project will protect the people who use the facility. The 2026 applications were due at noon on July 8, with awards expected in the fall.

What we provide when a grant is involved

We do not write grant applications and we do not tell an organization whether it qualifies; that is between the organization and the state. What we can supply is what a grant file usually needs from an installer: a written, door-by-door scope that names every device, a price taken from our published Brooklyn list, our New York State license number and insurance, and, after the work, dated photos and an itemized invoice in the organization’s legal name. If the organization has its own vulnerability assessment, we build the scope from it rather than inventing priorities of our own. If its rules require several quotes or bar the company that helped write the specifications from bidding, we follow them.

Cultural spaces and visitors

Weeksville’s best-known cultural institution, the Weeksville Heritage Center at 158 Buffalo Avenue, runs guided tours of the Hunterfly Road houses for pre-registered groups of up to 14 at set times, according to its own visitor pages. We have not worked there and do not describe its systems. Readers on the staff and storage doors, a schedule on the public door, and a log the director can export once a quarter cover most of it.

New Affordable Buildings: Taking Over the Doors a Developer Chose

Some of Weeksville’s newest housing went up on land that sat empty for decades. Settlement Housing Fund describes Weeksville Place, on Prospect Place between Ralph and Buffalo Avenues, as an eight-story building with 45 apartments for seniors, individuals and families, some for households coming from shelters, with a community room, laundry, roof terrace, bike room and a rear yard for gardening. Access Control in Weeksville: Questions Answered

Walk-ups, row houses and the doors inside them

HPD’s list for our six-unit walk-up includes a missing peephole on one apartment door. Should the apartment doors be dealt with on the same visit as the street-door reader?

It is worth doing them together, for practical reasons more than legal ones. The street-door reader and the apartment doors are separate systems: the reader controls the common entrance, and each apartment door keeps its own key lock, peephole and chain guard, which the Housing Maintenance Code expects owners to provide. A missing peephole is not something an access system replaces. But when a technician is already in the building with the right drill bits and the tenants have been told someone will be there, adding or replacing a peephole is a small job, and it means the violation can be corrected and documented at the same time as the new entrance goes in.

What we do not do is put readers or keypads on apartment doors in a rented building as part of a street-door job. The Tenant Data Privacy Law says an owner may not require smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment, and tenants should keep their keys. If an owner wants to talk about apartment door hardware, we treat it as a separate conversation with its own written scope.

Our street door was replaced with a hollow-metal door years ago, but the original wood frame stayed. Where does the strike go, and does the frame have to be replaced first?

An electric strike goes in the frame, not the door, so the frame is what we check. A new hollow-metal door in an old wood frame is common in Weeksville walk-ups, and it can work well, but only if the wood around the latch is sound. We look for splits from past break-ins, soft wood from water at the bottom of the jamb, and the size of the pocket left by earlier strikes and lock changes. If there is solid wood to cut into, the strike goes in with a reinforcing plate and longer screws into the stud behind. If the jamb is split or bored out, we either repair that section with a hardwood or metal insert or recommend a new steel frame. A frame replacement is a bigger job and we would price it separately, but it is cheaper than a strike that pulls loose the first time someone shoulders the door.

The strike on our street door clacks loudly every time it releases, and the ground-floor tenant’s bedroom is right behind that wall. Can it be made quieter?

Usually, yes. Loud buzzing or clacking often comes from an older strike running on alternating current, which hums for as long as it is energized, or from a strike whose keeper slams against a misaligned latch. A fail-secure strike running on direct current from the controller’s power supply is much quieter: it makes a short click when it releases and nothing else. We also check the alignment so the latch drops into the strike rather than striking its edge, and set the closer so the door does not slam shut. If the old buzzer board drives the strike, the release time may be set very long; on a new controller, we set it to the few seconds people actually need. The tenant behind the wall should notice the difference the first night.

Our corner building has several street numbers on St. Johns Place and another on Schenectady Avenue. Which address should the entry panel and the access records use?

Use the address the mail, deliveries and emergency services use for the door the panel is on, and say so on the panel. Corner buildings in Weeksville often carry a run of numbers on one street and a different number around the corner; openigloo’s listing for 235 Schenectady Avenue, for example, also gives 1347, 1349 and 1351 St. Johns Place. Couriers and visitors look for the number on the door in front of them. We mount the panel with its own street number and label each controlled door in the system by its location, such as the St. Johns Place street door or the Schenectady Avenue cellar door, rather than by tax-lot address. The owner’s door list and the written privacy policy name the building’s official address, so there is no doubt which building a record belongs to.

Near Kingsborough and the new buildings

Our private walk-up faces Kingsborough across Ralph Avenue. Could our building’s reader accept the fobs Kingsborough residents already carry?

No, and it would not be a good idea even if it were technically possible. Kingsborough’s fobs are NYCHA’s credentials, issued and cancelled through NYCHA’s system, and a private owner has no way to know when one is switched off or reassigned. Enrolling another organization’s credentials in your building would also make your entry records depend on a list you do not control, which is the opposite of what the Tenant Data Privacy Law expects of you. If one of your tenants also works or visits at Kingsborough, they will simply carry two fobs. What a private building can borrow from a public housing rollout is the idea, not the credential: encrypted fobs, door contacts on every controlled door, a battery that carries the door through an outage, and a short, clear policy for tenants.

Our senior building stands on public housing land under a long ground lease. Who is allowed to hire a company to change the entry system, the developer, the managing agent or NYCHA?

Whoever the lease and the management agreement say is responsible for the building’s systems, and we will ask to see that in writing before we start. On ground-lease projects the land belongs to one party and the building is built and run by another, and the paperwork usually spells out who maintains what. For an access system that is normally the owner entity and its managing agent, not the agency that owns the land, but the regulatory agreement may require approvals for capital changes. We work on the written authorization of the party that controls the building, list every change in the quote, and hand administrator access to that party at the end. If the authorization is unclear, we wait until it is clear; doors in a building full of seniors are not the place to discover that two parties think they are in charge.

Our row house is a block from the Hunterfly Road houses. Does being that close to a landmark mean the Landmarks Preservation Commission has to approve a reader?

Not because of the distance alone. The Hunterfly Road houses off Bergen Street, which the Weeksville Heritage Center preserves, were made city landmarks in 1970 and listed on the National Register as the Hunterfly Road Historic District in 1972. That designation covers those houses, not the surrounding blocks. Landmarks approval is needed for buildings that are individually designated or inside a designated district, and being next door does not bring a house inside one. The way to be certain is to look the address up on the commission’s own map before work starts; we ask owners to do that, and if a building turns out to be designated, we plan the smallest device in the least visible place and supply the product sheets an application needs.

Congregations, gardens and storefronts

Our congregation owns an 1890s church, a newer sanctuary next to it and a parsonage behind. Can one controller run doors in all three buildings?

Yes, as long as there is a way to get a cable or a network connection between the buildings. A single controller in the main building can run doors in the neighboring ones if a cable can pass through a shared wall, a basement or an underground conduit; otherwise each building gets a small controller of its own and they share one list of people over the church’s network. Either way the trustees see one list: the pastor, staff, ushers, volunteers and outside groups, each with the doors and hours that fit their role. The doors themselves differ, wood in the old church, steel or aluminum in the newer one, a residential door at the parsonage, so each gets the right lock, and the parsonage normally stays on a separate, private list for the family who lives there.

Our block association runs a community garden on a fenced lot with no electricity. Can the gate take a keypad?

Yes. A battery-powered keypad lock made for gates does not need a power line or an internet connection; it runs on batteries that typically last months, and codes can be added and removed at the keypad or from a phone held close to it. Each member gets their own code, so when someone leaves the garden, only their code is removed and nobody has to collect keys or change a padlock. The gate itself has to be stiff enough to hold the lock square, and chain-link gates often need a mounting plate. What a battery lock cannot do on a lot with no power is send alerts or keep a long record off-site; if the association wants those, a small solar or cellular option adds cost, and we would say so plainly in the quote.

Our laundromat has an attendant only part of the day. Can the front door stop letting people in at closing while customers already inside finish and leave?

Yes. The door is set on a schedule: free entry during staffed hours, entry only by code or fob after the attendant leaves, and no new entry at all after the last-wash time. Exit stays free the whole time, so customers who are already inside can always leave by pushing the door or the exit button, which the Building Code requires anyway. Regular customers can be given codes for unattended hours if the owner wants that, each with its own schedule and an end date. A door contact tells the owner if the door is left standing open after closing, and a camera over the door, if the owner adds one, shows who came in during the unattended hours.

“Fobs are always safer than keys.”

NYCHA said almost exactly this to CBS New York in 2025: “Layered access fobs and door systems are objectively safer than traditional keys and locks.” In principle, yes. “Face recognition is the most secure way into a building.”

For the street door of a rented building in central Brooklyn, the case is much weaker. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law lists biometric data among the authentication data it regulates, and the attempt to put a facial recognition system on Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville drew a formal opposition from more than 130 rent-stabilized tenants, whose attorney argued that entering your home should not depend on surrendering biometric data.

An encrypted fob or a code does the same job at the street door, can be cancelled in seconds, and does not ask tenants to hand over their faces. We install fobs and codes for residential entrances and leave biometrics to settings where they actually fit.

“A landlord can switch the building to fobs whenever it wants.”

Quick answers often treat the street door as the owner’s business alone. For a building without rent-stabilized apartments, an owner does have wide latitude over the entrance hardware, as long as the door still meets the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Building Code.

Field Notes

Field Notes: We Ask Who Holds the List Before We Ask About Fobs

This describes how we start a Weeksville job, not a particular customer. Before we talk about readers, we ask three questions. Who owns this door? Who is allowed to change who can open it? And who will be on the list on the first day? The answers are rarely as simple as they sound. In a six-family the owner may live in another borough while a cousin collects the rent. In a church the trustees may have changed last spring and the old chair still has the only master key. In a new building the managing agent may not yet have the login the developer’s contractor created.

Then we count keys. We ask how many copies of the street-door key are out and who has them, and the usual answer is a guess. That number is the real reason most people call us, even when they start by asking about fobs. Only after that do we open the street door fully, let it go and watch whether it closes and latches by itself. By the time we write the quote, the owner usually knows three things they did not know before the visit: who should hold the administrator login, how many people need a credential on the first day, and whether the first line on the quote should be the closer.
