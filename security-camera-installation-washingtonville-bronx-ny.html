The Neighborhood

Why a Washingtonville Camera Plan Starts at the End of the Line

Few maps still print the name. Washingtonville was a small settlement on the New York and Harlem Railroad, which laid its tracks through this part of the Bronx in the 1840s. The station that served it was called Washingtonville, and Wikipedia's history of the Metro-North stop says it was renamed Wakefield sometime between 1894 and 1905. The village itself was folded into Wakefield in 1889, and the whole area became part of New York City in 1895. One old map shows the northernmost section of Wakefield as its own section labeled Washingtonville, and that is the part of the Bronx this page is about: the blocks around East 241st Street and White Plains Road, between the railroad and the county line. The Historic Districts Council uses the same name for what is now Woodlawn Heights, after an 18th-century redoubt George Washington built near today's Woodlawn Cemetery, so you will see the name applied to both sides of the tracks.

What sets this corner of the borough apart is that so much ends here. The 2 train finishes its run at Wakefield–241st Street, which opened on December 13, 1920 and sits at White Plains Road and 241st Street, with the 239th Street Yard just south of it. The Metro-North Wakefield station, the northernmost Harlem Line stop in the city, is on East 241st Street, with stairways from the north side of the street. And three blocks north of the subway, White Plains Road crosses into Westchester County. Every evening, people coming off both lines walk home from the same few corners, and most of the businesses in the neighborhood line White Plains Road beside them.

The homes they walk to are mostly houses. Homes.com describes Wakefield as a mix of one-, two- and three-family homes with a large share of apartments and co-ops, much of it prewar brick, with semi-detached brick colonials, detached Victorians and bungalows. City Planning's zoning study for Wakefield and Eastchester describes the area as mostly one- and two-family detached homes. A StreetEasy record for one house on East 241st Street lists a two-story, two-unit house built in 1920, the year the subway arrived. A commuter on a rail forum summed it up in a line we hear on visits too: some apartment buildings, but mostly houses, and it can get loud.

So a camera plan here is less about a lobby and more about a single house and its edges: the steps and front door, the car at the curb or in a short driveway, the narrow passage down one side, the yard behind, and, in a two-family, a second household with its own door and its own idea of privacy. On White Plains Road, it is about a store door that may be buzzed open after dark, a counter, a register and a stockroom, often under the elevated tracks. Every one of those spots needs a camera aimed for a reason, and every reason is a little different on these blocks than it would be in a neighborhood of six-story walk-ups.

What the sources tell us about cameras in and around Washingtonville

  • Store doors get buzzed open. ABC7 reported that in January 2026 a 24-year-old man was shot during a dispute inside a store on East 236th Street, a few blocks south, at about 6:30 p.m. Video showed two of the men, one wearing a surgical mask, walking up to the door and waiting to be buzzed in.
  • A store break-in can take less than three minutes. News 12 Bronx aired surveillance video of a Wakefield bodega burglary in which four people approached, three went in and one kept lookout. When picking the lock failed, one of them shattered the glass door. The owner said the whole thing took under three minutes, and the station's report put the loss at $7,000.
  • Cars and parking are real worries. A resident review on Homes.com says people break into cars and take their sideview mirrors. A review on Apartments.com says safety and parking are getting worse if you do not have a driveway.
  • Packages go missing between neighbors. A TikTok post from Wakefield shows a woman the poster says was taking packages from the poster and the neighbors.
  • The precinct numbers are not small. The 47th Precinct, at 4111 Laconia Avenue, covers Wakefield along with Woodlawn, Williamsbridge, Baychester, Edenwald and Olinville. Wikipedia lists 300 burglaries, 758 grand larcenies and 461 grand larcenies of autos there in 2022, and the Bronx Times police beat reported a 42.9% rise in burglaries in the precinct in February and March 2024 compared with a year earlier.
  • More buildings are coming to the corridor. The Bronx Times reports that City Planning is working on a plan for White Plains Road under the 2 and 5 lines, from 241st Street to Adee Avenue in Allerton, and that the Community Board 12 chair has watched one- and two-family homes replaced by larger buildings.

The Walk Home

From the Terminal Stairs to Your Front Steps: Cameras on the Streets Around East 241st Street

Two rail lines unload at the north end of this neighborhood. Riders come down from the elevated 2 train terminal at White Plains Road, and commuters climb down the stairways from the Metro-North platforms on East 241st Street. Many of them walk the last few blocks home on Furman, Bullard and Richardson Avenues, or on the numbered streets from 238th to 242nd. For the houses on those blocks, the stretch between the corner and the front door is the part of the day a camera can actually help with, and it is the part most house systems leave out because every camera is pointed at the house itself.

The approach, not just the door

A doorbell at the front door shows whoever rings it. What it rarely shows is the twenty seconds before: someone walking up behind you from the corner, waiting by a parked car, or crossing the street toward your gate. A camera high on the front corner of the house, aimed along the sidewalk toward the nearest corner, records that approach. On a mid-block house it is usually aimed toward whichever corner people come from on the way back from the trains. On a corner house it may need to look down both streets, which is a job for two cameras, not one.

Aiming at the street without staring at the neighbors

The houses on these blocks face each other across narrow streets, and the view along the sidewalk inevitably takes in the fronts of the houses opposite. We aim sidewalk cameras low enough to put the sidewalk and curb in the middle of the frame, and then draw privacy masks over the windows of the houses across the street, so those parts of the picture are blacked out live and in every recording. You get the sidewalk and the public way. Your neighbor's front room stays out of it.

Light on the last few steps

Streetlight on a residential block reaches the curb and fades by the time it gets to a front door set back behind a small yard and a gate. A camera in night mode will still record something in the dark, but faces in infrared are flat and hard to recognize. A porch or entry light on a dusk-to-dawn sensor, or a motion light aimed at the steps, does two things at once: it gives the camera a face in color, and it makes the last few steps feel less like a dark corner. On many houses the fixture is already there; it just needs a brighter, warmer bulb. We point that out on the visit rather than selling a camera to make up for a burned-out bulb.

Loud blocks and constant alerts

The same thing that makes a camera useful near the stations makes it annoying: traffic. Buses, cars, people heading to the trains at 6 a.m. and coming back at 7 p.m. will set off a camera that alerts on any motion in its view. We record the whole view and alert only on part of it: the steps, the gate, the driveway apron. You get a notification when someone comes onto your property, not when the morning commute walks past.

Cars and Driveways

Watching the Car When It Lives at the Curb, and When It Lives in the Driveway

When residents here write about the neighborhood, the car comes up again and again. One review on Homes.com says people break into cars and take the sideview mirrors. Another on Apartments.com says parking is getting worse if you do not have a driveway. And the 47th Precinct's own totals include hundreds of car thefts a year across its whole area. For a household with one car, the camera that covers it is often the one that matters most, and on these blocks the car is in one of two places.

At the curb

Without a driveway, the car sits in whatever space opened up, which may be in front of your house or three doors down. A camera can only cover the stretch it can see, so the honest plan is to cover the curb in front of your house and your immediate neighbors, from a mount high on the front corner, and to park in that stretch when you can. That camera is aimed along the curb, not across the street, so it sees the driver's side of a car parked at your curb and anyone standing beside it. Sideview mirrors and windows are on that side; so are the people who go after them.

In a short driveway

Many detached and semi-detached houses here have a narrow driveway along one side, often ending at a garage or a parking pad behind the house. The best driveway view usually comes from a camera mounted on the house wall near the back of the driveway, looking toward the street. It sees the whole car, the gap between the car and the house, and the face of anyone walking in from the sidewalk, because they are walking toward the lens. A camera at the street end looking back sees mostly backs and the rear of the car.

What a curb or driveway camera can and cannot do

A camera does not stop someone from snapping off a mirror in five seconds. What it does is put a time, a direction of travel and, with the right light and angle, a face on the record. It also shows you, quickly, whether the mirror was broken by a passing truck or by a person, which matters to an insurer. For plates, we are honest: reading a plate on a moving car at night takes a camera aimed and set for that one job, close to the lane the cars use. A general driveway camera will show a car's make, color and direction, which is often enough for neighbors to compare notes, but not always a plate.

Garages behind the house

Where there is a detached garage at the end of the driveway, it gets its own camera on the outside covering the door and the apron, and sometimes one inside if tools or a second car live there. The cable runs from the house to the garage in outdoor-rated cable, either buried in conduit or carried along the fence or the side of the driveway where digging through concrete is not practical. We test that run before the camera goes up, because fixing a damaged run is far easier before the ground is closed.

Steps, Porches and Packages

The Front of a Washingtonville House: Steps, Porch, Door and the Box Left Beside It

Front entrances in Washingtonville come in a few common shapes. Brick two-families often have a short run of steps to a door with a small landing and maybe a metal awning. Semi-detached brick colonials have a front door with a stoop and a small yard behind a low fence or gate. Detached Victorians often have a full porch, sometimes one that wraps around a corner of the house. Bungalows sit lower, with a door a few steps up from a walk. Delivery drivers leave boxes in whichever spot is closest to the door and least visible from the street, which is rarely where the doorbell is looking.

The doorbell is the first camera, not the only one

A video doorbell is good at the face of whoever stands at the door. It is poor at the landing beside it, the porch floor behind a railing, and the steps below. On houses where packages have gone missing, the fix is usually a second camera mounted high above the door or in the corner of the porch ceiling, angled down across the landing and the steps. It catches the box wherever it was left and the face of anyone climbing the steps, because people tend to look up as they climb.

When the taker is a neighbor

The Wakefield TikTok post about package theft described someone the poster said was taking boxes from the poster and the neighbors. When the person is local, the useful footage is the face and the clothing on the way up the steps, and the direction they walked on the way out. That is why the camera covering the steps should also take in a little of the sidewalk on either side. A clear clip can be shared with the police; it can also settle a quiet conversation on the block before anything else happens.

Talking to the door without opening it

The January 2026 shooting on East 236th Street began with people waiting to be let in. At a house, the same lesson applies to anyone home alone: see the person clearly, then decide. A wired doorbell with two-way talk lets you answer from your phone, ask who it is, and tell them to leave the box or come back, without unlocking anything. We set it up on every adult's phone in the household and spend a few minutes with whoever is home during the day, often an older family member, so they are comfortable answering it.

Wired over battery at the front door

Battery doorbells are simple to put up and they work. Their trouble is winter, when cold drains the battery faster, and Wi-Fi, which may be weak at the front door of a brick house with the router in a back room. Most older houses here already have doorbell wiring to a chime. A wired doorbell on that circuit has no battery to charge, and if it also records to the house recorder, the front door is on the same timeline as every other camera.

Shared Houses

One House, Two or Three Households: Camera Rules That Keep the Peace

A large share of Washingtonville's houses hold more than one household. A brick two-family may have the owner on one floor and a tenant on the other; a three-family may be fully rented; a family may share a house across generations, with grown children upstairs and parents downstairs. A camera system in that kind of house works only if everyone in it understands what it covers and who can see it.

Shared ground first

Before any camera goes up, we sort the property into shared and private ground. Shared: the front walk and steps, the street door, any shared hall and stairs, the driveway, the side passage, the yard if both households use it, and the trash and recycling spot. Private: each apartment and its own entrance, and any part of the yard that belongs to one household by agreement. Cameras cover the shared ground. None of them looks through an apartment door when it opens or into anyone's windows.

Who sees which camera

One recorder can hand different people different views. The owner usually sees everything. A tenant household sees the street door, the steps, the sidewalk view and the camera on its own entrance, but not the owner's entrance. Each adult gets a personal login on their own phone, nobody shares a password, and a tenant's logins are removed the day they move out. That arrangement takes a little setup time and no extra hardware.

Putting it on paper

Tenants should know where the cameras are and what each one sees. We give the owner a one-page list of every camera, its location and its view, which many owners attach to the lease or hand over at move-in. In our experience the arguments in shared houses start with a camera someone did not know about, not with the cameras themselves.

Cross-generational houses

In a house shared by family, the questions are softer but real. Grandparents may not want an app, or may want to see who is at the door from a screen downstairs. Adult children upstairs may not want their comings and goings logged for their parents. The fix is the same: decide in advance which views each person has, and keep alert settings per person, so the parent who worries gets driveway alerts at night while the teenager upstairs does not get a buzz every time the parent goes out to the car.

Brick two-families: the basement is the highway

The best news on a brick two-family is the basement. In most of these houses it runs under the whole footprint, often with open joists, and the front wall, side wall and back wall are all reachable from below. Camera cable leaves the recorder, travels along the joists on hooks, and exits through a sleeved hole in the foundation directly below each outdoor camera, or rises inside a wall cavity to the second floor. On the outside, mounts are anchored into solid brick, not mortar alone, and every penetration is sealed. Where a camera has to go high on the second floor or under the eave, we look for a closet or a wall cavity that lines up, so nothing runs down the face of the house.

Semi-detached houses: one open side

A semi-detached house shares one wall with its twin, which leaves only one side open. That side usually holds the driveway or the passage to the yard, and it carries most of the camera work: the driveway view, the side door and the route to the back. On the attached side we avoid anything that would sit on or aim along the shared line, because a camera on the corner nearest the neighbor tends to look straight down their steps. If both halves of a pair want cameras, doing them on the same visit lets each owner cover their own steps and their own side without either camera looking at the other's door.

Detached Victorians: porches, siding and long walls

The older frame houses bring porches, which are good camera positions with an easy cable path through the porch roof, and siding, which needs care. Mounts on aluminum or vinyl siding sit on a mounting block so the siding does not dent or crack, and holes are sealed. Older frame houses sometimes have open framing bays that run from the basement toward the attic, which can make getting cable up to an eave easier than it looks from the outside. A detached house also has four sides, so the camera count goes up: front, driveway side, the other side, and the back.

Bungalows: low eaves and short sight lines

On a bungalow the eave may be within reach of a tall person on the front walk. Cameras go as high as the house allows, in vandal-resistant housings where they are within reach, and angled down. Because the house is close to the walk, a camera with a wider view covers the front without needing a second one. Cable often runs through the crawl space or the low attic.

Apartment buildings: common areas only

For the pre-war buildings near the station and the newer buildings on White Plains Road, the work is in the common areas: the street door and vestibule, the lobby and mailboxes, the package area, the stairs, the cellar and the roof door. Those jobs are covered in their own section below.

Passages and Yards

Side Passages, Back Doors and the Yards Behind Washingtonville Houses

Behind the street fronts, most Washingtonville lots work the same way: a passage or driveway down one side of the house, a back door or a basement door at the rear, and a yard that touches other yards on two or three sides. The back of a house is where someone can work unseen, and it is also where a careless camera causes the most friction with neighbors.

The passage is a corridor

A side passage is a long, narrow space. The camera belongs at one end looking down its length, not in the middle looking across it. Mounted high at the back corner of the house, aimed toward the street, it shows the face of anyone coming in from the front gate. Where the neighbor's side windows face the passage a few feet away, we mask them out of the image. In a narrow passage at night, a camera's own infrared can bounce off a light-colored wall nearby and wash out the picture, so on tight passages a small motion light often works better than relying on infrared.

Back doors and basement steps

Many houses here have a back door at the top of a few steps and a separate basement door at the bottom of an outside stairwell. Both are entrances, and both are invisible from the street. A camera high on the back wall looking down covers the back door and the top of the stairwell; a second may be needed to see into the stairwell itself. In a two-family, the back door may belong to one household and the basement door to the owner, which feeds into who sees which camera.

Yards that touch other yards

A rear camera mounted high sees your yard and, without care, the yards around it. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a homeowner or tenant a civil claim against someone who films them in their back yard without consent; as Nolo explains it, the claim depends on the filming being meant to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. A security camera aimed at your own back door is not that, but the simplest way to keep it that way is to aim at your own yard and doors, mount a little lower so the camera sees less over the fences, and mask any neighboring yard that still falls in the frame. Then show your neighbor what the camera sees. That conversation prevents most disputes.

The edges of the neighborhood

Some houses back onto something other than another yard. Near the terminal, the 239th Street Yard runs south of the station. The Metro-North tracks run through the area, and the Bronx River forms Wakefield's western edge. A house whose back faces a long fence line or rail embankment has no neighbors' windows to mask on that side, but also no neighbors watching. Those rear views need cameras with real low-light ability, a motion light on the back of the house, and attention to the specific points where someone would come over or through, such as a gate or a low run of fence, rather than trying to cover an entire fence line with one wide camera.

Systems That Never Worked Right

Rescuing a Camera System That Was Hung in a Hurry

A lot of the systems we see in Washingtonville were put up quickly: a kit from a store, mounted by a relative or a handyman over a weekend, with Wi-Fi cameras that drop off in winter and a recorder plugged in wherever there was an outlet. Others were installed years ago by a company that is no longer around. They have something in common: nobody is quite sure what they record, how long they keep it, or who can log in.

Who else can see it?

The first thing we check on any inherited system is access. A recorder or a cloud camera that a previous owner, a former tenant or an old installer can still reach from their phone is a system someone else may be watching. We reset administrator passwords, remove every old user, unlink cloud accounts tied to people who should no longer have them, and then set up phone viewing fresh for the people who should.

What is actually working?

Next we trace each cable, test each camera, and check the recorder's drive and how many days it actually holds. It is common to find a camera that has been recording a wall since a ladder knocked it, or a drive that holds two days instead of two weeks. You get a camera-by-camera list: keep, re-aim, replace or remove.

What is missing?

Hurried systems usually cover the front door and stop. The gaps we find most often here are the curb where the car is parked, the driveway, the side passage, the back door and the basement stairwell. We fill them on the existing recorder where it makes sense, or replace the recorder if it cannot take more cameras.

Old cable is not always junk

Where an older system ran coaxial cable to each camera, that cable can sometimes carry a modern camera with adapters, which avoids pulling new cable through a finished house. Where the cable is thin, chewed or weathered, it is replaced. We test before deciding, and the quote shows which runs are reused.

Through the Year

Keeping a Washingtonville Camera System Sharp Through Winter, Summer and Everything Between

Winter nights

In December an outdoor camera spends most of its day in night mode. Snow and rain catch infrared light and fill the picture with bright streaks, and wet snow clings to cameras without a hood. On the views that matter at night, the front steps, the driveway and the back door, a light on a sensor lets the camera stay in color and avoids most of the glare. Battery cameras drain faster in the cold, which is one more reason to wire the doorbell and the cameras that watch the car.

Snow and the car

After a storm, cars at the curb get buried and dug out, and plows push snow onto driveway aprons. A curb camera aimed low may spend a few days looking at a snowbank. Mounting curb and driveway cameras high enough to look over a plow pile avoids that.

Spring and summer growth

Trees in front yards and along the sidewalk leaf out and can grow into a camera's view by June. Spiders like the warmth of infrared housings and spin webs across lenses, which set off motion alerts at night. A look at every view in late spring and a soft brush over each housing keeps summer recordings clean. If a city street tree is the problem, the camera is re-aimed or moved; the tree is a question for NYC Parks, not for a pair of shears.

The recorder in the basement

The recorder should sit on a shelf off the floor, away from the boiler and any corner that has ever been damp, on a battery unit that rides out short outages and the flickers of summer storms. A recorder that loses power suddenly can damage its drive.

A monthly two-minute check

Once a month, open the app, look at each camera, and scroll back to the oldest recording. If the oldest clip is much newer than it should be, the drive may be failing. We set the recorder to send a phone alert if a camera goes offline or the drive reports an error, so problems show up before you need the footage.

People change, logins should too

When a tenant moves out, a relative moves away or a phone is lost, delete that person's login the same day. Because everyone has their own login, removing one does not lock out anyone else.

On Your Phone

Setting Phone Alerts So a Busy Block Does Not Drown Out the One That Matters

Near two train stations and a commercial corridor, the most common complaint about home cameras is the phone buzzing all day. Within a week the alerts get switched off, and then the camera is just a recorder nobody checks. Alerts deserve as much care as aim.

Draw the zones on your property

Each camera records its whole view but alerts on only part of it. On a Washingtonville house we draw zones over the top step, the gate, the driveway apron, the side passage gate, the back door and the basement stairwell. The sidewalk and street stay recorded but silent.

People and cars, not shadows

Modern cameras and recorders can tell a person or a vehicle from a moving shadow, a branch or a cat. Person detection goes on for doors, gates and the yard. Vehicle detection goes on for the driveway. On the curb camera, an alert for a person stopping beside the car is usually more useful than one for every car that passes.

Schedules that fit the household

A side-gate alert is welcome at 2 a.m. and a nuisance at 3:30 p.m. when the kids come home. Alerts follow a schedule per camera and per person: the owner may get every driveway alert at night, a tenant only their own door. Households with night-shift workers set quiet hours during the day while recording continues.

From alert to decision in seconds

When an alert arrives, the app should open to a short clip. We show each household member how to watch it, save it, and share it with the police if needed, so a moment of worry turns into a decision instead of a scroll through hours of video.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Washingtonville: Questions Answered

Stores and late-night businesses

Our corner store buzzes customers in after dark. Where should the door camera sit so the clerk sees a face before pressing the button?

At face height, close to the door, looking at the spot where people stand to wait, with a monitor at the counter that shows that one view big enough to read. A ceiling camera looking down at the door, which is what many stores have, shows the top of a hood or a cap, so the clerk is deciding blind. The January 2026 video from East 236th Street shows men walking up and waiting to be buzzed in, which is exactly the moment this camera is for. Light the waiting spot well, so the picture is in color, and keep a second camera covering the sidewalk on either side of the door.

The Wakefield bodega burglary on camera was over in under three minutes. Can a phone alert reach us fast enough to change anything?

It can reach you in time to call 911 and start saving the clip, but not in time to stop someone who is already through the glass. Three minutes is long enough for an after-hours alert on the door camera to wake you, show you the live view and let you call while it is still happening. What changes the outcome is the combination: an outside camera that records the lookout as well as the people going in, an inside camera that does not depend on the door staying intact, and a recorder hidden somewhere other than under the counter, so the recording survives the visit.

Our lounge checks IDs at the door. Can the door camera show each guest's face as they come in without recording the ID itself?

Yes. The camera goes just inside the entrance, aimed at the doorway at face height, so each guest walks toward it, and the ID check happens at a spot slightly to the side of its main view. Give the door its own steady white light that does not change with the room's colored lighting. If the check station falls in the frame, a privacy mask can black out the counter surface where IDs are laid down. After the March 2026 lounge shooting News 12 reported, the images police released came from surveillance cameras, which is the kind of record a clear entrance view produces.

Houses and households

Our block of 1920s two-families has a driveway shared between our house and the next one. Can one driveway camera serve both families?

It can, if both families agree first. The simplest version is one camera at the back of the driveway looking toward the street, on one house's recorder, with a viewing login for the other family. A camera cannot show one family only their half, so if either side wants privacy for its own door, the better plan is two cameras, each aimed at its own side door and car, with the other house's door masked. Write down who owns the camera and who pays for it, because the arrangement outlasts the people who made it when a house is sold.

Our detached Victorian has a porch that wraps around a corner of the house. How many cameras does a porch like that need?

Usually two, plus the doorbell. A wraparound porch has two runs that meet at the corner, and a camera at one end cannot see past the turn. We mount one high in the porch ceiling near the front door, looking down across the landing and the front steps, and a second near the far end of the side run looking back toward the corner, so anyone who walks around the porch is seen from the front. Cable reaches both through the porch ceiling from inside. If the side run ends at a second door, that camera covers it too.

Our semi-detached colonial has round columns holding up the porch roof. Can a camera be clamped to a column?

It can, with a band-style pole mount, but it is rarely the best spot. A column is often hollow or decorative, a band mount on a round surface can slowly turn, and cable has to run down the outside of the column where it shows. The porch ceiling or the house wall above the door is usually a sturdier, neater spot with an easier cable path. We use a column only when nothing else gives the view, and then on a column that is solid enough to hold a mount without flexing.

If someone gets past the outside cameras, where does one indoor camera do the most good in a two-story house?

Facing the main route through the house, usually the foot of the stairs or the hall between the back door and the front rooms, at a height that shows faces of people walking toward it. That one view catches anyone moving between floors or rooms, whichever door they came in by. Turn it off, or set it to record only while the house is empty, so the household is not being recorded at home, and never aim indoor cameras into bedrooms or bathrooms. In a two-family, an indoor camera belongs only inside the owner's own apartment.

In our two-family, should the recorder sit in the shared basement or upstairs in the owner's apartment?

The basement is the easier place to wire, because the cable to every outdoor camera already runs there. But a basement both households can reach is also a place where anyone can unplug the recorder. The usual compromise is the basement in a locked cabinet with ventilation, on a battery unit, away from the boiler. If the basement is shared and cannot be locked, a closet on the owner's floor works too, with the cables rising to it inside a wall, and it keeps the recorder out of reach of anyone who should not be touching it.

Relatives in Guyana and Jamaica want to check our front camera too. Will the picture load for them over their internet?

Usually yes, if the app is set to show them the camera's lower-resolution second stream rather than the full-resolution one. Every camera we install sends two streams: a sharp one that is recorded at home, and a lighter one meant for phones on slow connections. Live view from abroad depends on the upload speed of your home internet and on their connection, so we set the remote default to the lighter stream. Give each relative their own login with view-only rights, and if a clip matters, save it at home in full quality rather than relying on what they saw live.

Choosing equipment

Would a couple of dummy cameras on the front of the house do the same job for less money?

Not really. A fake camera can make someone hesitate, but it records nothing, so when the side mirror is snapped off or the box disappears there is no time, face or direction to go on. People who work a block regularly also learn which houses have cameras with no wire or no indicator. If budget is the issue, one real camera aimed well at the steps and one at the car will do more than four dummies. You can add more later on the same recorder.

Is a floodlight camera over the driveway better than a camera plus a separate light?

It depends on where the light and the camera each need to be. A floodlight camera is one unit in one spot, which is tidy, but the best place for a camera to see faces in a driveway is often not the best place to throw light, and a floodlight right beside the lens can glare on rain and snow. On most Washingtonville driveways we put the camera at the back of the house looking toward the street and the light where it lights the car and the path, sometimes in the same area, sometimes not. Where one spot does both jobs well, a combined unit is fine.

Can someone knock out wireless cameras with a jammer before breaking in?

Wireless cameras depend on a radio link to the router, and radio links can be disrupted, deliberately or by ordinary interference. A camera that loses its link stops sending video until the link comes back, unless it also records to a card inside itself. Wired cameras do not use radio at all, so they keep recording to the recorder whatever happens to the Wi-Fi. That is one of the reasons we wire the cameras that cover doors and cars. Where a wireless camera is the only option, pick one that records locally to a memory card as well.

The Rules We Work Within

New York Law and Washingtonville Cameras, in Plain English

Cameras on a house raise the same legal questions as cameras on a large building, with the neighbors closer. This is not legal advice; for a specific dispute, talk to a lawyer. These are the rules we install around.

Licensing for installers, and the owner exemption

New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license for anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance. State regulations, at 19 NYCRR section 195.2(c), exempt a property owner installing a system they bought themselves on their own property. So a homeowner can mount their own cameras; a person charging others to do it should hold a license. Ask any installer for the number.

Permits

Low-voltage camera work on a house does not usually need a Department of Buildings permit, though some situations, such as work tied into a building's fire alarm or certain electrical work, do. We tell you on the quote if anything about your job needs one.

Landmarks

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras, and its staff can approve cameras that meet its rules on designated buildings. We found no LPC historic district in Wakefield, so cameras on ordinary houses here do not go through Landmarks. If your building is individually designated, tell us.

Neighbors' yards

Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a homeowner or tenant a civil claim against someone who films them in their back yard without consent, where the filming is meant to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten, as Nolo summarizes it. Security cameras aimed at your own doors and yard, with neighboring yards masked, stay well clear of that.

Sound

Recording conversations raises its own questions under New York law. A New York attorney answering a homeowner's question on Justia advised turning audio off on outdoor and garage cameras unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation. Two-way talk on a doorbell, where you are speaking with the visitor, is a different matter from a camera quietly recording people talking on the sidewalk. We leave continuous audio off on outdoor and shared-area cameras.

Private spaces

Recording people without consent where they expect privacy, such as bathrooms, bedrooms and changing areas, is against the law in New York. No camera of ours points into an apartment, a bedroom window or a restroom.

Landlords and tenants

The lease governs what a tenant can mount on a building and in shared spaces, and a Bronx public-housing tenant was once served an eviction notice that said her cameras breached her lease. Tenants should get written permission before putting anything on a building. Landlords should tell tenants in writing where cameras are and what they cover, and should not aim cameras to track one tenant.

"Installation costs a fixed amount per camera."

Cost guides and marketplaces like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national per-camera ranges. They are averages across houses with attics, garages and drywall, not 1920s brick two-families with tenants upstairs.

On these blocks, the time is in the cable route. A camera over a door with an open basement below is quick. A camera on a second-floor eave above an occupied apartment, or on a garage at the end of a concrete driveway, takes longer.

That is why our quotes are line by line after photos or a visit, and why the only fixed price we publish is the $225 first hour of a setup-only visit.

"The NYPD's cameras already cover the area."

Public cameras, where they exist, are aimed at public spaces and are reviewed by the police. They are not aimed at your steps or your driveway, and you cannot pull their footage.

In the incidents we found in and around this neighborhood, the footage that showed what happened came from private cameras: a store's door camera on East 236th Street, a bodega's interior cameras, a lounge's surveillance system, a camera on a residential block that recorded the sound of the shots.

If you want a record of what happens at your own door, it has to come from your own camera.

"Cameras on a house don't need a licensed installer."

Half true. New York exempts a property owner who installs a system they bought themselves on their own property, under 19 NYCRR section 195.2(c).

But anyone in the business of installing security systems for others needs a license under General Business Law Article 6-D. A summary that tells you anyone can install cameras for pay is wrong.

If someone is charging you to install cameras, ask for the license number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

"A landlord can put cameras anywhere in the building."

An owner can put cameras on shared spaces: the front walk, the street door, a shared hall, the driveway, the yard. That is not the same as anywhere.

Cameras do not belong inside an apartment, aimed through an apartment door, or into a tenant's windows, and New York law treats recording people in private spaces seriously. Leases and fairness also matter: a camera that seems aimed to track one tenant's comings and goings breeds disputes.

In a Washingtonville two- or three-family, the camera list, the views and the logins should be agreed and written down before installation. That is part of every quote we write for a shared house.

FAQ

Washingtonville Security Camera Installation FAQ

A supply house told me I needed an installer's license just to buy cameras for my own house. Is that right?
+

Not for an owner working on their own property. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D licenses people in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems, and state regulations at 19 NYCRR section 195.2(c) exempt a property owner installing a system they bought on their own property. Some stores still set their own sales policies. If you mount cameras yourself and want help with the recorder, phones and logins afterward, that is setup work we do on a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour on our Bronx rate.

We rent a space in our driveway to someone who parks there on weekdays. Should they be able to see the driveway camera?
+

It is reasonable to give them view-only access to that one camera, if you are comfortable with it. The recorder can give them a personal login that shows only the driveway view, with no access to the front door, the yard or any other camera, and no rights to delete clips or change settings. Put it in your written parking arrangement, and remove their login the day the arrangement ends. If you would rather not, they can ask you for a clip if something happens to their car.

We also own a house just across the line in Mount Vernon. Can you do both, and can we watch both in one app?
+

Yes. We work in the Bronx and in Westchester County. The Washingtonville house is quoted on our Bronx rate and the Mount Vernon house on our Westchester rate, each on its own written quote. If both systems use the same recorder brand, one app on your phone can list both houses side by side, each with its own cameras and logins, so tenants in one house never see the other.

On our 1920s brick front, do you drill into the brick itself or into the mortar joints?
+

For the anchors that hold a camera mount, we drill into sound brick, because old mortar can be soft and an anchor in a crumbling joint can work loose. For the cable hole, we often go through a mortar joint, which is easier to patch neatly and leaves the brick face untouched. Every hole is sleeved where needed and sealed. If the brick on your front is cracked or spalling where a camera would go, we move the mount rather than anchor into damaged masonry.

Where a short piece of camera cable has to show on our white window trim, can it be painted to match?
+

Usually yes. Most outdoor cable jackets take exterior paint, and a short exposed section along trim can be run in a slim cover that is painted with the trim. We keep exposed runs as short as possible by routing cable inside the house and coming out right behind the camera. Where a run has to cross the outside, we follow a corner, a downspout line or the edge of trim so it reads as part of the house rather than a wire across the brick.

Do we need a yearly maintenance plan, or do we just call when something goes wrong?
+

There is no required plan. The system records without any monthly or yearly fee, and we set the recorder to send an alert if a camera goes offline or the drive reports a problem, so you hear about trouble without paying for a contract. If you want a check-up, a cleaning of the camera housings or adjustments, that is a setup-only visit at $225 for the first hour on our Bronx rate, then 30-minute blocks. Most houses go long stretches without needing one.

Should we show our next-door neighbors what our cameras will see before they go up?
+

We recommend it, especially on blocks where houses sit a few feet apart. On the visit we can show you exactly what each planned camera will take in, and where we will mask a neighbor's window or yard. Sharing that with the neighbors beforehand answers the questions they would otherwise ask after a camera appears on the wall. Some neighbors ask to share a view of the driveway or the sidewalk; others just want to know their back yard is masked. Either way, the conversation is easier before the drill comes out.

If we decide a month after the install that a camera should point somewhere else, is moving it covered?
+

Readjusting a camera after the job is finished is a service visit, not warranty work, because the camera did what it was set to do. Before we leave, we walk every view with you, day and night, so changes happen then at no extra charge. If you later want a camera re-aimed, a mask redrawn or a view moved to a new spot, that is a setup-only visit at $225 for the first hour on our Bronx rate, and several adjustments can be done in the same visit.

The street tree in front of our house fills in every summer and blocks the front camera. What can be done?
+

Move or re-aim the camera rather than the tree. Mounting the camera lower, under the canopy, or at the other front corner of the house often clears the view of the steps and the curb. If the tree itself needs attention, that is a question for NYC Parks, since sidewalk trees belong to the city. We check views in late spring for exactly this reason, because a picture that is clear in March can be all leaves by June.

Is the camera's shape on the front of a brick two-family just a matter of looks, turret or bullet?
+

Mostly, but not entirely. A turret sits in a cup and can be aimed in almost any direction from a wall or eave, and its night light sits outside the lens window, which avoids glare. A bullet is longer and more visible, which some owners want as a deterrent, and its built-in shade helps in rain. On most house fronts here we use turrets; for a long, narrow driveway view, a bullet with a slightly longer lens can be the better fit.

What should a tenant in a Washingtonville two-family do if they want a camera on their own door?
+

Ask the owner in writing first, because the lease usually governs anything mounted on the building, and a Bronx public-housing tenant was once served an eviction notice over cameras. Many owners say yes when the doorbell is wired properly instead of stuck on with adhesive, and some prefer to add it to the house recorder so it does not depend on a battery or a separate cloud plan. Inside their own apartment, a tenant can generally keep a camera aimed at their own space, as long as it does not look out a window into anyone else's home.
