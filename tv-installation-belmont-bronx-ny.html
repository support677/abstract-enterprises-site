The Neighborhood

Why Belmont Properties Need TV Installation

Belmont's housing was finished before the television was invented, and almost every TV job here starts from that fact. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood, bounded by Fordham Road to the north, Bronx Park to the east, East 181st Street to the south and Third Avenue to the west, as mostly five- and six-story walk-up and elevator buildings, the majority put up before 1939, many with Art Deco or Tudor detailing. The city's own lot records say the same thing in numbers. In the part of Community District 6 that falls in ZIP code 10458, the PLUTO database lists 662 lots with three or more apartments. Of those, 386 were built between 1901 and 1929 and another 114 in the 1930s; only 153 date from 1968 or later. Put plainly, three out of four apartment buildings in that slice of the district are pre-war, and the wall you want a 65-inch screen on was built for a picture frame and a calendar.

The shape of those buildings matters as much as their age. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes five-story walk-ups rising along Bathgate, Hughes and Crescent Avenues, and modest apartments over shops lining Arthur Avenue and East 187th Street. In its 2016 Great Streets profile of Arthur Avenue between East 184th and East 187th Streets, the American Planning Association went further and said that all buildings on that stretch are mixed use. A sales listing for 2476 Arthur Avenue, at the corner of East 189th Street, gives a typical example: a four-story walk-up with ten apartments above roughly 1,200 square feet of commercial space. The PLUTO records count 96 lots in that part of the district in the city's "walk-up with stores" class, carrying 1,641 apartments at an average of about four and a half floors. A TV going into one of those apartments is going up a staircase, onto a wall that may be shared with the next building, above a business that may be open while the drill runs.

Inside, the walls are not what most mounting instructions assume. Bracket boxes are written for drywall on wood studs. A long StreetEasy Talk thread about mounting a TV in a pre-war building describes something different: interior walls of plastered gypsum block, blocks about two to four inches thick with plaster on both faces, which tenants describe as cement-like. One poster could hang the TV but could not drill into it or hide the wires; another warned that cutting a channel for cables and digging out boxes is a major mess, "the price you pay for solid walls." Exterior and party walls in buildings of this age are usually brick. The StreetEasy blog's guide to hanging things on brick notes that mortar is easier to drill but in older buildings may be crumbling and too unstable for something as heavy as a TV, so the anchor belongs in the brick itself. Every one of those details decides which anchor, which drill and which line of our price list your job needs, and none of them can be seen from the paint.

Then there are the people who live behind those walls. Homes.com notes that many owners of Belmont's small multi-family buildings live in one of the apartments and rent out the others, so the person deciding whether a tenant may drill is often the person living two floors up. Wikipedia adds that Belmont houses a large population of Fordham University students, in the university's off-campus housing and in private apartment buildings. The Fordham Ram's April 2025 off-campus housing guide found student housing concentrated in the triangle formed by Third Avenue, Crescent Avenue and Cambreleng Avenue, at an average of $900 to $1,200 a month, with landlords noting that students graduate and leave at the same time and that leases are passed from one group of students to the next. For a TV installer that produces a very particular kind of call: a renter who wants a big screen, a lease that may say nothing at all about brackets, and a move-out date that is already on the calendar.

The last piece is the avenue itself. Belmont is the Bronx's Little Italy, and Arthur Avenue is where it eats, shops and watches games. In its June 19, 2026 guide to watching the World Cup in the Bronx, the Bronx Times reported that Yop City Restaurant at 2324 Arthur Avenue was packed for Ivory Coast's first group-stage match and was hosting free watch parties for the next two games, and it listed specials at Tino's Delicatessen at 2410 Arthur Avenue, the Bronx Beer Hall inside the Arthur Avenue Retail Market, and Joe's Italian Deli at 685 East 187th Street, while warning that not every location was live-streaming games. On July 19, 2026, News 12 described the Bronx Beer Hall packed with Argentina and Spain fans for the final, with its co-founder saying the bar comped the entire tab. We have no information about how any of those businesses mounted their screens, and we do not claim them as customers. We mention them because they show what a restaurant or bar TV on Arthur Avenue is asked to do: hold a crowd's attention for ninety minutes and more, in a room that was a pushcart market or a tenement storefront long before it was a sports venue.

Gypsum block, the wall that feels like concrete

The partition between a Belmont living room and bedroom is very often the wall the StreetEasy Talk thread describes: blocks of gypsum about two to four inches thick, plastered on both faces. It is the reason so many tenants say their walls are "concrete." It is not concrete, and that matters. Gypsum block is softer than concrete and much softer than brick, so a hammer drill at full force can blow out the back of the hole or shatter the block around it, and a plastic expansion plug from a bracket box has very little to grip. Done properly it holds a TV well. We drill with the hammer action off or set low, step the hole up in size, look at the dust that comes out, which is white and chalky in block and red or brown in brick, and set anchors made for hollow and friable masonry, sized to the bracket and the weight of the screen. Where a partition is thin or has already been drilled full of old holes, we spread the load with a wider bracket plate or move the screen to a stronger wall, and we tell you before we start which it will be.

Brick, and why we stay out of the mortar

Brick walls are common in Belmont apartments, especially the long side walls that the building shares with its neighbor and the outside wall with the windows. The brick itself is usually sound. The mortar between the bricks is a different story in a building a century old. The StreetEasy blog puts it simply: mortar is the easier place to drill, but in older buildings it may be crumbling and too unstable for something heavy like a TV, and the wall can crumble around the hole and fail to hold the anchor. We drill a test hole, look at the dust and feel how the bit cuts, then lay out the bracket so its anchors land in the face of whole bricks wherever the bracket's slots allow. That sometimes means the screen sits an inch to one side of where you first pointed, and we show you the marks before we commit. On our price list a brick or stone wall adds $65 to the base mount.

Drywall in the gut rehabs and newer buildings

Belmont also has newer interiors. The Alpert Group describes Belartes Court, at 1892–94 Arthur Avenue and 1899 Belmont Avenue, as a 73-unit, three-building, five-story elevator gut rehab, and the Norwood News reported in August 2017 on new student buildings in Belmont from the developer AB Capstone, including Artu Viale on Arthur Avenue and a residence on Lorillard Place. Renovated apartments like those are more likely to have drywall, which is the simplest wall we mount on and the base line of the price list at $209.99. The catch is what is behind it. Gut rehabs often use metal studs, which carry a TV only with fixings made for them, and drywall is sometimes laid straight over the old block or brick, which means longer fixings to reach something solid. We scan, test and choose the fixing for what we find.

What a test hole tells us

All of this comes down to one small hole. Before any bracket goes up, we drill a single narrow hole in a spot the bracket plate will cover, and we read three things from it: how the bit cuts, what color the dust is, and what the bit meets at the back. Soft white dust and a quick bite mean gypsum. Gritty red dust and steady resistance mean brick. A sudden give after half an inch means a hollow behind the surface, whether lath, a furred-out wall or drywall over a gap. That hole costs nothing extra and is invisible once the TV is up, and it is the single most important thing we do on a Belmont job.

Renting in Belmont

Third Avenue to Crescent Avenue: Getting a TV Up When Your Name Is Not on the Deed

A large share of Belmont TV calls come from people who rent, and many of them are students. The Fordham Ram's map of off-campus housing placed most student apartments in the triangle between Third Avenue, Crescent Avenue and Cambreleng Avenue, south of the Rose Hill campus across Fordham Road, and named six realty companies working there: Belmont Realty, LSANY, Fordham Realty, Rhamco, NV Properties and Bluesky. One unit it described came with WiFi, water, furniture and biweekly cleaning included. Landlords told the paper that students graduate and leave at the same time and that leases are often passed between students. Not every renter in Belmont is a student, of course, and plenty of families have lived in the same walk-up for decades. But the renter's question is always the same: can I have the TV on the wall, and what happens to the wall when I leave?

Start with the lease, then ask in writing

Brick Underground's advice to New York renters is a good starting point. Look at your lease's alteration clause first; a ban on hanging things that is not in the lease would not be enforceable, but a landlord is within their rights to ban it if it is. Even where the lease is silent, ask permission before installing something that makes bigger holes, and the article names TV brackets specifically. A nail-sized hole is generally treated as normal, but larger holes count as damage, and a landlord can deduct for damage beyond normal wear and tear from the security deposit, with an itemized list and the rest of the deposit returned within two weeks of you moving out. In a Belmont building where the owner lives on the top floor, a short text with a photo of the wall and the bracket usually settles it. We are happy to talk to the owner directly, or to send a description of the holes the bracket will leave, before you book.

The no-holes route for furnished and short-stay apartments

If the answer is no, or you would simply rather not argue about the deposit in May, the TV can stand on its own feet or a console and look nearly as tidy as a wall mount. Placing a TV on its stand is $137.99 at the Bronx rate. We assemble the stand or feet, set the screen at a sensible height, connect the cable box, streaming device and console, and gather every cord into the console or behind it with straps so nothing trails across a rented floor. A soundbar set on the console in front of the screen is $79. In a furnished apartment, we work with the furniture you have and do not move the landlord's pieces without your say-so.

If the bracket is allowed, keep the exit simple

When the landlord agrees to a bracket, we plan the job around the day you move out. That means a flat or tilting bracket in a single, sensible position rather than an arm that needs six heavy anchors, cables run under a paintable cord cover at $59 rather than cut into the block, and a note of exactly how many holes were made and where. The StreetEasy Talk thread makes the same point from the other side: capping the wires with a surface cover needs only two small holes. When you leave, the bracket comes off, the anchor holes are filled and painted, and the cover's screw holes are tiny. We can come back to take the TV down for you, which is $85 on the list. Leases that pass from one student to the next are a separate question: whether a bracket stays for the next tenant is a matter between you, them and the landlord, and we are glad to leave it in place or remove it, whichever the landlord prefers.

Most of the time lost on a Belmont TV job is lost on the way in: a van circling for a spot on a weekend when Arthur Avenue is closed to traffic, a 75-inch box that will not turn at the third-floor landing, or a store downstairs that opens just as the hammer drill starts. Measure from the seat, not the ceiling

Many Belmont apartments have generous ceiling heights and long, narrow rooms, and the instinct is to hang the TV high to "use the wall." The right height depends on where you sit. Sit on the sofa you actually use, look straight ahead, and have someone measure from the floor to your eyes. In most rooms the middle of the screen should sit near that height or a little above it. Then measure from the sofa to the wall. In a narrow front room, a very large screen at the end of a short wall can end up uncomfortably close; in a long railroad-style room, a modest screen can look small from the far end. Send us both measurements and we will tell you what works in rooms that shape.

Rules and practical limits for business screens

  • New outlets need a licensed electrician. The NYC Department of Buildings says no installation or modification of electrical systems may be done without a permit, and that general electrical work is performed by a NYC Licensed Master Electrician with a permit and inspection. A new receptacle high on the wall behind a bar screen is that kind of work. Our work is the mount and the low-voltage side, and our quote says which part needs an electrician.
  • Cords do not go inside the wall. A TV's own power cord pushed through a wall is the shortcut StreetEasy contributors warn against; where power has to be hidden, we use a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, or keep the cord on the surface under a cover.
  • Exits stay visible. A screen must never cover an exit sign or narrow a path to an exit. We check the sight lines to exit signs before any bracket goes up in a dining room or bar.
  • Insurance papers. Landlords of commercial space often ask contractors for a certificate of insurance before work begins. Send the request with the booking so it is ready on the day.
  • Quiet for the neighbors. In a mixed-use building the tenants upstairs hear the drill as clearly as the staff. We drill early in the day rather than late at night, and keep the noisy part short.

Field Notes: Reading the Dust on a Fourth-Floor Walk-Up Wall

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's apartment unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow when the job is the kind Belmont is full of: a fourth- or fifth-floor walk-up, a partition the tenant calls concrete, and a new TV leaning against the radiator.

The box stays sealed while we look. We sit on the sofa and look at the wall with you, because the right height is about your eyes. We knock along the wall and listen for the dull note of block against the flat, dead note of brick. We scan for wiring and pipes, paying attention to the corner where the radiator riser climbs. Then we drill one narrow hole where the bracket plate will hide it, hammer action off, and catch the dust on a strip of tape. White and chalky means gypsum block, so the anchors change and the drill stays gentle. Red means brick, so we look for whole bricks to land in and stay clear of soft joints. We tape the outline of the screen on the wall, step back to the doorway, and only after you have looked and agreed does the first real anchor go in.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Belmont: Questions Answered

Block and brick walls

The plastic plugs from our bracket box just spin in the living room wall of our Hughes Avenue walk-up. What does that tell you?

It usually tells us the wall is gypsum block or old plaster rather than drywall or brick, and that the plugs were the wrong fixing for it. Plastic expansion plugs grip by pressing against the sides of a tight hole. In soft, crumbly block the hole widens as you drill, especially with the hammer action on, and the plug has nothing firm to press against, so it turns with the screw. Do not keep tightening; that only enlarges the hole. We test the wall, move the bracket a little if the first holes are ruined, and use anchors made for friable masonry, sized for the screen's weight. The old holes are filled and hidden behind the bracket plate where possible.

A previous tenant left the wall in our Bathgate Avenue apartment full of old anchor holes. Can the new bracket go in the same spot?

Sometimes, but rarely in exactly the same holes. Old anchor holes in block or plaster are usually wider and weaker than they look, and a new anchor set in one can pull out under load. We look at the pattern of the old holes, the new TV's mounting pattern and the bracket's slots, and in most cases shift the bracket an inch or two up, down or sideways so every new anchor lands in sound material. If the area is badly broken up, a wider bracket plate or a different wall is the safer choice. We tell you which before drilling, and the old holes are filled and painted where the screen will not cover them.

The only long wall in our apartment is the one we share with the building next door. Is it all right to drill into a party wall?

In most Belmont buildings the party wall is brick, and fixing a TV bracket into its face with masonry anchors is a normal job; the anchors go in a couple of inches, nowhere near the far side of a wall that is usually several bricks thick. What we watch for is the mortar, which in a building this age may be soft, and anything running along the wall, such as a radiator pipe or an old cable. We test the wall first, set the anchors in whole bricks, and keep the drilling short, because in a shared wall the neighbors hear it too. On our price list a brick wall adds $65 to the base mount.

Renting near Fordham

Four of us share a flat near Fordham and the lease passes to new students in June. Who should end up owning the bracket?

That is a question for you, the incoming tenants and the landlord, and it is worth settling before the bracket goes up. Brick Underground's advice to New York renters is to check the lease's alteration clause and ask permission before installing a TV bracket, since larger holes can be treated as damage against the deposit. If the landlord is happy for a bracket to stay for the next group, we can mount one that suits a range of screen sizes and leave a note of how it was fixed. If the landlord wants the wall back as it was, we can return before you move out to take the TV down and remove the bracket, and the holes can be filled and painted.

Our realty company furnished the apartment with a TV console. Can it carry the 65-inch TV we just bought?

Often, but not always. A console has to be wider than the TV's feet, deep enough that the screen cannot be knocked forward, and strong enough for the weight on its top. Many TVs now have feet near the outer edges, which means a narrow console that suited the old set may be too short for the new one. We check the TV's foot spacing and weight against the console, and if it is marginal we suggest a center stand if the model offers one, or an anti-tip strap fixed to the furniture. Setting a TV on its stand is $137.99 on our Bronx list, and nothing is drilled into the landlord's walls.

Can you show my landlord exactly what the bracket will do to the wall before I commit?

Yes, and it often makes the conversation easy. Send us the TV model and a photo of the wall, and we will tell you which bracket we would use, how many anchors go into the wall, roughly how far apart they sit, and whether the cables would run under a surface cover or into a console. You can forward that to the owner or the realty office. On the day, if the landlord lives in the building, the technician can show them the marked-up position before drilling. We will not drill a rented wall until you tell us the owner has agreed.

Stairs and streets

Sometimes it will and sometimes it will not, and a photo of the tightest landing usually tells us. A 75-inch box is long, and large panels must be carried upright, never laid flat or flexed, so the question is whether the box can be tipped through the turn while staying upright. Where it cannot, two technicians take the set out of its box in the lobby, protect the panel between blankets and carry it up by hand, which takes longer. It can. Above the stores and in the restaurants

Usually off to one side of the counter, angled toward the line, rather than above the menu board where it competes for the same eye level. We plan the screens and their cables with that in mind, so each screen can be fed and heard the way you want. The simplest version is a soundbar under the screens in the bar area, with the dining-room screens muted, which you can switch from the remotes. Owners and rebuilt apartments

I own a small building on Belmont Avenue and two apartments are empty between tenants. Can screens go up in both on the same visit?

Yes. Doing both while the units are empty is easier for everyone: there is no furniture to move, no tenant to schedule, and we can test each wall at our own pace. Our apartment is in a gut-renovated elevator building off Arthur Avenue and the scanner keeps finding metal. Will those walls hold a 65-inch TV?

They can, with the right fixings. Gut rehabs often frame new walls with light-gauge metal studs, which is the metal your scanner is finding. A metal stud will not hold a lag bolt the way a wood stud does, so we use fixings designed for metal studs or toggles rated for the load, spread across more than one stud where the bracket allows. Some rebuilt walls are also drywall laid over the old block or brick, in which case we drill through to the masonry behind. We test to find out which you have before choosing, and the quote says so.

The building's cable comes in through our front window frame, but we want the TV on the opposite wall. How does the signal get there?

There are a few ways, and the right one depends on the room and what you watch. If most viewing is streaming, a WiFi streaming device at the TV may make the cable unnecessary at that wall, and the cable box can stay by the window with the router. If you need the cable box at the TV, the line can run along the baseboard under a paintable cover to the far wall. We avoid running new cable through partitions in a rented apartment unless the owner agrees. We look at where the line enters and suggest the tidiest route before we start.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Belmont

Quick online answers about TV mounting are mostly written for a suburban house with drywall on wood studs. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor, Thumbtack and TaskRabbit publish cost guides and tasker pages that feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, but they are not a plan for a gypsum partition on the fifth floor of a Belmont walk-up. These are the claims we see most often, set against the sources on this page and our own practice.

"Old New York walls are concrete, so use concrete anchors and a hammer drill."

Tenants call their walls concrete because a nail bends and a screw will not bite. In many pre-war buildings the partition is something else. Contributors to a StreetEasy Talk thread about mounting a TV in a pre-war building describe plastered gypsum block, blocks two to four inches thick with plaster on both faces.

Gypsum block is much softer than concrete. A hammer drill at full force can blow the back off the hole or shatter the block around it, and a concrete wedge anchor set in a crumbling hole has little to grip. That is how a bracket that seemed solid on the day starts to lean a month later.

In Belmont, where the PLUTO records date 500 of 662 apartment-building lots in the ZIP 10458 part of the district to before 1940, we treat every partition as unknown until the test hole shows the dust. Then we pick the drill setting and the anchor for what is actually there.

"Drill into the mortar joint; it's softer and easier."

It is easier to drill, which is why the advice spreads. The StreetEasy blog's guide to brick walls says the same thing and then adds the warning: in older buildings mortar may be crumbling and too unstable for heavy items like a TV, and the wall can crumble around the hole.

Belmont's brick party walls and outside walls are mostly a century old or close to it. Mortar of that age can rub away under a fingernail, and an anchor set in it is only as strong as the weakest grain of sand around it.

We lay out the bracket so its anchors land in the face of whole bricks, test the brick before loading it, and move the screen an inch if that is what it takes to stay out of the joints. We show you the marks before we drill.

"Just feed the TV's power cord through the wall between two holes."

This is the most common shortcut in online answers and the one we refuse. In the StreetEasy Talk thread on hiding wires in a pre-war building, contributors point out that burying speaker wire and HDMI is acceptable but that power cords should not be channeled into the wall.

A TV's own cord was never meant to live inside a wall, where it can be pinched or crushed and heat up out of sight. In a gypsum-block wall there is often no cavity to feed it through anyway, so the shortcut becomes a trench chiseled into the block and plastered over.

When power has to be hidden, we use a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list, where the wall has a cavity and the owner agrees. Where it does not, a paintable cord cover at $59 keeps the cord on the surface, neat and safe, and easy to remove at move-out.

"TV mounting costs about $100 to $130."

That figure is real; it is the national average Thumbtack quotes on its Bronx TV mounting page. The same page lists examples from $40 for a bare mount with no bracket or cable work to well over $300 for a mount with hidden wires and speakers, and notes one company charging an extra $99 for brick. TaskRabbit's Bronx page starts taskers at $45 an hour with two-hour minimums at some.

An average across the country tells you little about a 65-inch screen on a gypsum partition four floors up, with the cables to be hidden and a soundbar underneath. The wall, the stairs and the extras move the total far more than the borough does.

Our Bronx list starts at $209.99 for the base wall mount, adds $45 for plaster or gypsum block and $65 for brick, and lists each extra separately, so you can add up your own job before calling. We never quote a number that is not on that list.

"Renters can mount a TV as long as they patch the holes."

Patching helps, but it is not the whole rule. Brick Underground's guidance for New York renters is to check the alteration clause in the lease first, and to ask permission before installing items that cause bigger holes, naming TV brackets specifically.

The same article notes that larger holes can count as damage beyond normal wear and tear, which a landlord can deduct from the deposit with an itemized list. In a Belmont building where the owner lives upstairs, or a student flat run by a realty office, asking first is usually quick and saves an argument in June.

Online answers blur both. Wikipedia places Belmont between Fordham Road, Bronx Park, East 181st Street and Third Avenue, in Bronx Community Board 6, with Fordham University's Rose Hill campus across Fordham Road to the north. "A handyman can add an outlet behind the TV while he's there."

Many answers suggest moving an outlet up the wall as part of a mount. In New York City that is electrical work. The Department of Buildings states that no installation or modification of electrical systems may be done without a permit, with general electrical work performed by a NYC Licensed Master Electrician, and even minor replacements and repairs require a licensed electrician and a permit.

In a Belmont walk-up, with wiring that may date from several eras of renovation, that rule protects the whole building, not just your apartment.

Our Installation Process

Every Belmont TV job follows the same eight steps, whether it is a 43-inch set in a student bedroom or a row of screens in a dining room on Arthur Avenue. In buildings this old, the order matters: a wall read too quickly is the usual reason a bracket leans or a block partition cracks.

1. Send the photos and the floor

A photo of the wall, the TV model and size, the street and cross street, and which floor you are on, so we can match the job to the Bronx price list before you book.

2. Settle permission and arrival

Confirm the owner has agreed to drilling, check for weekend closures on Arthur Avenue or East 188th Street, and pick a time that suits the store below.

3. Test the wall

Scan, tap and drill one small test hole behind where the bracket plate will sit, and read the dust to tell gypsum block, plaster, brick and drywall apart.

Anchors chosen for the material we found, set in brick faces rather than soft mortar, with the bracket levelled to true rather than to an old floor.

A code-compliant in-wall kit where the wall and owner allow it, a paintable cord cover where they do not, or everything gathered into the console.

Two people lift the TV onto the bracket, then the soundbar is placed or mounted and the streaming devices, cable box and console are connected.

8. Test, tidy and hand over

Every input tested from the seats, the remotes shown to you, the dust swept up, and a note left of the bracket and fixings for the next move.
