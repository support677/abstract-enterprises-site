Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Greenpoint's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly all of our camera work in Greenpoint. The camera may be identical, but the building decides where the cable runs, where the recorder lives and who has to say yes to the repair.

Walk-ups and railroad flats

Brick buildings with a street door, a stair hall and two railroad apartments per floor, each with a door into the kitchen and another into the front room. The cameras watch the street door, the mailboxes, the stairs and the cellar, and the recorder is usually somewhere downstairs.

Flathouses with shops below

Buildings on Franklin Street, Manhattan Avenue and Greenpoint Avenue with stores at street level and flats above, the pattern the LPC report traces back to Franklin Street's early 1850s buildings. Often there are two systems in one building: the shop's and the landlord's.

Sided frame houses

Wood houses on Milton, Noble and nearby streets that went up for shipyard workers, many now covered in asphalt or aluminum siding. The camera mounts and cable entries on these houses are the parts that fail.

Brick rowhouses in the historic district

Italianate and neo-Grec brick houses from the 1870s to the 1890s, many with original double wooden doors. Here the question is less how to fix the camera than where it may be fixed to the front.

Converted factories and studio buildings

Pencil-factory, warehouse and manufacturing buildings turned into condos, studios and workshops, from the Eberhard Faber blocks to the creek. Long runs, exposed conduit, shared freight doors and several systems under one roof.

New condos and waterfront towers

Small condo buildings on former garage lots and towers at Greenpoint Landing, with cameras wired into the building's network and often installed by an integrator who has since left.

Walk-ups and railroad flats: the long hall and the shared door

A Greenpoint walk-up gives a camera system a hard job in a small space. The street door is shared by every apartment, the vestibule is narrow, and the stair hall runs straight up with apartment doors on each landing. In a railroad layout, a resident on r/Greenpoint described it this way: "Usually a door into the kitchen and one into the front bedroom. 2 apartments on each floor with a hallway in the middle." A hallway camera at the top of the stairs has to cover two or four doors with nothing to work with but the light from a ceiling fixture. The usual repairs are a camera powered from the fixture box on a timer that goes dark when the bulb is switched off, a cable stapled up the stair stringer and broken where a painter or floor crew pulled it, and a cellar recorder running hard on a dusty shelf.

We start at the recorder in the cellar, test each run up the building, and only then go to the cameras on the landings. Where a hallway camera depends on fixture power, we move it to its own supply or to PoE from the recorder, so a tenant flipping a switch or a dead bulb no longer blinds it. Where a camera has been knocked, tilted or covered, we look at whether it can sit higher, deeper in the corner or behind a small vandal-resistant dome, so it sees faces on the stairs rather than the tops of heads.

Flathouses with shops below: two owners, one cellar

The LPC's 1982 report describes Franklin Street as the area's first commercial center, with "shops on the ground floor and flats above," and describes flathouses "originally designed with two stores on the ground floor and apartments for two families on each of the three upper stories." Many Greenpoint buildings still work that way, and their cameras often fall into two systems. The shop put in its own cameras and recorder for the register, the door and the stockroom, and the landlord put in a separate set for the street door, the hallways and the cellar. Both recorders end up in the same cellar or the same back room, on the same outlet strip, sometimes sharing one internet line.

The repair starts by sorting out which camera belongs to which system and who gets to approve the price. We label every run at both recorders, confirm which business or person owns each login, and put each recorder on its own protected power so a storekeeper unplugging something in the back office doesn't take out the building's hallway cameras.

Sided frame houses on Milton and Noble streets

The LPC report notes that "many of the frame houses on Milton Street and Noble Street were erected to house the workers employed in the shipyards," and that some were built by shipwrights and ship carpenters. Decades later many of them were "covered with asphalt siding" or "sheathed in aluminum siding." For a camera, siding is a soft skin over hard wood. A bracket screwed only into siding works loose in the wind. Each loose cycle widens the holes, water gets behind the siding, and the camera ends up drooping toward the sidewalk with its connector soaked. Asphalt siding cracks when it is drilled cold, and aluminum siding dents and passes vibration along to the camera.

The fix is to land the camera on a proper mounting block that bears on the wood frame behind the siding, seal the cable entry so it sheds water, and leave a drip loop. That way the cable carries rain down and away, not back into the hole. Where an old camera left a cluster of holes, the new block covers them. Inside, cables on these houses tend to run through closets and floor openings rather than down a cellar wall, so we trace them room by room before cutting anything.

Brick rowhouses in the historic district: where the camera may go

Most of the historic district's houses, the LPC report says, "are brick, dating from between 1870 and 1892, and are designed in the Italianate or neo-Grec styles," and the report notes again and again where original double wooden doors survive. The Commission's Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety equipment sets the rules: cameras "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork" and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." It also says that "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting" of safety equipment needs no permit. In practice, replacing a dead camera with a similar one in the same spot is ordinary repair, while moving it onto a carved lintel or adding a long, visible conduit run is not.

So on a district rowhouse we try to keep the repair inside the existing footprint: the same mortar joint, the same cable entry, a camera of similar size, painted to blend where that helps. If the old camera was fixed somewhere the guidebook now rules out, we explain the choice before any new holes go in: a quick move to a plain masonry joint, or a camera inside the vestibule or parlor window instead.

Converted factories and studio buildings: long runs, shared doors

Greenpoint's industrial buildings now hold condos, artists' studios, workshops and small manufacturers. The Eberhard Faber Pencil Company Historic District, designated in 2007, covers factory buildings on Greenpoint Avenue and Kent and West streets dating from the 1880s to the 1920s. On Manhattan Avenue near the creek, the Greenpoint Manufacturing and Design Center's complex at 1155–1205 Manhattan Avenue has 76 small business and artisan tenants and 360 employees, by its own count. In buildings like these the camera runs are long, they often travel in conduit along brick or timber, and one freight door or loading dock is shared by many tenants, each of whom may have put up a camera of their own.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Working a Greenpoint Camera Repair From the Cellar Up

These notes describe the routine our technicians follow in Greenpoint walk-ups, frame houses and shops. It is the order of work, not a story about one job.

Copy first. If anyone needs video from a recent night, that comes off the recorder before any cable is moved. The technician notes the recorder's clock against real time, exports the clip with its original timestamps, and only then begins testing. A flood-damaged or failing drive gets even more care, because it may give up one read and no more.

Look at the cellar before the cameras. In most Greenpoint buildings the recorder, the power supply, the PoE switch and the internet box sit together in the cellar or a closet. The technician checks the outlet and any battery backup, looks for the high-water mark on the walls and boxes, notes how far each piece sits off the floor, and reads the recorder's log for when each camera dropped out. A run of cameras that all failed at the same minute points to power. Cameras that faded one at a time over weeks point to drives, connectors or water.

Follow every run up the building. From the cellar, each cable is traced through the walls, risers, stair halls and closets to its camera, and labeled at both ends. The fault is often where a run passes a door frame, a radiator pipe or a newly patched wall.

Test at the far end. At each dark or weak camera, power and signal are measured at the camera itself. A camera that tests dead with good power and good signal is replaced. A camera that comes back with a new connector is kept. Outside, the technician checks the mount and the cable entry. On sided frame houses and brick fronts that is where water gets in.

Price before parts, then leave a record. You hear the cause and get a written price before any part is replaced. After the repair, every camera is checked on playback, not just live view. Health alerts are switched on, so you hear about the next failure the day it happens, not the day someone asks for footage. The recorder is labeled, and the logins go to whoever owns the system.

Fault by Fault

Brownouts, Wet Cellars, Siding, Long Halls and Lost Logins: Why Greenpoint Camera Systems Fail

Five causes explain most of the camera failures we see in Greenpoint.

Brownouts: when the voltage drops but the lights stay on

Con Edison's July 3, 2026 notice of an 8 percent voltage reduction in Greenpoint and the neighborhoods around it is the kind of event most owners never notice, because the lights only dim a little. Camera power supplies notice. A cheap plug-in adapter or an aging PoE switch running near its limit can drop below what a camera needs, and the camera reboots, comes back, and reboots again. Recorders can restart in the middle of writing to the drive. One r/Greenpoint resident woke to find "some of my lights not working and the fridge off," which is what a partial outage looks like from inside an apartment. In a building like that, the cameras on one circuit stay up while the recorder on another goes dark.

The repair is to test every supply under load, replace the weak ones, and size a battery backup to carry the recorder and the switch through the short sags that cause most of the trouble. A backup sized for a computer often can't carry a 16-channel recorder and an eight-port PoE switch together for more than a few minutes, and an undersized one is often what lets the recorder drop.

Wet cellars: damage that starts below the recorder

The flooding near Meeker and Manhattan avenues in October 2025, and the city's April 2026 warning to basement residents in Greenpoint, matter for cameras because so many recorders live in cellars. Water rarely reaches the recorder itself. It soaks the outlet strip on the floor, wicks into the bottom of a cable bundle, or leaves the air in the cellar damp for weeks. Then connectors corrode, drives wear early and power supplies short. A recorder that "came back fine" after a wet week can fail a month later.

After any water, we check the connectors down there, the power strip and every supply, and read the drive's health rather than trusting that the recorder turns on. Where the cellar is likely to take water again, moving the recorder, the switch and the backup up onto a wall shelf or a higher floor is often the most valuable part of the repair.

Siding and facades: mounts that loosen and let water in

Frame houses with asphalt or aluminum siding, brick fronts that have been repointed, and storefronts with a new sign or awning all have the same weakness: the camera's mount. A bracket that bites only into siding works loose. A cable entry drilled straight in carries water straight in. A facade crew working on the front of the building may unplug a camera, paint over its dome or leave its cable hanging, and nobody notices until the next time someone wants footage.

Most of these repairs cost less than a new camera. We re-seat the camera on a proper block, re-terminate the connector, reseal the entry and re-aim the view. We replace the camera only when water has got inside the housing or the lens has been damaged.

Long halls and narrow shops: cameras asked to see too far

Railroad-flat hallways, deep narrow storefronts on Manhattan Avenue and long factory corridors all ask a camera to see a face at the far end of a long, poorly lit space. A wide-angle camera at one end will show that someone walked down the hall, but not who.

Where the existing camera is a varifocal model, a lens and focus adjustment often fixes it. Where it is not, a second camera at the far end is usually cheaper and better than a costly replacement at the near end.

Lost logins: the cameras work, but nobody can get in

Turnover is constant in a neighborhood where most people rent and shops change hands. Recorders end up registered to a tenant who moved, a shop owner who sold, a super who retired or an integrator who left after the building was finished. The system may be recording perfectly, but if no one can log in, no one can pull a clip, and in practice the system is as good as broken.

We recover access through the manufacturer's reset routes that keep recordings and settings wherever the recorder allows. We change default and shared passwords, set up separate logins for owners and for people who only need to watch, and write down where the admin details are kept, so the next change of tenant or super doesn't lock everyone out again.

Back doors and the alley: lessons from the Franklin Street burglaries

The September 2026 burglaries followed a familiar pattern. Several businesses were hit the same day, the doors that gave way were at the back, and in one case the way in was a hatch, not a door. A restaurant's back door often has one camera, put up when the place opened and not looked at since. It may sit above the door, aimed straight down so it only sees the top of a head, or face the alley under one bulb that burned out last winter. It may also record to a recorder in the office the burglar walks straight into.

For a restaurant or bar on Franklin Street or Greenpoint Avenue, a repair visit covers four things. The back-door camera has to see a face, not a scalp, at the distance where someone will stand with a tool. Night mode has to work with the light that is actually there after closing. The recorder has to sit somewhere less obvious than the manager's desk, or be locked away. And the owner's phone has to get an alert when the back door camera sees a person after hours.

Shops that keep getting asked for the street view

The r/Greenpoint posts asking for footage after a hit and run or a crash usually end up at a shop, a bodega or a café. If your shop is often asked, a repair can set the front camera up for it. You still decide whether to hand over any clip. Brick Underground has noted that landlords aren't obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and the same caution applies to shops.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and its rules extend that to closed-circuit television systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. An owner who works on their own system, or has an employee do it, doesn't need a license. A company you hire does. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras where people work. New York Labor Law section 203-c bars video recording in employee restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms, and state civil rights law requires private employers to give employees notice of electronic monitoring. When a repair re-aims or adds a view of a work area, we flag anything that points into a space where it shouldn't.

Landmarked storefronts. Inside the Greenpoint Historic District, the LPC guidebook keeps cameras off cast iron and decorative surfaces. That affects a camera mounted on an old storefront pilaster or cornice. Ordinary repair of an existing camera needs no permit, but replacing one in a new spot may.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Greenpoint: Questions Answered

These are situations Greenpoint shop owners, landlords, condo boards and homeowners bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Each answer is the short version of what the technician will look at.

Shops and restaurants on the avenues

Two people set a ladder against the side of our Nassau Avenue building at 1:30 in the morning. Our camera only shows the sidewalk. Can it be set to watch the wall above the shop as well?

Often, yes, without a new camera. Many storefront cameras are aimed down at the door because that is where the installer stood. If yours is a varifocal model on an adjustable mount, it can usually be tilted and zoomed to take in the sidewalk and the first few feet of wall above the sign. If it is a fixed wide lens, a small second camera tucked under the cornice or the sign box, looking along the wall, covers the facade without losing the door. The surveillance video of the Invader mosaic theft on Nassau Avenue in 2026 is a reminder that what happens on a wall at night is often what the camera was never pointed at.

Our shop camera recorded the crash at the corner, but the cars are only a few pixels tall. Would a different lens setting on the same camera have shown the plate?

Possibly, but a single camera can rarely do both jobs. A wide view of a whole corner like Morgan and Nassau shows what happened. A narrow view of one lane shows who did it. If your camera is varifocal, we can narrow it to the curb and the near lane where cars stop, and leave the wide view to a second camera or a neighbor's. If it is fixed, we can tell you what lens would read a plate at that distance. Night plates need more than a lens: a faster shutter, careful exposure and often a camera chosen for that job.

Our clothing shop has one camera at the door and one at the register. After the break-in down the block, can the two we have be re-aimed so they follow someone from the door to the back?

Two cameras can usually cover more than they do now, but rarely the whole path. We start by walking the route a burglar would take, from the front door to the stockroom and back, and marking where each camera loses the person. Often the door camera can be swung to look into the shop rather than out at the street, and the register camera widened to catch the aisle toward the back. The four people in the 17 Kent Avenue burglary came in through the front door together, and covering a group like that well usually takes a third camera at the back looking forward.

The camera on our storefront hangs from a cast-iron pilaster inside the historic district. When it is replaced, can it go back in the same spot?

The Landmarks guidebook says cameras must not be attached through cast iron, so a new camera in that exact spot is the part to check first. Where the old camera was put up before the rule, or without approval, we usually suggest moving the replacement to a nearby mortar joint, plain masonry, the wood of the storefront frame or the inside of the display window. That keeps the repair simple and avoids a permit question. If the old bracket's holes are in the iron, we leave them alone and don't make new ones there.

Walk-ups, railroad flats and rentals

Every apartment in our walk-up has two doors on the hall, one into the kitchen and one into the front room. The hallway camera only catches one end. What is the usual fix?

In a railroad-flat hall one camera at the top of the stairs sees the doors nearest it well and the far ones badly, especially at night under a single ceiling light. The usual fix is to move the existing camera into the corner of the landing so it looks along the hall, not across it, and to switch it to its own power rather than the light fixture. In a longer hall, a second small camera at the far end looking back covers both doors of every apartment. We test it by having someone stand at each door while we watch the playback.

Bikes keep getting cut loose in our building's cellar bike room, and the camera there shows only the door. Can it be moved to cover the racks?

Yes, and that is usually where it should have been. A camera on the bike room door records who came in, but if it is too high or too wide it won't show who did the cutting. We look at whether the existing camera can move to a corner that sees both the door and the racks, or whether the room needs a second camera. Cellar bike rooms are often dark, so night mode and the room's light are part of the repair. In some buildings the bikes ended up in the cellar because the fire code keeps them out of the halls.

Our landlord says a new camera system can be charged to rent-stabilized tenants as a major capital improvement. Can a repair of the old cameras be charged that way too?

By the state's own rules, generally no. Homes and Community Renewal's fact sheet on major capital improvements says an MCI has to be a new installation, not a repair, and that MCIs are prohibited in buildings where 35 percent or fewer of the units are rent-regulated. It also requires a written order from the agency and gives tenants 60 days to respond. HCR's useful-life schedule lists "Television Security" at 10 years. We repair cameras for the owner and give an itemized price. Whether any of it can be passed on is a question for HCR, not for us.

The camera on our street door is in the intercom panel. Can its picture be recorded on the same recorder as our hallway cameras?

Often, yes. Many video door stations can send their camera picture to a network recorder, and some older ones can feed a coax recorder through an adapter. Where the station's maker supports it, we add the door camera as a channel on your recorder, so its footage shows in the same playback and export as the rest of the building. Where it doesn't, a small camera mounted just inside or above the door does the same job. The door camera is often the best placed in the building, since every visitor stands right in front of it.

Frame houses, lofts and condos

Our frame house on Noble Street still has its old asphalt siding. The camera keeps sagging toward the sidewalk. Is it the camera or the mount?

Almost always the mount. Asphalt siding is brittle and thin, and a bracket screwed only into it works loose until the camera points at the ground. Each time it loosens, water gets into the holes and behind the siding. We take the camera down, check its connector and seal for water, and remount it on a block that reaches the wood frame behind the siding, with a sealed cable entry and a drip loop. If the camera itself still tests good, it goes back up. Then we re-aim it and check the view on playback.

We live in a converted pencil-factory building, and the exposed conduit to our lobby camera cracked when a delivery cart hit it. Can it be replaced the same way?

Inside the building, usually yes: we cut out the damaged section, replace it with matching conduit and a new cable if the old one was crushed, and add a guard where carts keep hitting it. On the outside of a building in the Eberhard Faber Pencil Company Historic District, the LPC guidebook prefers conduit concealed through plain masonry and allows staff to approve only about ten inches of visible conduit. A like-for-like repair of what is already there is ordinary maintenance. Extending it or rerouting it across the facade may need the Commission's sign-off first.

Four of us own the units in a small condo, and the recorder sits in one owner's storage cage. Who should hold the admin login?

The condo, not one person. We set the recorder up with an admin account held by whoever the condo's bylaws or board name as responsible, usually the managing agent or the board president, plus separate view-only logins for each owner. That way the recorder in the cage still serves everyone if the owner who stores it moves out or stops answering. If the cage is locked, we can suggest moving the recorder to a common closet or a wall-mounted locked box, so whoever needs it can reach it without asking a neighbor for a key.

Power, water and the building's systems

During a partial outage some of our lights stayed on and others went out. The cameras on the upper floors kept working, but the recorder in the cellar went dark. Why?

Because they are on different circuits, and possibly on different legs of the building's supply. In a partial outage or a brownout one leg can drop while the other stays up, so whatever is plugged into the dead side, often the cellar outlet with the recorder and the switch, goes down while the cameras' own power upstairs keeps running. We check where every piece of the system is plugged in. Where it helps, we put the recorder, the switch and the camera power on one protected supply with a battery backup, so they all ride through together. Anything that needs a new outlet or circuit is a job for your electrician.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. In Greenpoint that ranges from coax cameras put up in walk-ups twenty years ago to network systems in new towers, with plenty of Wi-Fi cameras bought by tenants and shop owners in between.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog systems to newer HD-over-coax models, common in walk-ups and older shops. Most failures are at the connector or the power supply, not the camera.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected by one data cable from a PoE switch or recorder, standard in newer condos, towers and studios. Faults are usually a port, a cable end, an address conflict or a firmware change.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that stores the footage, usually in a cellar, a closet or a back office. Drive replacement, fan and dust problems, power faults, settings recovery and full replacement when the board has failed.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras whose zoom can be adjusted after mounting, useful in long halls and narrow shops. Many "blurry" calls are a lens that drifted or was knocked, fixed with a focus reset.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving and fisheye cameras on shop ceilings, studio floors and yards. We repair motors, presets, power and the settings that make them useful on playback.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Plug-in and battery cameras in apartment windows, back yards and cafés. In brick buildings their weak point is usually the Wi-Fi signal or the plug, not the camera.

Video door stations

Intercom panels with built-in cameras at street doors. We repair the camera side and, where the maker allows, connect it to the building's recorder.

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, often in a cellar beside the router. A tired or overloaded switch is a common cause of cameras dropping out together.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. We test them under load and size backups to carry the recorder and switch through the brownouts Greenpoint gets.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Greenpoint

These claims turn up in AI-written answers, cost-guide summaries and help pages when people search for camera repair. Each has some truth in it, and each misses something about how cameras actually fail on Greenpoint buildings.

"Wi-Fi cameras don't work in old brick buildings, so the only fix is rewiring"

Vendor pages often say that thick masonry walls make Wi-Fi cameras unreliable and that wired cameras are the only serious option in older New York buildings.

Brick does weaken Wi-Fi, and a walk-up's party walls and floors are real obstacles. But most Wi-Fi camera dropouts we see in Greenpoint come down to something smaller: a router in a back bedroom of a railroad flat when the camera is in the front window or a camera stuck on a crowded 2.4 GHz band.

So the repair starts by measuring the signal where the camera sits, and only then decides. Sometimes moving the router or adding a wired access point fixes it. Sometimes the right answer is a wired camera on a cable already in the wall. A full rewire is the last answer, not the first.

"Most camera repairs cost a hundred dollars or two"

National cost guides and quick answers often give low repair figures, built on one or two consumer cameras on a suburban house. HomeAdvisor, by contrast, puts the average Brooklyn surveillance camera install or repair job at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145. That figure mixes new systems in with repairs.

Neither number describes a Greenpoint repair. On our Brooklyn list, a connector or PoE port fix is $150 to $350, a single camera replaced on a working system is $250 to $600, a drive swap is $200 to $450, and a recorder replacement with drive is $700 to $1,500. What pushes a job up here is reach, as with a camera at the top of a building front or behind a shop sign, and runs that crews have damaged, not the ZIP code.

The useful step is to send a photo of the recorder screen first. That is usually enough to tell you which band your repair falls in before anyone visits.

"If your camera recorded the crime, the police will come and get it"

Some answers suggest that once you report a crime, officers will collect any footage that exists.

On r/Greenpoint, a resident who watched a package thief on Morgan Avenue at 5 a.m. wrote: "We saw it happening, caught the thief and called the police. They never came." Detectives do canvass for video after serious incidents, but for everyday thefts and crashes the job of keeping and handing over footage usually falls on the owner, and on the neighbor who asked.

Export the clip yourself as soon as you know you need it, write down the date, time and camera, and keep the original on the recorder. If you can't export it, call us and we make that the first task of the visit.

"Any work on a camera in a landmark district needs a permit"

Some answers warn that anything on the front of a landmarked building needs Landmarks Preservation Commission approval.

The Commission's own Permit Guidebook says no permit is needed for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting" of health and safety equipment. Swapping a dead camera for a similar one in the same place is ordinary repair. What the guidebook does govern is new or moved equipment: mounting at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or on decorative surfaces, and keeping visible conduit to a minimum.

So in the district, repair what is there, keep the footprint the same, and talk to us before moving a camera to a new spot. If a move is needed, the guidebook points to where it can go.

"A battery backup keeps your cameras safe in an outage"

Outage advice often says to plug the recorder into a UPS and stop worrying.

Greenpoint's outages are often not clean blackouts. They are brownouts like Con Edison's 8 percent voltage reduction in July 2026, or partial outages where one leg of a building's supply drops. A small UPS bought for a computer may carry the recorder but not the PoE switch, so the recorder keeps running with no cameras to record.

Size the backup to the recorder, the switch and the router together, put them on the same protected supply, and test it by pulling the plug while someone watches the cameras. We do that test on every repair where a backup is part of the fix.

"Keep the recorder in the basement, out of the way"

Setup guides often say to put the recorder somewhere out of sight, and in a Greenpoint walk-up that almost always means the cellar.

Out of sight is good. Low and damp is not. After the flooding near Meeker and Manhattan avenues in October 2025 and the city's 2026 warning to basement residents, the cellar is the riskiest room in many buildings for a recorder and its power supplies.

A wall shelf well above the floor, a locked cabinet or a closet on the first floor keeps the recorder hidden without putting it in the water's path. We move recorders as part of a repair when the cellar has taken water before.

"Camera repair is a handyman job"

Listing sites like Angi and Thumbtack put camera repair next to general handyman work.

Some of it, like cleaning a lens, resetting a phone app or plugging in a camera that came loose, really is something an owner can do. But New York requires a Department of State license for companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for other people.

If your problem is simple, we will tell you over the phone or from a photo, and you won't need a visit. If it isn't, the $150 service call buys a licensed technician who will copy your footage first and explain the fault before anything is replaced.

FAQ

Greenpoint Security Camera Repair FAQ

Does a recorder need a screen plugged into it to keep recording?
+

No. A recorder records whether or not a monitor is connected, and many in Greenpoint cellars run for years with nothing attached. The screen only matters when someone wants to look at the cameras or change settings there. If your recorder shows nothing when a screen is plugged in, the fault may be the output setting, the cable or the recorder itself, and we check which before assuming the footage is gone.

Our recorded video plays back like a slideshow, jumping a second at a time. Is the recorder failing?
+

Usually not. Choppy playback with smooth live view most often means the recording frame rate was turned down at some point to save drive space, or the recorder is struggling to keep up with more cameras than it was set for. We check the recording settings for each camera, the drive's health and the recorder's load, and set a frame rate that gives smooth motion while still keeping enough days of video.

How long does it take to copy a night of video off our recorder?
+

It depends on how many cameras and hours you need and how fast the recorder exports. A clip of a few minutes from one camera usually takes a few minutes. A full night from several cameras can take an hour or more on an older recorder. We start the export first on the visit and work on other checks while it runs, so the copy doesn't hold up the repair.

Can you repair the camera mounted on the hood of our shop's roll-down gate?
+

Yes. Cameras on gate hoods are common on Greenpoint storefronts, and they take a beating from the vibration every time the gate goes up and down. The usual faults are a mount that has worked loose, a cable worn where it passes the hood edge, and water at the connector. We re-secure the mount, protect the cable where it rubs, reseal the connector and re-aim the camera so it still sees the sidewalk with the gate down.

Can the repair bill be split between the owners in our small condo?
+

We send one invoice to whoever the condo names as responsible, usually the board or the managing agent, and the condo divides the cost among its owners as its bylaws say. If you need a breakdown by camera or by area of the building to help with that split, ask when you book and we will itemize the written price that way.

Should our recorder have its own electrical circuit?
+

It helps when the recorder shares a circuit with something that trips the breaker, such as a space heater, a window air conditioner or a dehumidifier in the cellar. If the recorder restarts whenever one of those runs, a dedicated circuit is worth asking your electrician about. We don't do electrical work, but we can tell you what the camera system draws and put it on a protected supply with a battery backup in the meantime.

Our bar is kept dim at night on purpose. Can the inside cameras still show faces without brighter lights?
+

Often, yes. Many newer cameras are built for low light and can show color and faces in a dim room, while an older camera in that light gives a grainy blur or flips to black-and-white infrared. We check the camera's settings first, then where it sits relative to the light you have. Sometimes a small change, such as one fixture near the door or the register, does more than a new camera.

Our outdoor camera is powered by an extension cord run out a window. Can it be powered properly without an electrician?
+

In most cases, yes. A network camera can take its power and its data over one low-voltage cable from a PoE switch or recorder indoors, so it doesn't need an outlet outside at all. We replace the extension cord with a proper low-voltage run and a sealed entry through the wall or window frame. If the camera can't take PoE, we can usually power it from a low-voltage supply indoors instead.

Can our system record without being connected to the internet at all?
+

Yes. A recorder with cameras wired to it records locally whether or not it is online. The internet connection is only there for watching on your phone and getting alerts. Some owners prefer to keep the system offline for privacy. The tradeoff is that you only find out about a dead camera or drive when someone checks the recorder in person, so we set a reminder schedule for those checks instead.

If a clip from our camera ends up in court, can anyone show it wasn't edited?
+

The safest export is the recorder's own format, copied straight from the recorder to a USB drive with its built-in player, its original timestamps and the camera name intact. We note who made the copy, when, and what the recorder's clock showed against real time.
