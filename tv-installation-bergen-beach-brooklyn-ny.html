The Neighborhood

Why Bergen Beach Properties Need TV Installation

Bergen Beach is one of the few corners of Brooklyn where nearly every TV we hang goes on the wall of a house. Cityneighborhoods.nyc describes it as "a quiet, residential enclave surrounded by water on nearly three sides," with Avenue N to the north, Paerdegat Basin to the east, Mill Basin to the south and East Mill Basin, 68th Street and Veterans Avenue on the west, and it notes the near-total absence of apartment towers. The same profile traces how it got that way: a short-lived seaside resort in the 1890s, decades of marsh and farmland, the Belt Parkway in 1940, then land reclamation in the 1950s and 1960s that made stable ground for building. What went up on that ground was mostly low brick and stucco houses, detached and semi-detached, many with a driveway, a garage and a small yard. For a TV installer, that history means the wall behind your screen is rarely the steel-stud drywall of a new rental. It is far more often brick, block, plaster from the 1950s and 1960s, or drywall that a later owner put up during a renovation, sometimes over the original wall.

Ownership shapes the job too. The NYU Furman Center's 2024 profile of Community District 18, the Flatlands and Canarsie district that includes Bergen Beach, puts the homeownership rate at 61.2 percent, against 32.7 percent for the city as a whole. That is a very different customer from a renter in a walk-up. Owners in Bergen Beach usually want the TV to look built in, with no cords showing, and they are free to open a wall to get there. They also tend to have more than one TV: a big screen in the living room, another in the finished basement, one in the main bedroom, sometimes one in a garage or on a patio. Several of the listings we read while preparing this page show exactly that kind of house. A brick detached house on East 74th Street built in 1940 has a finished basement, volume ceilings on the first floor and a detached garage behind a three-car driveway. A 1970 house on East 71st Street lists two separate living areas and a backyard with a sauna, a built-in barbecue, a dining area and a seating lounge. Another Bergen Beach single-family lists a fireplace, a two-room finished basement, radiant heated floors and a heated saltwater pool. Each of those rooms is a different TV job with a different wall, a different cable route and a different risk.

The two-family house is the other half of the neighborhood. A 2003 two-family at 7119 Bergen Court, zoned R3-1, has a private three-car driveway and a garage. A brick two-family at 1242 East 68th Street pairs an owner's triplex with a one-bedroom rental that has its own private entrance, an interior garage and parking for two more cars. When we mount a TV in a house like that, there are usually two households and two sets of preferences under one roof, and the wall the tenant wants to drill belongs to the owner. We handle both sides: the owner who wants a TV in every room of the triplex, and the tenant upstairs or downstairs who wants one screen up in a way the owner will approve. And then there is Mill Harbor at 1561 Royce Street, a 208-unit, four-story gated condominium built in 2005, where an association, not the individual owner, sets the rules about contractors, insurance and working hours. Its walls are newer and lighter than the brick houses around it, which changes the anchors we bring.

Water is part of every conversation here. News 12 has reported on residents "fed up with ongoing flooding woes" on Bergen Beach streets, and on heavy rain flooding the neighborhood. New York City's hazard mitigation plan notes that new or substantially improved buildings in the city's highest-risk flood zones may not have basements at all, which tells you how seriously the city treats water at the lowest level of a house near the bay. Most Bergen Beach basements were built long before those rules, and many are finished family rooms with the best TV in the house. That has a direct effect on how we install: we keep power strips, cable boxes and game consoles off the floor, we raise the outlet behind a basement TV with an in-wall kit rather than leaving a cord on the carpet, and we talk with you about where the router and the internet box should live if the basement has ever taken water. None of that costs anything to discuss, and it is the kind of thing that only comes up in a neighborhood like this one.

The last piece is the street itself. Bergen Beach is quiet, with houses set back on side streets and driveways in front, which makes it one of the easier places in Brooklyn for us to work. There are no elevators to book, no doormen to clear, and the van can usually park in front of the house or in the driveway. The trade-off is distance: the house might be wide, the TV wall might be at the back of a long living room or two floors above the basement where the cable comes in, and the cable run can be longer than in any apartment. We plan those runs from your photos so we arrive with the right length of cable and the right kit, and so the price you agreed covers the route the cable actually has to take.

Same-Day Repair in Bergen Beach

Need Repair in Bergen Beach? A Screen Drooping Off Brick, a Bracket Loose in Plaster, or a Basement TV Moved After Water

Urgent TV problems in houses built like Bergen Beach's tend to fall into three kinds. The first is a TV on a brick or block wall that has started to droop or rock. When a bracket like that comes down, the cause is usually one of two things: the bracket was screwed into the mortar joints between the bricks instead of into the bricks themselves, or it was held with plastic plugs meant for drywall. MantelMount's own installation guide puts it plainly: "Mortar joints are much softer than the bricks themselves." Mortar crumbles a little every time a full-motion arm is pulled out and pushed back, and after a year or two the anchors have room to move. The second kind is a bracket on old plaster that is pulling forward, because the screws gripped the plaster coat and nothing behind it. The third is specific to houses near the water: a TV and its boxes that were in a finished basement when water came in, or that the family moved upstairs in a hurry and now want put back properly.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back against the wall and do not lean on it to straighten it. If two people can lift it safely, take it off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, the new mount is priced by the wall it goes on, with the $85 take-down added, and the technician moves the fixing points onto sound brick or into framing rather than reusing holes that have already failed. If a basement TV was near water, we will not reconnect anything that sat in it. The TV, the cable box and the power strip should be checked or replaced first, and we will tell you which parts we can see are affected. We say plainly when the problem is the wall itself, a cracked block or a loose run of brick, so you can bring in a mason before a new bracket goes on it.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Bergen Beach's Architecture

Bergen Beach has fewer building types than most of Brooklyn, but the ones it has vary a lot inside. These are the six we meet most, and what each one usually means for the wall behind the TV.

The postwar brick house

Detached or semi-detached, often from the 1950s to the 1970s, with masonry outer walls, plaster or drywall inside, and a living room that runs the depth of the house.

The stucco-front house

Stucco over block or frame on the outside, which matters for porch and patio screens, and a mix of original plaster and newer drywall on the inside.

The large renovated single-family

High entries, fireplaces, radiant floors, finished basements and backyards built for entertaining, with several TVs and a lot of cable to plan.

The two-family with a rental unit

An owner's duplex or triplex and a separate apartment with its own door, so two households, two TV plans and one owner approving the holes.

The gated condo

Newer construction like Mill Harbor, with lighter interior walls, shared walls between units and an association that sets the rules for contractors.

The garage, cabana and outbuilding

Detached garages, home gyms and pool houses, where the wall may be unfinished block or bare framing and the air can be damp, hot or cold.

The postwar brick house: solid outer walls and a long room to plan

The typical Bergen Beach house is low and brick, and in many of them the living room runs from the front window toward the back of the house, with the TV wall on one of the long sides. If that long side is the outside wall, it is usually brick or block behind a coat of plaster or a layer of drywall on furring strips. That is excellent for holding a heavy TV, much better than a hollow wall, but it takes a hammer drill, masonry bits and sleeve anchors set into the face of the brick. If there is drywall over furring strips in front of the brick, the fixing has to go through the furring and into the masonry behind it, or the bracket ends up hanging on an inch of air. We confirm which you have with a single pilot hole placed where the bracket plate will cover it, so if the guess from your photos was wrong, nothing visible is lost.

The inside walls of these houses are a different story. Partitions between the living room and the kitchen or hallway are usually wood framing with plaster or drywall, and they are hollow, which is where an in-wall power and cable kit can go. That leads to a common choice in Bergen Beach living rooms: the outside brick wall is the stronger place for the TV, but the inside framed wall is the easier place to hide the wires. We will show you both options and the price of each. The brick wall with a paintable cord cover ($179.99 plus $65 plus $59, or $303.99 for a TV up to 55 inches) usually costs less than the framed wall with an in-wall kit ($179.99 plus $169, or $348.99), and the framed wall usually looks cleaner. Neither choice is wrong. It depends on where you sit, where the windows are and whether the glare off the bay side of the house falls across the screen in the afternoon.

The stucco-front house: the outside wall is not the same material as the inside

Cityneighborhoods.nyc names brick and stucco together as the neighborhood's typical exteriors, and stucco houses deserve a separate note because stucco is only the skin. Behind it there may be concrete block, wood framing with lath, or in a renovated house, foam board over sheathing. Inside, the walls are usually plaster or drywall like any other house. For an indoor TV, the stucco outside makes little difference, except that on an outside wall the cavity may be full of insulation, which slows down fishing an in-wall kit. Where stucco matters is outdoors: a TV on a covered porch or patio mounted directly on the stucco face. Drilling stucco cleanly takes the right bit and patience to avoid cracking the coat around the hole, and every hole through the outer skin has to be sealed afterward so rain cannot run behind it. If the stucco is over block, the anchors go into the block; if it is over framing, the bracket has to find the framing.

The large renovated single-family: several TVs, one plan

Some of the bigger houses in Bergen Beach have been rebuilt from the inside out. The listings we read describe twenty-foot entry halls, fireplaces, radiant heated floors, two-room finished basements, saunas and heated saltwater pools. In a house like that, the TV work is rarely one screen. It is the living room, the basement, the main bedroom and often somewhere outside, and the right approach is to plan them together. Where does the internet come in? Where does the cable or fiber box sit? Does the family want one streaming box per TV or a cabinet of equipment in one place? A twenty-foot ceiling also means a TV that might be mounted higher than a normal ladder reaches, which changes the crew and the time, so we want to see the wall in a photo before quoting it. Radiant floors matter too: if the heating runs under the floor, we do not drill into the floor for a cable, and we keep any floor-level work above the finished surface.

The two-family with a rental unit: two households, one owner, separate cables

The brick two-family on East 68th Street with an owner's triplex and a one-bedroom rental behind its own private entrance is a good picture of how these houses are used. An owner may want both units done in one booking, either because the tenant asked or because the rental is being set up between tenants. That is efficient: one visit, two TVs, each priced on its own wall from the same list. The detail that matters is keeping the two households separate. Each unit should have its own TV wiring and its own path to its own internet or cable box, so a tenant moving out does not mean disconnecting something in the owner's half of the house. If the internet for both units comes in at one point in the basement, we label each line for its unit at both ends, so nobody has to guess later which cable belongs to whom.

When the tenant is the one who calls, we ask for the owner's OK before booking a wall mount, and we offer the options that leave the smallest trace: a fixed bracket on a framed wall with the cables in a paintable cord cover, or simply the TV set up on its own stand for $119.99, which needs no holes at all. If the owner prefers a particular wall or wants the bracket left behind when the tenant goes, it is easiest to agree that before we drill. We are happy to talk to the owner directly if that helps.

The gated condo: lighter walls, shared walls and the association's rules

Mill Harbor, the 208-unit gated complex at 1561 Royce Street built in 2005, is the clearest example of condo living in Bergen Beach, and condos like it behave differently from the brick houses around them. The walls are newer, and in buildings of that era interior partitions are often drywall on light framing, sometimes steel. A wall shared with the next unit may carry sound insulation and fire-rated construction that we do not want to cut into for a cable. So in a condo we usually mount on a wall the owner controls, choose fasteners for the framing we actually find, and keep the in-wall kit within one wall bay rather than running cable sideways across studs. The association may ask for a certificate of insurance naming it before the visit and may limit work to certain hours. Send us the management office's instructions with your photos and we will work inside them.

The garage, cabana and outbuilding: bare walls and air that changes with the seasons

Detached garages show up again and again in Bergen Beach listings, and some of them are no longer just for cars. A TV in a garage gym or a workshop is a reasonable thing to want, but the space is different from the house. The walls may be bare block or exposed studs, which are actually easy to mount on, and the cable may have to cross from the house in a way that is protected from the weather. The bigger issue is the air. An unheated garage in January and a closed garage in August are both hard on an ordinary indoor TV, and a pool house beside a heated saltwater pool adds humidity and salt. We will mount a TV wherever the wall can hold it, but we will tell you honestly when a standard indoor set is likely to have a short life in that spot, and what would last longer.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Where a Technician Looks First in a Bergen Beach House

These notes describe how our technicians approach houses like Bergen Beach's. They are our working practice, not a story about any one address.

Start where the signal comes in, not at the TV. In a house, the first stop is usually the basement, the garage or a utility closet, wherever the fiber terminal, the cable line or the router lives. From there we can see how far the TV is from its source, whether an existing coax or network cable already runs toward that room, and whether the basement ceiling is open enough to carry a new one. If you are not sure where your internet comes in, a photo of the box your provider installed is enough.

Find the outside wall. Standing in the living room, the technician works out which walls face outside and which are partitions. On a brick house the outside walls are where a heavy TV holds best and where cables are hardest to hide; the partitions are the reverse. The knock test helps: a hollow drum sound on an inside wall, a flat, hard knock on an outside one.

Check for what must not be drilled. Radiant heat in the floor, a gas line near a fireplace, electrical conduit in an old house, a sprinkler line in a condo ceiling: each of these changes where a cable or bracket can go. We scan before we drill, and in rooms with radiant floors we do not go through the floor at all.

Look at the floor of the basement. If there is a tide line on the paneling, stained baseboards or a dehumidifier running, we take that as information. The TV can still go up, but the boxes go on a shelf or into a cabinet off the floor, and the outlet behind the TV goes up the wall.

Agree the height with someone sitting down. The center of the screen at the eye level of the person on the couch is the usual target. Above a fireplace, the answer is often a tilting bracket or a pull-down mount, and sometimes it is a different wall. We tape the outline on the wall before any drilling so you can sit and look at it.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Stucco, Plaster and Basement Block: What Holds a TV in a Bergen Beach House and What It Costs

The base price of $179.99 assumes drywall on wood studs and a TV up to 55 inches. Most Bergen Beach living rooms are not that, which is why the wall is the first thing we ask about. Here is what each wall type means in practice.

Brick and stone, +$65. Solid brick is the strongest wall in the neighborhood and the one people worry about most. The worry is usually about damage: will drilling crack the brick? Drilled correctly, it will not. The holes go into the face of the brick, not the mortar, with a hammer drill and a masonry bit sized for the anchor, and the anchors are sleeve or wedge types rated well above the TV's weight. MantelMount's guidance is that the total anchor capacity should be at least four times the TV's weight, which is the kind of margin we work to. If the brick is old and soft, or a brick moves when we drill it, we stop and show you, because that is a mason's problem before it is a TV problem.

Plaster, +$45. Plaster in Bergen Beach houses is newer than in brownstone Brooklyn, since much of the housing went up after the Second World War, but it still behaves like plaster: hard on the surface, brittle and easy to crack if the hole is forced. MantelMount's advice is short: "Plaster is brittle; always pre-drill and let the anchors do the work." The fixing has to reach the framing or the masonry behind it, not just the plaster coat. On a plaster wall over wood lath, we find the studs and fix into them; on plaster applied directly to block or brick, the anchors go into the masonry. A Brownstoner forum thread about mounting a TV above a Brooklyn fireplace captures the common frustration well: stud finders work poorly on plaster, so we rely on the scanner, the knock and a pilot hole together.

Basement block and concrete. Finished basements here are often block foundation walls behind paneling or drywall on furring strips. The paneling itself holds nothing. The bracket has to go through it into the block, which takes masonry anchors and the same care as brick. Block and poured concrete have their own line on our full Brooklyn list, so we confirm that add-on from your photos and include it in the total before you book. Where the basement wall is drywall on its own framing a few inches off the block, we fix into that framing and can usually run an in-wall kit in the gap. Either way, the cable box and the console go up off the floor.

Drywall on wood studs, no add-on. Renovated rooms, additions and many second floors in Bergen Beach have ordinary drywall on wood studs. This is the base price, and it is also the easiest wall to hide cables in.

Above a fireplace, +$130. The fireplace add-on covers the extra work of mounting on a chimney breast, which is usually masonry, at a height that needs a taller ladder and more care. It does not make every fireplace wall a good idea. If the fireplace is used for real fires, heat rising off the mantel can reach the bottom of the TV, and a participant in that Brownstoner thread raised exactly that concern. Another said a TV that high "invites a sore neck." We check the heat with you, look at the viewing angle from the couch and suggest a tilting bracket or a different wall when that serves you better.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common mistake we find behind wall-mounted TVs is the TV's own power cord pushed through a hole in the wall and plugged into an outlet near the floor. It looks clean, and it is not allowed. As EC&M's summary of the National Electrical Code puts it, section 400.12 says flexible cords and power-supply cords "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." The legal way to put power behind a TV is an in-wall power kit, which is a pair of connected outlets joined by cable rated to live inside the wall, plus a pass-through for the low-voltage cables. That is the $169 in-wall power and cable kit on our list. It works in any hollow wall, which in a Bergen Beach house usually means an inside partition, a drywall addition or furred-out drywall in front of masonry.

On solid brick or plaster directly on block, there is no cavity to hide anything in, and cutting a channel into masonry for a cable is rarely worth the mess. That is where a paintable cord cover at $59 makes sense: a slim channel on the surface of the wall, painted to match, carrying the power and HDMI cables down to the console. Done carefully, it is barely visible from the couch. The third option, if the cable box and console sit in a cabinet below the screen, is a short drop straight behind the TV into the cabinet, which needs neither.

A business screen is a different job from a living-room TV. It runs longer hours, it hangs where the public walks, it often needs to be seen from across a room, and the landlord or the property manager usually has a say before anyone drills.

Shopping center storefronts

The biggest difference between a home job and a business job is who approves it and when it can happen. A store in a shopping center usually has a lease that controls alterations, and the landlord may want to know exactly where the screen and its power will go. A restaurant can only lose its dining room for a short window, which usually means early in the day before service. An office may want the screen up after hours so the work does not disturb patients or clients. We plan around all of that before the visit, and we can send a certificate of insurance to the landlord or the managing agent if they ask for one.

The second difference is how the screen is used. A TV in a living room is watched from the couch, a few feet away and at one height. A screen in a business may be watched by people standing in line, sitting at a counter and walking past, all at once. That changes the height, the bracket and sometimes the screen size. It also changes the cabling. A menu board fed from a small media player behind the screen, a sports TV fed from a cable box under the counter, and a waiting-room screen running a streaming app on its own each need a different path for power and signal, and the code rule about cords inside walls applies to businesses just as much as to homes.

Compliance notes for Bergen Beach businesses and shared spaces

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, wall-mounted objects whose leading edge sits between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may not stick out more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm pulled out in a hallway or a waiting room can break that rule, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount the screen above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. National Electrical Code section 400.12 does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit, or the cords run in a surface cord cover.
  • Exits, signs and sprinklers stay clear. We do not mount screens where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and we keep cables off the floor along any route people use to leave.
  • Landlord and association paperwork first. Shopping centers, managed buildings and condo associations often want a certificate of insurance naming them before work starts. Tell us the exact names and address they want on it.
  • Rated hardware for public screens. A screen in a business gets bumped, leaned on and occasionally pulled. Every bracket we hang is rated above the screen's weight, and on masonry the anchors are rated for the load, not just the bracket.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Bergen Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bergen Beach owners, tenants and businesses describe when they ask about a TV: basements and water, big rooms and heated floors, houses with several screens, two-families, and TVs that live outside or in the garage. Each one is answered here in full and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Basements and the lowest floor

We are finishing our basement this winter. Should the TV wiring go in before the drywall, and what should our contractor leave for you?

Yes, and it is the cheapest moment you will ever have to do it. Before the drywall goes up, a cable path to the TV wall costs almost nothing in labor because nothing has to be fished. Ask your contractor to put a few things in place: a solid backing board between the studs where the TV will hang, so the bracket can be fixed anywhere on that wall; an outlet set high on the wall behind where the screen will be, installed by the electrician; and an empty conduit or a low-voltage box at the same height with a pull string running to wherever the cable box, router or console will live. Keep that equipment spot off the floor. Then we come back after the paint, mount the TV, run the cables through the path that is already there and connect everything. If you send us a photo of the open wall with a tape measure in it, we can mark the height before the drywall goes up.

Our basement walls are block with wood paneling over them. Can a big TV hang on the paneling?

Not on the paneling alone. Wood paneling is thin and only nailed to furring strips, and it would pull away under the weight and leverage of a TV long before the bracket gave out. The TV has to be fixed through the paneling into whatever is behind it. If the furring strips sit directly on the block, the anchors go through the paneling and the strip into the block with masonry fasteners. If there is a framed wall standing a few inches off the block, we fix into the framing. We find out which with a pilot hole the bracket will hide. The paneling also limits how cables can be hidden, since it is hard to open and patch neatly, so a paintable cord cover or a drop straight into a cabinet below the screen is usually the cleaner answer in a paneled room.

Big rooms and special floors

Our entry hall rises close to twenty feet. Can a TV go high on that wall, and how does anyone reach it?

It can, but we want to talk about it first, because a TV mounted that high is usually for show rather than for watching. If the screen is meant to be seen from a balcony or an upper landing, a high mount can make sense. If it is meant to be watched from a sofa in the room below, the angle will be uncomfortable for anyone sitting down, and a tilting bracket only helps a little. Mounting at that height also needs a taller ladder or a scaffold platform and often a second technician, so we do not price it from the base line. Send a photo of the wall with something for scale and tell us where people will sit, and we will either price the high mount honestly or suggest a lower spot that will be used more.

We have radiant heat in our floors. Does that change where the TV cables can run?

It does. Radiant floor heat means tubing or heating cable runs under the finished floor, often close to the surface and close to the walls. We never drill through a floor with radiant heat, and we do not fasten anything to the floor near the wall, because a single hole in a water line under tile or wood is an expensive repair. That rules out the old trick of dropping a TV cable straight down through the floor to the basement. Instead, cables go up and over through the wall cavity and ceiling, or along the wall in a paintable cord cover to a cabinet. If you have the heating plans or the contractor's photos from when the floor was installed, send them. They tell us where the lines run, which makes planning faster.

The mantel in our living room sticks out several inches. Does a deep mantel change how high the TV has to go?

It changes how close the bottom of the TV can come to the mantel shelf, and that changes the height. A deep mantel sticks out into the space under the screen, so a flat bracket would put the bottom edge right above it and the top of the TV very high. Leaving room for whatever you keep on the mantel, candles, frames or a clock, pushes it higher still. There are three ways around that. A tilting bracket points the screen down toward the couch. A pull-down mantel mount lets you lower the screen to eye level when watching and raise it again afterward. Or the TV goes on a different wall entirely, with the fireplace left as the room's other focal point. The fireplace add-on on our list is $130, and we will measure the mantel depth from your photo before suggesting any of the three.

Several TVs in one house

Four cables come out of our basement wall with no labels. Can you work out which one reaches the living room?

Yes. Unlabeled cables are normal in houses that have had several owners and several internet providers. We trace each one with a tone generator and probe, or with a tester at both ends, and confirm which runs to the living room, which goes to a bedroom and which goes nowhere at all because it was cut during a renovation. Then each one gets a printed label at both ends. If the cable that reaches the living room is old coax and the TV now needs a network connection, we tell you whether the old cable can be used as a pull string for a new one, which is often the easiest way to get a fresh cable through a finished wall without opening it.

Should each TV in the house have its own streaming box, or can one box in the basement feed several rooms?

For most Bergen Beach houses, one small streaming device behind each TV is simpler, cheaper and more reliable than one central box. Each TV gets its own apps, its own remote and its own power, and if one fails it does not take the others down. A central box makes more sense when you want the same picture on several screens at once, such as a party with the game in the living room, the basement and on the patio, or when a cable box rental for every room has become expensive. That kind of distribution needs dedicated cabling and equipment and is quoted separately. Tell us how the house actually watches TV and we will suggest the simpler setup that does the job.

Two-families and rental units

We are renting out the downstairs unit for the first time. Should the TV bracket stay with the apartment when the tenant leaves?

It is your choice, and it is worth deciding before we drill. Leaving a bracket on the wall as part of the apartment is common in two-families and saves the next tenant the cost of a new mount, but it ties the TV location to one spot and one size range, and a tenant with a much bigger or smaller TV may still need something different. If you plan to leave it, a fixed or tilting bracket with a wide bolt pattern fits the most TVs, and an in-wall power and cable kit means the next tenant does not need to run anything. If you would rather each tenant bring their own TV and stand, setting the TV up on its stand for $119.99 leaves no holes at all. Either way, write it into the lease so nobody argues about it at move-out.

Our new TV is still sealed in its carton. Should we open it before the technician comes, or leave it boxed?

Leave it boxed, but take one photo first. The sticker with the model number is usually printed on the outside of the carton, and that model number tells us the weight, the screen size and the bolt pattern on the back, which is everything we need to bring the right bracket and bolts. An unopened carton is also the easiest way to carry a screen from the front door to the room, and if anything is wrong with the TV, a sealed box makes the return simpler. On the day we open it with you, check the panel before it goes on the wall, and set the packing aside so you can keep it or recycle it.

Patios, garages and the yard

We want a TV under the covered patio by the barbecue. Will an ordinary indoor TV last a year out there?

Probably not as long as you hope. MantelMount's own guide says indoor TVs outdoors are "not recommended by television manufacturers," because of "exposure to both water and wind," and a Bergen Beach yard adds damp air off the water, salt, summer heat under a roof and freezing nights in winter. A covered patio protects against direct rain, but not against humidity, wind-blown spray or the temperature swing. If the TV is used now and then and comes indoors for winter, an indoor set on a covered wall can be a reasonable compromise. If it will stay out year round, an outdoor-rated TV is the honest answer, and its cables need a drip loop and protection where they pass through the wall. We mount either and will tell you plainly which we think your spot needs.

Our yard gets real wind off the water. Should a patio TV be on a fixed bracket or an arm that swings out?

On an open patio near the water, a fixed or tilting bracket is usually the safer choice. A full-motion arm extended away from the wall gives the wind a lever to work against, and a gust that catches the screen can stress both the arm and the anchors over time. If you need the screen to angle toward the pool or the table, an arm can work under a solid roof with walls on two or three sides, as long as it is folded back against the wall when nobody is watching. Whatever the bracket, outdoor hardware should resist corrosion, and every hole through the stucco or siding gets sealed so water does not run behind the mount.

Our garage is now a home gym with bare block walls and no heat. Can a TV go up there?

Bare block is easy to mount on, so the wall itself is not the problem. The masonry anchors go into the face of the block, not the mortar, and because nothing is finished, the power and cable can run on the surface in a neat channel. The concern is the air. An unheated, closed garage can be very cold in January and very hot in August, and condensation forms on cold surfaces when the door opens on a humid day. Many indoor TVs list an operating temperature range in their manual, and it is worth checking yours against what the garage actually does. A modest TV that you do not mind replacing, mounted away from the door and off the floor, is often the practical choice for a garage gym.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Bergen Beach

These are claims that show up in generic online answers and national cost guides about TV mounting. Each one is true somewhere. Here is how each one holds up in a Bergen Beach house.

"TV mounting costs about $100 to $130"

That range comes from national figures. Thumbtack, for example, puts the national average for television mounting at between $100 and $130. Answers built on numbers like that tend to repeat them for every city, as if a wall in Brooklyn were the same as a wall anywhere else.

Brooklyn numbers are higher, and the published ones say so. Angi reports an average of $280 for TV mounting in Brooklyn, with a typical range of $172 to $376. Our own base is $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, with each harder wall or room added as a fixed line.

In Bergen Beach the wall is what moves the total. The same 55-inch TV is $179.99 on drywall, $224.99 on plaster and $244.99 on brick, before any bracket. Above a fireplace on brick it is $374.99. Knowing the wall from a photo is what makes the quote real.

"You can mount a TV on any surface"

TaskRabbit's Brooklyn page says Taskers "can typically mount a TV on any surface," and many AI answers say the same. It is true in the sense that almost any wall can be made to hold a TV. It leaves out how differently each wall has to be handled.

Brick needs a hammer drill and anchors set in the brick face. Plaster needs pre-drilling and fixings that reach the framing or masonry. Paneling over block needs anchors that pass through both. Stucco outside needs sealing. Each of those takes a different tool, a different anchor and a different amount of time.

The real question is whether the person who arrives knows which surface they are facing and has the right kit with them. That is why we ask for a photo first and confirm with a pilot hole the bracket will cover, instead of finding out with the first full-size hole.

"Just find a stud and screw into it"

Generic instructions start with the stud finder. In a house with wood-framed walls that is good advice, and in Bergen Beach's renovated rooms and additions it often works. Find two studs, fix the bracket into them, and the TV will hold.

On the outside walls of a postwar brick house there may be no studs at all, only brick or block behind plaster or furring. A stud finder may beep at a furring strip that is far too thin to carry a TV, or read nothing useful on dense plaster, as Brooklyn homeowners on Brownstoner have complained.

The answer is to know what the wall is before choosing the fixing. On masonry, the brick itself is stronger than any stud. On furring over block, the anchors must reach the block. A scanner, a knock test and one small hole together tell us which.

"Hide the cords by running them through the wall"

Many online answers suggest cutting two holes, pushing the TV's power cord through the wall and plugging it in near the floor. It is cheap and it looks tidy, which is why it is so common behind wall-mounted TVs.

It is not allowed. National Electrical Code section 400.12 says flexible cords and power-supply cords are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors or ceilings. An ordinary power cord is not built to be enclosed in a wall cavity, where any damage to it is out of sight.

The legal way is an in-wall power kit with cable rated for inside the wall, which is the $169 kit on our list. On solid brick where nothing can be hidden, a paintable cord cover at $59 keeps the cables neat on the surface without breaking the rule.

"Above the fireplace is the natural place for the TV"

Design photos often show a TV over the mantel, and AI answers tend to treat that as the default spot in any room with a fireplace. Best Buy's New York page, for example, describes bringing the right tools to attach a mount to the brick over a fireplace.

Mounting there is possible, but it is not automatically a good idea. A wood-burning fireplace sends heat up the wall, and a high screen strains the neck; a Brooklyn homeowner on Brownstoner put it simply, that the height "invites a sore neck." A deep mantel pushes the screen higher still.

We check how the fireplace is actually used, measure the mantel and look at the angle from the couch. Sometimes the right answer is a tilting or pull-down bracket. Sometimes it is a different wall, with the fireplace left alone. The fireplace add-on is $130 either way it is mounted.

"The basement is the best room for a home theater"

Generic home theater advice loves the basement: it is dark, it is away from the bedrooms and it is often the largest open room in the house. In many Bergen Beach houses it is exactly where the family TV already is.

What that advice leaves out is water. News 12 has covered Bergen Beach residents fed up with repeated flooding, and the city does not allow basements at all in new or substantially improved buildings in its highest-risk flood zones. A basement close to the bay needs a different setup from one inland.

A basement TV can still be a great room. The difference is in the details: the cable box, console and power strip on a shelf or in a cabinet, not the floor; the outlet raised behind the TV; and the router placed where a few inches of water will not reach it.

"An indoor TV is fine under a covered patio"

Some answers suggest that a roof over the patio is enough protection and that any TV will do. It is an appealing idea in a neighborhood of backyards, barbecues and pools, where a screen outside for the summer seems like a small thing.

MantelMount, which sells mounts for both, says indoor TVs outside are not recommended by television manufacturers because of water and wind. Near Jamaica Bay, a covered patio still sees humid air, spray carried on the wind and a large swing between summer and winter temperatures.

If the TV will come indoors for the winter, an indoor set on a covered wall can be a reasonable compromise. If it stays out all year, plan on an outdoor-rated TV, corrosion-resistant hardware, a drip loop in the cable and every hole in the stucco sealed.
