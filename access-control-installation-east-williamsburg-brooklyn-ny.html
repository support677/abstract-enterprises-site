The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Access Control

East Williamsburg is one of the few places in Brooklyn where the typical building was not designed for the people who now use it. The city’s North Brooklyn Industry and Innovation Plan describes the industrial district that runs through Greenpoint, East Williamsburg and Bushwick as 1,066 acres and the third largest industrial hub in New York City by employment, with about 19,500 jobs, most of them industrial. The same study notes that the historic loft buildings across that area are generally three to five stories tall. Those buildings were put up for one owner who ran one business. Their street doors were meant to be unlocked in the morning by a foreman and locked at night by the same foreman. Today one of those doors may serve twenty tenants.

That shift is the reason access control matters more here than on a block of brick rowhouses. Between 2010 and 2016, according to the same city plan, the number of non-industrial businesses in the district grew by 61 percent and industrial businesses by 12 percent, and more than a third of the new jobs were in offices, retail, arts and entertainment. In practice that means a floor that once held one garment shop now holds a recording studio, a ceramics studio, a small architecture office and someone’s storage room. Each of them was handed a key to the street door. Some made copies for staff and friends. Nobody, including the landlord, knows how many copies are out there, and changing the cylinder means cutting new keys for everyone at once.

Fobs, cards, codes and phone keys solve that in a way a locksmith cannot. Each person gets their own credential. A company that leaves can have every one of its credentials switched off in a minute, without touching anyone else’s. A tenant who works nights can be given hours that differ from the landscaper who comes on Tuesdays. And the building owner, who in a multi-tenant loft is often the only party responsible for the street door, can finally answer the question every insurer, lender and new tenant asks: who can get in here?

Residents make the picture more complicated. Some East Williamsburg lofts became homes long before they were legal, and the state’s Loft Law, overseen by the city’s Loft Board, exists to convert those buildings into safe, rent-stabilized housing. The process is still running. In December 2024 PincusCo reported plans for 43 residential units to legalize an East Williamsburg interim multiple dwelling, with 57 Thames Street named in its photo caption. A building in that position has residents with legal protections, commercial tenants on lower floors, and fire-safety work still to come. Every lock decision has to fit all three.

The ordinary rental buildings have their own pressures. HPD inspection records show hundreds of self-closing door violations written each year in the East Williamsburg neighborhood tabulation area, 302 of them in 2026 through November 11, and a run of violations for intercoms that do not work. A reader on a door that does not latch protects nothing, so the doors come first on every job we do here. Turnover is the second pressure: in a walk-up near the Grand Street or Montrose Avenue station, every move-out is another key that may never come back, and a lock change is the only fix a key system offers.

Then there is the industrial edge. Toward Newtown Creek and along the truck streets, the doors are roll-ups, steel personnel doors and yard gates, used early and late by drivers and crews. Theft here tends to target equipment, not apartments. On May 2, 2026, News 12 reported, two people broke into two food trucks parked outside 599 Johnson Avenue about ten minutes apart and took a generator worth about $2,500 from each. Access control cannot guard a truck at the curb, and we say so. It can make sure the yard, the storage room and the shop door those generators should have been locked in will only open for the people who belong there.

We do not sell any of this with fear, and we do not sell anyone a system for its own sake. A two-family house with one tenant may need nothing more than a keypad. A forty-studio building probably needs a controller, a reader at every entrance and a clear rule about who manages the list. The point of the first visit is to tell those apart.

Building Specialists

Access Control for East Williamsburg’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware long before the customer does. These are the building types we plan for in East Williamsburg and what each one usually means at the door.

Multi-tenant industrial lofts

Three- to five-story brick or concrete buildings with heavy steel street doors, a freight elevator and many small commercial tenants. The landlord controls the entrance; each tenant controls its own suite. Access control has to respect both.

Interim multiple dwellings

Lofts where people live under the protection of the Loft Law while the owner works toward a residential certificate of occupancy. Residents may share doors with commercial tenants, and exits are often still being brought up to code.

Legal residential loft conversions

Lofts already converted to condos or rentals, often with a developer-installed fob system and a lobby carved out of a former loading area. The questions here are usually about taking over a system someone else set up.

Older walk-ups and tenements

Four- and five-story rental buildings with a street door, a vestibule door and a narrow stair. Hardware has to suit an old wood or steel frame, and rent-regulated tenants have rights that shape how fobs are issued.

One-story warehouses and yards

Block or metal-clad buildings with roll-up doors, a personnel door and a fenced yard, often near the creek. Crews start early, vendors come and go, and the controller has to be somewhere a burglar will not reach first.

Large co-op and public housing campuses

Multi-building complexes with many entrances and thousands of residents. Public housing doors belong to NYCHA, which runs its own projects; private co-ops hire their own installers and have boards to satisfy.

What the materials mean for the install

Industrial buildings are strong, and that cuts both ways. A hollow-metal door in a steel frame takes an electric strike well once the frame is opened up cleanly, but many of these frames were grouted solid with concrete when they were set, and some were welded into the opening. We find out before we quote, by tapping and, if needed, drilling a small test hole on the hinge side where it will never show. A concrete-filled frame adds labor; it rarely changes the plan. Where a frame has been bent by years of hand trucks and pallet jacks, we straighten or replace it first, because a strike cannot hold a door that sits half an inch off its stop.

Brick and concrete walls mean cable has to travel in a sensible path. In most lofts that path is up from the cellar, along a ceiling, or inside the steel channel of the frame itself, and where none of those exist we use metal raceway painted to match. Metal-clad warehouses take readers on a backing plate bolted through to the structure behind, never on the sheet metal alone. Older walk-ups are the gentlest: a wood or steel frame, a short run to the cellar and a vestibule that keeps the reader out of the rain.

One Street Door, Twenty Leaseholders: Planning a Multi-Tenant Loft

Hyperallergic’s 2012 visit to 56 Bogart Street, across from the Morgan Avenue L stop, described a building with artist studios mainly on the second and third floors, artists who had been there for a decade, and galleries arriving on the ground floor. That layout is the clearest picture of the access problem in this part of Brooklyn: one front door, a public-facing ground floor with its own hours, and upper floors full of tenants who need the building at any hour and do not want the public wandering past their doors. We do not know what is on that building’s doors today and we are not describing it. We use it because almost every studio building near Morgan Avenue has the same shape.

We plan these buildings in three layers. The street door belongs to the landlord, so the landlord or the managing agent holds the master administrator login and decides the building-wide rules: when the door is locked, who may hand out credentials, how long a departing tenant keeps access. The floors come next. A stair door or elevator lobby on each floor can be its own controlled door, so a gallery visitor who reaches the ground floor cannot walk up to the studios. The suites are the third layer. A tenant who wants a reader on its own door can have one, either on the landlord’s system as a separate group the tenant manages, or on a small system the tenant owns outright if the lease says so.

The biggest decision in a building like this is not the hardware. It is who is allowed to add people. If every tenant can add credentials to the street door, the building ends up exactly where it started with keys: nobody knows who has access. If only the landlord can, the landlord becomes the help desk for every intern and every new assistant. The setup that works best in our experience gives each tenant a sub-administrator account limited to its own group and its own suite, with a cap on how many street-door credentials it can issue, and lets the landlord see the totals without seeing anyone’s comings and goings.

What the move-in and move-out rules should say

Write it into the lease rider before the system goes live: the tenant’s credentials end on the last day of the lease unless the landlord extends them, lost fobs are reported within a set number of days, and the tenant is responsible for removing its own staff when they leave. The system enforces the end date automatically, so nobody has to remember. That single rule removes most of the arguments we hear about in buildings where a former tenant still had a key three years later.

Freight elevators and loading doors

Most of these lofts have a freight elevator or a loading door that was the real entrance when the building was a factory. Left on a key, it becomes the back door that undoes everything at the front. We put it on the same controller, usually limited to the hours the building allows deliveries and to the tenants who actually move goods. If the elevator opens directly into suites, the car itself can be limited by credential, so a delivery for the third floor cannot stop at the fourth.

Locks in a Building Still Becoming Legal: Interim Multiple Dwellings

An interim multiple dwelling, or IMD, is a former commercial or manufacturing building where people have lived long enough to be covered by the Loft Law. The Loft Board describes its job as overseeing the legal conversion of these buildings into safe, rent-stabilized housing, and the law requires the conversions to meet minimum standards of safety and fire protection under Article 7-B of the Multiple Dwelling Law. Units must be at least 400 square feet, cannot be in a cellar, and generally cannot be in most Industrial Business Zones. That last rule matters here, because so much of East Williamsburg is zoned for industry.

For access control, an IMD is a building where two things are true at once. The residents have the same need for a door that locks behind them as anyone in a walk-up, and they have legal protections that a landlord cannot simply override with a new entry system. At the same time, the building’s exits may still be the original industrial doors, some of them on the path that people would use to leave in a fire, and the owner is in the middle of a process that will bring those exits up to code. A lock that seems sensible today can block an exit tomorrow, or have to be torn out when the architect’s egress plan is approved.

So we never install in an IMD without asking for the current egress plan or, if there is none yet, talking with the owner’s architect or expediter. Every door gets sorted into one of three groups. Doors on a required exit path get hardware that always lets people out with one motion and no key, and, where they are electrically locked from outside, they release on a fire alarm and on loss of power. Doors that are not exits, such as a storage room or a freight elevator, can lock however the owner wants. And doors whose future is uncertain get the least permanent hardware possible, usually a surface-mounted reader and a strike that can be moved, until the plans are final.

Residents and commercial tenants in one building

Many IMDs still have businesses on the lower floors. The fair arrangement is two credential groups on one controller: residents get round-the-clock access to the street door and their floors, commercial tenants get the street door during their hours and their own floors only, and neither group can see the other’s records. Residents keep a key path to their own homes no matter what. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which covers smart access in multiple dwellings, applies to the residential side, and we set up the system so the owner can meet it without extra work, with a written data policy and records that clear on schedule.

When to wait and when to go ahead

If the street door does not lock today, waiting for the legalization to finish is rarely the right answer; a reader and a strike can go on that door now and move later. If the work is about interior doors on exit paths, waiting for the approved plans almost always saves money. We will tell you which case you are in at the first visit.

Rentals, Co-ops and the Rules on Fobs: What Tenants Are Owed

East Williamsburg has a lot of regulated housing: rent-stabilized walk-ups, Loft Law units, NYCHA campuses and, at its western edge, Lindsay Park, a Mitchell-Lama cooperative of seven buildings and 2,702 apartments completed in 1965. In each of these, moving the street door from keys to fobs is not purely the owner’s call. DHCR has approved fob systems in rent-stabilized buildings, but with conditions. In a 2016 ruling reported by landlordvtenant.com, the conditions included keeping the building accessible by metal key in an emergency, recording entrances but not departures, a 48-hour battery backup, free fobs for every occupant including children plus up to four free fobs for guests or employees, a replacement charge of no more than $25, accommodation for Sabbath observers, and no name, address or photo on the fob itself.

Those conditions came from one case, and DHCR decides each application on its own facts, so we treat them as a benchmark rather than a rulebook. But they are a good benchmark, and every one of them is something a well-designed system can do. When we plan a regulated building we build them in from the start: a keyed override cylinder at the street door that only the owner and the super control, readers that log entry only, a battery sized for well over a day, and a credential plan that does not cap households at two fobs.

The city adds its own layer. HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law covers fobs, cards, apps and biometric readers in multiple dwellings. Owners must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, collect only a minimum list of information, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it or use it to track tenants outside the building. The law came after a long Brooklyn fight: in 2019 tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers, a 718-unit rent-stabilized building in Brownsville, organized against their landlord’s plan for facial recognition entry, and Council Member Brad Lander proposed the KEYS Act to require physical keys, according to the Brooklyn Eagle. A rollout that ignores that history, in any Brooklyn building, starts badly.

Co-ops: who gets a fob

Co-op boards set their own rules, and Mitchell-Lama co-ops often tie fobs to the people listed on the income affidavit. A Habitat magazine board discussion of exactly that situation shows the range of answers: one manager gives each unit owner six fobs with extras at the owner’s cost, another issues fobs by the number of residents and decides emergency-contact fobs case by case. We do not make that policy, but we set up the system to carry out whichever one the board adopts, including fobs that expire, fobs flagged as emergency-only and a report the board can review.

The day the keys stop working

The worst outcome of a fob project is a building full of locked-out residents. CBS New York reported in April 2025 that when NYCHA switched Queensbridge Houses doors to electronic entry, tenants said the fobs did not work, doors jammed and some neighbors duct-taped doors open; the resident association president said resolving a problem took “anywhere between one and three days.” That is what a rushed cutover looks like. We run both systems side by side for a set period, hand out fobs in person over several days with evening hours, and only retire the old cylinder once nearly every household has a working fob in hand.

Inside the industrial business zone

The North Brooklyn plan found that industrial jobs in the district grew by 1,200 between 2010 and 2016 even as offices, arts and entertainment moved in. That mix shows up on our jobs. A woodworker and a coffee roaster can share a building with a photography studio, and each one has a different idea of when the street door should be open. Industrial tenants want early starts and late finishes, deliveries at the roll-up and no visitors. Office and creative tenants want clients buzzed in during the day and nobody else. A single schedule satisfies neither. We set up the street door with tenant-specific hours instead, so the roaster’s staff can open it at 5 a.m. while the studio’s clients cannot, and we put the shared loading door on a separate schedule tied to the building’s delivery rules.

Equipment is the other concern. The Johnson Avenue food-truck thefts in May 2026 targeted generators, which are easy to sell and easy to carry. Access control does nothing for equipment left outside, so our advice in these blocks is practical: put the valuable things behind a door that logs who opened it, put that door on a reader rather than a padlock, and add a door contact so a door forced at 3 a.m. sends an alert to someone who can act on it. Where a business wants cameras watching the same door, the reader and the recorder can be planned together so the footage and the door record line up.

Rules that apply to East Williamsburg business doors

  • Exits stay exits. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked egress door needs a release on the egress side, unlocks on loss of power and on activation of the fire alarm or sprinkler system, and a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds. Venues and other places of assembly get panic hardware on their exits; we do not electrify public exits to control the crowd.
  • Listed equipment. Locks and controllers on egress doors should be listed to UL 294, and we install only equipment that is.
  • Apartments above businesses. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, entrance doors of class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, and a building with eight or more apartments needs an intercom with voice and a door release at the main entrance. A shop’s system cannot replace those for the residents.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or biometric readers, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to the residential side. A building with only businesses is outside it, but we still keep records short and access to them limited.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State security or fire alarm installer license, with 81 hours of qualifying education, fingerprinting and an exam. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Your landlord’s door. In a multi-tenant building the street door, its frame and the wall around it are usually the landlord’s. We ask for written permission before drilling a shared door, and we leave the landlord a key override.

Loft Law buildings and converted factories

Our building is an interim multiple dwelling and the architect is drawing the legalization plans now. Which door hardware should go into those drawings instead of being added afterward?

Anything on a door that is part of a required exit path, and anything that needs power or cable through a fire-rated wall. That usually means the street door, the stair doors on each floor and any door between the residential part of the building and a commercial floor. If the architect knows those doors will be electrically locked from outside, the drawings can show the release devices, the fire alarm connection and the power supply, and the hardware gets reviewed once with everything else instead of being questioned at the end.

Doors that are not exits, such as a storage room, the freight elevator or a tenant’s own suite, can be left off the drawings and added at any point. We are happy to send the architect a one-page list of the door hardware we propose, with the model numbers and how each door behaves in an alarm and a power cut. We do not design the egress plan; that is the architect’s job, and we work to it.

In a Loft Law building, where does a tenant’s complaint about a new fob system actually go?

Loft Law units are rent-stabilized, but the Loft Board, not DHCR, is the agency that oversees interim multiple dwellings, and it describes part of its job as adjudicating and mediating disputes between owners and tenants. So a dispute about entry in an IMD that is still being legalized is a Loft Board matter in the first instance. Where the line between the Loft Board and DHCR falls depends on how far the building is through legalization. We are not lawyers, and an owner planning a change in a covered building should check the current rules with counsel before the first fob goes out.

What we can do is design a system that gives nobody a reason to complain: free fobs for every resident, a key path that still works, a written privacy policy and records that clear on schedule. In practice the fights we hear about are almost never about the hardware. They are about being locked out, being charged and being watched, and each of those is a setup choice.

The ground floor of our loft is leased to a small manufacturer and the floors above are homes. Should the manufacturer’s loading door and the residents’ street door share one controller?

They can share the controller; they should not share the rules. One controller with two credential groups is cheaper than two systems and gives the owner one place to see that every exterior door is locked at night. The manufacturer’s staff get the loading door and their own floor during their working hours. Residents get the street door and the stairs at all hours, and never the loading door. Neither group can open the other’s spaces, and the manufacturer’s manager can add and remove their own staff without seeing any resident’s records.

The one thing we would not do is make the loading door a way into the residential stair. If the building’s layout means the loading area connects to the stairs, the connecting door gets its own reader and stays locked to the commercial group, so a delivery driver who is let in at the roll-up does not end up on the residents’ floors.

The front of our warehouse is corrugated metal over concrete block. Where can a reader go so it is solid and the cable is not hanging on the outside?

On the door frame or on a steel backing plate bolted through the siding into the block behind it, never on the sheet metal alone. Corrugated panels flex when a truck passes or when someone leans on them, and a reader screwed to the skin will work loose within a season. The frame of a steel personnel door is usually the best spot, because the cable can run inside the frame’s own channel and come out on the inside of the building where nobody can reach it.

Where the frame is too narrow or full of concrete, we core a small hole through the block beside the frame, seal it against water and cover the outside with the reader itself, so there is no exposed cable at all. On the inside the cable runs in conduit to the controller, which goes in a locked office or enclosure rather than on the wall next to the door.

Studios, venues and industrial blocks

Our event space is in a converted steel building with a dozen exterior doors, and most of them are exits. Which of them should get readers at all?

Usually two or three: the staff door, the delivery or load-in door and, if the venue has one, the door to the office or the cash room. Everything else that is a public exit should stay a plain exit with panic hardware that opens with a push and an alarm contact that tells staff when it has been opened. Putting readers on public exits adds cost and creates doors that must be programmed to release perfectly every time the fire alarm sounds, with nothing to gain, since nobody should be entering through them anyway.

The useful job for access control in a venue is controlling who comes in from outside when the building is not open to the public: crews, artists, cleaners, vendors. Those doors get credentials tied to event days and the hours around them, and the exit doors get contacts that report a door propped open after load-out, which is when most venues lose equipment.

Our block is busy with trucks all day and almost deserted after seven at night. Should a reader door on a quiet industrial street be set up differently from one on a shopping street?

Yes. On a shopping street the main risk is someone slipping in behind a tenant during the day. On a block that empties out at night the risk is someone working on the door for half an hour without being seen. So the hardware is heavier: a strike with a high holding force, a latch guard plate over the gap so the bolt cannot be pried, a reader with a metal housing and the controller well inside the building. We also add a door contact, so the system knows the difference between a door opened with a credential and a door forced, and sends that alert to a phone.

Lighting matters as much as hardware. A reader in a dark doorway invites tampering and makes the person using it nervous. If the entrance has no light, we suggest one on a sensor, wired with the reader so it comes on when someone approaches.

Our bakery’s night crew comes in through the side door and the day staff use the front. Can the two doors swap roles at the shift change without anyone flipping a switch?

Yes. Each door can follow its own schedule, and each credential group can have its own hours on each door. The side door can accept the night crew’s fobs from late evening to early morning and refuse them after, while the front door unlocks for the day team’s fobs once the shift changes. If the front door should stay unlocked for customers during opening hours, it can do that on a schedule too, locking itself again at closing without anyone touching it.

We set these schedules with you at handover, and you can change them from the app. If the business adds a third shift or changes its hours for the holidays, it is a few minutes of setup, not a service call.

Contractors drive into our building-supply yard to pick up orders at a counter. How do we keep them at the counter and out of the yard?

Separate the two with a controlled door or gate. The counter area stays open during business hours, and the door or gate from the counter into the yard gets a reader that only staff credentials open. Contractors who load their own trucks in the yard can get credentials of their own that work only during your opening hours and only on the vehicle gate, not on the warehouse doors. If they lose a card or leave a company, you switch off that one card.

Where pick-ups happen at a vehicle gate rather than a counter, a call station at the gate with a camera lets the office see the truck and open the gate remotely, so the yard staff are not walking out to the gate every few minutes.

Our food trucks park on the street overnight, and generators were stolen from two trucks outside 599 Johnson Avenue last May. Can access control help when we have no lot?

Not for the trucks themselves; a reader cannot guard a curb. What it can do is change where the valuable equipment spends the night. If your business has a commissary, a garage or even a small storage room nearby, the generators, propane and cash box can go behind a door that only your staff can open, with a record of every opening and an alert if that door is forced. Moving a generator off the truck at night is a nuisance, but it is a smaller one than replacing a $2,500 generator, the value News 12 reported for each of the stolen units.

If you rent space in a shared commissary, ask the landlord whether your storage can be on its own reader. If the building already has a system, adding one door is a small job; if it does not, a standalone keypad on your storage door is the simplest start.

Apartments, co-ops and shared homes

Our Mitchell-Lama co-op gives fobs only to people listed on the income affidavit. Can a shareholder’s emergency contact who lives elsewhere have one?

That is the board’s decision, but the system can support either answer. If the board allows it, the emergency contact’s fob can be set as a separate type: it opens only the lobby and the shareholder’s own floor, it shows up on a report as an emergency credential, and it can be set to expire each year so it is reviewed along with the affidavits. If the board does not allow it, the shareholder can be given a way to open the lobby door remotely for a visitor from their phone or their apartment station instead.

Boards in the same position have answered differently. In a Habitat magazine discussion, one manager described giving six fobs per unit with extras at the owner’s cost, and another issued fobs by the number of residents and handled emergency contacts case by case. Whatever the policy, writing it down before the rollout prevents most arguments.

A tenant came home to find cash gone from the apartment and no sign of forced entry. What can an entry system honestly tell us after something like that?

It can tell you which credentials opened the doors it controls, and when. If the street door and the apartment door are both on the system, the record will show whose fob or code was used in the window when the theft happened, which narrows things down considerably. It cannot tell you who was holding a fob, and it says nothing about doors it does not control. A 2014 DNAinfo report of a theft in a Thames Street apartment near Morgan Avenue, where police found no forced entry and the tenant suspected a roommate, is the kind of case where a shared key tells you nothing and individual credentials tell you a lot.

In a residential building, remember that the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits how long authentication data is kept, generally 90 days unless it is anonymized. If something happens, ask the administrator to export the relevant records promptly, before they clear.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in East Williamsburg

These are the claims we see repeated in online cost guides, vendor blogs and the AI answers built from them, checked against what actually happens on East Williamsburg doors. Where we quote a figure, it comes from the guide named beside it, not from a search engine’s summary.

“Access control averages $2,000 to $4,000 a door.”

That range comes from Kisi’s cost breakdown, which also lists keypad doors at $1,000 to $2,500 and fob or card doors at $1,500 to $3,500 before software. ButterflyMX’s New York City guide puts fob and card systems at roughly $500 to $5,000 per door, and Swiftlane’s 2026 guide says $1,500 to $5,000 for a standard door.

Ranges that wide are honest about the country and nearly useless for one building. A door in an East Williamsburg loft can be at either end depending on whether the frame is full of concrete, whether the door is an exit, whether a controller already exists and how far the cable has to go.

Our Brooklyn list is narrower because it is for one borough and one kind of work: $1,500 to $2,500 for a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs. The written plan after the visit puts your building at a specific number.

“Plan on $30 to $100 a month in software.”

Kisi lists $30 to $100 a month for fob systems and $50 to $150 for mobile systems, and Swiftlane cites $5 to $10 per door per month. Those numbers are real for cloud platforms.

They are not unavoidable. A building that only needs to add and remove credentials on site can run on a local controller with no subscription at all. Cloud management earns its fee in buildings with many tenants, managers off site, or several properties.

We tell you which you need before you buy, and if you choose cloud management we show you the yearly cost in writing alongside the installation price, so the subscription is never a surprise in month two.

“Fobs only cost a few dollars each.”

ButterflyMX says fobs usually cost $1 to $5, and Swiftlane says $5 to $15. That is the price of the plastic. The real cost in a large building is the number of fobs, and who pays for them.

In a rent-stabilized building, DHCR has required free fobs for every occupant, up to four free extras for guests or employees and replacement fobs capped at $25 in the ruling we cite above. A studio building with forty tenants and their staff can easily need several hundred credentials.

We price fobs into the plan up front, use encrypted fobs that cannot be copied on a cheap cloner, and set up the system so lost fobs are switched off instead of reissued in bulk.

“Face recognition is the modern upgrade for apartment buildings.”

Some answers present biometric entry as the obvious next step. In New York City it is the step most likely to start a fight. The Atlantic Plaza Towers tenants in Brownsville organized against exactly that in 2019, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported it prompted a proposed bill requiring physical keys.

The Tenant Data Privacy Law that followed now governs biometric data in multiple dwellings alongside fobs and apps, with consent, minimum data and deletion rules. A biometric system in a rental building is possible, but it brings obligations a fob does not.

For homes we recommend fobs, codes and phone keys. For a business with no apartments, such as a workshop with a secure tool room, a fingerprint reader can make sense, with staff told plainly what is stored.

“Switching a building to fobs takes a day.”

Installing the hardware on one street door can take a day. Switching a building does not. Every household or tenant has to receive a working credential, learn it and stop relying on the old key before the old lock can go.

CBS New York’s April 2025 report from Queensbridge Houses shows what happens otherwise: tenants said the new fobs did not work, doors jammed and some neighbors duct-taped doors open, which defeats the point of the system.

We plan the cutover as a separate step with a date, run keys and fobs side by side for a set period and hand out credentials in person. The hardware day and the switch-over day are not the same day.

“Any building can go keyless.”

Generic answers treat keyless entry as a feature to switch on. In a regulated building it is a change in services. The DHCR ruling we cite required the building to stay accessible by metal key in an emergency, and Loft Law buildings carry protections of their own.

Keyless also fails in the most ordinary way: a dead battery on the controller, a lost phone, a power cut that lasts longer than the battery. A building with no key path at all leaves residents outside.

Every system we install keeps a keyed override at the street door, controlled by the owner and the super, and a battery sized for the doors it carries.

“Access control will stop theft.”

It controls doors. That is a lot, but it is not everything. The generators taken from food trucks on Johnson Avenue in May 2026 were on the street, where no reader reaches. A theft by someone with a legitimate credential is not prevented by the system that let them in.

What access control does well is remove the anonymous key, record which credential opened which door and alert someone when a door is forced or left open. Those are the facts that make the next decision, whether that is a lock change, a conversation or a police report, much easier.

We pair readers with good door hardware, door contacts and, where the owner wants them, cameras, and we tell you plainly what each piece will and will not do before you pay for it.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a single studio keypad and a forty-tenant loft. What changes is how long each step takes, and how many people have to agree before the next one starts.

1. Photos and a list of who holds keys

You send a photo of each door from both sides and a rough count of the tenants, households and companies that use it. We ask who controls the street door and who controls each suite.

2. Site visit and door survey

A technician checks every door, frame, closer and latch, taps the frames for concrete, traces a cable route and finds a dry, lockable spot for the controller and the battery.

3. Written door plan and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door plan naming the hardware, which groups get which doors and what each door does in a fire alarm and a power cut, priced from the Brooklyn list. Nothing is ordered until you approve it.

4. Doors made to close and latch

Before any electronics, every door is adjusted or repaired so it closes and latches on its own: closers set, frames straightened, latch guards fitted where a bolt could be pried.

5. Cable, controller and power

Cable runs inside frames, walls and ceilings or in neat metal raceway, and the controller and its battery go in a locked room or enclosure, off the floor and away from the door.

6. Credentials and groups

Fobs, cards, codes and phone keys are set up in groups for residents, studio tenants, staff and vendors, each with its own doors, hours and end dates, and each tenant administrator gets a limited login.

7. Test on battery and with the fire release

Every door is tested with mains power removed, on battery, and with the fire alarm release where the building has one, and every exit is checked to open with one motion from inside.

8. Handover and follow-up

Administrators get their logins and a one-page guide, keys and fobs are counted, the cutover date is set, and we follow up after the first weeks to adjust schedules that do not fit.

Every six months: a ten-minute building check

When the clocks change, open each controlled door and let it go: it should close and latch without a push. Press every exit button and push every panic bar from inside. Then look at the credential list and switch off anyone who has not used their fob in six months, and any tenant whose lease has ended. In a multi-tenant loft this is the single habit that keeps the system from drifting back into the old situation where nobody knows who can get in. If a door fails any of these checks, call us before it becomes a lockout.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Holds a Key Before You Look at a Single Door

This is how we approach a multi-tenant loft, not a story about one building. Before we look at hardware, we ask the owner or the managing agent for the rent roll, a floor plan if one exists and the list of companies and households in the building. Then we ask a simpler question: how many keys to the street door are out there? In a building that was a factory and is now a studio building, the honest answer is almost always “we don’t know.” That answer shapes the whole job. It tells us the cutover matters more than the reader, because the day the old cylinder is retired is the day those unknown keys stop working.

Then we walk the doors with the owner, from the roof down. We close every door and let it go. We look at what the freight elevator opens onto, which doors are exits, where the electrical room is, and whether anyone has propped a fire door with a wedge because their key does not fit it. The written plan comes out of that walk, and it nearly always includes one door the owner had forgotten about.

FAQ

East Williamsburg Access Control FAQ

Can a keypad or reader raise the motorized roll-up door on our East Williamsburg warehouse?
+

Usually yes. Most roll-up door operators have an input for an external opener, and a keypad or reader wired to that input raises the door just as the wall button does, while the system records whose code or card was used. We check the operator’s make and model on the visit, because a few older operators need an interface module. The steel personnel door beside the roll-up normally gets its own reader and strike, so staff are not raising a large door every time one person needs to walk in.

Residents of our Loft Law building want to keep their brass keys while the building is legalized. Can keys and fobs both open the street door?
+

Yes. An electric strike or electrified lock can keep its key cylinder, so the door opens with either a key or a credential. During a changeover period we often leave the existing cylinder working and later replace it with a restricted keyway held only by the owner and the super as an emergency override. Whether residents must give up their keys at all is a question about their rights under the Loft Law, which we do not decide, so we design the door to work either way.

What happens to our doors if the company that makes our readers stops supporting them?
+

The doors keep working on the hardware you have, but updates, new readers and the app may stop. That is why we prefer readers that use open wiring standards and encrypted credentials more than one platform can read, and controllers that keep working locally without the cloud. If a platform is discontinued, the strikes, exit devices, cable and power usually stay, and only the controller and readers change. We label every cable and leave a wiring diagram so any installer can do that work later.

The single-door price includes ten fobs. What if forty people use our studio building’s street door?
+

Ten fobs are included so a small building can start right away; larger buildings need more, and we price the full number into the written plan instead of surprising you later. In a studio building we often suggest fobs for tenants who come every day and phone keys or codes for staff who come occasionally, which keeps the fob count and the cost down. Every credential is individual, so forty people means forty separate credentials that can each be switched off.

Our loft already has a working Aiphone or two-wire intercom. Can a fob reader be added without replacing it?
+

Often yes. The intercom keeps calling apartments and releasing the door, and a separate reader and controller are wired to the same strike, so either one can open the door. Some intercom lines also offer a reader module that fits into the existing panel. The deciding factors are the condition of the intercom’s wiring, whether the strike is sound and whether the building wants one app for both. We look at the panel’s model on the visit and tell you which route costs less over the next ten years.

Do readers in an industrial building need anything special to cope with dust, forklifts and vibration?
+

They need the right housing and the right spot. In a dusty shop we use sealed readers and keypads rated for outdoor use even indoors, and we mount them where a pallet or a forklift mast cannot hit them, usually on the frame at the latch side rather than on a wall that loads are wheeled past. Strikes and door contacts on doors that get slammed are fitted with stronger brackets. Controllers go in a closed enclosure away from the work floor, not on a column beside a machine.

Is an electric strike or a magnetic lock better on a steel loft door?
+

An electric strike in most cases. A strike works with the door’s own latch and exit hardware, so people always leave the normal way and the door stays latched if power fails, when it is a fail-secure model. A magnetic lock holds the door with power, releases when power is cut and needs a motion sensor, a push-to-exit button and a fire alarm release under the Building Code. Mag locks have a place, on doors where a strike truly cannot fit, but they are not the easy fix they are often sold as.

What do you need from our landlord before drilling the shared street door of our building?
+

Written permission from the owner or managing agent that names the door, the work and who will own the equipment. If the reader is for your suite only and connects to nothing the landlord owns, the lease may already allow it, but we still ask. We also want to know whether the landlord plans a building-wide system, because a reader that can later join that system saves you from buying twice. The landlord gets a key override and, if they want it, administrator access to any door that is theirs.

The entrance to our loft is a pair of steel doors. Which leaf gets the lock?
+

Usually the leaf people use every day becomes the active leaf with the strike or electrified hardware, and the other leaf is held closed with flush bolts or a fixed mullion. If both leaves have to open for deliveries, a removable mullion lets the pair open fully when needed and lock as a single door the rest of the time. Pairs that are exits have their own rules about how each leaf must open, so we check that against the building’s egress requirements before choosing.

Our street door drifts open or slams. Does that have to be fixed before the readers go in?
+

Yes, and we fix it as part of the job. A reader on a door that does not close and latch on its own only records people walking through an open door. We adjust or replace the closer, straighten the frame if it is bent, set the latch so it catches every time and add a latch guard where the bolt could be pried. HPD writes hundreds of self-closing door violations in this part of Brooklyn each year, so a door that closes properly also helps keep the building out of trouble.

Can our studio tenants manage their own staff without calling the landlord every time?
+

Yes. Each tenant can have its own administrator login limited to its own group, so it can add and remove its own people and set their hours on the doors the landlord allows, without seeing anyone else’s list or records. The landlord sets a cap on how many credentials each tenant may issue and can see the totals. When a lease ends, the whole group can be switched off at once on the lease end date.
