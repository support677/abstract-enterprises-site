The Neighborhood

Why Suffolk County Properties Need Network Installation

Suffolk is not one market. It is ten towns — Babylon, Huntington, Islip, Smithtown and Brookhaven in the west and centre, then Riverhead, Southold, Southampton, East Hampton and Shelter Island on the East End — spread across about 912 square miles of land. The western towns hold the dense business addresses: the Long Island Innovation Park at Hauppauge, the Route 110 corridor through Farmingdale, Melville and Huntington Station, Bay Shore and Central Islip. East of the Pine Barrens the addresses thin out into farms, wineries, marinas, seasonal shops and houses that double as offices. A network design that is right in Melville can be wrong in Mattituck for reasons that have nothing to do with the equipment.

The first of those reasons is the wire coming in from the street. Optimum says its all-fiber network now passes nearly 900,000 locations on Long Island, and Verizon lists Huntington, Smithtown, Commack, Babylon, Islip, Bay Shore, Riverhead and Southampton among the places where it sells home internet — while telling buyers to check each address. Residents still post that Fios is “not available where I live” in northwest Suffolk, and the State Comptroller's 2021 broadband analysis counted nearly 51,700 people in Suffolk County without the last mile of wired broadband. So the first question on a Suffolk job is not how fast the plan is. It is whether the address has a second wired carrier at all, and what the network does when the only one goes dark.

The second reason is power. When Tropical Storm Isaias crossed Long Island in August 2020, PSEG Long Island's outage map showed 271,352 customers affected the next morning, and the utility estimated that 85% would be restored by the end of Friday — days, not hours. A network that is designed for a Suffolk building has to decide in advance what stays powered on batteries, what a generator can carry, and what shuts down cleanly so it comes back without a site visit.

The third reason is closer to home than most owners expect. In September 2022 Suffolk County's own government was hit by ransomware. Investigators traced the intrusion to the county clerk's office, which ran its IT separately from the rest of the county; the attackers had been inside since December 2021, found a folder of passwords to critical systems, and within three hours moved into the wider county environment. Email went down for more than 10,000 county workers and dispatchers took calls by hand for weeks. A business network is far smaller, but the lesson transfers exactly: segments that do not trust each other, administrator credentials that are not lying in a shared folder, and nothing reachable from the internet that has not been patched.

Most Suffolk businesses that call us also have cameras, door controllers or an alarm on the same network, so a network installation is usually the moment to put those on their own segment — and many pair it with access control in Suffolk on the doors the network now protects.

Suffolk facts that shape a network installation

  • Ten towns, 86 miles end to end — from the Nassau line to Montauk Point, with Shelter Island and the Fire Island communities reachable only by water — travel time is part of every design decision about remote management.
  • Nearly 51,700 Suffolk residents without last-mile wired broadband — in the Comptroller's September 2021 analysis of FCC data — and many more addresses with exactly one wired carrier.
  • About 900,000 fiber passings on Long Island — claimed by Optimum in October 2024, with up to 8 Gig symmetrical service where its fiber reaches; Verizon sells Fios or 5G home internet in named Suffolk towns but only by address.
  • 271,352 PSEG Long Island customers out — the morning after Isaias in August 2020, on the utility's own map as reported by RiverheadLOCAL; ABC7 reported almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million customers dark at the peak.
  • 26.1 million square feet of industrial space — in the Hauppauge, Central Islip and Bay Shore node, the largest on Long Island, where CoStar reported in 2024 that tenants were favouring smaller bays — so buildings keep being re-divided.
  • The county's own 2022 ransomware attack — began in one office's separately run network and spread county-wide; the county later approved about $16 million for outside vendors to repair the damage, Newsday reported.

Suffolk Network Down Right Now?

Everything showing “no internet”, phones that will not register, a card terminal that cannot reach the processor, or Wi-Fi that vanished after a storm — call (631) 407-2884 for the Suffolk line. Repair and diagnosis on an existing network is billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), and the first job is always to work out whether the fault is the carrier, the power or the network itself. Before you call, check these four things

  1. Is the carrier's box lit? The modem or fiber terminal near where the line enters the building usually has a status light. If it is red, dark or blinking in an unusual way, the problem may be on the street side and the carrier has to be called as well.
  2. Did the power blink? After a short outage some equipment comes back in the wrong order. A firewall that booted before the modem was ready sometimes needs one more restart. Note which devices are on a battery and which are not.
  3. Is it everything or one area? One dead wing or one floor usually means a switch, a cable between rooms or a tripped circuit. Everything dead at once usually means the internet connection, the firewall or the core switch.
  4. What changed recently? A new device plugged into the network, a carrier technician's visit, a moved desk or a new phone system are the most common causes of a fault that appeared out of nowhere.

Send those answers with your request and we arrive knowing whether to bring a replacement switch, a cellular router or a call already logged with the carrier. Most Suffolk outages we see are one of three things: a carrier fault, a device that did not restart after a power blip, or a single failed switch.

Building Specialists

Network Installation for Suffolk County's Architecture

The same twenty-user network goes in very differently depending on the building. These are the Suffolk building types we see most often, and what each one changes.

Re-divided Flex Buildings

Hauppauge, Central Islip, Bay Shore, Islandia, Ronkonkoma. One-storey steel and block buildings with an office front and a high-bay rear, often split into smaller bays since the original tenant left. Networks here inherit cable and closets that may serve the wrong bay.

Office Parks and Corporate Centres

Route 110 in Melville, Farmingdale and Huntington Station, Motor Parkway, Veterans Memorial Highway. Multi-tenant buildings with a landlord's telecom room, suite-level fit-outs and drop ceilings that make access-point placement straightforward.

Medical and Professional Suites

Smithtown, Commack, Stony Brook, Patchogue, Riverhead. Small suites in converted houses or low-rise buildings where the network closet is often a coat cupboard and patient Wi-Fi has to be kept away from clinical systems.

Farms, Wineries and Tasting Rooms

Riverhead, Southold, Cutchogue, Peconic, Mattituck, Bridgehampton. Several buildings on one property, long distances between them, and a tasting room that is busiest exactly when the cell network is most congested.

Seasonal Shops and Restaurants

Montauk, Sag Harbor, Southampton, Westhampton Beach, Greenport. Spaces that are full in July and nearly empty in February, often closed for weeks with nobody on site, where the network has to be manageable from a distance.

Lifted Houses and Home Offices

Lindenhurst, Babylon, Amityville, Mastic Beach and other South Shore streets where houses were raised after Sandy. Owners running a business from home want the equipment in the new ground-level enclosure; federal flood rules say what that space may be used for.

How the method changes from building to building

Steel and block. Flex buildings are usually steel roof deck on block walls. Cable runs along the joists in the high-bay and drops through the office ceiling; we keep runs off the deck where the roof heats up in summer and away from the overhead doors. Penetrations through block are sleeved and sealed, and a run that would exceed 90 metres from the closet means a second closet rather than a stretched cable.

Drop ceilings in office parks. Corporate-centre suites are the easiest Suffolk buildings to network, with one catch: the landlord controls the ceiling plenum and the main telecom room. We find out who owns the riser, whether cable in the plenum must be plenum-rated, and whether the landlord has a contractor list, before we quote.

Wood frame and converted houses. Medical suites in converted houses have small rooms, plaster or drywall partitions and closets instead of telecom rooms. Wi-Fi travels well through wood framing but not through bathrooms, fire walls or the foil-backed insulation sometimes added during renovation, so we measure rather than assume.

Outbuildings and open land. On farms and estates, the second building may be a barn, a cold-storage room or a tasting room several hundred feet away. Copper Ethernet between buildings brings lightning and ground-potential problems; we prefer fiber or a licensed-free wireless bridge with a clear line of sight, and we call 811 before anything is dug.

Salt air and unconditioned buildings. Marinas, beach clubs and barns on the East End expose equipment to salt spray, humidity and summer heat that a closet in Hauppauge never sees. Outdoor access points and enclosures there should be rated for it: a NEMA 4X enclosure adds corrosion resistance to the hose-down protection of NEMA 4, and stainless or sealed fittings outlast painted steel. Indoors, a barn or tasting-room cabinet needs ventilation or a small fan, and a UPS whose batteries are kept out of the hottest corner.

Raised houses near the water. FEMA's guidance for elevated buildings in flood zones limits the space below the lowest floor to parking, building access and storage. Network equipment belongs on the living floor, with only what must be below kept above the flood elevation and in a sealed enclosure.

A segment plan for a small Suffolk business

Whatever the building, we start from the same short list of segments and then remove the ones you do not need. Each is a separate network that cannot reach the others except through rules on the firewall.

  1. Staff. Computers, printers and file storage used by employees. The only segment that reaches shared files.
  2. Guests and customers. Internet only, with each device isolated from every other. On the East End in summer this is often the busiest segment in the building.
  3. Payment and point of sale. Card terminals and registers, allowed to reach their processor and nothing else, so that a guest device or a camera cannot see them.
  4. Cameras, door controllers and building systems. Devices that are rarely updated and should never be reachable from the internet. They talk to their recorder or controller and to nothing on the staff segment.
  5. Vendors. A place for the phone company's, the alarm company's or the software vendor's remote access to land, with time limits, instead of a tunnel into the whole network.

This is the structure the 2022 county investigation pointed toward: an intrusion that starts in one place should stop at that place's boundary. For most Suffolk businesses it costs a few segment lines on the quote, not a different budget. Owners who already have cameras on the network often move them onto that segment at the same visit; our business camera installation in Suffolk page covers the camera side.

Rules and practicalities a Suffolk business should know before the network goes in

  • County licensing is about trades, not data. Suffolk's Department of Labor, Licensing & Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement contractors and master and restricted electricians under Chapter 563 of the county code. Its published licence list has no separate low-voltage or network category, and the electricians' article does not apply inside a town or village that has its own licensing law. We work under our New York State low-voltage licence and bring in a licensed electrician for any new circuits.
  • Towns run their own permits. Building and electrical permits are issued by the town or village, not the county. The Town of Southampton, for example, runs its own electrical e-permit system. Low-voltage cabling inside an existing building rarely needs one, but new circuits, exterior trenching or a new structure do, and we check per address.
  • Dig nothing without 811. New York 811 covers Nassau and Suffolk, and asks for notice at least 48 hours and no more than ten working days before digging. Any trench for a conduit to an outbuilding starts with that call.
  • The Pine Barrens are protected land. In the Core of the Central Pine Barrens, about 105,000 acres across Brookhaven, Southampton and Riverhead, clearing or altering vegetation counts as development. A trench across a wooded Core property is a planning question first, which is one reason we often recommend a wireless bridge there.
  • Flood-zone enclosures are for storage. Under FEMA's rules for elevated buildings, space below the lowest floor is limited to parking, building access and storage. A rack belongs upstairs.
  • Card data has its own rules. If you take cards, your processor will ask you to keep payment devices away from the rest of the network. A payment segment is the simplest way to show that you have.

None of this is legal advice; it is what we check before quoting a Suffolk job, and we tell you on the quote which items apply to your address.

Your Questions Answered

Network Installation in Suffolk County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Suffolk owners, office managers and IT people ask about network installation that are specific to the county: one-carrier addresses, long outages, a famous breach, re-divided buildings, distance and licensing. Shorter practical questions are in the FAQ further down.

Where the Internet Comes From

Only one wired carrier serves our Suffolk address — how should the network be designed around that?

Assume the carrier will fail and decide in advance what the business does when it does. That starts with the firewall: choose one with two internet ports, or a spare port that can be assigned as a second connection, even if you only have one line on day one. The cost difference is small, and it means adding a backup later is a configuration change rather than a replacement.

Next comes the second path. On a one-carrier street that usually means a cellular router with an external antenna, a fixed-wireless service where a provider covers the site, or satellite for a farm or estate with clear sky. The State's ConnectALL programme has proposed awards that include fixed-wireless and low-orbit satellite service to unserved locations in Suffolk, which is a sign of where the gaps are — and of which technologies are expected to fill them.

Finally, decide what the backup carries. A cellular connection will not run a twelve-person office's video calls and cloud backups, but it will keep card terminals, phones and email alive. We write that priority into the firewall rules so the backup is used for what matters instead of being swamped by the first laptop that starts a large download.

Is a cellular backup line worth having on the East End, when summer crowds already overload the cell towers?

It is still worth having, but it has to be designed for the worst week of the year, not tested on a quiet Tuesday in March. Residents of Sag Harbor and Bridgehampton have been fitting signal boosters to their cars because towers struggle in peak summer, and North Fork residents were complaining about summer reception as long ago as 2013. A backup that works in April can crawl on the Fourth of July weekend.

Three things help. An external antenna mounted high, on the side of the building facing the nearest tower, makes a much bigger difference than the router itself. A router that takes two SIM cards from different carriers lets the network switch to whichever is less congested. And limiting what runs over the backup — payments and phones first, guest Wi-Fi last or never — keeps it useful when it is slow.

We test the backup during installation by pulling the primary connection and running the business on cellular for a while, and we write down the signal readings we saw. If the readings are poor at your address we will say so and suggest fixed wireless or satellite instead of selling you a router that will disappoint you in August.

Should the all-in-one box from Optimum or Verizon stay in charge of the network?

Usually not, though it should stay on the wall. The carrier's gateway is designed for a household: one network, a basic firewall and Wi-Fi from a single box in one corner. A business needs separate segments, rules about what can talk to what, logs, and Wi-Fi that reaches the far end of the building. We normally set the carrier box to pass the connection through to your own firewall, or place your firewall behind it when the carrier will not allow that.

Leaving the carrier box in charge also means the carrier's technician can change your network. Every time the box is replaced under a service call, its settings can return to defaults. If your firewall is the edge of your network, the carrier can swap their equipment without touching anything you depend on.

There is one exception: a very small office with three or four people, no card payments and no cameras. There, a properly configured carrier gateway with a separate guest network can be enough, and we will tell you so rather than sell you hardware you will not use.

Power Cuts, Storms and What Comes Back

We have a standby generator — does the network still need batteries?

Yes. Most standby generators take several seconds to start and transfer, and every switch, firewall and phone system on the network will reboot in that gap unless something carries it across. A modest UPS on the network core bridges the transfer and also smooths out the brief flickers that never trip the generator at all but still restart equipment.

The batteries also protect against the generator not starting, which is when you most need the network to shut down cleanly instead of losing power mid-write. We configure the UPS to signal the firewall and any storage device so they power off in an orderly way if the battery runs low.

What we size depends on what is on the generator circuit. If the generator feeds the network room, the UPS only needs minutes. If it does not, we will ask whether you would rather extend the generator circuit to the rack or buy a larger battery, and show you the cost of each.

When the battery is running down during a long outage, which equipment should stay on longest?

Decide this before the outage, because nobody will be in the building to decide it during one. In most Suffolk businesses the order we agree is: the firewall and the carrier's equipment last, so remote access and the cellular backup keep working; then the core switch; then the phones; then access points; and cameras and guest Wi-Fi first. After Isaias, when PSEG estimated 85% restoration only by the end of the week, the businesses that came through best were the ones that had kept a small core alive rather than everything for an hour.

Managed switches make this practical. Power over Ethernet to access points and cameras can be scheduled or switched off by port, so when the UPS reports low battery we can drop the high-draw devices and stretch the time left for the things that matter.

Equally important is what happens when power returns. We set every device to power on automatically, set the firewall to wait for the carrier's equipment, and check after installation that the whole network recovers from a pulled plug without anyone touching it.

After a thunderstorm a few switch ports stopped working — what does that say about the network?

It usually says that a cable leaving the building, or one running to an outbuilding, carried a surge back into the switch. Ports that feed a gate, a barn, a pole camera or a detached garage are the usual victims, and the ports nearest them on the same switch sometimes go too.

The fix is partly equipment and partly route. Communications cable that enters a building from outside needs a protector close to where it enters, and the electrical code requires one for cables exposed to lightning. Between buildings, fiber carries no current at all, which is why we prefer it for long outdoor runs on Suffolk farms and estates. Where copper has to go outside, it gets surge protection at both ends and a bonded ground.

We also look at where the switch is plugged in. A switch on the same circuit as a well pump, an HVAC compressor or a kitchen line sees more disturbance than one on a clean circuit behind a UPS. Moving the switch's power is sometimes the cheapest repair of all.

Lessons From the 2022 County Breach

What did Suffolk County government's 2022 ransomware attack show that a small business network should copy?

Three things, all of them cheap. First, a separate network for one office was not a protection in itself: the clerk's office ran its IT apart from the county's, but the attackers who got into it used passwords they found there to cross into the wider county environment within three hours. Separation only works if the boundary has rules, and if the credentials on one side do not open doors on the other.

Second, the way in was a known vulnerability in a public-facing system. Anything a Suffolk business exposes to the internet — a remote desktop, a camera recorder, a file server — needs to be either patched and monitored or, better, hidden behind a VPN so it is not exposed at all. We close inbound ports by default and open only what has a named reason.

Third, isolation has to be possible quickly. According to the Center for Internet Security's report, once the county was alerted early on September 8, the network was fully isolated by 11 a.m. On a business network that means a firewall you can reach, switches you can shut down port by port, and documentation that shows where everything is. We build all three in from the start.

Our alarm, phone and software vendors each want remote access — how should we let them in?

Through one door, onto one segment, for a limited time. Many small networks end up with three or four vendors each running their own remote-access tool on a staff computer, with permanent access that nobody remembers granting. Each of those is a route into the whole network.

We give vendors a separate segment that reaches only the equipment they support — the phone system, the alarm panel, the camera recorder — and a VPN account that can be switched on when they need it and off when they are finished. Their access is logged, so if something changes you can see who was connected.

When a vendor relationship ends, you remove one account rather than hunting for software on desktops. We write the list of vendor accounts into your documentation so whoever manages the network after us can see at a glance who can get in.

An employee who knew all the network passwords has left — what has to change?

More than the Wi-Fi password, and in a particular order. Start with the accounts that control everything: the firewall's administrator login, the switch and access-point management login, the carrier account and any cloud management portal. Then the VPN accounts, then shared Wi-Fi keys, then the logins on cameras and building systems.

If nobody else knows those passwords, a takeover visit is the fastest route. We reset the equipment where necessary, re-enter the configuration, and give the new credentials to the person you name, not to a technician. If the network was never documented, this is also the moment to do it.

Going forward, the fix is personal accounts instead of shared ones wherever the equipment allows it, so that removing one person does not mean changing everything. Most business firewalls and managed Wi-Fi systems support individual administrator logins; we set them up that way at installation.

Suffolk Buildings That Change the Design

Our Hauppauge flex unit used to be part of a bigger bay — how do we know which cables and closets are really ours?

By tracing them, end to end, before anything is reused. When a large bay is divided, cable that once served the whole tenancy often still runs to a closet that is now on the other side of a demising wall, or into a neighbour's suite. CoStar reported in 2024 that the Hauppauge, Central Islip and Bay Shore node had the most available industrial space on Long Island, driven partly by tenants wanting smaller bays — which means this situation is common.

We tone and test every run, label what is yours, and mark what belongs to the neighbour or to the landlord. Cable that terminates in somebody else's space is either re-routed to your own closet or abandoned and noted. Your network should not depend on access to a room you do not lease.

Then we check the carrier's line. In a re-divided building the demarcation point is often in the original tenant's area, and extending it to your space is a job the landlord and carrier have to agree on. We start that conversation early because it is usually the item with the longest lead time.

Our house was raised after Sandy and the new ground-level space looks perfect for the network equipment — is it?

No, and not only because of the rules. FEMA's guidance for elevated buildings in flood zones limits the enclosed area below the lowest floor to parking, building access and storage, and asks that equipment below the flood elevation be able to withstand flooding. The National Weather Service recorded two to five feet of water across much of the area south of Montauk Highway in Lindenhurst, Amityville and Babylon during Sandy, and repeated moderate flooding in the years since.

The equipment belongs on the living floor, in a closet or a small wall cabinet. Only the carrier's entrance needs to be lower, and carriers can usually bring their line up to a terminal above the flood elevation. Cables that pass through the enclosure run inside sealed conduit and come up through the floor.

For a home office this also improves Wi-Fi, because the access point ends up on the floor where people work instead of below it. If you also want cameras or a gate controller in the enclosure, we use outdoor-rated, sealed equipment mounted as high as the space allows.

We close our Montauk shop from November to April — can the network be left running with nobody there?

Yes, if it was designed to be. Leave on the firewall, the carrier's equipment, one switch, any cameras and environmental sensors you want to watch, and the cellular backup. Turn off guest Wi-Fi and staff access points by schedule. The remaining load is small enough for a modest UPS to carry through short winter outages.

Remote management is the whole point. The firewall and switches should be reachable over a VPN so that a fault in January can be diagnosed from the mainland instead of waiting for someone to drive out. We also set up alerts for a lost connection or a UPS on battery, sent to the owner and to whoever supports the network.

In spring, the network should come back with one visit. We keep a short reopening checklist with the documentation: re-enable the schedules, update firmware, check the battery, and walk the space with a meter before the first busy weekend.

A new building near the Ronkonkoma station handed us jacks that have never been used — what should be checked before move-in?

Every jack, against the drawings, before your equipment arrives. New construction is handed over in a hurry and the cabling contractor's test results, if they exist, are often for the building rather than your suite. We test each run for wiring and length, confirm which patch-panel port each jack lands on, and note anything that fails.

Then check where the carrier comes in. In large new developments like Station Yards, the carrier's entrance and the riser are often finished before the tenant spaces, and the path from the building's main room to your suite has to be confirmed rather than assumed. Ordering service early, against a confirmed demarcation point, is the difference between opening with internet and opening without it.

Finally, look at the ceiling. New buildings often have access-point locations roughed in by an architect who never saw your furniture plan. We check them against how the space will actually be used and move them before the ceiling is finished, which is far cheaper than moving them later.

Distance, Branches and Daily Use

We have an office in Melville and a branch in Riverhead — can they work as one network?

Yes. The usual design is a firewall at each site with a permanent encrypted tunnel between them, so files, phones and printers at one site are reachable from the other as if they were down the hall. The two sites keep their own internet connections, so a fault at one does not take down the other.

What makes it work in Suffolk is planning for the distance. Configuration has to be managed remotely, equipment at both ends should be the same family so one person can support both, and each site needs enough local capability — local printing, local Wi-Fi, local phone survival — to keep working if the tunnel drops.

We build and test both ends, document the addressing so the sites do not overlap, and include a home-office profile if owners or staff work from home. Adding a third site later then means repeating a known pattern instead of starting over.

How much of the network will our security cameras actually use?

Less than most people fear per camera, but more than the guest Wi-Fi expects in total. A recorder's own screen shows the bitrate each camera is sending; the recorder screen pictured on this page, from another AESS job, shows its active channels running between about 86 and 453 kilobits per second, and higher-resolution cameras or busy scenes send more. Twenty cameras recording continuously add up to a steady load that never stops.

The more important question is where that traffic goes. Cameras should talk to their recorder on their own segment and not cross the office network at all. If people view cameras from phones, that traffic leaves through the firewall, and the upload speed of a Suffolk cable line is often the real limit. We check the upload figure on your plan before promising remote viewing at full quality.

Power is the other cost. Every PoE camera draws from the switch's power budget. Under the IEEE 802.3at standard a device can draw up to 25.5 watts, and 802.3bt raises that to 51 or 71.3 watts. Pan-tilt cameras with heaters and newer access points use much of that, so we size switches for the cameras you plan to add, not only the ones on day one.

Staff say their phones have no signal inside our steel building — is Wi-Fi calling enough?

Often, yes, provided the Wi-Fi is designed for voice. Steel roof decks and block walls stop cell signal well, which is why so many flex buildings in western Suffolk are dead zones indoors. Most current phones can carry calls over Wi-Fi instead, and for a warehouse or a back office that is usually the cheapest fix.

Voice needs more than coverage. It needs access points close enough that a phone can move between them without dropping the call, a little priority for voice traffic on the network, and a guest or staff segment that the phones are allowed to use. We walk the building on a call during the post-installation check rather than only measuring signal strength.

If calls must also work for visitors or for 911 from every corner, or if the building is very large, a carrier-approved signal booster or distributed antenna system may be needed. That is specialist equipment with its own approvals; we will tell you when you have crossed that line rather than stretch Wi-Fi to cover it.

Licensing and Who Is Allowed to Do the Work

Does Suffolk's home improvement licence cover network wiring in a home office?

Suffolk's home improvement licence, issued through the county's Department of Labor, Licensing & Consumer Affairs, covers repairs and alterations to residential property, and RiverheadLOCAL reported that more than 9,000 such licences were active countywide in 2018, with civil penalties for unlicensed work. The county code separately excludes electrical work, as it defines it, from the home improvement article and licenses that through master and restricted electrician licences.

Network cabling sits awkwardly between the two. The county's published list of licence types has no separate low-voltage category, and the electricians' article does not apply in towns or villages with their own licensing laws. We work under our New York State low-voltage licence, carry liability insurance, and bring in a licensed electrician for any new power circuit, such as a dedicated outlet for a rack.

Our honest advice to a homeowner is to ask any contractor for the licence numbers and insurance certificate they work under and to check them on the county's online search. If the job involves opening walls in a house, ask specifically whether the contractor holds a Suffolk home improvement licence or is working under someone who does.

Do towns and villages in Suffolk add their own rules on top of the county's?

Yes, in two ways. Permits for building and electrical work come from the town or incorporated village, and each one sets its own process; the Town of Southampton, for instance, runs its own online electrical permit system. And Chapter 563 of the county code says the electricians' licensing article does not apply in a town or village that had already enacted its own licensing law, or in a town that opts out.

For most network installations inside an existing commercial building, no permit is needed for low-voltage cable. The exceptions are new electrical circuits, work that changes fire-rated walls or ceilings, exterior work such as trenching or new conduit, and anything in a new structure.

We check each address before quoting and note on the quote whether anything needs a permit, who pulls it, and how long that town usually takes. If the answer is “no permit”, the quote says that too.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Network Installation in Suffolk County

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so we cannot quote what it says about Suffolk on any given day. These are claims that cost guides, retailers and generic advice sites commonly publish about business network installation — the material AI answers draw on — with what we find on Suffolk jobs.

1. “A small business network costs a few thousand dollars”

National cost guides do publish figures in that range. Fixr's guide, updated January 31, 2025, puts the average hardwired home network at $3,800, within a range of $2,500 to $4,500 for about 2,000 feet of Cat6 and up to eight connections in new construction, and technician labour at $50 to $100 an hour.

Those numbers describe cable in an open house, not a business network. They do not include a firewall with segments, managed switching, access-point design, a backup carrier, batteries or documentation, and they assume new construction rather than a re-divided flex bay or an occupied suite.

Our Suffolk figures are published further down this page, line by line, so you can compare like with like. The honest summary is that the cabling is often the smaller part of a business network's cost; design and configuration are the larger part, and they are the part that decides whether it survives an outage or an intrusion.

2. “Faster internet will fix slow Wi-Fi”

Sometimes, but on Suffolk jobs the bottleneck is more often inside the building. A single access point at the front of a deep flex unit, a mesh kit hopping wirelessly through block walls, or a guest network competing with staff on the same channel will feel slow on any plan.

The opposite problem is real too. Where the only carrier offers modest upload speeds, cloud backups, video calls and remote camera viewing all compete for the same narrow upload, and no amount of Wi-Fi work will help.

So we measure both. A speed test at the carrier's equipment, a second at the firewall and a third on Wi-Fi at the far end of the building tell you exactly where the slowdown is before you spend money on either.

3. “Any electrician can wire a network”

An electrician can certainly pull cable, and in Suffolk master and restricted electricians are licensed by the county under Chapter 563, whose definition of the electrical field is broad. But terminating, testing and certifying data cable, designing segments and configuring firewalls and wireless are different skills.

The county's licence list has no separate network category, so the licence on a contractor's van tells you less than you might think. What matters is who designs the network, who configures it and what documentation you receive.

We hold a New York State low-voltage licence and bring in a licensed electrician for new circuits. Ask any contractor for test results for each cable and a copy of the configuration; if they cannot provide them, the work cannot be checked.

4. “Mesh Wi-Fi covers any building”

Mesh systems are excellent in houses and apartments, and some advice sites recommend them for small businesses too. Their weakness is that each satellite relays traffic wirelessly to the next, and every hop costs speed.

Suffolk flex buildings, with block walls, steel decks and racking in the high-bay, defeat wireless hops quickly. So do farm properties where the tasting room is across a lane from the office. In both cases the access points need a cable or a fiber or bridge link behind them.

Where a mesh kit is already installed, we can often reuse the units as wired access points if the model supports it, which keeps the cost down while removing the slowdown.

5. “Cat6 is all you need for access points”

Cat6 will carry today's access points, and for many Suffolk offices it is the right choice. But the telecommunications industry's own guidance for wireless access-point cabling, TIA TSB-162-A, recommends Category 6A for access-point runs, partly because newer access points can use more than one gigabit and draw more power.

The extra cost is in the cable and the time, not in the design, and it only matters on the runs that feed access points. Workstation drops can stay on Cat6.

On a building you expect to occupy for many years, we price both and let you choose. On a short lease, Cat6 to the access points is a reasonable saving, and we say so on the quote.

6. “A UPS keeps the network running through an outage”

A UPS keeps the network running through a short outage. Retail product pages tend to show runtimes at light loads, which look generous until switches full of PoE devices are connected.

Long Island outages are not always short. After Isaias, PSEG Long Island was estimating restoration for most customers by the end of the week. No reasonably sized battery carries a whole network that long.

The realistic design is a battery that bridges brief interruptions and a generator transfer, a shutdown order that keeps the firewall and backup carrier alive longest, and equipment set to restart on its own. That is what we install and test.

7. “The ISP's router has a firewall, so you are protected”

It does have a basic one, and for a household it blocks the most obvious traffic from outside. What it does not do is stop something already inside from reaching everything else.

That was the pattern in the county government's own 2022 breach: the attackers entered through one office and then moved across. On a business network the equivalent is a compromised laptop on guest Wi-Fi reaching the card terminals, or a camera recorder with a default password reaching the file server.

Segments with rules between them, closed inbound ports, individual administrator accounts and updated firmware are what turn a router into protection. None of them is exotic; they just have to be set up on purpose.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Carrier's Box, Not the Desk

On a Suffolk walk-through I go to the place where the internet comes into the building before I look at a single desk. Whose box is it, what lights are on, and is there a second line or the stub of an old one? On a western Suffolk office park there are usually two or three carriers in the landlord's room; on a farm lane east of Riverhead there may be one, on a pole across the road. That one box decides whether the design needs a cellular antenna, a fixed-wireless dish or a satellite terminal before we talk about access points.

Then I find the room that will hold the network and look at its power: which circuit, what else is on it, and whether a generator feeds it. I ask the owner what happened during the last long outage, because people remember exactly which systems they missed. Only then do we walk the space for Wi-Fi.

This is how we approach every Suffolk network job; it describes our method, not a particular customer's building.

Do you install networks in Suffolk homes, or only in businesses?
+

Both. Many Suffolk owners run a business from home, and a home office with a firewall, separate segments for family devices and work equipment, and a wired access point on the working floor is a common job. The pricing is the same line by line; a home simply needs fewer of the lines.

Can you design the network from our floor plan before anyone visits?
+

We can produce a first draft and a budget from a floor plan, photos of the ceilings and the network room, and the street address. The draft says what still needs to be confirmed on site, usually the carrier's entrance, the ceiling construction and the route to any second building. The site visit then turns the draft into a fixed quote.

What happens if the internet provider's installation date slips past our opening date?
+

We plan for it. The network can be installed, configured and tested on a temporary cellular connection, so that on the day the carrier arrives only the internet port changes. Where a cellular backup is part of the design anyway, it can carry payments and phones for the first days of trading while the main line is outstanding.

Will you need to get onto the roof or into the attic?
+

Only where the design calls for it, and we tell you in advance. Antennas for a cellular backup or a wireless bridge are usually mounted high on a wall or on a roof edge, and in some single-storey buildings the attic or roof deck is the only cable route. Roof work in a multi-tenant building needs the landlord's permission and sometimes their roofer's involvement.

Can the same network carry our desk phones?
+

Yes, and it usually should. Desk phones powered from the switch need PoE budget, a little traffic priority so calls stay clear, and a place on the outage plan so they keep working on battery. We coordinate with your phone provider on the settings their system expects.

How far ahead should an East End business book work before the summer season?
+

Early spring is best. The carrier's lead times, the landlord's approvals and any equipment orders all take longer than the installation itself, and the weeks just before Memorial Day are the busiest of the year for seasonal businesses. Booking in March or April leaves time to test the network, and the backup, before the first full weekend.

Can we check the network's status ourselves from a phone?
+

Most of the business-grade firewalls and Wi-Fi systems we install have a management app or web dashboard. We set up a read-only or full account for the person you name, so you can see whether the internet is up, how many devices are connected and whether the UPS is on battery, without needing to change anything.

If we move out of the building, can the network come with us?
+

The equipment can; the cable in the walls usually stays with the building, and your lease may say so. Firewalls, switches, access points and racks can be removed and reinstalled at the new address, and the configuration and documentation carry over. We recommend moving the design as well as the boxes, because the new building will have its own carrier situation and layout.
