We plan entry control from that minute between the car and the latch: driveway video you can check before you get out, side and back doors that open fast and lock themselves, garages and basements on the same codes, and locks that suit a hundred-year-old door.

The Neighborhood

Why Marine Park Properties Need Access Control

Marine Park grew up around water and a promise of open space. Gerritsen Creek, the westernmost inlet of Jamaica Bay, runs up through the middle of it, and around 1920 the part of the creek north of Avenue U was turned into an underground storm drain. In 1917 Frederick B. Pratt and Alfred T. White offered the city about 150 acres of marsh for a park; after a seven-year delay the city accepted, and the prospect of a park brought builders. By 1926 the new homeowners had formed the Marine Park Civic Association, which the present-day community association says was incorporated on January 10 of that year. The streets they settled are lettered avenues and numbered East streets in a tidy grid, mostly squared off, as Wikipedia puts it, by Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway.

The houses came in a short burst. Brick Underground’s 2022 guide to buying in the neighborhood describes most of the housing as one- and two-family detached and semi-detached residences dating from the 1910s to the 1940s, with frame houses and duplexes typically around 1,100 square feet and larger brick houses running from about 1,200 to 2,200. Brownstoner, writing in 2020 about a 1920s house on Avenue R, noted that newspaper ads of the period sold “English style” and “brick period” homes with a feature that was newly important then: a garage. That house had its garage at the rear, a driveway long enough for more than one car, a back yard paved over completely, and a basement that could be reached by a stair off the kitchen or from the rear yard. A 2026 comparison by a local sales team put Marine Park’s median lot at about 1,742 square feet and described detached or semi-detached homes with modest yards, garages and narrower driveways.

Then there is the commute. There is no subway station inside Marine Park; the nearest is Kings Highway on the Brighton line in Midwood, and residents ride one of about a dozen bus routes to reach a train or simply drive. Brick Underground’s brokers pointed out that the streets are not metered and have no alternate-side parking, and that the neighborhood is home to generations of city employees, notably police officers, firefighters and teachers. Put those facts together and you get a particular way of arriving home: by car, often at an hour when the block is asleep, parking in a driveway, a rear garage or at the curb, and walking to whichever door is closest. On a semi-detached house that is usually the side door. On a lot with a rear garage it is the back door.

The local news of the past year shows why that walk deserves attention. News 12 Brooklyn reported that on August 18, 2026, at 4:40 a.m., a group of six people pulled a 66-year-old man from his car in front of a house on East 33rd Street, two of them displaying guns, and drove off in it. On July 30, 2026, just before 6:30 p.m., a 71-year-old man was robbed at gunpoint as he was getting into his car in front of 2462 Flatbush Avenue. On February 23, 2026, at 4:30 in the afternoon, four people entered a home on East 34th Street and Avenue N and carried out two televisions. And in October 2018 the Brooklyn Paper reported a 5:30 a.m. home invasion on Madison Place in which a retired police lieutenant and his wife were attacked; police said the couple had been burglarized the previous July.

None of those incidents would have been prevented by a lock alone, and we say so plainly throughout this page. What they share is timing and place: the minutes around a parked car, the early morning, the empty afternoon house. Those are exactly the moments an entry plan can be designed around — less time standing at a door, a clear look at the driveway before you leave the car, a door that locks behind you without being checked, a record of what opened and when, and light where you walk. Marine Park’s houses also make that design specific: old wooden doors with glass panels, side doors set deep along narrow driveways, garages a full lot-length from the kitchen, and basements that open to the yard.

The 63rd Precinct, which patrols Marine Park along with Bergen Beach, Mill Basin and part of Flatlands, reported 119 burglaries and 135 grand larcenies of autos in 2022, according to the precinct CompStat figures cited by Wikipedia, against a drop of 82.7% in crime across all categories since 1990. Safer is not the same as untouched: cars are still stolen, houses are still entered, and residents trade tips about package thieves in a local Facebook group. Our job is to make the everyday route in and out of the house quicker, better lit and better recorded, without turning a family home into a fortress.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Marine Park’s Architecture

Almost everything in Marine Park was built in the same few decades, but not to the same pattern. The house type decides which door gets used, how far it is from the car and how cable gets to it.

Semi-detached brick pairs

Two houses sharing one wall, each with its own side. Expect a narrow driveway down the open side, a side door partway along it, a garage or parking pad at the back and a basement under the whole house. The side door is the daily door.

Detached brick houses

Free-standing “brick period” homes of the 1920s and 1930s, some with Tudor or Colonial Revival details, set on lots with a driveway on one side. Doors are often original, frames are solid, and cable can usually run under the first floor.

Frame houses and duplexes

Smaller wood-frame houses, many around 1,100 square feet, some with siding added later. Mounting is different: we fasten into framing, not siding, and seal every hole against water running down the wall.

Two-family houses

Owner downstairs, a relative or tenant upstairs, sometimes a shared entrance hall. These need one code list with two households on it, and a clear decision about who controls which door.

Houses with a converted rear garage

Garages behind the house rebuilt as offices, gyms or guest rooms, occasionally with a bathroom. The old garage door is often fixed shut and a people-door added; that door now needs house-grade locking.

Small co-ops, condos and mixed-use buildings

A handful of co-op buildings on the southern and western edges, small low-rise condominiums in the center, and shops with apartments above on the commercial streets. These follow apartment-building rules, covered further down this page.

What century-old doors, brick and siding mean for the install

Original wooden doors. A door hung in 1925 has had a century to sag, swell and be repainted. Before any keypad deadbolt goes on, the door has to close on its own and the bolt has to slide into the strike without anyone lifting the handle. If it does not, an electronic lock will jam, drain its batteries trying to throw the bolt, or report itself locked when it is not. A large share of our Marine Park quotes include planing, a new strike plate, longer hinge screws into the stud or a new door closer, priced separately so you can see it.

Glass in and beside the door. Many period doors have a glass panel at the top or a sidelight beside them. A keypad lock does not change what a broken pane allows: if a hand can reach the inside thumb-turn through the glass, a thief can unlock any deadbolt that has one. We deal with that by placing the lock out of reach of the glass, fitting laminated glazing film or a security glass insert, or using a lock whose inside turn can be disabled when the house is empty, chosen to keep the house easy to leave in an emergency.

Brick, block and siding. On brick houses the cable from an outside keypad or camera goes through the wall once, in a sleeve at a mortar joint, sloped so water runs out, and sealed. On frame houses we find a stud or block behind the siding before fastening anything, because aluminum and vinyl siding will not hold a reader and will let water in behind a careless screw. Surface cable on the face of the house is a last resort, run in paintable raceway.

Basements and joists. The basement is usually the best place in a Marine Park house for a door controller, a power supply and a battery, because cable can travel along the joists to the side door, the back door and up to the porch with very little drilling. The condition is that it is dry. Marine Park sits next to a tidal marsh, and residents on Reddit have warned newcomers about basement units in low parts of southern Brooklyn; we mount equipment high on the wall, never on the floor, and ask where water has reached before choosing the spot.

Car to Door

The Minute Between the Car and the Latch

Ask a Marine Park family how they get into their house and the answer is rarely “the front door.” It is the side door, because that is where the driveway goes, or the back door, because that is where the garage is. And they almost always start the trip sitting in a car. That is the stretch of the day this page is built around: from the moment the engine stops to the moment a lock clicks shut behind you. On a typical lot it is somewhere between twenty and forty steps, much of it along the side of the house, often out of sight of the street and, in winter, in the dark at both ends of the working day.

Each part of that walk can be shortened, lit or watched. Before you get out, a camera on the house can show you the driveway, the side path and the door. As you pull in, a light can come on. At the door, a code, a fob or a phone can open the lock in a second or two instead of a fumble for keys. Once you are inside, the lock can throw itself and confirm it. And if you are leaving at 5 a.m., the house can tell you the doors you did not check are locked, so nobody walks back to try them. None of this is exotic; it is ordinary equipment arranged around the route you actually use.

What it cannot do is stop a robbery on the sidewalk, and we will not pretend otherwise. The August 2026 carjacking on East 33rd Street happened to a man sitting in his car; the July 2026 robbery on Flatbush Avenue happened as a man was getting into his. A door system does not reach that far. What it can do is reduce the time you spend sitting or standing outside, let you look before you open the car door, and make the house itself a faster, safer place to get into and out of. Where a household wants more, the next step is usually better lighting and cameras, and sometimes an alarm, rather than more locks.

Household Hours

Doors for Houses That Never Keep Nine-to-Five Hours

A lot of Marine Park houses are run on rotating schedules. Uniformed city jobs work tours that start before dawn or end after midnight; hospital and transit workers do the same; teachers leave early and coach late; and the retirees who stayed in the houses they raised families in keep their own hours. That breaks the assumption built into a lot of consumer security equipment, which is that a house is empty from nine to five and occupied at night. A Marine Park house may be empty from 4 p.m. to 1 a.m. one week and from 6 a.m. to 3 p.m. the next, with someone asleep upstairs in the middle of the day.

We set systems up for that. Alerts are tied to whether anyone is home, not to the clock, so a door opening at 2 p.m. is noticed when the house is empty and ignored when a household member is asleep upstairs. Chimes and lock sounds can be turned down at the door nearest the bedroom. Each person has their own code, so the record shows who came in at 1 a.m. without anyone having to ask. And when a relative stays over to watch the kids during a double shift, they get a code that ends when the stay does, rather than a spare key that never comes back.

The same households often want one more thing: to be able to open the door for somebody from wherever they are. A phone-controlled lock can let in a plumber, a relative or a neighbor dropping off a package while you are at work, and a door station lets you see and talk to them first. We set those permissions so only named adults can unlock remotely, every remote unlock is recorded, and the lock relocks itself after a short delay whether or not anyone remembers.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Marine Park

Marine Park is mostly houses, but its edges and a few inner streets carry shops, restaurants, offices and small apartment buildings. The businesses named below are listed only to describe the neighborhood’s commercial streets; none of them is an AESS client.

Flatbush Avenue: the main commercial corridor

Brick Underground calls Flatbush Avenue the community’s commercial corridor, and its 2022 guide lists the kind of businesses that line the neighborhood’s streets: Italian dining rooms, pizzerias, a diner it described as Marine Park’s only remaining one, taverns and public houses, hero shops and delis. For a restaurant or a shop here, entry control is usually about the back and side doors: a staff door that opens to a code during working hours and stays locked otherwise, a cellar or stockroom door limited to a manager, and an opener’s and closer’s record that shows when the building was unlocked and locked.

Avenue U by the park

The southern edge of the neighborhood runs along Avenue U, where the Salt Marsh Nature Center sits at East 33rd Street. Businesses and offices on this side tend to be small, often with a single customer entrance and a rear door onto a lot or a lane. A keypad or reader on the rear door, a camera that looks at it from inside and a closer that actually shuts it are the core of most jobs.

Kings Highway on the northern edge

Kings Highway marks the top of Marine Park and links it to Midwood and to the subway at the Brighton line. Where a shop or office along it has an upstairs suite reached by a street door beside the storefront, it shares a pattern we see across Brooklyn. When that door serves both a business and apartments, the two groups need separate credentials and separate schedules on the same door.

Gerritsen Avenue and the inner avenues

Gerritsen Avenue, which Wikipedia describes as a major traffic corridor and illustrates with photographs of brick row houses, runs north–south through the neighborhood, and the lettered avenues carry corner delis and small groceries; Brick Underground names one on Quentin Road. A corner store with an apartment above usually needs the same two things: a shop door that locks and unlocks on its own schedule, and a residential door that never does.

Small co-ops and condominiums

The few co-op buildings on the southern and western borders and the small condominium developments in the center are run by boards rather than by individual owners. Their front doors fall under apartment-building rules, their residents need individual credentials, and their boards need someone besides the installer able to add and remove people. Our Brooklyn access control hub page covers larger buildings in more depth.

Codes and laws we work to

  • Getting out always comes first. The NYC Building Code’s egress-door rules in section 1010.1.9 require exit doors to open from the inside without a key or special knowledge; electrically locked doors released by a sensor or a push button must unlock if power fails (section 1010.1.9.8), and magnetic locks released by door hardware must release immediately with one motion (1010.1.9.9). On a house we meet the same intent: every door we electrify opens from inside with a single ordinary motion.
  • Apartment buildings have extra duties. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in covered apartment buildings and an intercom for buildings of eight or more apartments. Those rules apply to the co-ops and mixed-use buildings at the edges of Marine Park, not to a one- or two-family house.
  • Tenant data rules start at three apartments. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) governs keyless entry systems in multiple dwellings: owners must give tenants a written data policy, collect only minimal data and delete authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. A typical Marine Park house is outside it, but a small co-op or a building with apartments over shops may not be.
  • Listed equipment. We use access control units listed to UL 294 and, where a motorized gate is involved, operators and safety devices meeting UL 325.
  • Licensing. Low-voltage access control work in New York is licensed; ours is NYS license #12000287431. Line-voltage electrical work, where a job needs a new circuit, is done by a licensed electrician.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Marine Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Marine Park households and shop owners bring to us, grouped by where they come up: on the way in from the car, inside an older house, and in homes that run on shift-work hours. Each answer is written for this neighborhood; the general questions about cost and equipment are covered in their own sections below.

From the Curb and the Driveway to the Door

We get home from overnight tours around 5 a.m. Can we check the driveway and the side path on a phone before we get out of the car?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things we set up in Marine Park. A camera mounted high on the side wall of the house, under the eave or on a short arm clear of the gutter, can see the driveway from the street to the back of the lot, the side path and the door itself. You open the app while the car is still running, look, and then decide to get out. The view needs to load in a couple of seconds, so we put the camera on a wired connection back to the router in the house rather than relying on a weak Wi-Fi signal at the far end of the driveway.

Two details make the difference at that hour. The camera needs good night performance or a light it can rely on, because a grainy picture of a dark driveway is not reassuring. And it should be aimed so it shows your property and the path, not the inside of the neighbors’ windows across a narrow drive. On semi-detached houses we often use privacy masking so the part of the picture that falls on the house next door is blacked out.

Our side door sits halfway down a narrow driveway, out of sight of the street. Which way of unlocking it is quickest in the dark: a phone, a fob, a code or a key?

In our experience a fob or a code is quickest, and a key is slowest. A fob works in a second without looking: you hold it to the reader and the lock releases. A backlit keypad takes a few seconds and works with gloves if the buttons are large. A phone credential can be very fast if it works from the pocket or the watch, but slower if you have to unlock the phone and open an app with your hands full. A key in a dark doorway, on a ring with six other keys, is the thing most households are trying to get away from.

For a door that is out of sight of the street, we usually recommend two methods on the same lock: a fob or phone for everyday use and a personal code as the backup if the fob is left in the other car. A light over the door, triggered as you approach, matters as much as the credential, because it lets you see the keypad and see the path behind you.

A man was pulled from his parked car outside a house on East 33rd Street at 4:40 one morning. Can an entry system shorten the time we spend sitting in the car out front?

It can help with part of it, and it is worth being clear about which part. News 12 reported that the victim in that August 2026 case was sitting inside his car; no lock or reader on a house reaches into a car at the curb. What a well-planned entry does is take away the reasons people sit in parked cars outside their own homes: waiting to see whether the driveway is clear, finishing a call before a fumbling walk to a dark door, or looking for the right key.

So the practical steps are the ones that make getting from the car into the house fast and predictable: a camera view you check before you stop, a light that comes on as you arrive, parking as close to the door you use as the lot allows, and a lock that opens in a second. Once inside, the lock throws itself. If you see something on the camera you do not like, you do not have to get out at all. Beyond that, the right advice comes from the 63rd Precinct’s community officers, not from a door installer.

When we pull away before dawn, can the house confirm every door is locked without anyone walking back to check?

Yes. Each electrified door gets two pieces of information to the system: whether the bolt is thrown and whether the door is actually closed, from a small sensor in the frame. With both, the app can show one line that says every door is shut and locked, or tell you which one is not. Many households set the locks to throw automatically a short time after the door closes, so the only case that needs attention is a door left ajar.

Some systems can also lock everything when the last phone in the household leaves the area. We set that up only on request and always with a check, because a phone that loses its location for a moment can lock a door on someone still in the yard. A simple button in the app, pressed as you back out, is usually more reliable.

Our garage is at the back of a driveway we share with the house next door. Can the garage side door and our back door use the same code without the neighbors’ code working on either?

Yes. One controller, or one family of compatible locks, can run the back door of the house and the side door of the garage with a single list of people. Each person in your household has one code or one fob that works on both; the neighbors are simply not on your list, even if their own garage sits a few feet from yours and uses the same brand. The garage door can also get a narrower list than the house: a teenager may have the house but not the garage where the tools are kept.

The practical question is how the garage connects to the house. If a cable can run underground or along the fence line on your side of the property, the garage lock is wired like any other door. If it cannot, a battery lock with a wireless bridge or a cellular unit will do. We check the property line with you before running anything along a shared driveway, so nothing ends up on the neighbor’s side.

Inside a 1920s Marine Park House

Our side door opens onto a small landing, with steps up to the kitchen and steps down to the basement. Where should the lock and the sensors go?

This split landing is one of the most common layouts we see in 1920s Brooklyn houses, and it changes the plan. The outside side door is the obvious place for the keypad or reader, because that is the door the household uses. But the landing also connects the basement to the kitchen, and in many houses the basement has its own door to the yard; Brownstoner described a 1920s Avenue R house whose basement could be reached from the kitchen or from the rear yard. If the yard door is forced, the landing gives a straight route upstairs.

So we usually put the electric lock on the outside side door, a door sensor on that door and on the basement’s yard door, and a simple lock on the door at the top of the kitchen steps that can be set at night or when the house is empty. That inner lock always opens with one motion from the basement side, because the basement stair may be someone’s way out in a fire, and it never needs a key to leave the basement.

Our carport is open on two sides. Will a keypad mounted beside the door under it get wet?

It will get wind-driven rain and, in winter, snow blown in from the open sides, so we treat it as an outdoor location even though there is a roof overhead. That means a keypad or reader rated for outdoor use, mounted on the side of the door away from the open edge of the carport, with the cable entering from behind through a sealed hole, never from the top where water runs down the wall.

Keypads mounted under carport roofs also suffer from something less obvious: a car parked a foot away. Wing mirrors and car doors hit readers mounted at the wrong height. We look at where you park before choosing the spot, and on a tight carport we often use a slim frame-mounted reader instead of a wide keypad.

Our 1920s house still has its original doorbell wire running to the front and side doors. Can a new keypad or door station reuse it?

Sometimes, and it is always worth checking. Old bell wire is thin two-conductor cable, often stapled along a joist in the basement and up inside a door frame. It can be enough to carry power to a small door chime or to some battery-backed keypads, and it is a ready-made path through a wall that would otherwise need drilling. A video door station or a reader with a camera usually needs more conductors or a network cable than old bell wire can provide.

Where the old wire is too small, it still helps: we use it as a pull string to draw new cable along the same route, which saves opening plaster or drilling brick. We test the old wire before relying on it, because a century-old run may have been nicked, painted over or spliced in a wall, and a fault in a hidden splice is hard to find later.

How do you tell whether a hundred-year-old door frame is strong enough to hold an electric lock?

We look, push and measure before quoting. The signs of a weak frame are a strike plate held by short screws into thin trim, soft or crumbling wood at the bottom of the jamb where water sits, gaps between the frame and the wall, hinges that have been shimmed more than once, and a door that only latches when it is lifted. On brick houses we also check whether the frame is properly anchored to the masonry or held mainly by the trim around it.

Most frames can be strengthened rather than replaced: long screws through the strike and hinges into the stud or the wall behind, a reinforced strike plate, wood filler or a new section of jamb where rot has started, and shims where the frame meets the wall. If the frame is beyond that, we say so and price a new frame or a new pre-hung door, because an electric lock is only as strong as what it latches into.

The detached garage behind our house is now an office with a bathroom. How should its door be controlled compared with an ordinary garage?

Like a room of the house, not like a garage. Listings in Marine Park describe exactly this kind of conversion; Brick Underground noted a detached garage with a bathroom that could become a guest room or office. Once people work or sleep there and valuables live there, the people-door needs a proper deadbolt or keypad lock, a door sensor, and the same code list as the house, with the option of a narrower list if a client or a tenant uses it.

If the old garage door is still in place, it needs attention too: many are only latched from the inside with a slide bolt, and some still have an opener whose remote is clipped to a visor in a car parked at the curb. We either lock the old door out of use or put the opener on the same system so it cannot be opened without the household knowing.

Households That Keep City-Worker Hours

We both work rotating tours, so the house is empty at different times every week. Can door alerts follow that kind of schedule?

Yes, and they should, because a fixed schedule will be wrong half the time. Instead of telling the system that the house is empty from nine to five, we set it to know whether anyone is home: from a button pressed on the way out, from the locks being set from outside, or from the phones of the people who live there. A door opening while the house is set as empty sends an alert straight away; the same door opening while someone is home asleep does not.

We also give each household member their own code and their own notification settings. The person on nights can mute alerts during the day; the person on days can get a quiet notice when the other gets home. The record keeps everything either way, so nothing is lost by muting it.

After a first break-in, what should change at the doors before anything else?

The way in, and every key that might still work. In the 2018 Madison Place case the Brooklyn Paper reported, the couple had been burglarized in July and attacked in a home invasion that October. We cannot know from the reporting how either break-in happened, and we will not guess. But after any burglary the first questions are always the same: which door or window was used, whether it has been repaired to be stronger than before, and whether any key, garage remote or code could have been taken or copied.

In practice that means changing every lock or code the household cannot account for, repairing the forced door and frame with long screws and a reinforced strike, adding a door sensor and an alert to that opening, and putting light and a camera on the approach the burglar used. A keypad lock helps here because changing who can get in becomes a matter of deleting codes rather than calling a locksmith. Talk to the precinct as well; they will often have seen the pattern before.

Burglars carried two televisions out of a house near East 34th Street and Avenue N at 4:30 on a February afternoon. What would a door system have recorded that helps afterwards?

Mostly time and place. A system with door sensors on every outside door records exactly which door opened and when, whether it was opened with a valid code or forced, and how long it stayed open. If the doors also have cameras, the video is tied to those moments, so you are not searching hours of footage. For the police and the insurer, a time-stamped list that says “back door forced 4:31 p.m., open until 4:38” is far more useful than a general description.

It would also have sent an alert at 4:31 to whoever was set up to receive one, which is the part that can change the outcome. The News 12 report does not say how the four people got in, and an afternoon break-in through a window would show up as nothing on the door record. That is why we suggest sensors on accessible windows at the back of the house as part of the same system.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Marine Park

We could not view Google’s AI Overview while preparing this page, so the statements below are claims we hear repeated in search summaries, chatbots and sales pitches, not quotations from any one answer. Each is followed by what we find on Marine Park lots.

“Marine Park is quiet, so a good deadbolt is all a house needs.”

Marine Park is a great deal quieter than it was. The 63rd Precinct’s figures, as cited by Wikipedia, show crime down 82.7% across all categories between 1990 and 2022. The same figures show 119 burglaries and 135 grand larcenies of autos in the precinct in 2022, and 2026 brought News 12 reports of an afternoon house burglary, an early-morning carjacking and a robbery at a parked car within the neighborhood.

A good deadbolt is still the foundation; every plan we write starts with a solid door, a solid frame and a bolt that throws fully. What a deadbolt cannot do is tell you it was left unlocked, let a relative in while you are at work, stop working for a key that went missing years ago, or show you the driveway before you get out of the car.

So the honest answer is that a quiet block needs less than a busy one, not nothing. For most Marine Park houses that means one or two controlled doors, some light and a camera, not a commercial system.

“The front door is the one to secure.”

On Marine Park lots the front door is often the one the family uses least. Driveways run down the side, garages and parking pads sit at the back, and the everyday entrance is a side or back door that the street cannot see. Advice written for apartment buildings and suburban houses with attached garages misses that.

We start with the door the household actually uses and the doors that are out of sight: side, back, outside basement and garage. The front door often gets a keypad deadbolt for guests and deliveries and a video doorbell, and that is enough.

The quickest way to find out which door matters is the walk described at the top of this page. Most people are surprised by how much of their day starts at the side of the house.

“A smart lock is a fifteen-minute swap on any door.”

On a modern pre-drilled door, a retrofit smart lock can go on quickly. On a door hung in the 1920s it often cannot: the bore may be the wrong size or position, the door may be thicker or thinner than the lock allows, the latch may not line up with the strike after a century of settling, and a glass panel may be within reach of the inside turn.

An electronic lock on a door that does not close properly is worse than the old lock, because it fails quietly. It reports locked when the bolt has only half thrown, or it drains its batteries straining against a misaligned strike.

Plan on the door being fixed first. On many Marine Park quotes, the planing, strike work and long screws are a small line item that decides whether the lock lasts.

“National cost guides tell you what this costs.”

National lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that are useful for knowing what equipment exists. Their ranges are national averages built from device prices and generic labor, and they rarely account for running cable from a basement to a door forty feet down a driveway, or to a garage at the back of the lot.

We do not quote other companies’ figures. We quote our own, which are published: the Brooklyn rates in the pricing section below apply in Marine Park unchanged, and a written fixed price follows the survey.

The fair comparison is like for like: the same doors, the same locks, cable or wireless, and who will service it when it fails on a Saturday.

“My alarm company already takes care of the doors.”

Those companies are mainly alarm and camera providers, and many Marine Park houses already have one of them. Some of their equipment can control a compatible smart lock. That is not the same as planning entry around a particular house: which doors are electrified, how the garage connects, how the side door is lit, who gets which code and what happens when a battery dies.

We work alongside existing alarm systems rather than asking you to replace them. Where an alarm panel is already in place, door sensors and locks can often report to it, so arming the alarm and locking the doors become one action.

The difference is local service. When a side door lock fails at 5 a.m., the question is whether someone can come the same day.

“Codes are less secure than keys because people share them.”

One shared code is less secure than a key, because it spreads without anyone knowing. Personal codes are more secure than keys, because each one can be cancelled on its own, in seconds, and the record shows which one was used.

Keys in Marine Park houses have long histories: previous owners, contractors, relatives, dog walkers and tenants upstairs. Nobody knows how many copies exist. Moving to personal codes or fobs ends that.

The rule we set up with every household is simple: one person, one code, never written on the door frame, and changed when someone moves out or a worker finishes.

“Wireless is fine for anything, even a garage at the back of the lot.”

Wireless locks work well inside a house. A garage at the back of a Marine Park lot is a different case: the signal has to cross the house’s brick or block walls, the yard and often a metal garage door, and it competes with every other network on the block.

Where a cable can reach the garage, it is the most reliable choice. Where it cannot, we use a dedicated wireless bridge aimed at the garage or a cellular unit, and we test signal strength at the garage before promising anything.

A lock that drops off the network now and then still works with a code, but the app stops showing whether it is locked, and that information is half the reason for having it.

Field Notes: Park Where You Normally Park

This describes how we survey houses with driveways, not a particular customer. The most useful thing we ask a homeowner to do during a survey takes no tools at all: go out, get in the car, and park exactly where and how you park on a normal night. Nose in or backed in, close to the fence or close to the house, in the driveway or at the curb across the street because the driveway is blocked.

Then we stand where the driver’s door opens and look. What can you see from here? Can you see the side door? Is the path lit, and does the light come on before or after you step out? How far is it, in steps, to the lock? Where does a keypad have to go so that a car door or a mirror will not hit it, and so you are not reaching across a recycling bin to use it?

Half of the plan usually changes in those five minutes. A camera moves from the front of the house to the side wall. A keypad moves from the hinge side of the door to the latch side. A light that was aimed at the street gets pointed down the driveway. None of it costs more; it just puts the equipment where the household actually is at the moment it matters, which is getting out of the car.

FAQ

Marine Park Access Control FAQ

Will a keypad on a door that faces the afternoon sun fade or fail?
+

Cheaper keypads can: plastic housings yellow, rubber buttons harden and the printing wears off, and a black keypad in direct sun gets hot enough to stress batteries and electronics. We choose outdoor-rated units with metal or UV-stable housings for doors in full sun, mount them in the shade of the door frame or an overhang where the door allows, and check the manufacturer's operating temperature range before recommending a model.

What should we keep on paper in case the app, the phone or the lock company stops working?
+

Keep a one-page sheet with the make and model of each lock and controller, where the controller and battery are, where the mechanical keys are, the administrator login stored somewhere other than one phone, and how to reset each lock to factory settings. We leave that sheet at handover. If an app is discontinued, the hardware can usually be moved to another platform or run on codes alone, and the sheet makes that a short job.

Will the keypad's light or beeps let a passer-by tell whether we are home?
+

They can if they are set carelessly. Some locks show a different light or make a different sound when they are locked from outside, and a keypad that glows all night draws attention. We turn off status lights that reveal whether the house is occupied, set the keypad to light only when touched, and keep confirmation beeps quiet. The household still sees the full lock status on the phone, where passers-by cannot.

We have a second-floor deck with stairs down to the yard. Does the deck door need the same lock as the ground-floor doors?
+

It should be treated as an outside door. A deck with stairs to the yard is an easy, out-of-sight way up to a door that often has a weaker lock and a large glass panel. We usually fit a keypad or keyed deadbolt and a door sensor, and look at the glass. If the deck door is a sliding door, a secondary bar or pin lock and a sensor matter more than a keypad, because many sliding doors can be lifted off their tracks.

What lock grade should an outside door have?
+

For an outside door on a house we look for locks rated to BHMA Grade 1 or Grade 2, the two highest of the three residential grades, which are tested for strength and cycles. Grade 3 locks are fine for interior doors. The grade is only part of it: a strong lock in a weak frame fails at the frame, so long screws into the stud and a reinforced strike plate go with every outside lock we fit.

Is there anything we would need to buy that is not on the quote?
+

There should not be. Our written price lists every lock, reader, sensor, power supply, battery, fob and length of cable, plus any door or frame work. The two things we sometimes leave to the household are a smart switch or new outdoor light fixture if you want to choose the style yourself, and work by a licensed electrician if the survey shows an outlet needs adding. Both are named on the quote as separate items, so nothing appears afterwards.

One of us works nights and sleeps until early afternoon. Can you schedule the installation around that?
+

Yes. We ask about sleep schedules when we book. Drilling and coring through brick are the loud parts, so we do them first or last depending on when the household is awake, work on the far side of the house during sleep hours where we can, and keep the door usable with a key the whole time. If a quiet day is impossible, we split the job into two shorter visits.

Is a lever handle or a knob better with a keypad lock when we are carrying things in from the car?
+

A lever. You can push a lever down with an elbow or a wrist when your hands are full of bags or a child, and a knob needs a free hand and a grip. Levers are also easier for older family members with stiff hands. On outside doors we pair a keypad deadbolt with a sturdy lever set, or use a mortise lock with a lever, so the door opens with one push after the code is entered.

What happens if someone keeps trying wrong codes on our keypad?
+

Most keypad locks and readers stop accepting codes for a set time after several wrong tries, and a connected system can send an alert when that happens. We turn that alert on. We also suggest codes of at least six digits, no birthdays or house numbers, and an occasional wipe of the keypad, because worn buttons can hint at which numbers are used.

Our back door is a steel replacement door a previous owner put in. Can a keypad lock go on it?
+

Usually yes. Insulated steel doors are often pre-drilled for a standard lockset and deadbolt, which makes a keypad deadbolt a straightforward fit. What we check is the door's thickness, whether there is solid backing behind the steel skin where the strike side meets the frame, and whether the frame is wood or steel. Running cable inside a steel door for a wired lock needs a door with an internal channel or an electric hinge, so on many steel doors we choose a battery lock or a strike in the frame instead.
