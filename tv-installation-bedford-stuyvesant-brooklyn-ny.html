The Neighborhood

Why Bedford Stuyvesant Properties Need TV Installation

Bedford Stuyvesant runs from Flushing Avenue down to Atlantic Avenue and from Classon Avenue out to Broadway, and it takes in four older neighborhoods under one name: Bedford, Stuyvesant Heights, Ocean Hill and Weeksville. It had 181,949 residents and 79,140 homes in the Furman Center's 2024 count. Masonry row houses started going up in the 1870s; the first row, on MacDonough Street, dates from 1872, and the brownstones the neighborhood is known for were built for upper-middle-class buyers from the 1890s into the late 1910s. Wikipedia puts the number of buildings from before 1900 at roughly 8,800 and calls it the largest collection of intact Victorian architecture in the country. For a TV installer that is not a slogan. It means the wall behind your screen was finished by a plasterer working by hand, on whatever was behind him: wood lath on a partition, or the brick itself on a party wall.

That second case is the one that catches people out. In a row of attached houses, each house shares its long side walls with its neighbors, and in most of the brownstones we see, those party walls are solid brick with plaster applied directly to them. A Brownstoner forum member who had lived with such walls for decades put it plainly when another owner asked about hanging a TV over a fireplace: "In my house the fireplaces are on the party walls with no studs, just plaster over brick." He added that it is "much easier to mount things on a non-party wall, with plaster over wood lath." Both observations match what our technicians find. The party wall is the strongest wall in the house for a heavy screen, but only if the fixing goes into the brick. A plastic plug pushed into the plaster coat, or a screw driven into the soft mortar between bricks, can feel tight on the day and work loose over months.

The second thing that shapes a Bed-Stuy TV job is who lives in the house. The Furman Center's figures put the homeownership rate for the district at 19.7 percent in 2024, which means roughly four households in five rent, and many of those renters live inside a brownstone with an owner. A typical arrangement, the kind Brownstoner described at 99 Bainbridge Street, is an owner's triplex on the lower floors with a rental apartment on top. Another, described at 117 MacDonough Street, is a legal two-family that has been set up as three units: a lower duplex and two floor-through apartments above it. In both cases the tenant upstairs wants the TV on the wall, the owner downstairs owns that wall, and the person on the other side of the party wall is a different household again. We ask about all three before booking, because a two-line text to the owner saves more trouble than any anchor.

Then there is the newer layer. Between 2010 and 2025, the Furman Center counts 11,644 new apartments in buildings of four or more units across the district, from small infill buildings on side streets to larger rentals near the avenues. Their walls are mostly drywall, often on light-gauge metal framing, and the wall between two apartments is a rated assembly that a management office will not want opened casually. Add the public housing, which the Furman Center puts at 12 percent of the district's rental units, 9,802 apartments in 36 properties including Marcy Houses and Sumner Houses, and the shops, churches and restaurants along Fulton Street, Nostrand Avenue, Tompkins Avenue, Lewis Avenue and Broadway, and a single week of TV work here can mean an 1872 party wall on Monday, a 2019 metal-stud partition on Tuesday and a storefront ceiling on Wednesday.

The landmark districts add one more layer of questions, though it is smaller than people fear. Large parts of the neighborhood are protected: the Stuyvesant Heights district, the Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district designated in 2013 with about 825 buildings, the Bedford Historic District designated in 2015 with about 800, and Alice and Agate Courts. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates what the public sees. Its own guidance says interior work needs a Commission permit only when it also needs a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. A bracket on the inside of a party wall in a row house does none of those things. What does draw the Commission's interest is anything on the outside, such as a dish on a visible roof or a cable run up a brownstone front, so we keep every part of a TV job inside the house.

Same-Day Repair in Bedford Stuyvesant

Need Repair in Bedford Stuyvesant? A Screen Sagging Off a Party Wall, a Mount Pulling Plaster Off Lath, or a Cord Pushed Through the Wall

The urgent TV calls from old Brooklyn houses come in a few familiar shapes, and Bed-Stuy's walls produce all of them. The commonest is a screen that has started to droop at one corner or tip forward. On a party wall that almost always means the original fixings went into the plaster coat or the mortar joints rather than the brick. The forum member quoted above described the same thing with shelves: "I first mounted my shelves with short anchors; they stayed up for a few weeks." The plaster on a party wall can be more than half an inch thick, and a short anchor that ends inside it is holding onto old lime and sand. Every time a full-motion arm is pulled out from the wall, the top fixings take the whole leverage of the screen, and they slowly crush the material around them.

The second shape is a bracket on a lath-and-plaster partition that was screwed only into the lath. Lath strips are thin, and the nails that hold them to the studs were never meant to carry a TV. A mount like that tends to pull a section of plaster away from the lath with it, leaving a bulge or a crack around the plate. The third shape is not dangerous in the moment but is a fire risk over time: a TV's power cord fed into a hole behind the screen and out of another near the baseboard. The National Electrical Code, in section 400.12, does not allow flexible cords to be "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," or to pass through holes in the structure, so a cord hidden that way needs to come out.

If a screen is moving, do not push it back against the wall. Two people should lift it off its bracket if they safely can, unplug it and lay it face up on a soft surface, and then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same screen is going back up on a properly fixed bracket in the same visit, the mount is priced as a new one on that wall type, with the take-down added. If the plaster has come loose from the brick or the lath around the old fixings, we tell you plainly before we start, because that is a plaster repair for the owner or a plasterer, and a new bracket should go onto sound wall a little to one side or wait until the patch has been done.

TV Installation for Bedford Stuyvesant's Architecture

Six kinds of home cover nearly every Bed-Stuy TV call we get. What separates them is not the style on the front of the house but three things inside it: what the TV wall is made of, how the household is divided between floors, and who besides you gets a say over the drill.

The double parlor kept as one house

Front and back parlors joined by pocket doors, a mantel in each, pier mirrors between the street windows and party walls of plaster on brick along both sides.

The owner's triplex under a rental

The owner lives on the garden, parlor and second floors; a tenant has the top floor. The TV upstairs goes on a wall the owner downstairs owns.

The house split into floor-throughs

A legal two- or three-family run as a duplex plus apartments, each floor with its own living room running from the front wall to the back.

The house renovated before you bought it

Gutted and refinished for sale, with smooth drywall, recessed lights and no visible plaster, and an old masonry shell somewhere behind the new surface.

The four- and five-story flats building

The 1890s apartment houses the Landmarks Commission describes among the rows: a common stair, small apartments and plaster throughout.

Newer rentals and public housing

Buildings finished since 2010 with drywall and metal framing, and the masonry of Marcy and Sumner Houses, where the building sets the rules.

The double parlor kept as one house: three walls you would choose, and why two of them are tricky

In a brownstone kept as a single home, the TV conversation almost always starts in the parlor. A Bed-Stuy double parlor typically has a front room facing the street and a back room facing the garden, joined by a wide opening with pocket doors, each room with its own mantel. Brownstoner's 2021 feature on 117 MacDonough Street, a house built in 1872 and one of a row of nine in the Stuyvesant Heights district, describes exactly that: intricate plasterwork with a ceiling medallion in the parlor, and "pocket doors lead to the rear parlor or dining room." Its 2021 feature on 99 Bainbridge Street, designed by Magnus Dahlander in 1892, adds a pier mirror in the front parlor and a columned mantel with its own mirror in the rear one. Owners usually want to keep all of that, which narrows the choice to three walls.

The first is the party wall, the long side wall shared with the next house. It is usually plaster on brick, it holds a heavy screen superbly once the fixing reaches the brick, and on our list it carries the $65 brick line. The second is the chimney breast above a mantel, which is masonry too and adds the $130 over-mantel line on top of the brick line, and which puts the screen higher than most people find comfortable unless the mantel is low. The third is the wall between the two front windows, where the pier mirror normally hangs; it is the thick front wall of the house, and it faces the sofa from the brightest position in the room, which is a glare problem in the afternoon. In most Bed-Stuy parlors the side party wall wins, with the cables dropping into a console underneath or running in a paintable cord cover ($59) painted to match.

The fourth option, often overlooked, is the back parlor. A screen on the back parlor's party wall, seen through the open pocket doors, can serve both rooms without a single fixing in the front parlor's plasterwork. When we look at the photos, we ask where you actually sit in the evening, which room gets the afternoon sun and which details you would never want to drill near, and we show you two or three positions with painter's tape before anything else happens.

The owner's triplex under a rental: the tenant's TV on the owner's wall

The two-family layout Brownstoner described at 99 Bainbridge Street is one of the most common arrangements in the neighborhood: an owner's triplex running from the garden floor up through the second floor, and a rental apartment on the top floor with its own small kitchen and two or three rooms. The top floor of a brownstone usually has lower ceilings than the parlor, smaller rooms and, in many houses, plaster partitions that are lighter than the ones below. The tenant's best TV wall is often the same party wall as in the parlor, three floors further up.

Two things follow. The first is permission. The owner who lives below is usually also the landlord, will hear a hammer drill on a party wall clearly, and may have strong views about holes in a house they plan to keep for decades. We suggest the tenant send a short message first: which wall, how many holes, how big, and whether the wires go into a cover or the console. The second is the carry. A 65-inch box has to come up the stoop, through the vestibule doors and up three flights of a narrow brownstone stair with a turn at each landing. We ask about the stair before the visit, and we ask that the TV stay in its original carton until we are there.

The house split into floor-throughs: a living room from the front wall to the back

Many Bed-Stuy houses, including 117 MacDonough Street as Brownstoner described it, are legal two-families set up as three units: a lower duplex and two floor-through apartments above. A floor-through is the full depth of the house on one floor, with the living room at the front, a bedroom at the back, and a kitchen or bath squeezed between. In that layout the living room has the thick front wall with two tall windows, one or two party walls, and a partition toward the middle of the floor. The partition is usually the easiest TV wall: plaster on lath, framed, with room inside for an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) if the owner agrees to the two openings it needs.

The catch in a floor-through is the stair hall. In many houses the common stair runs along one side of each floor, and the wall between your living room and that hall belongs, in practice, to the whole building. It also often carries the old gas, steam or electrical risers. We do not fix TVs onto a wall shared with a common hall without the owner's say-so, and we scan it carefully if we do. A party wall or a partition inside the apartment is nearly always a better choice.

The house renovated before you bought it: new drywall over an old shell

A great many Bed-Stuy brownstones have been gut-renovated and sold with new finishes throughout. They look simple to work on. They are not always. Behind new drywall on a party wall there may be wood furring strips nailed to the brick, light-gauge metal studs standing an inch or two off the brick, or drywall glued straight onto the old plaster. Each one changes the fixing. On furring, the strips are too small to carry a TV and the anchor has to go through them into the brick. On metal studs standing off the brick, the gap behind the drywall means the fixing has to span empty space before it reaches masonry, which calls for longer anchors and spacers so the bracket plate does not crush the drywall as it is tightened.

The Brownstoner forum has a good example from 2011: an owner could not find studs on the street-facing wall between his windows, and a stud finder gave only false readings. When he eventually located framing using an outlet as a reference point, the wall turned out to be drywall over plaster with a skim coat, with studs at uneven spacing near the windows. That is why our technicians drill one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, and read what comes out. Grey gypsum dust followed by red brick dust tells a very different story from gypsum followed by a sudden drop into a void.

Renovated houses do have one real advantage. Where a flipper has put metal or wood studs in front of the brick, there is a hollow cavity behind the drywall, and that cavity can take an in-wall power and cable kit that a bare brick wall never could. We check whether the cavity is deep enough and clear of insulation and existing wiring before offering it.

The four- and five-story flats building: small rooms and one stair for everyone

The Landmarks Commission's designation report for the Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district describes, among the row houses, French flats of four and five stories from the 1890s: small apartment houses sharing a common stair, set into the rows on the same blocks. Apartments in those buildings are compact, the living room is often the front room with two street windows, and the walls are plaster throughout, on lath for partitions and on masonry for the outer and shared walls.

For a TV that means a short list of decisions. The front room's side wall, if it is shared with the next building, is masonry and takes the brick line. The partition toward the bedroom is framed and takes the plaster line. Because these are apartment buildings with a landlord and often a managing agent, opening a wall for an in-wall kit usually needs approval that a cord cover does not. The stairs are steeper and narrower than a brownstone's, so screens of 65 inches and up need two people and a careful carry.

Newer rentals and public housing: the building's rules first, then the wall

The apartments built in Bed-Stuy since 2010 are mostly drywall on metal or wood framing, with concrete or block around stairs and shafts. Inside the apartment, an interior partition between the living room and a bedroom is usually the best TV wall: it is hollow, it takes an in-wall kit, and nothing behind it belongs to anyone else. The wall shared with the next apartment, or with the corridor, is a different matter. It is built as a fire- and sound-rated assembly, and cutting two openings in it for a kit is the kind of alteration most management offices will refuse or want to supervise. A TV can still hang on that wall; the cables simply run in a cord cover or drop to a console.

Public housing works the same way with stricter rules. Marcy Houses, opened in 1949 with 27 six-story buildings and 1,705 apartments, and Sumner Houses, opened in 1958 with 13 buildings, have masonry walls and house rules set by the housing authority. Residents should check those rules before booking. We price any apartment from the same Brooklyn list, and if the wall is masonry, the brick-or-stone line applies.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Brownstone Party Wall Before the Bracket Goes Up

These notes describe how our technicians approach the walls of Bed-Stuy's row houses. They describe our standing practice, not one particular address.

Start in the cellar, not the parlor. If the house is open to us, the technician looks at the cellar first. That is where the party walls are bare, where you can see how thick the brick is and how good the mortar looks, where the internet line comes in and where the old risers start.

Look for what runs inside the wall. In the parlor, the technician notes every outlet, switch and radiator on the TV wall and the ones on the floor above, because pipes and conduit run vertically between them. Apartment Therapy's 2021 piece on old buildings, quoting the renovators known as the Brownstone Boys, makes the same point about cast iron: if there is a bathroom above, ask yourself "Could it be a pipe?" before drilling.

Don't trust the stud finder on a party wall. The same piece notes that on plaster over masonry "your stud finder will only find a solid brick wall!" The scanner still helps find metal and pipes, but the knock and the pilot hole matter more.

One hidden hole decides the anchor. A single small hole, drilled where the bracket plate will cover it, tells us whether the plaster is on lath, on brick, on furring or on metal. Red dust means brick; a sudden give means a void; grey grit and a hard stop can mean the stone or a lintel near a window, where we move over.

Into the brick, clear of the joint. The fixing goes into the face of a brick, a safe distance from its edges, never into the mortar line between bricks. In soft old brick we use more fixings, each carrying less load.

Wall by Wall

Plaster on Lath, Plaster on Brick, Furred Drywall or Metal Studs: What Holds a TV in a Bed-Stuy Wall

The $179.99 starting price on our Brooklyn list covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. In Bed-Stuy that wall is the exception, so here is how each of the walls we actually meet is handled, and which list line it carries. The add-ons exist because each wall needs different bits, different fixings and more time, not because a house is old.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The framed partitions of a row house, between the front and back rooms and around the stair, are lath and plaster: thin wooden strips nailed across the studs, with two or three coats of plaster over them. The plaster is hard on the surface and brittle; the lath is thin. A fixing that lasts goes through both into the stud, with the pilot hole drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack around it. Studs in these houses are rarely on modern spacing, so we find each one rather than measuring from the last.

Plaster laid straight onto brick (+$65). The party walls, the front and rear walls and many chimney breasts. There is nothing to find with a stud finder because there are no studs. The anchor passes through the plaster coat, which can be well over half an inch, and sets into the brick behind. We use sleeve or wedge anchors or masonry screws sized for the bracket, and we drill with a hammer drill set gently at first so the plaster face does not spall. Where the plaster is loose from the brick, which you can sometimes hear as a hollow patch when you knock, we move the bracket to sound wall rather than clamp loose plaster against the brick.

Drywall over furring on brick (priced by the brick behind it, +$65). The renovated party wall. The drywall and furring hide the brick but cannot carry the TV, so the anchor goes through to the masonry, with spacers where there is an air gap so the plate sits flat.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). Interior partitions in renovated houses and many newer apartments. This is the wall the base price describes. Lag bolts go into the centers of two studs.

Drywall on metal studs. Common in buildings finished since 2010. Thin steel studs strip out a lag bolt, so the fixings are toggles made for steel or metal-stud anchors, spread across two studs. This wall is not a separate line on the list we publish for Bed-Stuy; send the photos and we tell you its total before you book.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The request that follows "put it on the wall" is nearly always "and make the wires disappear." There is a wrong way and a right way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. National Electrical Code section 400.12 says flexible cords are "not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," and also that they may not run through holes in the building structure or stand in for permanent wiring. A cord inside a wall can be nicked by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected.

The right way on a framed wall is an in-wall power and cable kit. It has a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, rated cable inside the wall, and a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It is $169 on our list, kit included. It needs a hollow framed cavity, so it suits lath-and-plaster partitions, renovated walls with studs in front of the brick, and drywall in newer buildings; it does not suit plaster directly on brick, which is solid. On those walls, the honest choices are a paintable cord cover at $59, painted to match the wall, or a screen placed directly above a console so the cables drop behind the furniture.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Owners Downstairs and Deposits: Mounting a TV in a Bed-Stuy Rental

With roughly four in five Bed-Stuy households renting, the question of who can say yes to a hole in the wall comes up on most of our calls. What follows is what public guidance says and what we have learned doing this work, so you can ask the right questions before you book.

Read the alteration clause first. In Brick Underground's "Realty Bites" column of November 3, 2022, the attorney Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas was blunt: "You should check the alteration provision in your lease first." The same column reported his view that if the lease does not include such a restriction, a ban would not be enforceable. Many Bed-Stuy rentals are in houses owned by individuals rather than large companies, and their leases vary widely, from a standard form with a full alterations clause to a short agreement that says nothing at all. Know what counts as damage. The same column quoted Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group, whose company's leases say tenants cannot affix anything to their walls, explaining that a hole the size of a nail is fine and anything larger is damage, and that a tenant who removes a mounted TV and leaves significant damage has to fix it or pay for it. A bracket leaves several holes larger than a nail; an in-wall kit leaves two openings. On plaster those patch well, but patching plaster is a skill, and on a party wall the anchor sleeves stay in the brick unless they are drilled out. The column also noted that landlords must give an itemized list of deductions and return the rest of the deposit within two weeks of move-out.

The owner downstairs is a neighbor as well as a landlord. In an owner-occupied house, the person who decides is also the person who will hear the hammer drill. A short message before the visit, saying which wall, the TV size, the bracket type and whether wires go in a cover or a kit, and that the installer is licensed and insured, answers almost every question.

Not drilling is a real option. If the lease is strict or you expect to move within a year, a TV on its own stand on a good console is a proper job: $119.99 on our Brooklyn list, with the devices connected and the cables tidied, and a soundbar placed in front of it for $79. You get the same picture and none of the patching.

If you own a converted house. Owners of two- and three-family houses sometimes ask us to mount a TV in each rental unit between tenants. Compliance notes for Bed-Stuy businesses, churches and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path.   • Landmarked fronts stay untouched. In the historic districts, anything on the outside of a building draws the Landmarks Commission's interest. Screens, cables and equipment stay inside the storefront.
  • Paperwork before the visit. Commercial landlords often want a certificate of insurance naming them and their managing agent before work starts. We have it issued ahead of the visit.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bed-Stuy owners, tenants, congregations and businesses describe when they call, grouped by the part of the job they affect. Each one is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

What is really behind a Bed-Stuy wall

The sellers gut-renovated our 1890s brownstone, and the party wall in the living room is now smooth new drywall. What is probably behind it, and does that change how the TV is fixed?

Almost certainly the original brick is still there, a little way behind the drywall, and the question is what sits in between. In renovated row houses we commonly find one of three things: wood furring strips nailed onto the brick with drywall screwed to them, metal studs standing an inch or two in front of the brick, or new drywall glued over the old plaster. None of those layers is strong enough to carry a TV by itself, so the bracket's fixings have to pass through them and set into the brick, and where there is an air gap we use spacers so the plate does not crush the drywall when it is tightened. Our technician confirms which version you have with one small pilot hole behind where the bracket plate will sit. Because the load ends up in the brick, the job carries the $65 brick line on top of the $179.99 base. The good news is that the cavity in front of the brick sometimes has room for an in-wall power and cable kit ($169), which a bare plaster-on-brick wall never does.

Our TV is going on the inside of the house's front wall, between the parlor windows. How deep do the anchors go, and could they get anywhere near the brownstone on the street side?

Nowhere near it. The front wall of a Bed-Stuy row house is thick masonry, typically several courses of brick with the stone facing on the outside, and a bracket anchor goes only as deep as its rated embedment, a couple of inches past the plaster into the inside face of the brick. That leaves the bulk of the wall, and the facing stone, untouched. What deserves more care on that wall is what is around the windows: the inside of a window opening usually has a lintel above it and sometimes wood framing for the shutters or blinds, and we keep fixings clear of both. The bigger practical issue is light. A screen between two street windows faces the sofa and gets daylight from both sides, which washes out the picture in the afternoon. Before choosing that wall, we tape the screen outline there and on the side party wall, and ask you to look at both in the light you actually watch in.

My neighbor says the lead shields his father set into their brick decades ago are still holding his shelves. Are lead anchors still what goes into a party wall for a TV?

Lead shields have an honest track record in old brick, and the Brownstoner forum has a member describing long lead shields that reached into his brick and had held for about 40 years. They work because they expand into the masonry. For a TV, though, we usually prefer modern sleeve or wedge anchors, or heavy masonry screws, with a published load rating, because a TV bracket, especially a swing-out arm, pulls outward on its top fixings in a way that shelves do not, and a rated anchor lets us match the hardware to the weight and leverage of your screen. What matters far more than the brand of anchor is where it goes: deep enough to pass through the plaster coat, into the face of a brick rather than the mortar joint, and far enough from the brick's edges that it does not split it. If your house's brick is soft, we use more fixings each carrying less load.

A Brownstoner poster suggested screwing a pine or poplar board across the wall first and hanging the TV from that. When is a mounting board the right call?

It is a good idea in a few specific situations, and an unnecessary one in most. On a lath-and-plaster partition where the studs fall in awkward places, a solid board screwed into two or three studs gives the bracket something to fix to wherever the screen needs to be centered, and it spreads the load over more of the wall. On an old wall with a few loose patches, a board can bridge across a weak spot to sound plaster on either side. And where a previous bracket left a cluster of holes exactly where the new one needs to go, a board covers the damage and gives fresh fixing points. On a sound party wall of plaster on brick, a board adds nothing: the anchors go straight into the brick and hold better without anything in between. A board also adds an inch or so of depth, which matters if you want the screen tight to the wall. If we think your wall calls for one, we say so from the photos and include it in the total before you book.

Our new TV and its swing-out arm are heavy. How do you know a 130-year-old plaster wall will still be carrying that years from now, not just on the day?

By making sure the plaster is not what carries it. Old plaster is very strong when you press on it and weak when you pull on it, and a swing-out arm pulls: when you extend it, the top fixings take the weight of the screen multiplied by the length of the arm. So on a partition the fixings go through plaster and lath into the studs, and on a party wall they go through plaster into the brick. The plaster then only has to stay where it is, which it has done for a century. We also use every fixing point the bracket plate offers, choose an arm rated comfortably above your TV's weight, and check the wall around the plate for hollow sounds that mean the plaster has separated from its backing. If it has, we move to sound wall instead of clamping loose plaster in place. A properly fixed arm on a Bed-Stuy party wall should outlast the TV it carries.

Old-house features in the way

Our house still has the old dumbwaiter shaft running from the garden-floor kitchen up to the parlor. Could the TV's cables travel between floors through it?

The signal cables, often yes; the power, never. A disused dumbwaiter shaft is one of the few ready-made vertical routes in a brownstone, and a network cable, a coax line or an HDMI cable rated for in-wall use can be run through it on proper supports, from the router or cable box downstairs to the TV in the parlor. We look first at whether the shaft is open at both ends, whether anyone has used it for plumbing or wiring since, and whether its openings have been closed off in a way that matters for fire separation between floors. The TV's power cord is another matter: the electrical code does not allow flexible cords to run through holes in the building's structure or be concealed in walls, so power for a TV near the shaft comes from an outlet in the room, through an in-wall kit on a framed wall ($169), or by a licensed electrician adding a circuit. Running low-voltage cable is part of what AESS does every day as a cabling contractor, so we can price that run from photos of both ends of the shaft.

The lower part of our parlor wall still has its original wood wainscoting. Will a cord cover have to cross it, and what are the alternatives?

A surface cover running down the wall would have to cross the wainscot cap and the paneling to reach the outlet, and most owners who have kept original woodwork do not want plastic screwed to it. There are better routes. If the wall above the wainscot is a framed partition, an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) can put its recessed outlet behind the screen and its inlet just above the wainscot cap, close to an existing outlet, so nothing crosses the wood. If the wall is plaster on brick, where a kit cannot go, the cleanest answer is often to hang the screen directly over a low console or cabinet set against the wainscot, so the cables drop straight down behind it, and to bring power from an outlet on the console's side. A third option is to run the cover along the top of the wainscot cap to the corner of the room and down the corner trim, which is far less visible. We show you each route with tape before deciding.

The previous owner put a dropped ceiling in our garden-floor den. Can the TV's power cord run above the ceiling tiles to an outlet across the room?

No, and it is worth knowing why before someone offers to do it. National Electrical Code section 400.12 says flexible cords are not permitted to be "located above suspended or dropped ceilings," for the same reasons they are kept out of walls: they cannot be inspected there, they can be crushed or overheated, and a fire in that space spreads unseen. Signal cables are a different category, and HDMI, coax or network cable can run above a suspended ceiling if it is the right type and properly supported, not laid on the tiles. For power, the options are to put the TV within reach of an existing outlet, to use an in-wall power and cable kit on a framed wall ($169) to bring a recessed outlet up behind the screen, or to have a licensed electrician add an outlet where the TV will go. AESS is a low-voltage contractor and does not do electrical work, so we will tell you plainly if the third option is the one you need.

The TV is coming by delivery truck, and the only way in is up the stoop and through the double doors of the vestibule. Should it stay in its box, and who carries it up to the top floor?

Keep it in its original carton, unopened, until the technician arrives. The carton and its foam are designed to carry the screen on its edge, which is how it has to go through a vestibule and up a brownstone stair; a screen taken out and wrapped in a blanket is much easier to flex and crack on a tight turn. Many delivery services will bring a box to the door but not up interior stairs, so ask at the time of purchase. On the day, we carry it from wherever it was left, and for screens of 65 inches and larger we plan for two people on the stair. Measure the narrowest point on the way up, usually the turn at a landing or the door of the top-floor apartment, and tell us when you book. If the box will not make the turn, it is far better to find out with a tape measure than with the TV.

Rentals, landlords and the newer buildings

Our apartment in one of Bed-Stuy's newer rental buildings shares the TV wall with the apartment next door. Can an in-wall power kit go into that wall?

Usually not, and you should ask the management office before anyone suggests it. In buildings of this generation, the wall between two apartments is a rated assembly built to slow fire and block sound, and cutting two openings in it for an in-wall kit, even small ones, is the kind of alteration most leases and management offices forbid or want to control. A TV can still go on that wall. The bracket fixes into the studs with fasteners suited to the framing, and the cables run down in a paintable cord cover ($59) or straight into a console under the screen. If you want the cables fully hidden, look at an interior partition instead, such as the wall between your living room and a bedroom: it belongs to your apartment alone, it is hollow, and it is exactly where an in-wall kit ($169) is meant to go. We confirm which wall is which from your photos and the apartment layout before the visit.

Our landlord lives on the garden floor and says only his own handyman may drill in the house. Is there a way to work with that?

There are two ways, and both are common. The simplest is to show the landlord exactly what we would do: which wall, which bracket, how many holes and what size, which anchors, and where the cables go. Owners who refuse outside workers are usually worried about being surprised, and a clear plan from a licensed, insured company often changes the answer. If the landlord still prefers his handyman, we can do everything except the drilling: a setup-only visit, $195 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks, covers connecting the TV, signing in the streaming apps, setting up the soundbar and remote and tidying the cables once the bracket is up. If it checks out, we hang it; if not, we explain why.

Businesses, congregations and gathering rooms

Our church streams its Sunday service. Can a TV in the fellowship room downstairs show that live picture, so the overflow crowd can follow along?

Yes, and it is mostly a cabling question rather than a TV one. The screen downstairs needs a signal, and the most reliable ways to get it there are a network cable from the equipment that runs the stream, carrying the live picture over the church network to a streaming device or small computer behind the TV, or a dedicated video cable run between floors. Wi-Fi alone is the weakest choice in a masonry church building, because brick and plaster absorb signal between floors. AESS installs network cabling and switches as part of its everyday work, so we can run and test that line as well as mount the screen. The mount itself is priced from our list by size and wall, setup of the playback device falls under the setup-only rate, and the cable run is quoted from photos of both rooms and the route between them. We plan the screen height so it can be read over a seated crowd, and keep it clear of exits and fire devices.

Our café wants a screen that the people at our sidewalk tables can see through the front window. Can a TV face out through the glass?

It can, with a few honest limits. A normal indoor TV facing a street window competes with daylight on the glass, so during the day it looks dim from outside; screens built for windows are much brighter and are a different product from a home TV. The mount goes inside, on a ceiling or a wall beside the window, so nothing is fixed to the storefront, which matters on a landmarked block where the outside of the building is regulated. The screen should not block the view into the shop from the sidewalk or any sign the landlord or the city requires, and its cable and power come from inside, from a proper outlet. We look at which way your window faces and at what hours you want the screen on before recommending a size or brightness. Tell us the street and the block, too, because a south-facing window on Fulton Street and a shaded one on Lewis Avenue are very different problems.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant

These are claims that turn up in generic online answers and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Bed-Stuy's buildings actually show.

"Use a stud finder to locate two studs"

The standard advice is to run a stud finder along the wall, mark two studs and lag-bolt the bracket into them.

On a Bed-Stuy party wall there are no studs to find. The plaster sits directly on brick, and as Apartment Therapy's sources put it, on plaster over masonry a stud finder "will only find a solid brick wall." On lath-and-plaster partitions, uneven plaster thickness confuses the sensor too.

We confirm every wall with one pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate, then choose a masonry anchor or a stud fixing for what is really there.

"Plastic anchors are fine for a medium TV"

Some answers say heavy-duty plastic plugs will hold a mid-size screen on almost any wall.

In old plaster a plug grips only the plaster coat, which is brittle and can be thick, and it loosens each time a swing-out arm is pulled. The Brownstoner owner whose short anchors held shelves "for a few weeks" learned the same lesson.

Fixings go through the plaster into brick or framing, with a published rating above the load.

"Run the TV's cord through the wall to hide it"

Many how-to answers suggest two holes and the TV's own power cord fed between them.

NEC section 400.12 says flexible cords are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors or ceilings, or run above suspended ceilings. And a plaster-on-brick party wall is solid, so there is no cavity to feed anything through anyway.

Framed walls get a code-compliant in-wall kit at $169; masonry walls get a $59 paintable cord cover or a console below.

"Mounting a TV costs the national average"

Cost guides publish national or citywide averages that assume ordinary drywall.

An average does not know your TV wall is brick, that the screen is going over a marble mantel, or that it has to climb a stoop and three flights. Those are what change a Bed-Stuy total.

Our Brooklyn list starts at $179.99 and shows each add-on, so you can add up the total yourself before booking.

"Over the fireplace is the natural spot"

Design sites often present the chimney breast as the obvious home for the screen.

In a brownstone it is masonry, often with a deep mantel shelf, and it puts the screen high. The same forum member warned that mounting over a mantel "invites a sore neck," and Brownstoner's 99 Bainbridge feature shows some Bed-Stuy fireplaces still have working gas inserts below.

We tape the screen at mantel height and at a side wall, and you choose after sitting where you watch.

"Landmark rules stop you drilling in a historic house"

Some answers suggest anything done in a landmarked house needs Commission approval.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission says interior work needs its permit only when it needs a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark, and the Buildings Department lists painting and plastering among work that needs no permit.

A bracket inside a row house is ordinary interior work; we keep dishes, cables and equipment off the outside entirely.

"Renters simply cannot mount TVs"

Some answers treat any drilling in a rental as forbidden, and others say it never matters.

Brick Underground's attorney said the lease's alteration provision decides it, and a property manager said anything larger than a nail hole counts as damage to repair at move-out. With about four in five Bed-Stuy households renting, that is the rule most people live under.
