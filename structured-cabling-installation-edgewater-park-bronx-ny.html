The Neighborhood

Why Edgewater Park Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Edgewater Park is a neighborhood made entirely of houses, and that one fact separates a cabling job here from almost every other job we do in the Bronx. Wikipedia describes it as a small waterside cooperative community of 675 single-family homes on about 60 acres in the Throggs Neck section of the borough, north of the Cross Bronx Expressway near the Throgs Neck Bridge. The Department of City Planning's Resilient Neighborhoods report gives a slightly smaller figure, fifty-five acres, and puts the population at roughly 3,000 people. Either way, there are no apartment buildings, no shared cellars, no risers between floors and no telecom rooms. Every network in Edgewater Park is a household network: a provider's line arrives at one point on the house, a modem and router sit wherever the installer left them, and the family decides which rooms need a better connection than Wi-Fi alone can give. That sounds simpler than a forty-unit walk-up, and in some ways it is. But the houses themselves, the ground they stand on and the water at the edge of the community make the planning more particular than most homeowners expect.

Start with how the houses came to be. Wikipedia records that George T. Adee built a mansion on the land in 1856, and that the land was later leased to Richard W. Shaw Sr., an Irish immigrant and member of St. Ann's Episcopal Church in Morrisania, who invited church youth to camp on the grounds. The place became known as Edgewater Camp. Tents gave way to bungalows, and after World War I the community was divided into sections named A, B and C after Army camps, with D and E added later; the letters still follow the house numbers in addresses today. The planning department's report says the summer cottages and bungalows went up in the 1920s and were winterized to support a year-round community during the Great Depression, and that these converted homes still largely characterize the neighborhood. A sale listing for one house, 30D, gives the plain construction details: built in 1920, wood frame, baseboard heat, wall or window air-conditioning units, and a shed. For a cabling technician, every one of those details matters. A frame house is easier to drill than masonry. A house heated by baseboard and cooled by window units has no ductwork, which means no duct chase to borrow as a path between floors. And a house that started as a summer cottage and was closed in, insulated and added onto over a century rarely has wall cavities that line up the way a modern builder would lay them out.

Then there is the question of who owns what. A resident wrote on an appraisers' forum that the homes are co-ops that are individually owned: the family owns the physical house, but not the land it is built on. The planning department's report says the same thing in its own words: residents own their homes outright but lease the land, divided into shares, from the owners' cooperative, and pay dues to maintain communal areas and infrastructure. The co-op is governed through bylaws administered by an active board, it sets architectural and construction guidelines, and the report says it strictly enforces them. Sources disagree on when the cooperative formed. Wikipedia and the planning department say 1988, when residents who rented the land bought it from the primary leaseholder; a Substack history of Throggs Neck says the Shaw family sold the property to the tenants in 1986; CityNeighborhoods.nyc says a cooperative existed by 1910. We do not print one of those as settled. What matters to a cabling job is today's arrangement: the inside of your walls is yours, while the ground outside them, the lanes between houses and the community's shared spaces are the co-op's. Anything that leaves the house, a buried run to a shed, a new hole through the siding for a provider's line, a cable that would cross a lane, touches ground the co-op controls.

The community also runs more of its own infrastructure than most of New York. The planning department says the co-op maintains a private sewage network and a volunteer fire company, while the city handles sanitation and water; CityNeighborhoods.nyc goes further and says the community installed its own water, sewer and maintenance systems, along with private beaches, playgrounds and docks. The Architect's Newspaper reported in 2010 that Edgewater Park and its neighbor Silver Beach Gardens are private cooperatives with closed-off streets and a single point of entry, with security guards and signs that forbid loitering, trespassing and soliciting. Every contractor, including every internet provider's technician, comes through that one entrance. And the lanes past it are tight: New York State Homes and Community Renewal noted that many of the Fire Department's emergency vehicles are too big to navigate Edgewater Park's small streets, which is why the community's volunteer firehouse provides essential first response. A cabling crew that arrives unannounced, or parks a van across a lane for an afternoon, is not going to make friends.

Water is the factor that shapes the most decisions inside the house. The planning department chose Edgewater Park for its resiliency study because of the high flood risk it faces from its building type and its location on the Long Island Sound. Hurricane Sandy, in 2012, left some residents with up to five feet of water in their homes, according to a history of the volunteer fire company. The count of homes in the mapped flood zone depends on which report you read: City Limits wrote that 278 of the 675 homes sit in a flood zone, while the Bronx Times reported that the planning department explained approximately 111 homes are in it. The planning report lists A zones with a base flood elevation of 13 feet, Coastal A zones at 14 feet, and V zones along the most exposed edge. City flood standards require certain buildings to raise their lowest floor and their mechanical equipment above the design flood elevation, and a 2013 zoning text change lets Edgewater Park homes elevate as of right; the Bronx Times reported that the co-op voted to measure building height from the design flood elevation while keeping its 25-foot height limit. City Limits also reported, around 2019, that just one property in Edgewater Park had obtained permits to elevate the existing building since Sandy. Put together, that means most houses here have not been raised, many sit low, and the place a router, a modem or a patch panel ends up can decide whether it survives the next storm.

Wind is the other half of the weather problem. In June 2026, News 12 Bronx reported that along the Cross Bronx Service Road in Throggs Neck, multiple large trees were torn from the ground, leaving debris across the street and tangled with power and cable lines. Many houses in this part of the borough get their internet from a line that crosses the air from a pole, and when that line comes down, so does the household's connection. What happens between the pole and the house is mostly the provider's business. What happens from the point the line enters the wall to every room inside is cabling work, and that inside stretch can be built so that a storm costs you the provider's line and nothing more.

The neighborhood is also changing house by house. A listing for a vacant lot at 22B Edgewater Park marketed it for a custom two-story residence with a garage, and noted that flood insurance is required. Homes.com records sales through 2026, including a house at 143 D Edgewater Park that sold on March 27, 2026 for $415,000. Every new house is a chance to put cable in while the walls are open. Every sale brings a family that may work from home, stream on several screens and want cameras, and that finds a house wired for a 1990s telephone. And the community's own buildings are being upgraded too: Patch reported that state resiliency funds for the volunteer firehouse cover roof and gutter replacement, hurricane-proof windows, bathroom, electric and HVAC upgrades, a new fire alarm system and two new power generators. Upgrades like those usually come with low-voltage work behind them.

We want to be plain about what our research could not find. We looked for Reddit threads, local news and community posts about network wiring, internet installations or co-op rules for cabling in Edgewater Park and found nothing specific to the neighborhood. We could not see Google's People Also Ask boxes or AI Overview for this search from our build environment, and Bing, DuckDuckGo and AnswerThePublic were not available to us either. We found no provider coverage map for Edgewater Park itself, only borough-wide figures. We did not find the co-op's written rules on contractors or exterior work, so wherever this page says the co-op may need to be consulted, it is because the co-op owns the land and enforces construction guidelines, not because we have read a specific rule. We also came across claims elsewhere online about a named approvals system inside the co-op and about the share of homes in the floodplain that we could not confirm from any independent source, and we do not repeat them here. The way to learn your house is to walk it, and the way to learn the co-op's current requirements is to ask the management office at 1 Edgewater Park.

Edgewater Park facts that shape a cabling job

  • Houses only: 675 single-family homes on roughly 55 to 60 acres, per the planning department and Wikipedia, so every job is a house network rather than a riser in a multi-unit building.
  • Old frame cottages: the planning department says 1920s summer cottages were winterized during the Depression; one listing describes a 1920 frame house with baseboard heat and window units, which means no duct chases to borrow.
  • House owned, land leased: owners hold the house but lease the land in shares from the cooperative, which sets and strictly enforces architectural and construction guidelines.
  • One way in: closed streets, a single entrance and security guards, per The Architect's Newspaper, and lanes too narrow for many FDNY vehicles, per state housing officials.
  • Flood exposure: A zones at a 13-foot base flood elevation, Coastal A at 14 feet and V zones, per the planning report; estimates of homes in the flood zone range from about 111 to 278.
  • Clear borders: the Throgs Neck Expressway to the south and west, Weir Creek to the north and Eastchester Bay to the east, in ZIP code 10465 and the 45th Precinct.

Those devices ride on the same kind of cable and can be planned into the same visit, which saves drilling through the same walls twice. Who Decides What

Your Walls, the Co-op's Ground: Who Has a Say Over Each Stretch of an Edgewater Park Cable Run

In most of the Bronx, the hard question on a cabling job is how to get a cable through a building somebody else owns. In Edgewater Park the building is usually yours. The question moves outdoors: the moment a cable leaves your walls, it is on, under or above ground that belongs to the cooperative. That does not mean every job needs a formal approval; most cabling happens entirely inside the house. It means we sort the work into what is purely interior and what reaches outside, and we treat the second kind with care. The table below is our working guide. It is not a statement of the co-op's rules, which we have not seen in writing, and it is not legal advice; it is how we decide what to ask before we drill.

The gate is the first conversation

Every job here begins at the single entrance. We ask you, when you book, how the guard post expects contractors to be announced, whether a name, a company and a vehicle description should be left in advance, and what hours the community prefers for work. We send you the technician's name and the van's description so you can pass them along. If the lanes near your house are especially tight, tell us; we bring the smaller vehicle, carry equipment the last stretch on foot if needed, and keep the lane clear. Nobody in Edgewater Park needs a contractor's van blocking the way a volunteer fire truck would come.

Drilling the outside wall

The planning department describes a co-op that sets architectural and construction guidelines for the built form of the community and enforces them strictly. We have not seen those guidelines, so we do not assume either way whether a cable entry, a small box or a short run of conduit on the outside of a house falls under them. What we do is minimize what shows. Where the provider's line already enters, we bring our cable to that same point from the inside. Where a new opening is truly needed, we choose the least visible spot, use a weatherproof entry fitting, seal it so wind-driven rain off the bay cannot follow the cable in, and give you a description and a photo you can show the office before the work, if they ask for one.

Below the lawn

CityNeighborhoods.nyc says the community installed its own water, sewer and maintenance systems, and the planning department says the co-op maintains a private sewage network. We do not know where those lines run under any given yard, and neither will most homeowners. That is reason enough not to trench casually. When a family wants a cable to reach a shed or the far end of a yard, we look first at routes that stay above ground and on the house, such as along the foundation or under a deck, protected in conduit. If digging is the only sensible way, we ask you to confirm with the management office what is buried there before anyone breaks ground, and we plan the depth and the protection around the answer.

Your neighbor is a few feet away

The planning report says structures sit immediately adjacent to one another on narrow streets, and a resident on a city-data forum wrote that usually all that separates you from a neighbor is an alley. On the side of a house that faces a neighbor, there may be no room to set a ladder without stepping onto their side. We do not do that without asking. If a route depends on reaching the outside wall that faces a neighbor, we tell you in advance, ask you to speak with them, and pick a time that suits both households. Often the better answer is to keep the work inside, where only your family is affected.

Above the Water Line

Planning Cable Around the Flood Line in an Edgewater Park House

A network is only as good as the first piece of equipment the provider's line reaches. If the modem, the router or the panel sits on a basement floor or a low shelf in a garage that floods, everything downstream goes with it. In Edgewater Park that is not a theoretical risk; it is what Sandy did. So on every house here we start the plan by asking where the water could reach, then put the equipment and the cable routes above it. The figures below come from the planning department's report and city flood rules; your own property's elevation certificate or the city's flood maps tell you what applies to your house, and we work from those, not from a guess.

Where the house sitsWhat the planning report saysHow it changes the cabling plan
A zoneBase flood elevation of 13 feetPanel, modem and router placed above the design flood elevation where the house allows; cable fed down from above rather than up from below
Coastal A zoneBase flood elevation of 14 feet, with wave actionSame as above, with extra attention to sealing every exterior entry against wind-driven water
V zoneThe most exposed edge, subject to wavesNothing of ours below the lowest floor; exposed runs protected and kept to a minimum
Outside the mapped zoneLower mapped risk, though the same storms reach the whole shorelineWe still avoid floors and low shelves for equipment; it costs nothing to place it higher

Where the panel and router should live

City flood standards require certain buildings to raise their lowest floor and their mechanical equipment above the design flood elevation. A home network panel is not a boiler, and we do not claim the rule names it, but the reasoning carries over exactly: anything that powers or connects the house should sit above the water's reach. In a one-story cottage that usually means a closet shelf or a high spot on an interior wall of the living floor, not a crawl space or a utility corner on a concrete pad. In a two-story house it can mean the upper floor, with the provider's line brought up to it. In a raised house it means the new living level, never the space created beneath it. We choose the spot on the walk-through and show it to you before we commit.

Feed from above, not from below

The easiest path in many houses is under the floor, along the joists of a crawl space or a cellar ceiling. In a flood zone, that path is also the one that goes underwater first. Where a house has a usable attic or a space above the ceilings, we usually prefer to run cable across it and drop down inside interior walls to each room, so the network's cable sits as high as the house allows. Where there is no space above and the only path is below, we keep cable as high as possible against the underside of the floor, support it properly, use cable suited to damp spaces, and keep every connection point out of the low space. A cable can sometimes survive a soaking; a jack, a splice or a panel usually cannot.

What a flood leaves inside the walls

Homes that took water in 2012 were opened up, dried and rebuilt in many different ways by many different contractors, and some of the cable inside them came through it and some did not. Water that reaches copper conductors at a cut end or a loose connector can corrode them over time, and the result is a jack that worked for years and then turns slow or unreliable. We do not assume old cable is bad, and we do not assume it is good. We test it. Runs that pass are labeled and kept. Runs that fail, or whose ends were clearly underwater, are replaced, and the new run is routed higher than the old one where the house allows.

Raising a house is the best rewiring window there is

The 2013 zoning change lets Edgewater Park homes elevate as of right, and the co-op's decision to measure height from the design flood elevation means a raised house can keep its full height above the new floor. Few owners have done it so far, but those who do will have their house lifted, its utilities reconnected and often its lower walls and floors opened. That is the moment when cable can go anywhere at almost no extra effort: into every room, up to the attic, out to an outdoor camera position, and into a panel on the new living level. If you are planning an elevation, call us before the utilities are reconnected, not after.

Count the devices, then add one more per room

In a small house it is tempting to wire only the one desk that needs it. Walk the house anyway. A cottage with two bedrooms may still hold a home office, a TV in the living room, a console in a bedroom and a printer somewhere. A family that works from home today may add a second remote worker next year. Each cable you add during the same visit costs far less than coming back later, because most of the effort in a drop is opening the path, not the cable inside it. Where a room might need two connections someday, we usually suggest pulling two now.

Tell us what the last contractors did

Houses here have been patched, enlarged, flooded and rebuilt by many hands. If you know that a previous owner closed in a porch, that a provider ran a line along the outside of the house, that walls were opened after Sandy, or that someone already fished a cable to a bedroom, tell us. Every piece of history narrows down where we look first, and it often reveals a route that is already half-built.

Structured Cabling for Edgewater Park's Architecture

Every house in Edgewater Park is a single-family home, but they are far from identical. A century of conversions, additions, flood repairs and new construction has produced a handful of types, and each one changes where cable can travel, where the panel can sit and how much of the work shows. These are the types the planning department, the listings and the neighborhood histories describe, and how we approach each.

The Winterized 1920s Cottage

A former summer bungalow closed in for year-round living, like the 1920 frame house at 30D with baseboard heat and window units. Small rooms, frame walls, no ductwork, and spaces above and below that vary house to house.

The Cottage With Additions

An original cottage that has grown a bedroom, a kitchen extension or an enclosed porch. The seam between old and new framing is where cable routes usually stop and need a plan.

The Multi-Story Bungalow

The planning department mentions multi-story bungalow-style homes. A second level means a floor to cross, and without a duct chase the path between floors has to be found.

The Raised House

A home lifted above the design flood elevation under the 2013 rules. A new living level on top, a space beneath it that should hold nothing of the network's.

The New Two-Story Home

Winterized cottages: read the house before you drill it

A cottage built for summer and closed in for winter usually tells its story in its walls. The original framing may be lighter than a modern house's. Insulation may have been blown into cavities decades after they were closed, which can make fishing a cable through them slower. Interior wall finishes vary from one room to the next depending on when each was redone. With baseboard heat and window air-conditioning, there are no ducts to share, so the paths a cable can take are the ones the house happens to have: the space above the ceiling if the roof allows it, the space below the floor if the house sits on piers or a crawl space, and closets that line up. We open every access point on the walk-through, look at what is there, and choose the route that keeps cable highest and least visible. Where no hidden route exists, a neat, paintable surface run inside a closet or along a baseboard is often better than opening a finished wall.

Additions: the seam is the problem and the opportunity

When a cottage grew, its old outside wall often became an inside wall, with the original siding or sheathing still in it. That wall can be harder to fish through than any other in the house, and the floor and ceiling levels on each side may not match. It is also, very often, the wall the router is on, because the provider's line came in through what used to be the outside of the house. We trace where the original cottage ends, find a place where the two sets of framing can be joined cleanly, typically in the ceiling or under the floor at the seam, and plan every run to the addition through that single crossing. An enclosed porch is a special case: it may sit lower than the rest of the house, have a different floor structure, and be closer to the water, which affects whether we put any connection there below a certain height.

Multi-story bungalows: find the vertical path

Getting cable up a floor is the hardest single step in a house without ductwork. The usual answers are a closet stacked directly above another closet, the space beside a plumbing vent, or a corner where a short, painted surface raceway runs from floor to ceiling. In a frame house we can usually drill through a top or bottom plate cleanly once we know where it is, and we look for spots where the hole will sit inside a wall or a closet rather than in a room. Once one vertical path exists, every upstairs drop can share it, which is why we spend time getting it right.

Raised houses and new builds: plan it once

A house that is being raised, or one going up on an empty lot, is the rare case in Edgewater Park where cable can go exactly where it should. We ask the builder for the floor plan, mark the panel location on the living level, run cable to every room that might need it, add an empty conduit from the panel toward the garage and toward the outside walls where cameras or a doorbell panel might go later, and leave a pull string in each one. The garage on a new house, like the one in the 22B listing, may sit at a lower level than the living floor; anything we put there is kept high and limited to cable, not equipment. When drywall goes up, the cable is already in, and the only visit left is for terminations, testing and labels.

Rules and practical limits for business and community cabling

  • Permits for low-voltage data work. NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A hiring guide we read gives the effective date as December 21, 2025 and says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the code defines it. On a commercial scope we confirm the filing requirement before work begins.
  • Alarm, camera and access systems are licensed separately. A licensing roadmap we read notes that burglar alarm, fire alarm, CCTV and access control work requires a New York State Article 6-D license. We say clearly on every quote which parts of a job are data cabling and which are security systems.
  • Fire alarm wiring stays out of data jobs. Fire alarm circuits have their own licensing and filings, and we never mix fire alarm cable into a data installation.
  • New outlets are electrical work. Where a rack, a recorder or an access point needs a new receptacle, that circuit belongs to a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Cable jackets match the space. Air-handling ceilings in commercial buildings need plenum-rated cable; runs outdoors or in damp spaces need cable made for them.
  • Authorization comes first. In a co-op building or a leased store, the owner or the lease decides what may be altered. We ask for that agreement, and any insurance certificate the owner requires, before the visit.

The co-op and the gate

The co-op owns the land under our house. Do we need its permission before network cable goes in?

For cable that stays entirely inside your walls, floors and attic, the work is to your own house, and in most cases that is your decision. The planning department describes a co-op that sets and strictly enforces architectural and construction guidelines, so the moment work reaches the outside, a new opening in the siding, a box on the wall, conduit along the foundation or any digging in the yard, it may fall under those guidelines. We have not seen the co-op's written rules, so we do not guess. We separate the interior and exterior parts of the plan, describe the exterior part in writing with a photo of the spot, and suggest you show it to the management office before work starts. That usually takes far less time than undoing something later.

How does your crew get through the single entrance on the day of the job?

The same way any expected visitor does: by being expected. When you book, tell us how the guard post likes contractors announced, whether by name, company, vehicle or all three. We send you the technician's name and a description of the van a day ahead so you can pass it along, and the technician arrives with identification. If the community restricts contractor hours, tell us and we schedule inside them. Arriving unannounced at a gated community wastes everyone's morning, so we treat the gate as part of the job.

Water, wind and salt

Network cable in our walls sat in floodwater during Sandy. Is it still safe to use?

It may be, but nobody can tell by looking. Cable whose jacket stayed intact can come through a soaking, while cable with an open end, a splice or a jack underwater can corrode slowly and fail years later. We trace each run, test it end to end with a cable analyzer, and check the connectors. Runs that pass are labeled and kept. Runs that fail, or that had connections in the wet zone, are replaced, and where the house allows, the new run goes higher than the old one did. Testing first means you only pay to replace what actually needs it.

When a storm brings down the line to our house, is that the provider's problem or a cabling problem?

The line from the pole or the street to the house belongs to the provider, and so does the repair when a tree takes it down, as happened along the Cross Bronx Service Road in Throggs Neck in June 2026, when News 12 reported trees tangled with power and cable lines. Your cabling starts where that line enters the house. What we can do is make sure the inside is ready for a quick reconnection: a clear, labeled entry point, a modem placed where the provider's technician can reach it, and an inside network that comes straight back up once the provider restores service. We do not repair or climb the provider's lines.

Does the salt air off Eastchester Bay damage outdoor network cable and connectors?

Salt air is hard on exposed metal, and the parts of a network most at risk are the connectors and any outdoor jack, not the cable jacket itself. On the outside of a house near the water we keep connections indoors wherever possible, so a camera's cable passes through the wall and is terminated inside. Where something must be terminated outdoors, it goes into a sealed, weather-rated enclosure, and any fittings and fasteners we use are chosen to resist corrosion. We also check outdoor connections when we return for other work, because the shoreline will find a weak seal eventually.

The only route to our back bedroom is through the damp crawl space under the cottage. What does cable need there?

A crawl space that stays damp is a hostile place for anything electrical, and it may also be the first space to flood. If it truly is the only route, we use cable rated for damp or wet locations, support it up against the underside of the floor rather than letting it lie on the ground, keep it away from any standing water line, and put no splices, boxes or connections down there at all; the cable passes straight through and is terminated in the rooms above. Before choosing that route we always check whether the attic or a closet offers a drier path, because the best crawl-space cable is the one that is not there.

Should a house on the water have surge protection where the network cable comes in?

It is worth considering on any house with long outdoor runs or an aerial line exposed to storms. Lightning nearby can push a surge along cable just as it does along power wiring, and the equipment at the end, a router, a switch, a camera recorder, is what takes the hit. For cable that runs between the house and a shed or out to outdoor cameras, we can add surge protection at the point where it enters the house, and for longer runs between buildings, fiber carries no electrical surge at all. Protection on the provider's own incoming line is the provider's to arrange. Grounding of protective devices involves the house's electrical system, and where that needs an electrician, we say so.

Raised, new and grown houses

If our house is lifted above the flood line, what happens to the cable already inside it?

The cable inside the walls usually goes up with the house, but anything that crossed between the house and its old foundation, or ran through a space that is about to become the open area beneath the raised floor, will be cut or left behind. The provider's line will need to be reconnected at the new height anyway. A lift is the cheapest time there will ever be to fix a house's network, so we suggest testing the existing runs before the lift, deciding which to keep, and planning new cable and a panel on the living level while the lower walls are open. Call us before the utilities are reconnected, so the provider's new entry point can be placed where the panel will be.

Should cable in a flood-zone house travel through the attic or under the floor?

Through the attic or the space above the ceilings, whenever the house allows it. Cable that runs overhead and drops down inside interior walls stays above the water in all but the worst case, and the jacks and panel it connects to sit at a sensible height. Under-floor routes are faster in many cottages, but they put the cable in the first space a flood reaches. Some low-roofed cottages simply have no usable space above, and then we keep the under-floor cable high and connection-free, or use a neat surface route inside the living floor instead. We show you both options on the walk-through with what each would cost.

We are building a new house on a lot where flood insurance is required. Where should the internet come into the house?

At a point on the outside wall above the design flood elevation, with the modem and panel just inside it on the living level. That keeps the most important equipment out of the water and keeps the provider's line from running through a garage or the space under the house to reach it. We mark the entry point and the panel location on the builder's plans, run an empty conduit from that entry down to where the provider is likely to bring the line, and run cable from the panel to every room. The provider still decides how its line reaches your wall, but giving its technician a clear, high entry point makes the right answer the easy one.

Our living room sits in the original cottage and the bedrooms are in an addition on a different floor level. Can one cable get across?

Usually, yes, once we find where the old outside wall sits inside the house and where the two sets of framing meet. The crossing is typically made in the ceiling above the seam, where joists from the old and new sections run side by side, or beneath the floor if the levels allow. We drill through the doubled framing once, at the best spot we can find, and send every cable for the addition through that single crossing. If the floors are at different heights, a short run inside a closet or along a step can bridge them. What we avoid is drilling blindly through the old exterior wall, which may still carry siding inside it.

Is fiber internet available inside the Edgewater Park gate?

We could not find out from public sources. BroadbandNow reports that fiber is available to 84.7 percent of the Bronx, with Verizon Fios and Optimum as the fiber providers, but that is a borough-wide figure, and we found no coverage information for Edgewater Park itself. The private lanes and single entrance may affect how providers build here, but we have no source saying so either way. The honest step is to check your exact address with each provider. Whatever arrives at your wall, the inside of the house works the same way, and a house wired with good cable to a panel above the flood line is ready for whichever service you choose.

FAQ

Edgewater Park Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Our lane is barely wide enough for one car. Where does your van go while you work?
+

Wherever it blocks nobody. State housing officials have noted that many FDNY vehicles are too big for the community's small streets, so we take the narrow lanes seriously. We bring the smallest vehicle that carries the job, ask you on the phone where visitors normally park, and carry tools and cable the last stretch on foot if that keeps the lane open. If the van ever has to stop briefly to unload, someone stays with it and moves it the moment a neighbor needs to pass.

Will wiring our cottage help when we sell it someday?
+

We cannot promise it adds a dollar figure, and we have no local data that measures it. HomeAdvisor's cost guide asks the same question, which tells you buyers think about it. What we can say is that a house with tested cable to every room, a panel above the flood line and a printed port map is easier to describe in a listing and easier for the next family to use. Many buyers now work from home, and a wired home office is a practical feature they can see.

Our bungalow is small. Is running the Ethernet ourselves a reasonable project, or should we hire it out?
+

A single cable along a baseboard inside one room is a fine thing to do yourself. Inside the walls of an Edgewater Park cottage the picture changes: old framing from the winterizing era, additions with old exterior walls inside them, damp crawl spaces, a flood line to respect and the possibility that work outside the walls touches co-op ground. Those are the things that turn a weekend into a month. If the job involves any of them, or you want every run tested and documented, a licensed installer is usually the better value.

My parents live a few houses down. Can our two houses share one internet connection over a cable?
+

Physically, a cable between two houses would cross lanes or side yards, and that ground belongs to the co-op, so it could not happen without the co-op's agreement and both neighbors' consent along the way. Separately, many internet plans are meant for a single household, so you would want to check your provider's terms before sharing service. In practice, each house having its own service and its own well-wired network inside is the cleaner answer, and we can wire both houses on the same trip.

Can the work be scheduled around the Labor Day Olympics and other busy community weekends?
+

Yes, and we would rather. The Bronx Times has covered the centennial of Edgewater Park's Labor Day Olympics, and the community also holds a Ragamuffin parade and the fire department's Memorial Day parade. On those days the lanes fill with people and nobody wants a contractor's van in the way. Tell us the dates your household or the community wants left clear, and we schedule around them.

Could new cable or wall openings cause problems with our flood vents or flood insurance?
+

We do not drill through, block or run cable across flood vents or any opening built to let water pass through a lower enclosure, and we keep our equipment out of spaces below the flood line. Every hole we make through an outside wall is sealed. We are not insurance advisors, though, so if your policy or elevation certificate has specific conditions, read them or ask your agent before the work, and tell us anything we should avoid. We will plan around it.

If the co-op office asks what was done to the outside of our house, will we have something to show them?
+

Yes. Any exterior work is described in the written plan before it happens, so you can share it in advance, and when the job is done we hand over photos of each outside penetration and any exposed run, along with where it is on the house. Together with the port map and test results, that gives you a clear record for the office, for a future buyer or for the next contractor who works on the house.

If there is no way through the inside of the cottage, can cable go around the outside instead?
+

Sometimes, but it is our last choice rather than our first. An outside route needs outdoor-rated cable, protection where it is exposed, sealed entries at both ends and a path that stays on your side of the property, and because it changes the outside of the house it may be something the co-op office wants to see first. Near the bay it is also exposed to wind and salt air. Before suggesting it, we look hard at closets, attics, under-floor spaces and surface runs inside, which usually work.

Can our home network reach the community dock or the beach area?
+

Not by running a cable there from your house. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes the beaches, playgrounds and docks as the community's own, which means anything installed there is a decision for the co-op, not for one household. If the co-op ever wanted connectivity or cabling at a shared space, we would work on its authorization. For your own deck or yard, a cabled access point on the house, aimed the right way, is usually how families get a signal outdoors.

Will our electricity need to be shut off while the network cable goes in?
+

Usually not. Data cable is low-voltage and is installed separately from the house's electrical wiring, so the lights and the refrigerator stay on. We keep our runs apart from electrical cables in the walls. The only time power becomes part of the job is when something needs a new outlet, such as a panel location with no receptacle nearby, and that circuit is work for a licensed electrician, which we would note on the quote.

If we decide halfway through that we want more jacks, does any co-op check have to start over?
+

Extra jacks inside the house are usually just an interior change between you and us: we write the addition into the plan, with its price, and you approve it before we pull anything. If the new request reaches outside, such as another wall entry or a run to the shed, then it is new exterior work, and you may want to show that part to the management office the same way as before. We will tell you which kind of change it is when you ask.
