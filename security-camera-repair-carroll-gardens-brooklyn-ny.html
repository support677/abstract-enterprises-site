The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Carroll Gardens is a small, dense neighborhood in Brooklyn Community District 6, in ZIP code 11231, roughly bounded by Degraw and Warren Streets to the north, Hoyt and Smith Streets to the east, Ninth Street and the Gowanus Expressway to the south and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway to the west. Its streets are mostly three- and four-story rowhouses from the late 1860s to the early 1880s, many of them inside a city historic district, with older tenements and walk-ups thickening toward Hoyt and Bond Streets and two shopping streets, Court and Smith, running north to south through the middle. That is the backdrop. For a camera repair the part that matters is how those buildings lay a camera system out, and where along that layout things tend to break.

Start with the gardens that give the neighborhood its name. A Brooklyn law of 1846 set deep courtyards in front of the houses on certain blocks between Henry and Smith Streets, and the Department of City Planning's 2008 study measured the result: on First, Second, Third and Fourth Places between Henry and Smith, and on Second, Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt, the mapped street is 100 to 130 feet wide, even though the sidewalk and roadway together take up only about 50 feet. The rest is front garden, usually behind an iron fence. For a camera system, that garden is distance. The camera that watches the gate either sits on the house, a long way from the gate, or sits on or near the gate itself, with a cable that has to cross the garden to reach the house. In our repair work, that cable is one of the first places a Carroll Gardens system fails.

Then the facade. The Carroll Gardens Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on September 25, 1973, covers about 130 rowhouses, including the blocks of Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt, built between 1869 and 1884 in late Italianate and neo-Grec styles, with uniform setbacks and fenced, landscaped front yards. Cameras on those fronts are equipment the Commission has rules about. The LPC's 2018 rules amendments codified staff practice for installing objects on facades and areaways, security cameras included, and its permit guidebook gives a specific path for replacing an existing camera in kind. That makes the way a camera is repaired on a district house different from the way it is first installed, and it is one reason we ask where a camera sits before we quote replacing it.

Under the house is the cellar, and in this part of Brooklyn the cellar is where water goes. The Gowanus Community Advisory Group's notes describe the city Department of Environmental Protection's plan to re-sewer all of Carroll Gardens and Gowanus, from Court Street to the canal and from Douglass Street to Fourth Street at Public Place. In neighboring Gowanus, BK Reader reported a resident who found two feet of water in her basement during Hurricane Ida, in an area with many basement units that predate federal flood protection standards. We do not claim any particular Carroll Gardens block flooded in that storm. But recorders, power supplies and PoE switches are commonly installed on cellar shelves or on the cellar floor beside the meters, and a recorder that has been wet, or has spent a summer in a damp cellar, is a frequent repair.

Finally, the street. Court and Smith Streets carry the neighborhood's shops, bakeries, bars and restaurants, with apartments above, and the zoning study maps commercial overlays along both. Each of those stories is, for a camera owner, a question about whether the system was recording, at what quality, and whether anyone could get the clip off it the next morning.

So camera repair here is rarely only "make the black tile come back." It is finding which part of a long system failed, fixing it without disturbing a landmarked front or a carefully planted garden, getting the recorder out of harm's way if water is the problem, and leaving the owner with alerts that say when something stops working. Owners often pair the repair with a look at the doorbell or intercom at the same gate or vestibule, because on these houses the same front door carries both.

Garden-block rowhouses: thirty feet of cable nobody can see

On the courtyard blocks, the most useful camera is the one that shows who comes through the gate, and the most fragile part of it is the cable that reaches it. Installers handle the garden in a few ways. Some mount the camera on the house and run nothing across the yard, which keeps the cable safe but leaves the camera far from the gate. Some fix a camera to the fence or a gate post and staple the cable along the fence, under the hedge or along the edge of the path. Some bury it, with or without conduit, under the path or a bed. Each of these fails in its own way. Stapled cable gets chewed, pulled by vines, cut by hedge trimmers or crushed by a fallen branch. Buried cable without conduit takes water at every nick in its jacket and eventually corrodes inside. Even buried conduit can fill with water if its ends were not sealed, and the connector at the gate end sits in that water.

The repair starts by proving the fault is in the run and not the camera. We test the camera on a short known-good cable at the gate, then test the run from the house end. If the run is bad, the choice is between replacing it along the same path, pulling a new cable through existing conduit if there is one, or rerouting it along the fence line where it can be reached next time. We tell you which disturbs the garden least and what each costs before any bed is opened. If the garden is being redone anyway, that is the moment to put a sleeve under the path, and we will say so, but we do not dig up a healthy garden to fix a connector.

Historic district houses: putting it back the same way

On a house inside the district, the question for any facade camera repair is whether the work is a replacement in kind. The LPC's permit guidebook, in its chapter on health and safety equipment, has a criterion for in-kind replacement of existing light fixtures, intercoms, security cameras and mailboxes that show no or minimally exposed conduit, and it asks for manufacturer cut sheets for the new item. Its broader rules for new cameras ask that they be small, well spaced, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and with very little visible conduit. In practice, a dead camera replaced with a similar one on the same bracket and the same anchors, with its cable leaving the way the old one did, is the simple case. Moving it, adding one, or swapping a small dome for a large bullet on a long arm is a different conversation, and we have it with you before any drill touches the brownstone.

Brownstone is soft and spalls; a new hole a few inches from an old one can take a corner of carved stone with it. So even off the district, we reuse existing anchor holes where they are sound, seal old holes we do not reuse, and fix to joints rather than to the face of the stone. If you are not sure whether your house is inside the district, the LPC publishes a map searchable by address, and the boundary runs lot by lot, so the house next door may be in when yours is out.

Rowhouses on the streets without deep gardens: the stoop and the cellar

Not every Carroll Gardens house has a long front yard. On many cross streets and on the avenue blocks, the house has an ordinary areaway, a stoop and an under-stoop door to the garden floor. The camera system is shorter, but the faults are familiar: a camera over the parlor door whose dome has gone cloudy, an under-stoop camera that got knocked or sprayed, and a recorder in the cellar under the stoop that nobody has looked at since it was installed. On these houses the cellar matters as much as the facade. Cable comes down through the vestibule floor or the front wall, and in a wet season the bottom of that run, where it enters the cellar, is often where corrosion starts.

Tenements and walk-ups: the landlord's box and the tenants' complaints

Toward Hoyt and Bond Streets and above the shops on Court and Smith, older walk-ups have a street door, a narrow hall and a stair, with cameras in the hall and at the door and a recorder in the cellar or in a locked closet. Tenants notice first when a hallway camera dies, especially after a package goes missing. City rules matter here in a specific way. The Multiple Dwelling Law's section on entrances covers doors, locks and intercom systems, and a broken intercom in a covered building is a hazardous violation that HPD can enforce. There is no general city law that requires cameras in apartment buildings, although insurers often ask for them. So a dead hallway camera is not usually a violation in itself, but the intercom or door station on the same wall may be, and we check both on the same visit if you want.

Small condos and co-ops: who holds the password

Carroll Gardens has many rowhouses converted into a few condo or co-op units. Their camera systems were often installed by the developer or by one owner on everyone's behalf, and years later nobody knows the admin password, whose phone the alerts go to, or who is allowed to pay for a repair. A court case written up by one law firm turned on the fact that a four-unit condominium was too small for the Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom provisions to apply, which is a reminder that small buildings are often governed more by their own bylaws than by city rules. Before we repair a shared system, we ask the board or the owners to name one person who approves the work and receives the admin login, and we leave a written note of what was changed so the next owner does not start from nothing.

Storefront buildings on Court and Smith: two systems, one building

A typical storefront building on Court or Smith Street has a business on the ground floor, a basement used for storage, a walk-in or a prep kitchen, and apartments upstairs reached by a separate street door. The business usually owns its own cameras and recorder. The landlord may have a separate set covering the residential door and stair. When something fails, the first job is sorting out which camera belongs to which recorder and who owns each one. We label every cable and channel as we go, so the next call does not start with an hour of tracing, and we make sure a repair for the tenant does not quietly change the landlord's system, or the other way round.

This is the way our technicians approach a Carroll Gardens camera call, written as working method, not as a story about any particular customer or house. When an owner says a camera is out, the first question we ask is not which camera, but where the recorder lives and what the floor around it looks like. On a rowhouse here the answer is usually the cellar, often under the stoop or beside the boiler, and that single fact changes the visit. The second question is about the garden. Three short answers by text usually mean the technician arrives with the right parts and a plan the owner has already agreed to, instead of a first visit that only finds the problem.

Fault by Fault

Wet Cellars, Long Garden Runs, Weathered Facades, Worn Drives and Missed Nights: How Carroll Gardens Camera Systems Fail

Wet cellars: what water does to a recorder, even after it dries

A recorder that has stood in water is not fixed because it turns on again. Water leaves minerals and corrosion on the board and the connectors, and a recorder that boots fine the week after a storm can begin restarting, losing its drive or dropping cameras weeks later as corrosion spreads. Hard drives are sealed against dust, not against water, and a drive that sat in a flooded cellar may still read for a while and then fail without warning. Plug-in camera power supplies and PoE switches usually live on the same shelf and suffer the same way. Even without standing water, a cellar that stays damp through a humid summer does slow damage to the same parts.

The repair after water has three parts. First, copy whatever footage matters to a separate drive, because the original drive is now the part most likely to fail. Second, test every powered part in the chain, not just the recorder: the supply, the switch and the backup unit, if there is one. Third, move the equipment so the next storm cannot reach it. That might mean a wall shelf well above the floor, a ventilated cabinet on the garden floor, or a closet upstairs with the cables re-run to it. When the recorder moves, a few long cable runs may need to be extended, which is priced on the visit. Given DEP's stated plan to re-sewer the whole area from Court Street to the canal, we would rather move a recorder once than replace it twice.

Long garden runs: water, roots, shovels and voltage drop

A cable that crosses a front garden lives in soil, mulch, rain and frost, and it was not always the right cable for that. Indoor-rated cable stapled along a fence lasts a few seasons before its jacket cracks in the sun. Cable laid under mulch without conduit lets water travel along its length into the connector. A spade or an edging tool finds a shallow run within a year or two of planting. Long runs also lose voltage. A camera powered by a small plug-in supply in the cellar, sixty or eighty feet of cable away by the time the run climbs out of the cellar and crosses the garden, may work in summer and drop out on cold nights when its infrared turns on and it draws more power. A PoE camera on a long run can fail the same way if its switch port gives only the minimum.

The fix depends on what we find. A corroded connector is cut back and replaced with a sealed one, in a weatherproof box above the soil. A damaged run is replaced with outdoor, direct-burial cable along the same path or through a sleeve. A voltage problem is solved by powering the camera from a better supply, from a PoE switch that gives more power per port, or from a supply nearer the camera. We measure the voltage at the camera end before and after, because a run that fails only on winter nights will not show its fault on a mild afternoon.

Weathered facades: fog, haze and failed seals

Cameras on south- or west-facing fronts take years of sun, and cameras under window hoods and cornices take the drip line of every storm. The commonest failures are a dome cover yellowed and scratched until the picture is hazy, a seal that has let moisture in so the lens fogs from inside on humid mornings, and a connector behind the camera that has turned green inside its junction box. A hazy cover can sometimes be cleaned or replaced on its own. Fogging from inside usually means the seal has failed, and a camera that has fogged once will fog again, so replacing it is the honest answer. A corroded connector behind a working camera is the cheapest repair on this list, but it means opening the box and re-sealing it properly, not wrapping it in tape.

Worn drives: the part that never stops working

A recorder writing four or eight cameras around the clock wears its hard drive out, and our own Brooklyn repair notes put the usual life of a drive in that duty at three to five years. When a drive starts to fail, the recorder may keep showing live video while saving nothing, or saving in pieces, so the problem is discovered only when someone goes looking for a clip. Most recorders can warn about a failing or full drive, but only if alerts were set up and go to a phone someone reads. In a Carroll Gardens cellar, nobody sees the warning light on the front of the box. Replacing a drive is one of the most common and least expensive repairs we do; the important part is copying what can be copied first and setting alerts so the next drive does not fail in silence.

Missed nights: live view fine, recording empty

Several of the Carroll Gardens incidents in the news were overnight. Patch's Carroll Gardens police blotter described a trattoria on Columbia Street, just across the expressway, burglarized at 4:30 on a Sunday morning, with the intruder caught on video. A Yahoo News report described a burglar who cut a hole above the Smith & 9th Street Deli and Grocery on Ninth Street near Smith Street around 3:15 a.m. on a November 1, and who was last seen on camera boarding a Manhattan-bound train. Overnight is exactly when a badly set recorder lets an owner down. Some systems record only on motion, with a sensitivity too low to wake for a person in a dark shop. Some keep a low-quality stream to save space. Some have a schedule that stops recording after closing because whoever set it up thought that was sensible. The camera is fine, and the recording is useless. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

The gate, the garden and the front of the house

Our gate camera is bolted to the iron fence at the sidewalk end of a front garden more than thirty feet deep. It works on dry days and blacks out when it rains. Is that the camera or the cable crossing the garden?

A fault that comes and goes with rain is almost always water getting into a connection, and on a gate camera the likely spots are the connector behind the camera, a junction where the cable was spliced along the fence, or the point where it enters the house. Cameras themselves are sealed and rarely fail only when wet unless their housing has cracked. We test the camera on a short known-good cable at the gate, then test the run from the house, and dry-test the suspect joints. In most cases the fix is a sealed connector in a weatherproof box above the soil, not a new camera.

A landscaper re-edged the beds beside our front path, and the gate camera has shown nothing since. Will fixing it mean taking up the path?

Usually not. A cable cut by an edging tool is normally cut where it ran shallow along the edge of a bed, and that is the spot we look first, with a cable locator if the run is buried. If the cut is in a reachable place, a sealed splice or a short new section along the same line fixes it. If the run is damaged in several places or sits under the path itself, we price both a replacement along the old route and a new route along the fence where it can be reached next time, and you choose before anything is dug.

Landmarks staff asked us for a manufacturer's cut sheet before our dead facade camera is swapped. What is that, and can you provide it?

A cut sheet is the manufacturer's one- or two-page product sheet showing the camera's size, shape, color and mounting, usually with a dimensioned drawing. The LPC's permit guidebook asks for cut sheets for security cameras, light fixtures, intercoms and mailboxes, including on in-kind replacements of existing equipment. We send the cut sheet for the exact model we propose, choose a model close in size and color to the one being replaced, and plan to reuse the existing bracket and cable exit so that little or no conduit shows. Filing remains the owner's step; we give you what the application needs.

Our gate camera came back after a summer repair, but now it drops out on cold nights once its night lights switch on. What changed?

Most likely the power, not the camera. When a camera's infrared lights come on at dusk, it draws noticeably more power, and on cold nights some cameras also run a small heater. On a long run from the cellar across the garden, a small plug-in supply or a weak switch port that was just enough in summer is no longer enough, so the camera browns out and restarts. We measure the voltage at the camera end with its lights on, and fix it with a stronger supply, a PoE port with more power, or a supply nearer the gate.

The cellar and the power

Our recorder sits on the same cellar shelf as the outlet for the sump pump. When the pump kicks on in a storm, the recorder sometimes restarts. Are the two connected?

Very possibly. A pump motor starting on the same circuit, or on an outlet strip shared with the recorder, pulls a sharp dip in voltage for a moment, and some recorders and supplies restart on a dip they cannot ride through. Every restart risks the drive and leaves a gap in recording, exactly during a storm. The fix is to put the recorder on its own outlet, ideally on a different circuit from the pump, through a small battery backup sized for the recorder and switch. Moving the outlet or circuit is an electrician's job, which we do not do; we tell you exactly what is needed and set up the camera side.

Every spring the front wall of our cellar under the stoop gets damp right where the camera cables come in, and the two front cameras fail more often than the rest. Is that a coincidence?

Probably not. Where a cable passes through a damp masonry wall, it usually has a connector or splice on the inside face, and that joint sits in damp air for months. Corrosion there causes flicker, drop-outs and eventually a dead camera, while the cameras on dry runs keep working. We open the joint, cut back to clean cable, use sealed connectors, mount the junction a little higher on the dry side of the wall, and seal the cable entry. If the wall itself is wet, that is a mason's job, but the camera side can be made to tolerate it.

Shops and restaurants on Court and Smith

After the break-ins around Smith and Ninth this fall, a detective asked shops near us for overnight video. Ours covered the register, but the clip is too blocky to read a face. What needs to change on the recorder?

Usually the recording settings, not the camera. Many recorders keep a low-resolution sub-stream, or a heavily compressed main stream, to stretch the drive, and in a dark shop at night that compression turns faces into blocks. We set the register and door cameras to record their full-quality stream continuously, raise the bit rate to suit a low-light scene, check that the camera's night mode is not smearing motion, and size the drive to keep at least a week at that quality. Then we export a test clip so you can see the difference before we leave.

Our Court Street shop shares a basement with the building's meters and boiler, and our recorder is plugged into the landlord's circuit. When the super shuts that breaker for work, we lose recording. How should this be set up?

The recorder should run from a circuit the business controls, or at least from an outlet nobody switches off for routine work, through a battery backup that keeps it running for a short shutdown and closes it down cleanly on a long one. We also turn on the recorder's power-loss and video-loss alerts so you know the moment it goes off, and label the outlet and breaker so the super knows what it feeds. Rewiring the outlet is for the landlord's electrician; we set up everything on the camera side and tell them exactly what is needed.

We opened on Smith Street this year in a space where the last tenant left dome cameras in the ceiling but took the recorder. Can those cameras work with a new recorder?

Often, yes. We first find out what they are: coax cameras need a DVR of the matching type, and network cameras need an NVR or a switch and software that support them. Network cameras left behind are usually still locked with the old tenant's password and need a factory reset. We test each camera and its cable, tell you which are worth keeping, and price a recorder that fits them. If the cameras are old or a mix of types, we will tell you when a new recorder plus a few new cameras costs less than nursing the old ones along.

Walk-ups, condos and footage

Packages keep going missing from the hallway of our walk-up toward Hoyt Street. The landlord says the hallway camera records but will not let us watch it. Do tenants have a right to see the recording?

Not automatically. Lawyers quoted by Brick Underground said landlords generally are not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and that city buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. One of them, attorney Catherine Grad, added, "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord" to review footage when thefts keep happening. Our tenement's only street-door camera is the one built into the intercom panel. The panel still rings upstairs, but the recorder shows no picture from it. Which part failed?

If the panel rings and talks, the station itself is working, so the fault is usually the video link from the panel to the recorder: a converter, a separate video cable, a network port or a setting on the recorder. Some door stations were never connected to the recorder at all and only show video on the apartment monitors. We check how it was wired, test the video at the panel and at the recorder, and tell you whether recording it needs a repair or a small addition. A bike chained inside our vestibule was taken, and the vestibule camera shows only the top of a head. Can it be lowered without new wiring?

Usually, yes. DNAinfo once reported a thief on Smith Street who removed part of a vestibule banister to free a chained bike, so the thief at the bike, not the street door, is the face the camera needs. A camera mounted high and aimed down sees scalps. Moving it a foot or two lower on the same wall, or re-aiming it across the vestibule at head height, usually needs only a short extension of the existing cable. If the vestibule has a glass outer door, we also check for glare and night reflections at the new angle, and confirm the recorder keeps the full-quality stream from that camera.

We are selling our Carroll Gardens rowhouse. Should the camera system be repaired, reset or removed before the closing?

That depends on the contract, so check with your lawyer whether the cameras are fixtures staying with the house. If they stay, it is fair to the buyer to leave them working, factory-reset with your accounts removed, with the admin login and the recorder's location written down for them. Either way, your own footage should be copied off or wiped and your phone removed from the app. If you are taking the system, we can take it down; leftover holes in the facade are the masonry contractor's work, not ours, so plan that before closing.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

The older systems on many rowhouses, wired with coaxial cable to a DVR in the cellar, often powered from one multi-camera supply. Connectors corrode at damp walls and garden joints, and the supply tends to fail first. Many can be upgraded in place on the same cable.

Network (IP) cameras

Powered and connected by one cable to a PoE switch or NVR, common in newer shop systems and renovated houses. Faults are usually a switch port, a connector, a power budget too small for a long garden run, or an address change after a router swap.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the video, usually in a cellar. Drives wear out, damp corrodes boards, outages wipe settings and clocks. A hybrid recorder lets a house keep working coax cameras while adding network ones.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras whose lens can be zoomed after mounting, useful at a gate far from the house, over a narrow shop counter, or down a walk-up hallway where a face must fill more of the frame.

Outdoor bullets on arms and brackets

The usual facade and gate camera. Sun yellows them, drip lines from cornices and hoods find their seals, and brackets loosen in soft brownstone. Replaced on the same fixings where we can.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Motorized or fisheye cameras in larger shops and on some rear walls. Motors, saved positions and fogged domes are what we fix most often.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Consumer cameras in apartments, on stoops and in back gardens. Weak signal through brick walls, batteries in winter, and app accounts tied to someone who moved away are the usual problems.

Video door stations

Intercom stations with built-in cameras at walk-up street doors and some garden gates. Sometimes recorded by the main system, sometimes not; we check which before promising footage.

PoE switches, supplies and battery backups

The parts that power everything, and the first to suffer from damp, power dips and long runs. Checked on every call and sized properly when replaced.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Carroll Gardens

Search summaries and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr hand out general advice on camera repair. Much of it is right anywhere. Some of it misses how camera systems are laid out and how they fail on these blocks. These are the claims we hear repeated most, and what we actually find on Carroll Gardens repairs.

"If a camera keeps disconnecting, replace the camera"

On a camera next to its recorder, that might be a reasonable guess. On a Carroll Gardens gate camera, the camera is usually the least likely part to have failed. Between it and the recorder there may be a garden, a fence line, a foundation wall and a flight of cellar stairs, and every joint along that route is a candidate.

A camera that drops out in rain points to water in a connector. One that drops out on cold nights points to power on a long run. One that died the day the garden was worked on points to a cut cable. None of those is fixed by a new camera, and a new camera on the same faulty run fails the same way.

So we test the camera on a short cable at its own location before we suggest replacing it. If it works there, the money goes into the run, which is where the fault is.

"Wireless cameras are the easy fix when wiring fails"

In an open suburban yard, sometimes. In a Carroll Gardens rowhouse, the router is usually indoors behind a masonry front, and the gate may be thirty or forty feet beyond that, past an iron fence. Our own Brooklyn notes record signal at brownstone back yards and garden floors around -85 dBm, too weak for a camera to stream reliably.

A wireless camera at the gate can work if an outdoor access point is placed to reach it, and if it still gets steady power. But "wireless" cameras are rarely power-free; battery models need charging, more often in winter, and wired-power models still need a cable.

Replacing a failing gate cable with a well-sealed outdoor cable in a sleeve is often more reliable than any wireless option, and it fails less often. We price both when both make sense.

"Landmark rules mean you cannot touch a camera on a historic house without a permit"

The LPC's rules do cover cameras on facades; its 2018 amendments codified staff practice for installing security cameras and other equipment on facades and areaways. But its permit guidebook also sets a specific criterion for in-kind replacement of existing security cameras, intercoms and light fixtures with no or minimally exposed conduit.

In practice, a dead camera replaced with a similar one, in the same spot, with the cable leaving the way it did before, is the simple case. The application asks for the manufacturer's cut sheet for the new camera, which we provide.

What draws more scrutiny is new or moved equipment, a much larger camera, or new visible conduit. We tell you before the visit if a repair crosses that line, so it does not turn into a facade problem after the fact.

"If the recorder turns back on after a flood, it's fine"

Recorders and drives that have stood in water often boot normally at first. Corrosion on the board and the connectors keeps working for weeks, and a drive that was wet can fail without warning months later. A cellar that floods is the exact place where a recorder keeps the footage you might need.

The first step after water is to copy anything important to a separate drive, then test every part of the chain, including supplies, switches and any backup battery, rather than trusting the live view.

The step most people skip is moving the equipment. With DEP planning to re-sewer the whole area from Court Street to the canal, a recorder that goes back on the cellar floor is simply waiting for the next storm.

"Motion recording saves space and catches everything"

It saves space. It does not catch everything, especially in a dark shop at 3 a.m. Motion detection that is not tuned may wake late, miss a slow-moving person, or trigger on headlights and miss the person behind them. Smart detection is better but still has to be set up for the scene.

The overnight burglaries reported around Smith and Ninth Streets, and the older Patch reports of restaurants and delis broken into before dawn, are exactly the hours when a shop is empty and every second of footage counts.

For the register, door and back-door cameras of a business, continuous recording at full quality is the safer setting, with motion recording reserved for quieter cameras. The drive is sized to match. That is a settings repair, not new hardware.

"Any handyman or electrician can fix a camera system"

In New York, companies that install or maintain security systems for others are licensed by the Department of State, and the state's rules count CCTV and video surveillance as part of that work. An electrician can add the outlet a recorder needs; the cameras, cabling, recorder and network are licensed low-voltage work.

The tools are different too. Most of the faults on this page are found with a cable tester, a PoE tester, the recorder's logs, a moisture meter and a voltage reading at the far end of a garden run, not with a meter on the house wiring.

If someone has already been in, tell us what they changed. It saves time, and it is common for a well-meant fix to have moved the fault rather than cured it.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

General national guides put a service call at $75 to $150 for the diagnosis and a typical repair at $150 to $400. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 across all job sizes. Both are averages of very different jobs.

On our Brooklyn list, a settings or connector fix can fall inside the $150 service call or run $150 to $350, a single camera replacement runs $250 to $600, and a recorder replacement runs $700 to $1,500. A gate run replaced across a garden is priced on the visit by length and route.

The expensive repairs are the ones done twice: a camera replaced when the cable was the problem, a drive replaced when damp was killing the recorder. Testing in order is how we avoid paying for the same fault two times.

How many days of video should the recorder in a Carroll Gardens rowhouse keep, and what decides it?
+

We aim for at least a week at the quality that shows a face, and two weeks or more where the drive allows. What decides it is the number of cameras, their resolution, whether they record continuously or on motion, and the size of the drive. Because burglaries here have often been discovered when owners came home, and package thefts are noticed days later, a few extra days of history is worth more than a little extra sharpness. On a repair we read the recorder's actual retention, not the box's claim, and set the drive and schedule to reach your target.

Can our repaired gate camera alert us only when a person comes through the gate, and not for every passing car or windblown branch?
+

Usually, if the camera or recorder supports person detection, which most systems from the last several years do. We draw the detection zone around the gate and the path rather than the sidewalk, turn on person or vehicle classification, and set a short delay so one visitor sends one alert. Older cameras without smart detection can still be improved with a tighter zone and lower sensitivity. If yours cannot do it at all, we tell you what a camera that can would cost before suggesting it.

Our system is supposedly still under the original installer's warranty, but the company no longer answers. Will your repair affect that?
+

A warranty that nobody honors is not worth much, but check your paperwork first. If the installer's warranty covers parts and they will not respond, the manufacturer's own warranty on the camera or recorder may still apply, and we can help you find the model and serial numbers for a claim. Our repair does not take away a manufacturer's warranty on equipment we do not open. We note on the invoice exactly what we changed, so any later claim is clear about what was original and what was replaced.

Can our recorder copy its video to the cloud, so a flooded or stolen box does not take everything with it?
+

Many can, either through the maker's own cloud service or by sending event clips to an online storage account, and some cameras can save to a memory card of their own as a second copy. Continuous cloud recording of several cameras needs a strong upload connection and usually a subscription, so most owners keep full recording on the recorder and send only alerts and short clips offsite. For a cellar that takes water or a shop where the recorder sits near the register, that second copy is cheap insurance.

Our house still runs an older coax camera system. Is it worth repairing, or should we move to network cameras?
+

It is often worth repairing. Coax that is already inside the walls and across the garden is valuable, because replacing it is the costly part of any new system. Newer HD-over-coax cameras and hybrid recorders can raise the picture quality on the same cable, and a hybrid recorder lets you add network cameras later where you want them. We move a house to all network cameras when the old cable is damaged in several places, when you want features coax cannot offer, or when the repair would cost close to a new system.

We have masons coming to repoint our brownstone front. What should we tell them about the cameras, and should you be there?
+

Tell them where every camera, bracket and cable runs on the facade, and ask them not to cut, bend or paint over any cable. If cameras must come off for the work, it is better to have us remove them, cap the cables and put them back afterward, ideally into the same joints so a landmarked front keeps its in-kind replacement. Facade work is one of the commonest causes of cut or painted cables, and a short visit before and after usually costs less than finding the damage later.

Our shop has its own cameras and the apartments upstairs have the landlord's. Can both run on one recorder?
+

Technically yes, but it is rarely a good idea. One recorder means one owner of the drive, the logins and the footage, and a shop and a landlord usually need separate control, separate retention and separate people to answer a detective. Two recorders also mean one failure does not blind both. What we often do instead is label both systems clearly, put each recorder on its own protected outlet, and agree in writing which cameras belong to whom, so a repair to one never disturbs the other.

Is there a simple way to know each morning that the cellar recorder is still alive without walking down there?
+

Yes. Most recorders can send alerts when a camera loses video, when the drive fails or fills, and when recording stops, and many apps show whether the recorder is online. Some can also send a daily snapshot or a scheduled health email. We set up whichever your system supports, send it to a phone or address someone actually reads, and trigger each alert once in front of you so you know what it looks like. A quick playback of a random hour each month catches anything the alerts miss.

Can the gate camera's picture show on a tablet or screen in the parlor floor, so we can see who is outside before we walk out?
+

Yes, on almost any working system. A tablet or small monitor can run the recorder's app or connect to the recorder directly, and it can be set to open the gate camera when the doorbell rings or when someone is detected at the gate. On a house set far back from the sidewalk, that view saves a walk down the stoop and through the garden every time someone pauses at the fence. We set it up on the visit, or as a small add-on to a repair.

The cable to our gate camera has failed twice. Would a wireless camera at the gate be the better repair?
+

Sometimes, but only if two things are solved: power and signal. A camera at the gate still needs steady power, and battery models need charging more often in cold weather. Signal from a router behind a masonry front to a gate thirty or more feet away is often too weak to stream reliably without an outdoor access point. Where both can be solved, wireless is a reasonable choice. Where they cannot, a properly sealed outdoor cable in a sleeve along the fence usually outlasts either.
