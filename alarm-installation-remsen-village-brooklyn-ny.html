The Neighborhood

Why Remsen Village Properties Need Alarm Installation

Remsen Village is the eastern end of East Flatbush, and its edges depend on who is drawing them. Brownstoner's 2013 Building of the Day describes it as "a neighborhood generally considered a subset of East Flatbush" and gives its borders as Kings Highway, Ralph Avenue, Ditmas Avenue and Rockaway Parkway. The city's 2020 neighborhood tabulation areas list "East Flatbush-Remsen Village" separately from "East Flatbush-Rugby" to its north. Wikipedia folds the two together as the eastern section of East Flatbush, records that the Remsen Village name only came into use in the mid-1990s, and notes that "Rugby", chosen by developers in the 1890s, was the older name. The Brooklyn Eagle described Remsen Avenue, which shares the neighborhood's name, as "a big, busy thoroughfare" that starts where Empire Boulevard, Utica Avenue and East New York Avenue meet and runs southeast to Canarsie.

The houses are newer than the names. Brownstoner explains that the land stayed farmland in the old Town of Flatlands until the 1920s, when the Nostrand Avenue subway reached Brooklyn College, and that most blocks were built between the 1920s and the 1940s for working- and middle-class buyers. Its example is the row at 649 to 677 East 91st Street, between Avenue A and Avenue B: detached two-family houses from 1932, designed by Irving Kirshenblit, each with two apartments and a two-car garage at the back, one garage for each apartment. Brownstoner called them a turn "to the car-dependent suburbs". A local neighborhood profile adds brick row houses, semi-detached houses and garden apartment buildings, many in red or yellow brick with small front yards. In practice that means a lot of houses where the owner lives on one floor and rents the other, with a driveway, a garage and a cellar door that nobody looks at from the street.

The people who live here are the other half of the story. Wikipedia describes East Flatbush as 51 percent foreign-born, mostly from the Caribbean, and calls it the largest West Indian neighborhood in New York City. Many of the owners bought decades ago. That combination produces a pattern we see on almost every survey here: a house that is fully lived in for most of the year and completely empty for some of it. Families go home for Carnival, for Christmas, for a funeral or a wedding. Older owners go into hospital or rehabilitation, or spend the winter with children in another state. And when an owner dies, the house can stand empty for months while an estate is settled.

An empty house in East Flatbush has drawn a particular kind of attention. In December 2020 the Brooklyn Eagle reported on homeowners here pushing for a cease-and-desist list against aggressive buyers, with "We Buy Houses" signs stapled high on lamp posts; one lifelong resident told the paper, "There are people who scheme and scam their way into getting people's properties." In July 2026 amNY reported, and Brownstoner republished, the Attorney General's 23-count indictment of a man accused of forging the deed to a house on East 95th Street that a couple had owned since 1976, renting rooms to outsiders while the elderly owner, who has dementia, and her ex-husband were confined to one small bedroom. In January 2020 CBS New York reported on a vacant house on Clarendon Road whose neighbors had dealt with squatters, rodents and a leaking roof for years. None of those stories is a classic break-in. All of them begin with a house that somebody believed nobody was watching.

What We Install

A responder plan

Before any device goes up we write down who receives which alert, who may go to the house, who holds a key and which codes exist. The panel is then programmed to match that plan, not the other way round.

Door, window and garage contacts

Contacts on every door people use, the cellar door, windows reachable from the yard or the driveway, and the side or rear door of a detached garage when it can be reached by a wireless sensor.

Interior motion for long absences

Pet-tolerant motion sensors at the foot of the stairs and in the main hallway, aimed away from radiators, vents and windows so they can stay armed for weeks without false alarms.

Temperature and water sensors

Low-temperature sensors near pipes on outside walls and water sensors under the water heater, the washing machine and the lowest point of the cellar, reporting to the same app as the burglar alarm.

Two areas for owner and tenant

For two-family houses, one panel split into two areas, so the owner's floor can be armed away while the tenant's floor carries on as normal, or an empty unit between tenants can stay armed around the clock.

Monitoring and alerts

Central-station monitoring billed month to month from $19.99, or self-monitoring through the app. Either way the call order is set around who can actually reach the house, not only who owns it.

Shop and restaurant alarms

Glass-break behind the windows, contacts on the back door and the roll-down gate, motion in the stockroom, personal staff codes and a not-armed-by-closing message to the owner.

Takeovers and estate houses

Panels inherited with a house or left by an alarm company that no longer exists, tested, re-coded and moved to cellular reporting where the old wiring is sound; codes nobody remembers are cleared.

Repairs and false alarms

Beeping keypads, trouble lights, motion sensors that react to heat, and doors that will not show closed, with the event history read back to find the cause rather than guessing.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Remsen Village's Architecture

The houses here were built over two or three decades for families who owned a car and often rented out a floor. These are the building types we meet on surveys, and what each one changes about the alarm.

Detached two-families with rear garages

Like Brownstoner's 1932 row on East 91st Street: two stacked apartments, a driveway down the side and garages at the back. The rear of the house and the garages are out of sight from the street, so the back door, the cellar door and the driveway-side windows come first.

Brick semi-detached houses

Pairs of houses sharing one side wall, with a driveway or a side yard on the other side. The open side carries most of the reachable windows; the shared wall carries the risk that a siren is louder for the neighbor than for anyone at home.

Brick row houses

Attached rows with small front yards. Front door, rear door onto the yard, ground-floor rear windows and the cellar entrance make up most of the job, and a wireless system avoids opening finished walls.

Larger older houses

Scattered older houses, such as the one at 8807 Avenue B that the Brooklyn Eagle photographed in 2017, have more rooms, more windows and often old wiring left in the walls from an earlier system.

Garden apartment buildings

Low-rise buildings set back behind lawns or courts. The alarm protects the apartment door and any ground-floor window facing the court; the building entrance belongs to the owner or the board.

Apartments over stores

Walk-ups above shops on Church, Utica and Remsen Avenues. The apartment gets its own system, kept separate from the store's, so neither depends on the other's codes or the other's tenant answering a call.

Method notes by building type

Rear garages. A garage at the end of a long driveway is often too far for a wireless contact to reach the panel reliably through two brick walls. We test the signal from the garage door before promising coverage there. Where it works, the garage's side door gets a contact; where it does not, a wireless repeater in the rear of the house or a separate camera alert covers it instead. We do not trench cable across a yard unless the owner asks.

Cellars. Most houses here have a cellar with a door to the yard or the driveway and small windows at ground level. Those windows are low, hidden and far from the bedrooms, which makes them the first openings we cover in a house that will stand empty. The cellar is also where the water heater, the boiler and the main shutoff valve live, so the water and temperature sensors go there too.

Shared walls. On a semi-detached or row house, a siren mounted on the party wall is heard better next door than anywhere else. We place sirens on interior walls facing the house's own rooms and keep the run short when the house is empty, so a neighbor is not left listening to a siren nobody is coming to switch off.

Old wiring. Houses that had an alarm in the 1980s or 1990s often still have wired contacts in the door frames. When those wires test clean, a new panel with a cellular communicator can reuse them, which is cheaper than replacing every contact. When they do not, we leave them in the wall and go wireless.

Owners opening walls for an alarm sometimes add Ethernet to the second floor at the same time; we can quote network cabling for a Remsen Village house on the same visit.

The Empty-House Problem

Three Risks to a House Nobody Is Living In: Entry, Occupation and Title

Most alarm marketing is about one moment: a stranger forcing a door while the family sleeps upstairs. That moment matters, but it is not what worries most of the Remsen Village owners we talk to. Their worry is the six weeks in the islands, the three months in rehabilitation after a fall, or the year an inherited house spends in probate. During those stretches there is nobody to hear a window break, nobody to notice a new padlock on the side gate, and nobody to open the mail from the city register. We find it helpful to separate what can go wrong into three risks, because each one needs a different tool and only two of them are an alarm's job.

Entry is the familiar risk: someone gets in, takes what they can carry and leaves. A conventional alarm handles it well. A contact on the door or window, a motion sensor in the hall and a monitored call bring police or a relative within minutes, and the event history records when it happened.

Occupation is the risk that someone gets in and stays. CBS New York's 2020 report on the vacant Clarendon Road house in East Flatbush described neighbors dealing with squatters for years. The State Senate's April 2024 release on the state budget explained the change in law that followed a run of such cases across New York: real property law now says "a tenant shall not include a squatter", defined as someone who "enters or intrudes upon real property without the permission of the person entitled to possession" and continues to occupy it. An alarm cannot evict anyone, but it can do two useful things. It reports the first entry while the person is still inside, and it keeps a time-stamped record of that entry, which is exactly the kind of fact that matters later when someone claims they have been living there for months.

Title is the risk that someone never touches the house at all and instead files a forged deed. No sensor can detect that. The East 49th Street case the Brooklyn District Attorney prosecuted involved a forged deed filed with the city register in May 2015 while the 84-year-old owner lived with family in New Jersey. The East 95th Street case the Attorney General brought in 2026 involved a house allegedly stolen in 2023. The tool for this risk is paperwork: the New York City Department of Finance runs a free Notice of Recorded Document program that emails or mails a registered owner whenever a deed, mortgage or related document is recorded against the property, usually the day after recording. Registration is per property and costs nothing. We mention it on every Remsen Village survey because it covers the one gap no alarm can.

The two clocks that start when the house empties

Two deadlines run in the background whenever a house is left empty, and neither has anything to do with burglars. The first is the insurance clock. Policygenius's guide to vandalism coverage, updated in 2022, says a standard homeowners policy will not cover vandalism once a home has been vacant for more than 60 days, and suggests a vacancy endorsement or a separate vacant-home policy. Policies differ, so the only reliable answer is in your own policy and from your own agent, but the 60-day figure is common enough that every owner planning a long absence should ask. An alarm does not stop that clock, though a monitored system and a certificate from the installer are things an insurer may ask about when you request the endorsement.

The second is the learning clock. Some of the smarter alarm features depend on the system knowing what normal looks like. Alarm.com's Unexpected Activity alerts, as one dealer's help page explains, learn a property's usual patterns and only begin once the system has collected 30 days of activity. Install the alarm the week before a long trip and those features will not have started. Install it a month or more ahead, live with it normally, and they will be ready before the house is left alone.

What "armed away" should mean in an empty house

In a house where people come and go every day, "away" mode usually means the doors, the windows and the interior motion sensors are all armed for a few hours. In a house that is empty for weeks, the same mode has to run for hundreds of hours in a row, and that changes the details. Entry delays can be shorter on doors nobody will use. Motion sensors must be placed where nothing in the empty house moves: not over a radiator that comes on with the thermostat, not facing a window where sunlight crawls across the floor, and not in a room where a mylar balloon from a birthday party is still drifting. Temperature and water sensors, which are a convenience in an occupied house, become the most important devices on the system in January.

The alerts change too. In an occupied house most owners want to hear about every door. In an empty house every door is news, but so is anything that suggests someone is living there: motion in the bedrooms at two in the morning, lights switched on manually, the thermostat turned up, or water sensors near the bathroom picking up a puddle that only happens when a shower is used. Most of that can be read from the system's own history; some of it needs a smart plug or a smart thermostat paired with the same app. We decide with the owner, before we leave, which of those signals are worth a phone alert and which belong only in the weekly history review.

Who Goes to the House

The Responder Plan: Deciding Before You Leave Who Answers a Remsen Village Alarm

An alarm in an empty house is only as good as the person who can stand on the porch twenty minutes after it goes off. When the owner is in Kingston, Port of Spain or a hospital bed, that person is a relative, a neighbor or a tenant downstairs, and nobody should find out they are the responder at two in the morning. So the first document we produce on a Remsen Village job is not a parts list. It is a one-page responder plan that the owner and every person on it have read.

The plan answers six questions. Who receives a burglary alert, and who receives a water or temperature alert, which may not be the same person? In what order does the central station call people, and what is the passcode each of them gives? Who holds a key, and does that person also have a code? Who is allowed to go inside, and who should only drive past and wait for the police? What should a responder do if they find a door forced, a lock changed or somebody inside? And when the owner is back, which codes are deleted?

Keys, codes and the people who hold them

The weakest point in many empty-house plans is the spare key. It ends up with a neighbor, then the neighbor's daughter, then a contractor who needed to get in once. An alarm makes that history visible. Every person who needs to enter gets their own code, and the panel records each use by name. If the owner sees "Carpenter" disarm the system at 9:40 p.m. on a Sunday, that is a question to ask the next morning. If a door opens and no code is entered at all, it is a burglary alert, whoever turns out to be holding the key.

Codes can also be limited in time on most current panels: a code that works only on weekdays during working hours, or only until a set date. For an estate house that several heirs need to visit, each heir gets their own code rather than a shared one, so nobody has to wonder who was in the house last. The executor, or whoever is managing the estate, holds the master code.

Coming home after weeks away

The end of an absence deserves the same care as the start. On the way from the airport, open the app and read the history for the whole trip, not just the last day: every visit should match a name on the responder plan, every temperature reading should look normal, and there should be no door openings at hours nobody was expected. If anything does not fit, ask your responder to meet you at the house rather than walking in alone. Once inside, delete the temporary codes, put the call list back in its everyday order and switch the system back to the settings you use when the family is home. Then check the mail for anything from the city register, since the recorded-document notice may have arrived by post.

The call to the police

A monitoring center can ask police to respond to an alarm, but it cannot promise how fast they will come or what they will do on arrival, and an empty house adds a wrinkle: there is nobody to meet them. Monitoring notes can tell the dispatcher that the house is unoccupied, which entrance is normally used and that a family member is on the way. The responder who arrives should stay outside, call 911 if anything looks wrong and wait for officers rather than going in alone. That is the instruction we write on every Remsen Village responder plan, and it is the one we most want people to remember.

Where the house is shared with a tenant, the plan also says plainly what the tenant is and is not responsible for. A tenant may be happy to receive a water alert for the cellar and check it; a tenant should never be expected to confront an intruder in the owner's empty apartment upstairs. Two areas on one panel make that separation real, because the tenant's code works only on the tenant's area.

What a Remsen Village shop alarm needs

A long-closure setting as well as a nightly one. A shop that closes every night at nine and a shop that closes for two weeks in August need different things from the same system. For the long closure, the interior motion sensors stay armed around the clock, the not-armed reminder is switched off so it does not cry wolf, and the owner's relative or a neighboring business is added to the call list. When the shop reopens, the owner switches back with one setting change in the app.

Glass, gate and back door. Glass-break detectors behind the display windows, a contact on the roll-down gate where the housing allows one, and contacts on the back door and any roof hatch. Motion in the stockroom points away from the compressor of a cooler or the warm air from a bakery oven, both of which can make a sensor misbehave.

Staff codes that end. Each worker gets a personal code, removed on the day they leave. A relative who covers the shop while the family is away gets a code that works only for those weeks.

Apartments above stay separate. When there is a tenant upstairs, the shop's siren and codes belong to the shop alone. The tenant should not be woken by the store's alarm or be expected to check on it.

Rules a Remsen Village business alarm has to respect

Sirens must stop on their own. Section 24-238 of the city's Administrative Code requires a building's audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes. In a shop with tenants upstairs, we set it much shorter.

Installers must be licensed. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires anyone installing alarms for paying customers to hold a Department of State license, which involves training, an exam and fingerprinting. Our number, #12000287431, is on every quote; ask any installer for theirs.

A burglar alarm is not a fire alarm system. Mixed-use buildings may need a code-compliant fire alarm system, designed and filed separately. See fire alarm installation in Remsen Village.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Remsen Village: Questions Answered

While the family is away

A neighbor will come in once a week to run the taps and flush the toilets while we are abroad. How do we set that up so her visits never send the police?

Give her a code of her own, limited to the day and hours she plans to come, and put the door she uses on a comfortable entry delay. When she opens that door, the keypad counts down and she enters her code; the history then shows her name and the time, and you can get a push alert each time she disarms, so you know the visit happened. Walk her through it once while you are still home, including how to re-arm on the way out, and give her the monitoring passcode in case she ever trips it by mistake. If she would rather not handle codes at all, a key fob does the same job with one button.

Can I tell from overseas whether the person who just opened the front door is the cousin I asked to check on the house?

Yes, if the cousin has a code of their own. The app shows the door opening and, a few seconds later, which user disarmed the system. If no code is entered before the entry delay ends, the alarm goes off and you know it was not your cousin. A video doorbell or a small indoor camera near the entrance adds a picture to that alert, which is useful when you are deciding at 3 a.m. your time whether to phone the police or go back to sleep.

If the motion sensors stay armed for six straight weeks, what usually causes a false alarm in an empty house?

In order of how often we see them: warm air from a radiator or a vent that starts when the heat comes on, a balloon or a curtain moved by that air, sunlight sweeping across a floor through an uncovered window, mice in the cellar, and a picture or shelf that finally falls. The fixes are mostly placement. We aim motion sensors along a hallway rather than at a window, keep them away from heat sources, use pet-tolerant models that ignore small animals, and remove anything that floats before you leave. If one sensor keeps tripping while you are away, you can bypass it from the app until someone checks it.

Can the heating failing in January reach my phone before the pipes freeze?

Yes. A low-temperature sensor reports to the panel and the app when the room it sits in drops below a set point, typically chosen well above freezing so there is time to act. Put one near the coldest pipes, usually on an outside wall of the cellar or a kitchen at the back of the house, and send that alert to the person with a key who lives closest, not only to you. Ring's own Flood and Freeze Sensor, for comparison, detects water and low temperatures and sends phone and email notifications, but it needs a Ring Alarm base station and a Ring subscription.

When someone tries to move in

Squatters reportedly start by changing the locks. If someone swaps the lock on my empty house, will the alarm know?

Changing a lock almost always means opening the door, and a door contact reports the opening the moment it happens, whether the person is carrying a crowbar or a new cylinder. If the system is armed, that is a burglary alarm; if it was left disarmed, it is still an event in the history and, if you have set it up, a push alert. Either way you have the time of the first entry, which is far more useful than finding a new lock three weeks later. A smart lock paired with the system can go further and report when it is unlocked, by whom, or that it has stopped responding.

Can the alarm notice that someone is living in the house, not just that a door opened once?

It can notice the signs. A person living in a house moves through rooms at night, switches lights on, runs water and turns up the heat. Motion sensors in the bedrooms or the upstairs hall will report that movement even if the person entered through an opening the alarm does not cover. Water sensors near a shower or a toilet, a smart plug on a lamp and a connected thermostat each add another clue. Some platforms also learn the house's normal pattern: Alarm.com's Unexpected Activity alerts flag activity that does not fit the usual routine once 30 days of history have been collected. None of these is proof on its own, but together they are hard to explain away.

If I come back and find a stranger living in my house, can the alarm's history help show when they got in?

It can help. The event history records every door opening, every code used and every alarm with a date and time, and most platforms let you export or screenshot it. Under the 2024 change to state law, someone who enters without permission and stays is defined as a squatter, not a tenant, so the date of first entry matters. Save the history as soon as you find the problem, before anything is reset, and give a copy to the police and to your lawyer. We are not lawyers and cannot advise on removing anyone; what we can do is make sure the record exists.

Paperwork around an empty house

Can an alarm stop someone from stealing our deed?

No. Deed theft happens at the city register, not at the front door, and the forger may never visit the house. What you can do, for free, is register the property for the Department of Finance's Notice of Recorded Document program, which sends an email or a letter whenever a deed, mortgage, power of attorney or certain other documents are recorded against it, usually the day after recording. If a notice arrives that you did not expect, contact the Attorney General's deed-theft unit or the Brooklyn District Attorney at once. The alarm protects the building; the notice protects the title.

Our homeowners policy mentions vacancy. Does a monitored alarm keep the house covered after 60 days empty?

Not by itself. Vacancy clauses depend on whether anyone is living in the house, not on whether it is watched, and Policygenius's guide says a standard policy will not cover vandalism once a home has been vacant for more than 60 days. Ask your agent before you go whether your policy has such a clause, and whether a vacancy endorsement is available. A monitored alarm, temperature and water sensors and an installer's certificate may help when you apply for one, and we provide the certificate on request, but only the insurer can tell you what it covers.

We keep getting letters and calls offering cash for the house. Should anything about the alarm change because of that?

The offers themselves are not illegal, and most are just marketing. But they are a reminder that people outside the family are paying attention to who owns which house and whether it looks occupied. Three things are worth doing: do not tell callers when you will be away or who holds keys, keep the list of people with codes short and named, and register for the city's recorded-document notice so you would hear about a filing quickly. If a caller ever claims to be from the alarm company and asks for a code or a passcode, hang up and phone us on the number printed on your quote.

Remsen Village houses and shops

The downstairs apartment in our two-family is empty between tenants. Can it stay armed day and night while we live upstairs?

Yes, if the panel is split into two areas. The empty apartment is its own area, armed around the clock with its doors, windows and a motion sensor, while your area upstairs is armed and disarmed as normal. When a broker or a prospective tenant needs to see the unit, you disarm only that area from your phone or give the broker a code that works for one afternoon. Once a new tenant moves in, they get their own code for that area and the round-the-clock setting is switched off.

The outlet that powers our alarm panel is on the tenant's electric meter. What happens if the tenant moves out and the power is shut off?

The panel runs on its backup battery for a number of hours, depending on the model and how many wireless devices it supports, and then the system goes quiet. Before that it should send a power-loss alert, so you would know, but an empty house could still be unprotected for days. The fix is simple: move the panel's transformer to an outlet on the owner's meter, ideally on a circuit with nothing else that trips. We check which meter feeds the outlet during the survey, because in older two-families the wiring is not always where the owner expects.

Our shop on Church Avenue closes for two weeks every summer while the family travels. Should the alarm be set up differently for a long closure than for an ordinary night?

Yes. For an ordinary night the system expects to be disarmed the next morning, and many owners have a reminder if it is not armed by closing time. For a two-week closure that reminder becomes noise, the interior motion should stay armed throughout, and the first person called should be someone in Brooklyn, not the owner in another country. We save the long-closure settings once, so switching to them on the last day and back on reopening day is a single change in the app.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Remsen Village

Google's AI Overview could not be opened from our research environment, so nothing below is quoted from it. These are claims we hear from Remsen Village owners who have read a summary online or a listing on Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr, each followed by what we find on surveys here.

"An alarm protects your property."

It protects the building and what is inside it. It does nothing for the property's title, which is where some of the most damaging East Flatbush cases have happened: the forged East 49th Street deed the District Attorney prosecuted, and the East 95th Street house the Attorney General says was stolen in 2023.

For that risk, the city's free Notice of Recorded Document program is the right tool. It costs nothing and takes a few minutes per property.

An honest alarm installer should tell you this, even though it is not something we sell.

"Smart alarms learn your routine automatically."

Some do, but not instantly. Alarm.com's Unexpected Activity alerts, as one dealer explains, need 30 days of activity before they start, and resetting them restarts that period.

An owner who installs a week before a long trip will leave with those features still learning. Worse, if the house is empty during the learning period, the system learns that "normal" is silence.

Install early, use the house normally while the system learns, and only then rely on the clever alerts.

"A camera is all you need when you are away."

A camera tells you what happened if you look. An owner on a beach or in a hospital bed often will not, and motion alerts from an outdoor camera on a busy block quickly become noise.

A contact on a door is different: it reports one specific event, an opening, and it can start a monitoring call while someone is still inside.

For an empty house we treat cameras as the picture that goes with an alarm alert, not as the alarm.

"Vacation mode handles everything."

Many apps have a scene or mode for being away, but it usually just arms the system and maybe turns on a lamp schedule. It does not choose who gets the call, tell the monitoring center the house is empty or put temperature sensors near the pipes.

Those decisions are made by people, before the trip. In our experience the responder plan matters more than any mode in the app.

A good vacation setting is the last step of planning for an absence, not the first.

"Squatters can't be removed, so an alarm is pointless."

The law changed in 2024: the State Senate's release on the budget explains that real property law now excludes squatters from the definition of a tenant. How a particular case is handled is a matter for police, courts and lawyers.

What an alarm adds is time and evidence. An entry reported in the first minute is a burglary in progress; an entry discovered a month later is a dispute.

The time-stamped history is the record owners in those disputes most often wish they had.

"Alarm installation costs about $1,500 wherever you live."

National averages blur together apartments, mansions and everything in between. What moves a Remsen Village price is the number of openings, whether a two-family is split into two areas, whether rear garages are covered, and how many temperature and water sensors the house needs.

On our Brooklyn list, which applies here with no surcharge, basic wireless systems start at $600 and typically run $600 to $1,200, complete-home systems start at $2,500, and a panel replacement on existing wiring runs $800 to $1,500.

Every device is a line on the written quote, so you can see what the empty-house extras add.

"Any handyman can install an alarm for you."

An owner can install a kit in their own house. A business that installs alarms for paying customers in New York needs a Department of State license under General Business Law Article 6-D, which requires training, an exam and fingerprinted employees.

For an empty house this matters more than usual, because the installer ends up knowing that the house is empty, when, and who holds keys.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Will Stand on the Porch

This describes how we run a survey for a house that will stand empty; it is our method, not the story of one job. The technician starts at the kitchen table, not at the front door. He asks when the house is empty, for how long, and who in Brooklyn could reach it within half an hour at night. If that person is not in the room, he asks the owner to phone them and put them on speaker, because a responder who learns about the job afterwards is a responder who may not answer.

Then he walks the house from the back. Cellar door, cellar windows, rear door, garage, driveway windows, and only after that the front. He opens the boiler room and looks at the water heater, the main shutoff and the coldest pipes, and he asks which meter powers the outlet he plans to use for the panel. Upstairs he looks for radiators, vents and sunny floors before choosing where any motion sensor goes.

The last page of the survey sheet is the responder plan, filled in before the price. In our experience that page, more than the number of sensors, decides whether an owner who is four thousand miles away sleeps well.

Remsen Village Alarm Installation FAQ

What does an alarm for a Remsen Village house that sits empty part of the year cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

A one-family or small two-family house usually starts from the basic wireless figure of $600, which typically runs $600 to $1,200, and the temperature and water sensors an empty house needs are added per device. A detached two-family with a cellar and rear garages, set up as two areas, more often lands at or above the complete-home figure that starts at $2,500. Monitoring is optional from $19.99 a month, and sales tax is added on the written quote.

Should an alarm company sign go in the front yard of a house that is often empty, or does it tell people nobody is home?
+

A sign or window decal tells people the house is protected, not that it is empty; mail, uncut grass and a dark porch tell them that. We think a small sign or decal by the doors helps more than it hurts. What we advise against is any sign, sticker or note that names the alarm brand, the monitoring company or a phone number, because that information makes a phishing call or a fake service visit easier.

Can the alarm close the water main by itself if a pipe bursts while we are away?
+

It can, if a motorized valve actuator is fitted to the main shutoff and paired with the panel, so a wet water sensor both sends an alert and turns the valve. Whether that is practical depends on the age and type of the shutoff valve in your cellar; a seized old valve has to be replaced by a plumber first. We look at the valve during the walkthrough and quote the actuator only when the plumbing allows it.

If a fire starts in the house while nobody is living there, who calls the fire department?
+

With monitored smoke or heat detectors on the panel, the central station does. Fire signals are typically treated as emergencies and passed to the fire department without the long verification call used for burglary alarms, and the call list is then notified. Ask your monitoring provider to confirm its fire procedure in writing. Without monitoring, the only alert is a push notification to your phone, which may arrive while you are asleep in another time zone, so we recommend central-station monitoring for any house that is empty for long stretches.

Does the person who meets the police at the house need a key, the alarm code, or both?
+

Both, if you want them to be able to go inside, check the rooms and re-arm afterwards. Give that person their own code rather than yours, so the history shows their visit by name and the code can be deleted later without changing anything else. If you would rather they never go in, give them neither, tell the monitoring center they are a contact only, and make sure someone else can reach the house with a key the same day.

Can the alarm tell me if my mother, who lives alone, has not opened any door all day?
+

Some platforms can. Resideo's Total Connect 2.0, for example, has a No Activity alert that sends a text or email when nothing has happened on the system for a chosen period of 1, 4, 8, 12 or 24 hours, but only while the system is disarmed. That suits a parent who is at home and moving around normally. It is a wellness reminder, not a medical alarm, so it should sit alongside a phone check, not replace one.

Should we live with the new alarm for a while before leaving the house empty for weeks?
+

Yes. A week or two of normal use shows which sensor is noisy, which entry delay is too short and who on the call list does not answer. Some smart features also need time: Alarm.com's Unexpected Activity alerts, for instance, only start after the system has collected 30 days of activity. Book the installation far enough ahead that the system has been tested by your own family before it is trusted on its own.

A relative overseas is paying for the system. Can they pay for it and get the alerts without being on the deed?
+

Yes. Anyone can pay for the installation and the monitoring, and the account holder can add them to the app so they receive alerts. What we need before installing is the agreement of the person entitled to the house, usually the owner on the deed or someone acting for them, because the alarm records who comes and goes in their home. Once that is in writing, the paying relative and the owner decide together who controls codes and who is on the call list.

The owner on the deed has dementia and can no longer manage the alarm. Who should control it?
+

Whoever already has legal authority over the owner's affairs, usually an agent under a power of attorney or a court-appointed guardian, should become the account holder and keep the master code. The owner can still have a simple fob if that helps them feel at home. Keep the list of people with codes short and named, and register the house for the city's recorded-document notice, because cases like the one charged on East 95th Street involved an owner with dementia. We are not lawyers; if nobody has that authority yet, speak to one first.

Will one of your technicians come back to check the system while we are away?
+

Only if the system tells us something is wrong. A panel reports its own faults, such as a low battery, a sensor that stopped reporting or a lost power supply, to the app and, with monitoring, to the central station. If that happens, a service visit is booked at our standard rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and your responder lets the technician in. We would rather the panel's own reports decide when a visit is needed than charge for visits on a calendar.
