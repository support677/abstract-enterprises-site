The Neighborhood

Why Madison Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Madison grew up around the streetcar. CityNeighborhoods.nyc dates its real development to the years after 1900, when lines along Kings Highway, Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue made it practical to live here and work downtown, and most of the housing went up between 1910 and 1930. What was built then shapes every cabling job now. The interior blocks hold detached brick and stucco houses with driveways, lawns and small gardens; around them are two-family brick houses, limestone rows and modest single-family homes; and along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue sit low apartment houses and larger six-story buildings, many of them co-ops today. Not one of those buildings was designed with a path for a data cable, and the internet still arrives where the old telephone line did: through the front of the basement, at the point nearest the street.

That is the root of most calls we get from Madison. A detached house is wider than a row house and has rooms on all four sides, so a single router on the first floor or on a basement shelf is trying to cover a footprint it was never sized for. The far corner bedroom upstairs, the enclosed back porch that became a playroom, the attic that became an office and the basement that became a den all sit at the edge of its range. Add a garage at the end of the driveway, where many owners keep a workshop, a second refrigerator, a car charger or a camera recorder, and the Wi-Fi has to cross the yard as well. The usual response is to buy another mesh unit, then another, and each one relays a weaker copy of what the last one heard.

The two-family houses add a second problem. A brick two-family with an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other has one basement, often one service entry for each provider, and two households who each need a network that is theirs alone. Grown children set up a parent in the downstairs apartment and want to check on them; landlords want a simple way to supply internet without walking into a tenant's rooms; tenants want their own router where they can reach it. None of that works well when the only cable in the building is the provider's coax stapled along a basement beam. A little planning in the basement solves it: separate labeled runs to each apartment, equipment for each household kept apart, and a clear point where the provider's responsibility ends and the owner's begins.

The apartment buildings on the two main avenues have their own version of the problem. CityRealty lists 2425 Kings Highway as a six-story co-op of 137 apartments built in 1932 with no doorman, 2301 Kings Highway as a six-story, 96-apartment co-op from 1936 that carries addresses on Kings Highway, East 23rd Street and East 24th Street, and 2282 Ocean Avenue as a 36-apartment elevator co-op of 1938 between Avenue R and Avenue S. Openigloo counts 72 units at 2232 Ocean Avenue, built in 1927, and 72 more at 2306 Ocean Avenue, built in 1935. These are masonry buildings with a cellar, a superintendent, a board or a managing agent, and residents who all compete for the same crowded Wi-Fi channels. For a shareholder, the job is usually a few jacks inside one apartment under the board's alteration rules. For the building, it is the network that its cameras, entry panels, laundry machines and garage equipment depend on.

Newer buildings depend on the network even more. StreetEasy lists 2442 Ocean Avenue Residences, filed under the Homecrest and Sheepshead Bay side of Ocean Avenue, as a seven-story building from 2019 with 105 units, a package room, a gym, a roof deck and a "Virtual" doorman. A virtual doorman is a door, a camera, an intercom and a remote attendant joined by a network connection; when that connection is weak or badly routed, the front door feels it first. Owners and boards in buildings like this are no longer buying cabling for convenience. They are buying it so that the building's entry, packages and security keep working on a busy evening when every apartment is streaming.

Finally, Madison is a neighborhood of families who work from home and children who do schoolwork online, from the blocks around James Madison High School on Bedford Avenue to the houses near the Nostrand Avenue shops. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes tree-lined blocks that are home to Jewish, Russian, Caribbean and South Asian families, and many of those households are multi-generational, with three or four people on video calls at once and relatives abroad on the other end. Those calls are what fail first when a house relies on a single router. A wired access point on each floor, and a cable to each desk that matters, is what turns a fast plan into a connection the whole house can count on.

Structured Cabling for Madison's Architecture

Almost every Madison request comes from one of six kinds of property. What a crew needs to know about each is practical: where the provider's line enters, what is directly above the basement, whether the side walls are the owner's alone or shared, how far the farthest room or building is from the panel, and who besides the owner has to agree. The cards give the short version, and the sections after them describe how we approach each one.

The detached brick or stucco house

One- and two-story houses standing on their own lots on the interior blocks, with a full basement, a driveway to one side and often a detached garage at the back.

The two-family brick house

Brick houses of the 1910s and 1920s with one apartment per floor, one basement, and two households that each want a network of their own.

The limestone row

Attached limestone-fronted houses built in groups, with shared side walls, a basement under each house and stairs along one wall.

The house with an added unit

Houses with a finished basement apartment or an attic suite for grown children, parents or tenants, added long after the house was built.

The Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue co-op

Six-story masonry elevator buildings from the late 1920s to the 1950s, such as 2425 Kings Highway and 2282 Ocean Avenue, run by a board and a managing agent.

The newer elevator building

Concrete buildings from 2005 onward on Ocean Avenue, such as 2523 and 2442 Ocean Avenue, with a telecom room, a building provider and systems that rely on the network.

The detached brick or stucco house: four exposures and one basement

The detached house is the building Madison is known for, and it is also the one where Wi-Fi alone struggles most. A row house is long and narrow, and a router in the middle reaches both ends without much trouble; a detached house is closer to square, with bedrooms at all four corners and often an enclosed porch or a rear addition hanging off the back. The router the provider left beside the basement entry is under the front of the house, a floor and a half away from the rear bedrooms. Moving it is the first improvement, and cable is how you move it: one run from the provider's terminal to a central spot in the basement, and from that spot a run up to each floor.

The good news in a detached house is that the walls are yours. None of them is a party wall shared with a neighbor, so a route can go up an interior partition, a closet, a plumbing chase or, where the inside offers nothing, along the outside of the building in a protected raceway to an entry point on a side wall. In houses of this age the inside walls are commonly plaster on wood lath over wood studs, and a cavity that is clear can be fished from the basement to a jack on the first floor without opening anything but the hole for the plate. The second floor is reached either by continuing up the same cavity where it lines up, or through a closet that sits over another closet, or from above, by working down from an attic that has an accessible floor.

Stucco and brick exteriors call for care whenever a cable has to pass through them, whether for the garage run, a yard access point or a provider's new entry. We pick the point where the opening will be hidden and protected, often under an eave, beside a downspout or behind a porch column, drill once at a slight downward angle toward the outside so water runs away from the house, sleeve the hole and seal it. Outside, the cable sits in a raceway or conduit painted to match, with a drip loop where it turns into the wall. Inside, it lands at a surge protector before it touches any equipment. A detached house that sits on its own lot is more exposed to nearby lightning strikes than one tucked into a row, and copper that comes in from outdoors is the easiest way for a surge to reach a router.

The two-family brick house: a shared basement and a clean boundary

The two-family brick houses CityNeighborhoods.nyc dates to the 1910s and 1920s usually put one apartment on each floor over a shared basement, and that basement is where the cabling plan is won or lost. Both households' service normally enters there. The owner controls the space, the tenant needs reliable access to nothing more than a working jack upstairs, and both expect their devices to stay private. We set up the basement as a small meeting point: a wall panel where every cable in the house begins, a labeled run up to each apartment, and enough room for two providers' equipment side by side if the households keep separate accounts.

The vertical route to the upper apartment is the hard part, because the obvious paths, the stair hall and the closets, often belong to the lower unit or to a common hallway. In many of these houses the best route is a closet stack shared by both floors, a pipe chase beside the kitchen or bathroom, or the wall of the common stair, entered from the basement below. Where none of those works, a small, neat exterior raceway on a side or rear wall, between the basement and a window frame or a sleeve into the second-floor apartment, can be the cleanest answer and the least disruptive for a tenant who is living there during the work. We show the owner each option before we write the quote and note which rooms of which apartment will be entered and when.

The limestone row: shared walls and a front-to-back route

Madison's limestone rows behave more like the rows elsewhere in the borough. The side walls are shared masonry and are off-limits for burying cable, so the way up is through the interior: the stair wall, closets that line up, a chimney breast that a mason has confirmed is out of service, or a chase beside the plumbing. The service usually enters at the front of the basement, and the rooms that most need a connection tend to be at the back of the top floor, so the plan pairs one vertical route with short horizontal runs on each floor, behind baseboards, along door casings or inside closets.

Because the floors span from one party wall to the other, the joists run side to side, and a cable heading from the front of the house to the back crosses every one of them. Where the ceiling underneath is finished, the horizontal leg has to happen inside a wall, in a closet or along the surface. We describe each option on the walk-through, mark the openings on a floor sketch and write the chosen route into the quote, so you know before the work starts which rooms will see a hole and which will see nothing at all.

The house with an added unit: the basement apartment and the attic suite

Madison families often make room for one more household inside the house: a finished basement apartment for a parent, an attic suite for a grown child, a separate entrance at the side for a tenant. Those spaces were finished long after the house was built, and their walls and ceilings usually hide whatever wiring was there before. The basement apartment is often under a drop ceiling, which is good news for cabling, because the tiles lift and the route runs above them. The attic suite is usually reached through a knee wall or a small storage space under the roof, where a run can travel along the eaves to the room before dropping into a wall plate.

The bigger decision is whether the added unit shares the main household's internet or has its own. Sharing is simpler and cheaper, but it should still be a separate network, so the family upstairs and the person downstairs do not see each other's devices. A separate account means the provider brings a second line or a second terminal, and the unit needs its own cable from the basement panel. Grown children who look after a parent often want one more thing: a reliable connection for a camera, a video call tablet or a medical alert hub in the parent's apartment that they can check from wherever they live. That device deserves a wired jack or an access point within easy reach, not the last bar of the main house's Wi-Fi.

The Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue co-op: the board decides the route

The six-story co-ops along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue are masonry buildings with interior walls that are often plaster on block rather than hollow stud walls. Inside an apartment, that means a cable cannot simply be fished down a wall to a jack. We use closets, door casings, baseboards and, where the board permits, a small channel in a partition that is plastered over afterward. Most apartments have one cable outlet where the provider first installed service, usually in the living room or the foyer, and the bedroom at the end of the hall is the one that suffers.

Before any of that, the board's alteration agreement and the managing agent's rules govern the work: what may be opened, what insurance the contractor carries and who it names, which hours are allowed, whether the superintendent must be present, and whether a description of the work has to be submitted first. For a few jacks inside one apartment the paperwork is light, usually a scope letter and a certificate of insurance. Anything that leaves the apartment, a run into the public hall, a sleeve through the floor or a connection to a riser, needs the building's written approval, and we will not start that part without it. At 2301 Kings Highway, where CityRealty lists alternate addresses on East 23rd and East 24th Streets, the building's own network may also have to span more than one entrance, which is a job for the board, not one shareholder.

The newer elevator building: the telecom room and the systems that depend on it

Buildings like 2523 Ocean Avenue, which Openigloo lists as a 15-unit, six-story building from 2005, and 2442 Ocean Avenue Residences, the 105-unit, seven-story building of 2019 that StreetEasy describes with a virtual doorman, concierge and package room, arrive with their cabling largely designed in. Concrete floors, a telecom room, a riser and a jack or two in each unit are standard. A renter in one of these buildings rarely needs or is allowed new cable inside the concrete; what helps is a run from the unit's existing outlet to a desk or TV along a removable raceway, or an access point placed in the middle of the apartment instead of the router in the hall closet.

For the owner or managing agent, the more important cabling is the building's own. A virtual doorman, a package room with camera coverage, a garage gate and a gym with screens all depend on the building network, and every one of them fails in a way residents notice the moment a switch in the telecom room locks up or a single cable to the lobby is damaged. We document what is there, test the links to each system, label the panel, and where needed add battery backup, a second path to the lobby or a separate network for building systems so a problem on residents' service does not take the front door down with it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Survey a Madison Detached House With a Driveway and a Rear Garage

A survey of a detached Madison house follows the same sequence each time, because doing it in this order stops a crew from promising something the building will not allow. We start where the service enters, usually the front or driveway side of the basement, and note every box on that wall: the fiber terminal, any coax splitter, an old telephone block, a battery backup unit, and which of the cables leaving them still carry anything. Then we look up. Open joists mean the basement ceiling becomes the network's main corridor. A drywall ceiling means we look for an access panel, a soffit around a beam or a duct, or a route along the top of the foundation wall.

Next we find the house's way up. In a detached house we check the closets on the first floor for one that lines up with a closet above, the wall behind the kitchen sink where the plumbing rises, the side of the chimney if there is one, and the attic hatch, because sometimes it is easier to come down from above than to go up from below. We test a candidate wall with a small inspection hole inside a closet or a look from the basement at the bottom plate, so we know whether the cavity is clear, fire-blocked or packed with insulation before we price it.

Then we walk outside. We measure the path from the house to the garage, note what lies between them, whether the driveway is poured concrete, pavers or asphalt, where the fence runs, where a gutter or downspout could hide a raceway, and whether the garage has power and a place to mount a small switch or access point. We look at the provider's drop to see whether it comes from a pole across the street or underground, because that decides where the next provider will want to enter. Last, we test whatever wire is already in the walls, tone it to its far ends and certify what might be reused, so that by the end of the visit we can draw the route on a sketch of each floor and the yard and price it line by line.

Pathways

Across the Driveway and Into the Garage: Getting a Network From a Madison House to Its Yard and Outbuildings

The run that most separates Madison from the denser parts of Brooklyn is the one that leaves the house. Many of the detached houses here have a garage at the back of the lot, reached by a driveway along one side, and owners use those garages for far more than a car: a workshop, a home gym, storage with a second freezer, a camera recorder, an electric vehicle charger with its own app, or a small office. Wi-Fi from a router inside a brick house rarely makes it across the yard and through a garage wall with anything left. A cable does, if it is the right cable on the right route.

There are three ways to get there, and the survey decides which one a property allows. The first is overhead: a short span from the house to the garage on a messenger wire, at a height that clears the driveway and any vehicle that uses it, with an outdoor-rated cable lashed to it. It is quick and needs no digging, but it is visible, and on a house where the owner cares how the side yard looks it is often the last choice. The second is along a structure: a fence, a garden wall or the side of an attached carport, in conduit fixed to the structure, which works well when the fence or wall runs most of the way between the two buildings. The third is underground, in conduit at a proper depth along the edge of the driveway or across a lawn, which is the neatest and longest-lasting and also the most work, especially where poured concrete or pavers have to be crossed.

Whichever path is used, the cable itself has to suit the job. Copper network cable that runs outdoors or underground must be rated for wet locations, because ordinary indoor cable absorbs moisture in a buried conduit and degrades. Copper between two buildings also carries a risk that indoor runs do not, because the two buildings can sit at slightly different electrical potentials and a nearby lightning strike can push a surge along the cable. We fit surge protection at both ends of any outdoor copper link and bond it properly. Where the garage is far from the house, where the owner wants to remove that risk entirely, or where the link will carry a lot of camera traffic, we recommend fiber instead: it carries no electrical current, is immune to the surge problem and is not limited by the distance a copper run can cover.

Inside the garage, the cable ends at a small switch or a single access point, mounted where it stays dry and away from a car's exhaust and the heat of a water heater. If the garage has no outlet near the mounting point, the access point can draw its power over the same cable from the switch in the house, which is one reason we recommend a switch that supplies power over its ports. Cameras on the garage, the driveway light controller and an outdoor access point for the yard can all be fed from that same point. That is also where the network for the yard begins: an outdoor access point under the garage eave or the back of the house can cover a patio or a garden without a second router.

Openings sealed and runs protected

Every hole between floors and through an exterior wall is sealed as the cable goes through, with listed firestop where it passes from the basement into the living space or between two apartments, and with an exterior-grade sealant and a sleeve where it passes through brick, block or stucco. Outdoor conduit is supported at regular intervals and fitted with a weatherhead or drip loop where it turns into the wall, so rain runs off rather than in. Inside, runs in the basement are carried on J-hooks or other supports along the joists rather than stapled flat, so they stay clear of pipes, ducts and the boiler and do not end up crushed when the next plumber works overhead.

Network cable kept away from the house's other wiring

The same walls and basement ceilings carry electrical circuits, gas lines, heating pipes and, in many Madison houses, an older alarm, doorbell or intercom wire. We keep network cable away from power wiring where they run side by side, cross it at a right angle where they must meet, and never share a hole or a box with a new circuit an electrician is installing. Data cable stays clear of boiler flues and hot pipes. Where an alarm panel or a camera system already uses a closet or a route, we work around it and label our cables clearly, so whoever opens that closet next can tell which wires belong to which system.

Service Entry

Fios, Optimum or Spectrum: Where Internet Service Enters a Madison Building and Why the Network Should Start Somewhere Else

Every cabling plan starts with one fact: where your provider's line comes in. In a Madison house it almost always enters the basement on the street side, either underground through the foundation or down the facade from an aerial drop off a pole. A Verizon Fios line ends at a fiber terminal on the basement wall; cable service from Optimum or Spectrum ends at a coax splitter and a modem. BroadbandNow's figures for the whole borough, valid as of November 12, 2026, list Verizon Fios at 65.6 percent availability, Optimum at 49.5 percent and Spectrum at 37.7 percent, so many Madison blocks can choose between at least two wired providers, and plenty of houses have equipment from more than one of them on the basement wall.

The provider's technician puts the terminal where it is fastest to install and plugs a router in next to it. That is why so many Madison households have their router on a basement shelf at the front of the house or in a front room on the first floor, about as far as possible from the back bedrooms upstairs. Structured cabling separates the two jobs. The provider's box stays where the line enters. A single cable carries service from there to a central, dry spot, often the middle of the basement under the best route up, and the network spreads from that spot to every floor, the garage and any second apartment. When you change providers, only that first link changes. Nothing inside the walls moves.

Apartment buildings are different, because the owner decides how providers get in. Getting fiber into older buildings across the city has long depended on building access agreements. When Technical.ly Brooklyn reported in June 2015 that some Brooklyn buildings were still without Fios after the city's 2008 agreement with Verizon, a Verizon spokesperson put it plainly: "It is getting access into buildings that is the challenge." A city audit at the time said Verizon had refused orders from some residents, and Verizon disputed the audit. Whatever the history in a particular building, the practical point for a Madison co-op or rental is the same: a provider needs a path from the street to the cellar and from the cellar to each floor, and a building with an empty conduit or a clear riser is a building providers can serve more easily.

HPD's guidance on broadband in affordable housing describes the same physical reality. Wired service, it says, runs from the street or the utility poles into the basement, and fiber needs street access near the building and an accessible entry point in the basement. It adds that new wiring is easiest to install when electrical systems are being installed or replaced, and that wired wall jacks give the strongest coverage inside a unit, at a higher cost than shared hotspots. For a Madison board planning a cellar renovation, an elevator modernization or new electrical service, that is the moment to put in empty conduit and pull strings from the street entry to the telecom space and up the building.

Who controls the wiring in a Madison apartment building

The same HPD guidance summarizes the federal rules that now apply to deals between buildings and providers: exclusive revenue-sharing agreements between a landlord and a provider are banned, as are graduated revenue-share deals and sale-and-leaseback arrangements for a building's inside wiring, and exclusive marketing arrangements have to be disclosed. It also notes that existing wiring has to be in good repair to be reused and that an owner who owns the building's infrastructure has more control. For a Madison co-op, that is an argument for knowing exactly what wiring the corporation owns, keeping it labeled and documented, and making sure any provider agreement says who maintains it.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, Boards and Leases: The Rules Behind a Madison Cabling Job

Low-voltage permits. Local Law 128 of 2024 added section 28-105.4.7 to the city's Administrative Code, which says "An electrical permit shall not be required for the installation, alteration, maintenance, or repair" of wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits, "provided that such work is performed by a licensed master electrician, special electrician, or qualified person." The exemption took effect with the 2025 New York City Electrical Code on December 21, 2025. Network cabling falls inside it. The same law keeps wiring connected to life safety systems for licensed electricians, and fire alarm wiring is not part of a network job.

What the exemption does not change. Cable still has to be rated for the space it runs through, whether that is a wall cavity, a riser between floors, a space that carries heating or cooling air, or an outdoor conduit. Openings between floors and between apartments still need firestop. Outlets and circuits for equipment are electrical work and belong to an electrician; we tell you at the survey if the equipment location needs a new outlet, and we do not install it ourselves.

Our license and insurance. We work under New York State license #12000287431 and carry insurance for low-voltage work. On request, a certificate of insurance goes to the owner, co-op board, managing agent or landlord before the crew arrives, naming whoever the building requires.

Co-op boards and managing agents. In the co-ops along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue, the alteration agreement and the house rules decide what may be opened, when work is permitted, whether the superintendent must be present and what insurance is required. Shareholders should send us the building's requirements with the request, and we will prepare a scope letter that describes each opening in plain words for the board or the managing agent to approve.

Rentals and two-family houses. In a rental apartment, the lease governs: a tenant needs the landlord's written permission before anything is drilled, and we will not drill a rental unit without it. An owner of a two-family house who wants cable run into the tenant's apartment needs to give the tenant proper notice and arrange access, and we schedule that part of the work around the tenant.

Neighbors and lot lines. Detached houses in Madison sit close to their neighbors along the driveway side, and attached rows share walls. We never run cable across a neighbor's property, fence, wall or roof without the neighbor's written consent, and we do not bury cable in a shared wall. If a provider's line can only reach your house over a neighbor's property, that is a conversation between you, the neighbor and the provider.

Landmarks. We found no historic district covering Madison. The Wyckoff-Bennett Homestead near Kings Highway is an individual landmark and a private home. If your building is individually designated, changes to its exterior, including a new cable on the facade, generally go to the Landmarks Preservation Commission; interior cabling generally does not. This is a plain-language planning summary, not legal advice; the board's documents, the lease and the relevant agency have the final word.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Madison: Questions Answered

These are the situations Madison homeowners, families, boards, landlords and businesses describe when they contact us: detached houses with garages and yards, two households under one roof, wiring older owners left behind, co-op and newer apartment buildings, and businesses on the avenues. Each question is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Detached houses, garages and yards

The garage at the end of our driveway has a workshop and a second freezer, and we would like cameras and Wi-Fi out there. Can the network reach it without tearing up the driveway?

Usually, yes. If a fence, a garden wall or the side of the house runs most of the way to the garage, a conduit fixed along it carries the cable without any digging. If nothing connects the two buildings, a short overhead span on a messenger wire, high enough to clear the driveway, is another option. Underground conduit along the edge of the driveway or across the lawn is the neatest, and we price it separately so you can compare. Whichever path you choose, the cable has to be rated for outdoor or wet locations, copper gets surge protection at both ends, and for a longer distance or a camera-heavy link we often recommend fiber, which carries no electrical current between the two buildings.

Our house is detached, with bedrooms at all four corners upstairs. Is one access point on the second floor enough?

Often one well-placed ceiling access point covers a typical Madison second floor, as long as it sits near the middle of the floor, usually in the hall ceiling, and not at one end. Plaster walls and an enclosed rear porch or addition can leave a corner weak, and in that case a second, lower-powered access point at the far end is better than turning the first one up. We decide this at the survey by looking at the layout and, where helpful, measuring the signal in each room. Each access point gets its own cable from the basement panel and its power over that same cable, so there is no outlet to add on the ceiling.

We would like Wi-Fi on the back patio and in the garden. Should the access point go outdoors?

For a patio right behind the house, an indoor access point near a rear window sometimes reaches far enough. For a garden, a side yard or the area around the garage, an outdoor-rated access point mounted under an eave, on the back wall of the house or on the garage does the job much better. It is fed by the same kind of cable as the indoor ones, through a sealed opening with a drip loop, and protected by a surge device where the cable comes back inside. We aim it at the space you use rather than at the street, so you are not broadcasting your network down the block.

Two households under one roof

My mother lives in the basement apartment of our house, and we want her to have her own internet and a tablet for video calls. What is the simplest setup?

The simplest arrangement is one internet line for the house with a separate network for her apartment, carried on its own cable from the basement panel to a jack or access point in her living room. She gets a strong signal where she sits, her devices stay apart from the rest of the family's, and you can still manage it from upstairs. If you prefer a separate provider account for her, the provider brings a second line or terminal to the basement wall, and her cable starts there instead. Either way, put her tablet, any medical alert hub and a camera you check from elsewhere on wired jacks or close to the access point, not on the edge of the main house's Wi-Fi.

We own a two-family brick house and our tenant upstairs keeps losing his connection. The router is in our apartment. What would fix it for both of us?

Give each apartment its own cable and its own equipment. The usual fix is a wall panel in the basement where the provider's line arrives, one run up to your apartment and one to the tenant's, each ending at that household's router or access point, and labeled so either of you can find it. If the tenant has his own provider account, his cable starts at his provider's terminal; if you supply internet, we set up a separate network for his apartment so neither household can see the other's devices. The tenant then has equipment he can restart himself without knocking on your door.

What earlier owners and installers left behind

Our 1920s house still has old telephone jacks in most rooms. Can those wires be turned into network connections?

Not as they are. The telephone wire in houses of this age is a different and much older kind of cable, often with only a few conductors and little twisting, and it cannot carry a modern network connection reliably. What it does offer is a path. Where a phone wire runs from the basement to a jack upstairs, we can sometimes tie a new network cable to it and pull the new cable through the same route, then replace the old phone plate with a network jack. Whether that works depends on whether the old wire is stapled along its length, which we find out by testing a gentle pull before committing to it.

An electrician who worked on our house years ago ran some network cable, but none of the jacks work. Is that unusual?

It is common. Brooklyn owners have reported the same thing on Brownstoner's forums for years; one network architect there warned that "many electricians are not particularly good at terminating and testing coax and Ethernet cabling," and another owner said his electrician pulls data cable but "will not handle the terminations." The cable itself is often fine. What fails is the connector or jack at one end, a wrong wiring order, or a run that was never tested. We tone each cable, check how it was terminated, certify it, and re-terminate what passes. A run that still fails after re-termination is usually damaged inside the wall and is replaced, often using the old cable as a pull string.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Madison

"A bigger router will cover a whole house"

Search answers often suggest replacing the provider's router with a more powerful one. In a small apartment, that can help. In a detached Madison house with bedrooms at four corners, an enclosed porch and a finished basement, the router is still in the wrong place, usually at the front of the basement or the first floor.

A stronger router can shout louder, but your phone and laptop cannot shout back any louder, so the connection in the far bedroom stays one-sided and unreliable. Plaster on lath and brick exterior walls take a share of the signal at every turn.

Moving the source closer works better than making it louder. A cable from the panel to a centered access point on each floor puts a strong signal where people sit and lets the router stay where the provider left it.

"Mesh Wi-Fi does the same job as cable"

Mesh is a real improvement over a single router, and in a one-floor apartment it may be all that is needed. But a mesh node that relays its signal wirelessly shares its airtime between your devices and the next node. TWiT's May 2026 discussion of the topic put it directly: mesh systems "are fundamentally limited by their reliance on wireless backhaul."

The same piece said wired access points are preferable "especially in multi-story homes or challenging layouts," and that they give better "roaming between floors or rooms." A Madison house with a basement, two floors and a garage is exactly that kind of layout.

Many mesh systems can use a cable between nodes instead of the air. If you already own one, wiring its nodes back to the panel, known as wired backhaul, is often the least expensive way to get most of the benefit of a structured network.

"You can daisy-chain one cable from room to room"

The question comes up regularly; an owner on Brownstoner's forum asked about exactly this when looking for someone to install Ethernet ports. Telephone wiring was often looped from jack to jack, so it seems natural to do the same with network cable.

A network cable does not work that way. Each jack needs its own cable back to a switch, because a single run carries one connection, and splicing or looping it through several plates breaks the link or slows it badly. Where several devices sit in one room, a small switch at that jack can share the one cable among them.

The correct design is a "home run" from each jack to the panel. It uses more cable but makes every room independent, so a fault in one room never takes another room down, and each connection can be tested and labeled on its own.

"Any electrician can wire your network"

Many electricians can pull cable, and some do excellent low-voltage work. But terminating, labeling and certifying a network is its own specialty, and Brooklyn homeowners have described the gap for years. On Brownstoner, one reply to an owner planning to wire a house for Ethernet warned that "many electricians are not particularly good at terminating and testing coax and Ethernet cabling."

Another Brownstoner reply, to an owner looking for someone to install Ethernet ports and advise on the design, put it this way: "If you are tying this into other systems, a licensed low voltage person is the way to go." That matches what we see in Madison houses where jacks were installed but never tested.

The practical test is simple. Ask whoever you hire whether they will certify every run and hand you the results and a port map. If the answer is no, the job is not finished when they leave.

"Low-voltage wiring in New York City needs an electrical permit"

This was a common answer before the 2025 electrical code took effect and is still repeated. Since December 21, 2025, Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, says an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person.

The opposite claim, that cabling is entirely unregulated, is also wrong. The law keeps life-safety wiring for licensed electricians, cable still has to be rated for where it runs, and openings between floors still need firestop.

In a Madison co-op, the paperwork that actually matters is usually the board's alteration agreement and the certificate of insurance, not a city permit. In a rental, it is the landlord's written permission.

"Your internet provider will wire every room"

A provider's installation brings service to one point, usually at the basement wall nearest the street, and connects a terminal or modem there. Some providers will add an outlet or two. None of them plans a route through a detached house to every floor, runs a link to a garage or separates two households in a two-family.

In apartment buildings, the provider works under whatever arrangement it has with the owner, and getting into a building is often the provider's biggest hurdle. A Verizon spokesperson told Technical.ly Brooklyn in 2015, "It is getting access into buildings that is the challenge."

The division of labor is straightforward: the provider brings service to the building, a cabling contractor builds the network inside, and the two meet at one documented, labeled point in the basement or the telecom room.

"A national price per drop tells you what a Brooklyn job costs"

Cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages that blend every kind of house in their samples. HomeAdvisor's own Brooklyn page groups network wiring with general router and network services, which is a very different job from running cable through a two-family house.

UniFi Nerds, writing about New York City, lists the drivers that actually move a local job: building access rules, construction type, limited pathways and coordination with other trades. A Madison co-op with a board, a detached house with a garage run and a limestone row with shared walls each hit those differently.

The dependable way to price a Madison job is to walk the route. That is why we quote per job, in writing, and list every jack, opening and run so the number can be compared fairly with any other bid.

FAQ

Madison Structured Cabling FAQ

What kind of contractor should a Madison homeowner call to plan and price network wiring?
+

A licensed low-voltage cabling contractor who will survey the route, write an itemized quote, terminate and label each cable and certify every run. An electrician is still the right call for any new outlet or circuit the equipment needs, but the network itself is specialist work.

Can the phone and cable company handle the coax while a separate installer does the Ethernet?
+

Yes. The provider is responsible for bringing its line to the house and installing its terminal or modem. The cabling contractor builds everything from that point inward. The two connect at one labeled point in the basement, and it helps to have the inside cabling planned before the provider's visit so the terminal goes where the plan expects it.

Does a network cable have to run all the way back to the basement from every room?
+

Yes, each jack and access point gets its own cable back to the panel. Network cable cannot be looped from one jack to the next the way old telephone wire often was. Where several devices sit in one room, a small switch at that jack shares the single cable among them.

Where should the router live in a detached Madison house?
+

Usually not where the provider left it. The provider's terminal stays at the basement wall where the line enters, and one cable carries service to a central, dry spot in the basement or a utility closet. From there, a cable goes to an access point on each floor, so the router's location matters far less than before.

Should a link to a detached garage be copper or fiber?
+

For a short run, outdoor-rated copper with surge protection at both ends works well and can power an access point or camera in the garage. For a longer run, a link carrying several cameras, or an owner who wants no electrical path between the buildings, fiber is the better choice.

Will you patch the holes in our plaster walls?
+

The quote says exactly how each opening will be finished. Small openings behind plates disappear under the plate. Larger access openings in plaster are either patched by us or left clean and squared for your plasterer or painter, whichever you choose before the work starts.

Do we need to be home for the whole job?
+

Someone needs to let the crew in, confirm the jack locations at the start and see the walk-through at the end. Between those points you do not need to follow the crew from room to room, but any apartment we enter in a two-family has to be arranged with that household in advance.

Can our existing security cameras share the new cabling?
+

Network cameras can connect to the same structured network on their own cables and, where the switch supplies power over its ports, draw their power from it. They are best kept on a separate network segment from household devices. Older coax cameras use different cable and stay on their own recorder.

Will a newer Wi-Fi standard make cabling unnecessary?
+

No. Each new Wi-Fi generation is faster close to the access point, but brick, plaster and distance still weaken it, and every access point still needs a connection back to the router. Cable is what lets the newest access points run at full speed on every floor.

What do we do if we switch from one internet provider to another later?
+

Only the first link changes. The new provider installs its terminal at the basement entry, and the cable from there to your panel is moved to the new box. Every jack and access point in the house keeps working as before, and a spare empty conduit from the entry to the panel makes the change even simpler.

Can cabling be added to a finished basement with a drop ceiling?
+

Yes, and a drop ceiling makes it easier. The tiles lift out, the cable travels above them on supports, and it drops into the walls or to a ceiling access point. Where the basement is finished with drywall instead, we look for soffits, access panels and closets to keep openings to a minimum.

What paperwork does a Madison co-op shareholder need before cabling starts?
+

Usually the board's approval under its alteration agreement, a scope letter describing each opening and a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the managing agent. Send us the building's requirements, and we prepare the scope letter and insurance certificate for the managing agent before the work is booked.
