The Neighborhood

Why Clason Point Properties Need TV Installation

Clason Point was a place people came to on summer weekends long before it was a place people lived all year, and its living rooms still carry that history. Wikipedia records that the land was once the site of a large Native American settlement of more than seventy dwellings known as Snakapins, the "Land By The Two Waters," and that the point takes its name from Isaac Clason, the merchant who bought the eastern half of Cornell's Neck in 1793. In the early twentieth century it was a resort. The Clason Point Amusement Park operated from 1910 to 1935, the Sound View Avenue streetcar, first called the Clason Point Line, ran from 1909 until 1947, and Welcome2TheBronx recounts the storm of June 11, 1922 that blew down an operating Ferris wheel, killing six people and injuring twenty-two. When the amusement grounds closed, the site became the Shorehaven Beach Club in 1949, which ran until 1986. According to Apartments.com's neighborhood guide, the point stayed largely undeveloped until the 1940s, and only then filled in with apartment complexes, co-ops and houses. That sequence, resort first, homes later, explains why a TV job here can mean three very different kinds of building within a ten-minute walk of each other.

The first kind is the bungalow. City Lore's profile of Harding Park says the first bungalows went up in the 1920s and that the colony was named after the president of the day, Warren Harding. They were summer places. After the Second World War, the same profile explains, the housing shortage led many tenants to turn their bungalows into permanent homes, and the result is a colony of former cottages that were winterized, extended and rebuilt one house at a time. City Lore even notes bungalows that seem to have been fused together into one sprawling mega-bungalow. City Planning's Resilient Neighborhoods page for Harding Park describes the area as "characterized by a unique variety of lot sizes and construction types," and its 2016 summary report counts 226 single-family homes and about 400 residents, with homes that front narrow paved lanes without sidewalks. For TV work, that history has a direct consequence: we cannot assume anything about a Harding Park wall from its paint. A living room wall might be original cottage framing, a later addition built to a different standard, or the old outside wall of one bungalow that became an inside wall when two were joined.

The second kind is the townhome. Wikipedia records that the old beach club grounds became the Shorehaven Condominiums in 1999, a gated community of 1,183 multi-unit condominium townhomes, and city property records show the Harbour Pointe at Shorehaven condominiums going up between 2005 and 2008. Homes.com listings describe a community with 24-hour security, a fitness center and a swimming pool, and units that are largely duplexes and walk-ups, for example a corner second-floor walk-up duplex townhouse. One CS Realty listing mentions new recessed lighting throughout a condominium on both floors. Those details tell us what to expect: newer construction, finished ceilings and walls that are far more likely to be drywall than plaster, a TV that may have to be carried up an inside stair to a living room on the second level, and a gatehouse that needs to know we are coming. The street names in the listings, Surf Drive, Sunset Boulevard, Mermaid Lane, Admiral Court and Heron Lane, are the ones we expect to see on Shorehaven bookings.

The third kind is the apartment. Clason Point Gardens, the Bronx Times reported in September 2026 when the development marked its 85th year, opened in 1941 as the first public housing in the Bronx and NYCHA's first low-rise, garden-style development, mostly two-story homes on about 17 acres, many with front and back yards. The Real Deal places it within Story, Seward, Noble and Metcalf Avenues. Along the northern edge of the point stand the James Monroe Houses, which Wikipedia describes as twelve buildings of 8, 14 and 15 stories with 1,102 apartments, bordered by Soundview, Story, Taylor and Lafayette Avenues, and just south of Clason Point Gardens are the Soundview Houses, built in 1954 as 13 seven-story buildings, according to urbanareas.net. A tenant in one of those apartments has a landlord with its own house rules, often a provider box that came through a city program, and an elevator or a stairwell between the street and the living room.

Around and between those three are the houses on the city grid. Homes.com describes the local stock as single-family Craftsman cottages, ranch homes, townhouses and multifamily row houses, and Wikipedia notes that modern two- and three-unit rowhouses have been going up since the 1990s. Redfin shows listings such as a fully detached two-family house with a finished basement and its own separate entrance. Nextdoor puts homeownership in the neighborhood at 62 percent, and Homes.com calls the area almost suburban because so many residents live in single-family homes and own cars. So the typical Clason Point TV call is not about a doorman tower with poured concrete walls. It is more often an owner who wants a screen on the wall of a house they own, a two-family landlord deciding what a tenant may do downstairs, or a condo owner who wants to know which walls are theirs to drill.

Two features of the point shape almost every visit. The first is access. Untapped Cities describes narrow streets and small former bungalows with very little space between houses facing each other, and Forgotten New York notes that some streets here are interrupted midblock by properties and others do not appear on any official map. A GPS that drops a van at the wrong end of a lane, or a 75-inch box that will not turn a corner between two porches, wastes more time than any wall. The second is water. City Planning's Harding Park page describes exposure to storm-surge flooding, wave action and ponding from rain, made worse by limited stormwater infrastructure, and First Street estimates that 42.2 percent of Harding Park properties have flood risk this year, rising to 58.8 percent over thirty years. City Lore notes bungalows that plainly suffered in Hurricane Sandy but are still lived in. That does not change how a bracket holds, but it does change where we suggest the cable box, the power strip and the basement den TV should sit.

We also want to be plain about what our research could not find. We looked for Reddit threads, Yelp complaints, local news and city records about TV mounting, wall damage or landlord disputes over screens in Clason Point and found nothing specific to the neighborhood. We found no published survey of how Harding Park's bungalows or Shorehaven's townhomes were framed inside. Where this page describes the walls you are likely to have, it is reasoning from the age and type of the buildings in the public record and from what we find in buildings like them, not from an inspection of yours. The only way to know a wall is to look at it, which is the first thing we do.

Clason Point facts that shape a TV install

  • A small peninsula: Wikipedia gives Clason Point an area of 0.47 square miles in ZIP code 10473 and Bronx Community Board 9, with Soundview Avenue as its main road.
  • Three building types side by side: Shorehaven's 1,183 condominium townhomes, Harding Park's 226 single-family homes on private lanes, and NYCHA developments from the 1941 garden apartments to the 15-story Monroe Houses towers.
  • Owners more than renters: Nextdoor lists 62 percent homeowners, and Homes.com describes the area as almost suburban, with many residents owning cars.
  • A co-op on the land: The Neighborhoods newsletter reports that in 1981 the city sold Harding Park's land to its homeowners association for $700,000, making it the first cooperatively owned low- and moderate-income community in New York; NYC Parks gives 1982 and another source a higher price, so we use only the fact of association ownership.
  • Water close by: First Street estimates 42.2 percent of Harding Park properties face flood risk now and 58.8 percent within thirty years.
  • Few shops: Wikipedia calls the neighborhood almost entirely residential, with a handful of businesses on Soundview Avenue and the main shopping along Story Avenue, the Bruckner and White Plains Road.

Gatehouse, Lane, Stairwell or Elevator: How a TV Gets Into a Clason Point Home

Most TV pages talk about the wall first. On Clason Point the job starts earlier, at the point where a large, fragile carton leaves the van. A 65-inch box is roughly as wide as a person is tall and must travel upright; flexing the panel can crack it. Between the curb and your wall it may pass a guard booth, a lane barely wider than the van, an inside stair with a turn at the top, or a NYCHA lobby and elevator. Each of those routes has its own rules and its own way of going wrong, and planning it before the visit is the reason a job here takes the time it should and no more. Shorehaven: the gate decides the timing

The Shorehaven Homeowners Association's rules, effective September 1, 2020, say that members and their guests come through Security, that guests are announced on the member's authorization, and that truck and van deliveries and moves are limited to Monday through Saturday between 7 a.m. and 8 p.m., with Security told about deliveries in advance. The same rules say there is no guest parking inside the gated community and that the clubhouse and Security do not accept packages for members. Rules change, and the association may have updated these since, so we treat them as a starting point and ask you to confirm. In practice they mean three things for a TV job: we book inside the delivery window, you tell Security our company name and the date before we arrive, and the TV itself should already be in your home, because nobody at the gate will sign for it on your behalf.

Inside the gate, the carry depends on the unit. Listings describe many Shorehaven homes as duplexes, some with the living level on the second floor reached by an inside stair. We look at the stair before we lift: its width, whether it turns at the top, how low the ceiling is over the landing, and where the box can rest halfway. Large screens go up in their carton or on a padded carrier with two people, never flat and never balanced on a banister. If the stair is too tight for the carton, the TV comes out on the lower floor, on blankets, and goes up bare with its corners protected.

Harding Park: the lane decides the route

City Planning's 2016 report describes Harding Park's private streets as 9 to 13 feet wide in the southern part of the colony and its lots as narrow to the north, with homes fronting paved lanes that have no sidewalks. Forgotten New York adds that some streets on the point stop midblock or do not appear on official maps. A van can be wider than half of that lane and taller than the branches over it. So for Harding Park bookings we ask for the name of the lane, the nearest point a vehicle can stop, and a photo of the route from there to your door. We would rather carry a few extra houses' distance than block a lane that a neighbor needs to get out of, and we never leave a van where it stops the only way in or out.

The carry itself passes close to other homes. Untapped Cities notes the little space between bungalows facing each other, and porches, fences and steps often sit right at the edge of the lane. We walk the route empty first, then bring the TV. If the front door is narrow, or opens straight into a small room with no space to turn, we plan which way the box goes in before we lift it.

Apartment buildings: the elevator and the house rules

In a seven-story Soundview Houses building or a 14- or 15-story Monroe Houses building, the carry is about the lobby, the elevator and the hallway. A large carton often fits an elevator only standing on end, and a busy elevator can mean a wait. We bring the TV up in its box whenever it fits, because the box is the best protection the screen has. We also ask about house rules before we book. The NYCHA House Rules we read say that residents may install TV antennas only inside their apartments, and they set limits on alterations around apartment doors; we did not find a published NYCHA rule specifically about wall-mounting a TV, so we ask tenants to check their lease and with management first, and we offer a TV-on-stand setup at $137.99 where drilling is not allowed.

Ten Minutes With a Tape Measure: What to Check in a Clason Point Home Before We Come

Most of the time lost on a TV visit is lost before the first hole: a TV that is not in the house yet, a bracket that does not match the TV, a wall that is not what it looks like, a cable box that has to live where the cable cannot reach, or a van with nowhere to stop. A few photos and measurements prevent nearly all of it. Please do not open walls, move outlets or take down an old bracket yourself before we arrive; look, measure and send us what you find.

Sit where you watch, then measure

The right height for a screen is set by the eyes of the people watching it, not by the wall. Sit on the seat you actually use, look straight ahead, and have someone measure from the floor to your eye level. In most rooms the middle of the screen sits close to that height or a little above it. Then measure from the seat to the wall. In the small front rooms of many Harding Park bungalows, a very large screen can end up uncomfortably close; in the long open living level of some Shorehaven duplexes, a small one can look lost. Send both measurements and we will tell you what we see in rooms of that size.

Look at the window light at the time you watch

Clason Point has water on three sides and a lot of open sky over Soundview Park, and many homes here were placed to look at it. That is lovely in the room and hard on a screen. Before choosing the wall, look at it in the evening or whenever you usually watch, with the blinds as you normally keep them. If the window across from the wall throws a bright square on the spot, a slightly different position, a tilting bracket or a side wall may serve you better. We will look with you on the day, but your note tells us which walls to consider first.

Find what runs inside the wall

Before we drill, it helps to know what might be inside. Look at the other side of the TV wall and at the rooms above and below. A bathroom or kitchen behind or above the wall means pipes may run through it; an outlet or switch nearby means wiring. In a joined or extended bungalow, ask yourself whether the wall you want used to be an outside wall; if there is siding or an old window frame visible in the attic or crawlspace, that is a clue. In a duplex, the stair and its handrail are usually fixed to a wall, and that wall may carry the bathroom drain from above. We scan every wall ourselves, but your notes tell us where to look hardest.

TV Installation for Clason Point's Architecture

Clason Point fits several very different kinds of home onto one small peninsula. Each changes three things about a TV job: what the bracket is fixed into, how the cables can travel, and who has to agree before anything is drilled. These are the types the public record and neighborhood guides describe here, and how we handle each one.

Shorehaven Condo Townhomes

Built mostly in the 2000s, with drywall interiors, recessed ceiling lights in some units and living levels on one or two floors. The inside is the owner's; the building's exterior, under the 2020 rules, is the condominium board's.

Shorehaven Two-Family Homes

Two apartments in one house within the gated community. The 2020 rules say the two-family homeowner is always responsible for repairs, so the owner decides what a tenant may have drilled.

Harding Park Bungalows

Former summer cottages from the 1920s, winterized after the war, extended, sometimes joined into one larger house. Single-family homes on land owned by the homeowners association.

Newer Two- and Three-Unit Rowhouses

Built since the 1990s on the grid streets, stacked apartments with separate entrances. More predictable walls, and an owner, often living in one unit, who sets the rules for the others.

Older Houses on the Grid

Craftsman cottages, ranch homes and detached two-families with finished basements, some decades older than the rowhouses around them, with plaster, paneling or later drywall.

Public Housing and Apartment Buildings

Clason Point Gardens' two-story garden homes from 1941, and the seven-story Soundview Houses and 8- to 15-story Monroe Houses towers. Management's rules come before any drilling.

Townhome duplexes: newer walls, two levels

A Shorehaven townhome from the 2000s is, for the bracket itself, often the most straightforward wall on the point. Drywall over framing is the base line on our list, $209.99 for a TV up to 55 inches, and a stud finder reads it far more reliably than it reads old plaster. We still confirm each stud with a small probe hole behind where the bracket plate will sit, because builders do not always put framing where you would expect, especially around stairs and the openings of an open-plan living level. The harder part of a duplex is usually the cable. If the coax or network jack is on one level and the TV hangs on the other, we look first for a path that already exists, a closet stacked over a closet, or a run beside the stair, rather than opening a new route through the ceiling. If there is no clean way through, a streaming device on the home WiFi often makes a cable at the TV unnecessary.

Recessed lights, mentioned in at least one Shorehaven listing, also tell us something. A ceiling with recessed fixtures has wiring and fixture housings running through the space above it, and a wall that meets that ceiling may carry a cable up to a switch. We scan for live wiring along the top of the TV wall as well as near the outlets, and we keep fixings well clear of any run we find.

Bungalows: every wall its own story

Harding Park's houses are the reason we refuse to quote a wall we have not seen. Rules and practical limits for business screens

  • Cords stay out of the wall. National Electrical Code §400.12 says flexible cords may not be concealed behind walls, run through holes in walls or ceilings, or used in place of fixed wiring. A TV's own power cord pushed through drywall is exactly what that section forbids. We use an in-wall rated power and cable kit or a surface cover.
  • New outlets are an electrician's job. Our work is low-voltage and mounting. Where a business wants a new receptacle high on a wall behind a screen, that circuit work belongs to a licensed electrician, and Hoppler Design Build's guide notes that moving circuits or building recessed AV chases in NYC usually means a permit; our quote says so.
  • Things that stick out into a path. The 2010 ADA Standards limit how far wall-mounted objects may protrude into a circulation path at certain heights, so people with low vision do not walk into them. A screen beside the route to a restroom has to sit high enough or close enough to the wall to stay clear.
  • Exits stay visible. A screen must never cover an exit sign or narrow a path to an exit. We check the sight line to every exit sign before we fix a bracket in a dining room.
  • Paperwork for landlords. Many commercial landlords and managing agents ask for a certificate of insurance before contractors work in a space; send the request with your booking and we issue it before the visit.
  • Hours that suit the room. Drilling and lifting happen when the room is closed or quiet, and the apartments above a shop are taken into account when we choose the time.

Walls behind the paint

Our bungalow started life as a summer cottage and was winterized decades ago. What are its walls likely to be made of?

Something we will only know when we look, which is why we ask for photos and confirm with a probe hole. City Lore describes Harding Park's cottages being turned into permanent homes after the Second World War, and winterizing a cottage usually meant adding insulation and a new interior finish over light original framing, then more changes as families grew. In houses with that history we find wood paneling, plaster, drywall over older boards, or a mix across one wall, and framing that can be lighter and less evenly spaced than in a modern house. None of that rules out a TV. It does mean we find the wood stud by stud, test that it is continuous from floor to ceiling where the bracket will sit, and size the bracket and screen to what the wall can actually carry.

Two bungalows on our lot were joined into one house years ago. Is the wall where they meet a good place for the TV?

It can be, but it is the wall we test most carefully. City Lore notes bungalows in Harding Park that appear to have been fused into one larger house, and where that happens, an inside wall today may have been the outside wall of one cottage. That wall can be thicker than the others, can hide old siding or a filled-in window behind the finish, and may have framing that stops or changes where the two buildings meet. Yes, two things. In a newer two-unit rowhouse, which wall usually makes the better TV wall: the long side wall or the wall with the street windows?

Usually the long side wall, though your room decides. In a rowhouse living room the windows are normally at the front or back, and a screen on the window wall means looking toward daylight, while a screen opposite the windows catches glare. A long side wall often lets the screen sit across from the sofa with the window light coming from the side, which is easiest on the picture. The catch is that side walls in stacked rowhouses are more likely to carry pipes and heating lines from the apartment above, and in attached rows the side wall may be shared with the next house. What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Clason Point

What follows are claims that turn up again and again in quick online answers about TV mounting and about this corner of the Bronx, set against what the public record and our own field practice say. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, not as a quote for a wall on Clason Point.

"Clason Point is all public housing towers."

Quick summaries of the southeast Bronx tend to describe it by its largest buildings. Clason Point does have them: the Monroe Houses' twelve buildings of up to 15 stories, the seven-story Soundview Houses, and Clason Point Gardens, NYCHA's first low-rise development in the Bronx.

But the same peninsula holds Shorehaven's 1,183 condominium townhomes, Harding Park's 226 single-family homes and a grid of rowhouses, cottages and ranch homes, and Nextdoor puts homeownership at 62 percent.

For TV work that difference is everything. An owner in a bungalow or townhome decides about the wall; a tenant in a tower has to start with the lease. We ask which you are before quoting, because the answer changes what we can offer.

"You never need a permit to mount a TV."

For the mount itself, that is usually right: Hoppler Design Build's guide to NYC media walls says mounting a TV alone generally needs no Department of Buildings permit.

The same guide says that opening a wall for a recessed AV chase or moving circuits does. That is the line people miss when they ask for a new outlet behind the TV or a TV built into a niche.

Our in-wall power and cable kit is a listed kit that runs between two plates within the wall, not a new circuit. Where a job needs new wiring or a built-in chase, we say so and the electrical part goes to a licensed electrician with whatever filing it requires.

"Renters can mount a TV as long as they patch the holes when they leave."

Many quick answers treat patching as the whole story. Mounting NYC's guide to rental rules points out that most standard New York leases prohibit permanent alterations without written landlord consent, and that drilling for a TV mount technically counts.

Brick Underground has answered readers whose landlords told them not to hang things on walls at all, which shows how much depends on the individual lease.

So for tenants in Clason Point two-families, rowhouses and apartment buildings, the first step is the owner's written OK. Without it we put the TV on its stand for $137.99, or use a cord cover so nothing goes inside the wall if the owner allows the mount but not an opening.

"Put a satellite dish or antenna on the window for better reception."

That advice is harmless in some homes and against the rules in others. The NYCHA House Rules say residents may install TV antennas only inside their apartments.

Many NYCHA residents may not need a dish anyway. ACCESS NYC describes Big Apple Connect, which gives eligible NYCHA households internet and basic cable for as low as $0 a month through Optimum or Spectrum, and Optimum's list of participating developments includes Soundview and Monroe.

In those apartments the common job is connecting the provider's box to the new TV and setting up streaming apps, which a setup-only visit covers at $225 for the first hour, or which we do as part of a stand or wall-mount job.

"If you own the condo, you can mount anything anywhere on it."

Condo ownership is not the same as owning a house. Under the Shorehaven Homeowners Association's 2020 rules, repairs inside a home are the unit owner's, but residential buildings, including exterior walls and roofs, are the condominium Board of Managers', and the front of a unit is shared common property.

That means an indoor TV is your decision, while a TV, bracket or dish on the outside of the building needs the board's approval first.

We follow the same line. Inside, we work to your instructions; outside, we need to see the written approval before we fix anything to the building.

"A stud finder tells you exactly where to drill."

On a modern drywall wall a good stud finder is usually reliable, and in a 2000s Shorehaven unit it often is. In a Harding Park bungalow that has been winterized, extended and joined to its neighbor, it can be badly misleading.

Layers of paneling over boards, plaster patches beside drywall, old siding buried inside a wall that used to be outside, and framing that changes at a seam all confuse the sensor, and a bolt that misses the wood holds only briefly.

Our rule is the same in every home: the stud finder suggests, a small probe hole behind the bracket plate confirms. In a bungalow we check both the upper and lower fixing positions, because the wood we find at one height may not continue at the other.

"Hourly app pricing is always cheaper than a flat price list."

App marketplaces advertise low hourly starting rates for Bronx TV mounting, and that is where many AI answers start. TaskRabbit's own page notes that cable management or drilling into a facade can make the job take longer.

FAQ

Clason Point TV Installation FAQ

Shorehaven's Security doesn't accept packages. Should our new TV be delivered before the install date?
+

Yes, have it delivered to you, at home, before the day of the install. The Shorehaven association's rules say the clubhouse and Security do not accept packages for members, so a TV shipped while you are out has nowhere safe to wait. Once it has arrived, leave it in its carton, upright, in the room where it will go or as close as you can, and send us the model number so we bring the right fixings and know which lines on the Bronx list apply.

Our basic cable comes through Big Apple Connect with an Optimum box. Can you connect that box to the new TV in our Soundview Houses apartment?
+

Yes. Big Apple Connect provides internet and basic cable to eligible NYCHA households through Optimum or Spectrum, and Optimum lists the Soundview and Monroe developments among those taking part. We connect the provider's box to the new TV, set the inputs, and test the channels and any streaming apps you use. As part of a stand setup at $137.99 or a wall mount it is included; if the TV is already in place and only the box and apps need attention, a setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks.

NYCHA's house rules say antennas have to stay inside the apartment. Can an antenna be set up behind the TV instead of at the window?
+

Yes, an indoor antenna can sit behind or beside the TV and keep to that rule, since the NYCHA House Rules allow antennas only inside apartments. Reception in a lower apartment can be weaker than at a window, so we try a few positions within the room and scan the channels at each before settling on one. If the apartment already receives cable through Big Apple Connect, an antenna may not be needed at all, and we will tell you which gives the better result on the day.

The only outlet on our TV wall is a few inches above the floor, and our house is in a flood zone. Can the TV's power be moved up?
+

The in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list, puts a proper receptacle behind the screen, fed through in-wall rated wiring from the low outlet, so the TV plugs in high and the power cord never hangs in sight. It does not move the low outlet itself, which stays where it is. If you want the original outlet raised or a new circuit added, that is work for a licensed electrician. We also suggest keeping the cable box and power strip off the floor, on the console or the wall, so they are not the first things water reaches.

Our Harding Park lane doesn't show up properly on GPS. How will the technician find the house?
+

Tell us the lane name, the nearest cross street or point on Soundview Avenue, and a landmark near your door, such as a fence color or a porch, and send a photo of the route from the nearest place a vehicle can stop. Forgotten New York notes that some streets on the point stop midblock or are missing from official maps, so we do not rely on the GPS alone. The technician calls you when the van arrives so you can guide the last stretch.

If I send the wall photos tonight, how soon will someone get back to me about my Clason Point address?
+

Requests are booked within 2 hours of reaching us during working hours, and messages sent late in the evening are answered the next business morning. The quickest replies come when the photos show the whole wall, the TV's label and the route to the room, because the technician can then name the lines from the Bronx list straight away instead of asking more questions. The install date itself depends on the schedule and, inside Shorehaven, on the delivery hours.

Inside our Shorehaven townhome, are the walls ours to drill, or do they belong to the condominium?
+

Under the Shorehaven association's 2020 rules, repairs and work inside a home are the unit owner's responsibility, while the residential buildings themselves, including exterior walls and roofs, belong to the condominium Board of Managers. An indoor TV mount on an interior wall is therefore normally the owner's decision. Walls shared with a neighboring unit and anything on the outside are different; if you are unsure about a wall, check with the managing agent before booking and we will work to their answer.

Water came into our finished basement once. Where should the den TV and its cable box go now?
+

Up, and on the wall rather than the floor. We mount the screen higher than usual for a basement, keep the cable box and power strip on a shelf or the wall instead of under a low console, and leave the power cord with enough slack that the box can be lifted further before a storm. Use a surge-protected strip, and if an outlet in the basement was ever under water, have an electrician check it before anything is plugged into it. A cord cover at $59 keeps the run neat without opening a wall that may have been dried out and repaired.

The new TV goes on the living room wall and the old one should move to a bedroom on its stand. What does that come to?
+

Taking the old TV down is $85, setting it on its stand in the bedroom is $137.99, and mounting the new one, up to 55 inches on drywall, is $209.99, plus the bracket. If the old bracket suits the new TV and was fixed properly, it can stay on the wall and save buying another. We connect both TVs, move the cable box or streaming device where you want it, and test each set before we leave.

In a Shorehaven living room, is a soundbar better on the console or fixed to the wall under the screen?
+

If the TV is on the wall and there is no console under it, mount the bar, at $129, so it sits centered just below the screen on its own fixings. If there is a console, placing the bar on it, at $79, works well and leaves the wall with fewer holes. In open living levels, the bar's position matters less than how it is set up, so we finish by adjusting the sound from the seat you use.

Should we take the old TV off the wall ourselves before you arrive, to save the take-down charge?
+

We advise against it unless two people can lift it safely and you know how the bracket releases. Large TVs are heavier and more awkward than they look, and a panel dropped while unhooking it is the most common way a set is damaged. If you do take it down, keep the bracket on the wall so we can judge whether it can be reused. If you would rather not risk it, the take-down line is $85.

Do we need to be home for the whole TV install, or only at the start and the end?
+

We need you at the start, to agree the wall and the height, and at the end, to see the TV working and learn the remotes. In between you are welcome to get on with your day. At Shorehaven, someone needs to be reachable by Security when we arrive, and in an apartment building someone should meet us if the lobby has a sign-in.
