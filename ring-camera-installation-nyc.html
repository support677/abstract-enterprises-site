The Neighborhood

Why New York City Properties Need Ring Camera Installation

Ring sells its doorbells as a twenty-minute weekend project, and in a lot of the country that is roughly true. In New York City it often is not, and the reason is the age and shape of the buildings. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council's housing stock analysis puts the city at roughly 3.7 million housing units, with about 2 million of them, more than half, built before 1947 and more than 80 percent built more than 50 years ago. The same analysis describes a range that runs from mansions in Riverdale and small homes in Midland Beach to garden apartments in Sunnyside, high-rises near Central Park, brownstones and tenements. Each of those has a different front door. Some have a doorbell circuit wired when the building went up, some have a building intercom and no bell at all, some have a peephole in a fire-rated apartment door, and some have a storefront with a roll-down gate. A Ring device has to suit the door it goes on, and in New York the door comes first.

The single biggest fact about Ring in this city is that most New Yorkers do not own their front door in the way a suburban homeowner does. CHPC's analysis says 62 percent of the city's units are in buildings with six or more units, and in Manhattan the figure is 97 percent. For those residents, the door that visitors and couriers reach first is the building's street door, controlled by an intercom the owner is required to provide. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system, and according to an intercom vendor's summary, older buildings fall under the same rule once tenants in a majority of the apartments ask for it in writing. Habitat Magazine reported in February 2024 that courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service tied to the warranty of habitability. So the renter who wants a Ring is usually not replacing a doorbell. They are adding a camera to their own apartment door, or to the hallway outside it, inside a building where someone else controls the walls, the hallway and the entrance. That is a permission question before it is a wiring question.

The second fact is package theft, and it lands hardest on apartments. Security.org's 2026 package theft report found apartment residents were more than three times as likely as single-family home residents to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent, and noted that apartment dwellers may not be allowed to put doorbell or security cameras outside their units under their landlord's rules. A delivery-locker company's write-up on New York theft adds that many older buildings lack surveillance cameras or a set place for deliveries. The New York State Attorney General's 2024 consumer alert on package theft recommends a doorbell camera or video surveillance system to help deter thieves or help police identify them. CBS New York's report on a lobby theft at the Robeson on Lenox Avenue in Harlem shows how the problem plays out: a key code was needed to enter the building, police said there was no forced entry, and a tenant told the station her family had a Ring camera so they could see everything that went on in front of their door. That is the typical New York case for Ring: not a burglar on a lawn, but a lobby, a vestibule or a stoop where deliveries sit and many people pass.

The third fact is the walls. A real-estate guide to prewar apartments describes walls in those buildings that are often 12 to 18 inches of masonry. Masonry that thick is good for quiet and fire separation and bad for Wi-Fi, and every Ring camera and doorbell depends on a steady Wi-Fi or network connection to do anything useful. A router at the back of a railroad apartment and a Ring Peephole Cam on the front door can be separated by several brick partitions. A brownstone owner's router on the parlor floor may be two masonry walls and a stoop away from the bell at the garden-level door. Then there is the question of whether the household has home internet at all: the NYC Council Data Team, drawing on the American Community Survey, reported that 24.5 percent of city households had no broadband subscription in 2021 and 12.8 percent relied on a cellular data plan alone. A Ring doorbell cannot run on a phone's data plan. We check signal at the door, and the connection behind it, before we mount anything.

The fourth fact is the rules on the outside of buildings. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, plus whole historic districts full of brownstones and row houses. Its 2018 rule amendments addressed security cameras, intercoms and similar equipment on facades, and its proposed rules would bar many such objects from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative or an agency requires the location. A later LPC rule lets staff approve installations that will not damage or remove significant architectural features and will not be visible. For a brownstone owner in a historic district, that can change where a Ring doorbell or floodlight cam goes, how its wire is routed and whether the work needs an LPC permit first. We raise that before booking, not after a bracket is on the brownstone.

The last fact is privacy, which in New York has been front-page news. NYCLU, PIX11 and Patch all covered the NYPD's announcement that it would join Ring's Neighbors app, with PIX11 reporting that officers would not watch the app around the clock but would be able to ask homeowners for Ring clips, and NYCLU arguing the arrangement could conflict with the city's Public Oversight of Surveillance Technology Act. Ring's facial recognition feature, Familiar Faces, launched in the US in December 2025, and TechCrunch reported a class action filed in June 2026 alleging it stores images of passersby without consent. New Yorkers buying Ring now ask us as much about settings as about mounting, and a good installation includes walking you through them.

We want to be plain about the gaps in what we found. We looked for Reddit threads, local news and Yelp complaints about Ring installers in New York City and found no detailed threads or complaints we could quote; one blog describes a Reddit user in a New York apartment building who was unsure whether a personal camera was legal, and that uncertainty is exactly what we hear on the phone. We did not pull 311 complaint counts, building permit data or HPD violation totals for doorbells or cameras, so this page does not quote any. Where we describe what we usually find in old buildings, that is field practice, not a survey of New York homes.

New York City facts that shape a Ring installation

  • Old buildings: about 2 million units built before 1947 and more than 80 percent of housing over 50 years old, per CHPC, so original doorbell wiring and masonry walls are common.
  • Mostly shared doors: 62 percent of units are in buildings of six or more units citywide and 97 percent in Manhattan, while 83 percent of Staten Island's housing is one- and two-family, per CHPC.
  • Intercoms, not doorbells: MDL §50-a requires intercoms in class A buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, and city rule 1 RCNY §42-01 sets push buttons no higher than six feet and the speaking device between four and five feet from the floor.
  • Apartments lose more packages: 10 percent of apartment residents against 3 percent of single-family residents reported a stolen package in three months, per Security.org's 2026 report.
  • Connection gaps: 24.5 percent of households had no broadband subscription in 2021, per the NYC Council Data Team, which matters because Ring needs home internet.
  • No blanket camera mandate: one New York security firm's review of the city code found no law requiring every apartment building to install security cameras.

What New Yorkers often add next. Once a Ring doorbell or Peephole Cam is working at the door, the next request is usually a second camera where packages actually sit: the vestibule of a brownstone, the side yard of a Queens house, the back door of a shop. Building owners who start with Ring at the street door often follow with a wired intercom upgrade, since Ring's own intercom add-on is not sold in the US. Say so when you book and we plan the cable routes for both at once.

Manhattan: the camera is on your door, not the building's

In a borough where nearly every household lives in a multi-unit building, the street door belongs to the building. Visitors press a button on an intercom panel that the city's rule 1 RCNY §42-01 says must have its push buttons no more than six feet from the floor and its speaking device between four and five feet. You cannot put your Ring there. What you control is your own apartment door, and even that is partly the owner's: the Housing Maintenance Code makes the owner provide the lock on each apartment entrance door, and §27-2041 requires a peephole in it. That is why the Ring Peephole Cam matters so much in Manhattan. TechCrunch, reporting on Ring bringing the device back in 2023, said Ring insists it can be installed without permanent modifications to a door and that it includes Privacy Zones for blocking parts of the view. It replaces the peephole you already have, from the inside, and leaves the door itself unchanged. We still ask that renters and co-op shareholders tell the landlord or board in writing, because a door camera in a shared corridor is something many buildings want to know about even when no hole is drilled. For doorman buildings, we also plan the visit around the building's contractor rules and paperwork, which usually means a certificate of insurance at the desk before we go up.

Brooklyn: stoops, garden doors and the original bell

Brooklyn's mix means we see every type of Ring job there, but the one that defines the borough is the brownstone or row house. One New York electrician's video doorbell page describes the brownstone problem well: plaster walls, original wiring and a stoop-level entry make placement and wiring trickier. In a brownstone the main door is often at the top of the stoop, the garden-level door underneath, and the old bell button and its transformer may be anywhere from the vestibule to the cellar. A hardwired Ring doorbell needs a transformer that supplies enough power and a pair of wires that are still sound end to end. In houses that have been through several owners, we regularly find the bell wire spliced, painted over, chewed or connected to a transformer that hums and runs hot. We measure before we promise a hardwired install, and if the circuit is unreliable, the choice is a battery doorbell, a new low-voltage run, or a transformer replacement handled where code requires by an electrician. Brownstone Brooklyn also contains historic districts, and Brooklyn Heights was the first district the city designated, in 1965; owners there should expect the facade question to come up.

Queens: houses, porches and garden apartments

Queens holds a large share of the city's one- and two-family houses alongside its apartment buildings, and its garden-apartment neighborhoods, Jackson Heights among the earliest, put several households behind a shared courtyard or entry. On a Queens house, Ring usually means a wired doorbell at the front door and a floodlight or spotlight cam on the driveway or side passage, where bins, bikes and deliveries sit. The challenge is often Wi-Fi reach to the side or back of the house, through brick or stucco, and outdoor power for a floodlight cam where there is no existing light fixture to replace. In garden apartments, the questions look more like Manhattan's: who controls the courtyard door, and whether the board allows cameras on shared walls. Forest Hills Gardens, with its precast concrete panels described by The Cultural Landscape Foundation, is a reminder that "a house in Queens" can still mean an unusual wall to drill.

The Bronx: large complexes and the northern houses

The Bronx has some of the city's largest housing complexes. Parkchester, built by Metropolitan Life between 1939 and 1942, is 171 brick buildings of 8 or 13 stories, and Co-op City has 15,372 units in 35 high-rises and seven townhouse clusters. In buildings like those, a Ring device on an apartment door raises the same permission questions as in Manhattan, often with management or a cooperative board that has written rules. The northern and eastern Bronx also have streets of detached and attached houses where a standard wired doorbell plus yard cameras makes sense. Connection is a real issue in parts of the borough: the Council Data Team found 44 percent of households without broadband in the Melrose, Mott Haven and Port Morris community district, the highest share in the city. Where there is no home internet, a Ring device is the wrong purchase until the internet is in, and we say so.

Staten Island: driveways, yards and long Wi-Fi runs

With 83 percent of its housing in one- and two-family homes, Staten Island is the borough where Ring installs look most like the suburban jobs in Ring's own ads. There is a front door with a bell circuit, a driveway, a side gate and a back yard. The work is less about permission and more about distance and weather: getting a reliable signal to a camera at the far end of a yard, mounting a floodlight cam on siding or brick that faces the wind, and sealing every opening so water stays out. Two-family homes add one complication. If the house has two doorbells, one for each apartment, each Ring doorbell should be on its own account so neither household sees the other's visitors.

Ask the landlord in writing, and keep the answer

A New York security firm's guide to DIY versus professional camera installation puts it bluntly: drilling inside a rented apartment is not permitted without written approval from the landlord, and co-op and condo boards often require approval before any installation. A legal answer posted on JustAnswer for a Brooklyn apartment tenant made the same point about the hallway: common areas outside your door are the landlord's, you need permission to mount there, and a landlord can remove a camera installed without consent. A short email that names the device, where it will go and how it is fixed usually gets a quick answer. If the landlord says no to drilling, a Peephole Cam or an indoor camera facing the door is often still acceptable. Keep the reply; it settles questions later.

Look at the view your camera will have

Stand where the doorbell or camera will go and look at what it will see. In a corridor, will it look straight into a neighbor's door when that door opens? On a stoop, will it look into the parlor window across the street? A legal answer on Justia about a New York co-op said surveillance is legal so long as the camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent. Ring's motion zones and privacy zones help, but the simplest fix is a good position. Tell us what bothers you about the view and we will plan around it.

Check the power behind the bell

If you know where the old doorbell transformer is, in the cellar, a closet, or by the electrical panel, take a photo of its label. Old transformers often supply less power than a modern video doorbell wants, and that shortfall is a common reason a Ring doorbell keeps rebooting or the chime buzzes. We test it with a meter on arrival either way, but knowing where it is saves time in a house where the bell wire may run three floors.

Brownstones: stone, plaster and the bell in the cellar

Brownstone fronts are sandstone over brick, and the door surrounds can be carved stone or heavy wood. A Ring doorbell fixed to stone gets masonry anchors set in solid material, never in a crumbling face or a mortar joint that will let go, and the wire comes through the frame rather than across the facade. Inside, the bell wire usually runs through plaster walls to a chime in the hall and a transformer in the cellar or near the panel. If the original wire is sound, we reuse it. If it is broken somewhere inside a plaster wall, fishing a new run is slow and messy, and a battery doorbell or a run through the cellar ceiling to the stoop may be the better call. We tell you which before you pay for it. Where the house is in a historic district, the LPC permit guidebook's chapter on health and safety equipment covers security cameras, and its rules generally push equipment off the primary facade or toward places where it will not be visible; a doorbell at the door is a different case from a floodlight above the stoop, and we look at both with the rule in mind.

Walk-ups: thick walls and someone else's door

In a prewar walk-up, the apartment door is usually a heavy, fire-rated door hung by the owner, with the peephole the Housing Maintenance Code requires. A Ring Peephole Cam swaps out that peephole without changing the door, which is why we lead with it. The harder problem is signal. The Mammoth New York guide describes prewar walls of 12 to 18 inches of masonry, and a railroad apartment's router at the back can be several of those walls away from the door. We test before mounting and, if the signal is weak, place a Chime Pro or extender in a room between them. Hallway cameras in a walk-up are the landlord's decision, not the tenant's.

Co-ops and doorman buildings: paperwork before the device

A pre-war apartments guide notes that most New York buildings put up before the Second World War are organized as co-ops rather than condos, and co-op boards set rules for contractors: approval for alterations, proof of insurance, permitted hours and elevator bookings. Many treat anything on the corridor side of an apartment door as a board matter. We ask for the building's rules when you book, send our insurance paperwork ahead, and bring a copy on the day. If the board allows only a peephole replacement, that is what we install.

Houses: distance, weather and two households

On houses, Ring is straightforward in principle and fussy in practice. Siding needs the right fixings and a sealed opening; brick needs masonry anchors; eaves need a junction box rated for an outdoor floodlight cam. Wi-Fi has to reach the yard. In two-family houses, the two doorbells should go on separate Ring accounts, and the shared side door or driveway camera should be agreed between the households. A short conversation in the driveway saves months of arguments about who sees what.

Rules and practical limits for business Ring cameras

  • Post a notice. Penal Law §250.65, as summarized by Ambient.ai, protects systems where a written notice is conspicuously posted saying video surveillance is installed for security, or where the cameras are clearly obvious. A sign at the door is simple and removes doubt.
  • Facial recognition is a separate decision. New York City's biometric identifier law, Admin Code §§22-1201 to 22-1205, requires covered commercial establishments that collect biometric information to post a sign. If a shop turns on Ring's Familiar Faces, it should read the city's rules first. We leave the feature off unless the owner asks.
  • Audio is risky. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations under Penal Law §250.00, and a camera recording customers' and staff conversations is not a party to them. One New York security firm advises leaving audio recording off; for most shops we agree.
  • Staff areas and restrooms are off limits. Penal Law §250.45 makes recording people where they expect privacy a crime. Cameras cover doors, counters and stock, never restrooms or changing areas.
  • Roll-down gates change the view. A 2009 city law set July 1, 2026 for storefront security gates to be at least 70 percent transparent, and NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the City Council voted to repeal that deadline, sending the bill to the mayor. Either way, a camera inside a solid gate sees nothing after closing; we mount for the hours you need.
  • Licensed work. The city's business portal says a New York State license is required to install or maintain security systems. Our low-voltage work is done under NYS license #12000287431; any new 120-volt outlet for a floodlight is an electrician's job and the quote says so.

Your Questions Answered

Ring Camera Installation in New York City: Questions Answered

Renters, apartments and permission

Is a Ring camera actually legal in a New York City apartment building?

Yes, in general, with two limits that matter. The camera has to be where you have the right to put it, which for most tenants means inside your apartment or on your own door with permission, and it must not record places where people expect privacy, which Penal Law §250.45 makes a crime. A legal answer on Justia about a New York co-op said surveillance is lawful as long as the camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open. So a Peephole Cam showing the corridor outside your door is usually fine; one angled into a neighbor's open doorway is not. This is general information, not legal advice, and a lease or building rule can be stricter.

As a renter, do I need the landlord's permission before a Ring goes on my door?

For anything that drills into the door, the frame or the hallway wall, yes, and get it in writing. A New York security firm's guide says drilling in a rented apartment is not permitted without written landlord approval, and a legal answer on JustAnswer for a Brooklyn tenant said hallway areas outside a door belong to the landlord, who can remove an unapproved camera. A Ring Peephole Cam replaces the existing peephole without permanent changes, and an indoor camera on a shelf needs no holes at all; many landlords accept those readily, but we still suggest a short email so there is no surprise. We do not drill into a rental door without that written approval.

My apartment door has no doorbell, only the building buzzer downstairs. What Ring device actually fits it?

Usually the Peephole Cam. Your door almost certainly has a peephole, since the Housing Maintenance Code requires one in apartment entrance doors, and the Peephole Cam fits through that opening, showing a live view and alerts when someone knocks or rings its button. It runs on a battery, so it needs no wiring in the corridor. What it cannot do is replace the building buzzer at the street door, which stays the owner's equipment. Visitors still buzz up through the intercom, and the Peephole Cam shows you who is standing at your own door once they arrive.

I already put up a doorbell camera outside my rental door and the landlord objected. What are my options now?

Take it down neatly and switch to a version the landlord accepts, rather than wait for them to remove it. Based on the JustAnswer answer above, a landlord can remove a camera installed in a common area without consent. The common compromise is a Peephole Cam, which uses the existing peephole, or an indoor camera facing the inside of your door. If you drilled holes, we can remove the old doorbell and fill them so the door is back as it was. Then, if you still want a hallway camera, a written request naming the device and its view is the way to ask.

I own a small building. Can I keep a Ring on my own door while telling tenants not to install their own?

This exact question was posted on Avvo by a New York landlord, and the general principle is that the owner controls common areas and the building's doors and hallways, so rules about tenant devices in those spaces can usually be set by the owner through the lease or building rules. What happens inside a tenant's own apartment is different. Apply the rule fairly, put it in writing, and if the building is rent stabilized, a guide from one security firm advises documenting your security reasons and notifying tenants before installing cameras. Ask a landlord-tenant lawyer for advice on your lease; we can then install whatever arrangement you settle on.

Buildings, buzzers and common areas

Does the law require my NYC building to have security cameras in the first place?

Not as a blanket rule. One New York security firm's review of the city's Administrative Code found no law requiring every apartment building to install cameras. What the law does require in most larger buildings is an intercom: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a covers class A buildings with eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, and older buildings once a majority of tenants ask in writing, according to an intercom vendor's summary. Cameras are usually an owner's choice, driven by package theft, insurance or tenant requests, rather than a city mandate.

Our building's buzzer is broken. Can a Ring device take its place?

No, and this is one of the most common misunderstandings we hear. Ring's own help page says its Ring Intercom, the add-on that connects to a building buzzer, is not available in the US, so it is not something we sell or install here. A broken intercom is the owner's responsibility. Habitat Magazine reported that courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service under the warranty of habitability, and an intercom vendor's summary says HPD treats a broken intercom as a hazardous violation on tenant complaint; check with HPD or 311 for the current rule. In the meantime, a Ring on your own door still helps you see who reaches it.

Where can a landlord legally put Ring cameras in a building's common areas?

Entrances, lobbies, vestibules, mailrooms, package areas, stairs and corridors are the usual places, positioned to show who comes and goes without looking into apartments. Restrooms, laundry changing spaces and anywhere people expect privacy are off limits under Penal Law §250.45. Posting a notice that the area is under video surveillance brings the system within the Penal Law §250.65 protection that Ambient.ai describes. Ring cameras can work for a small building's entrance and package area; for larger buildings a wired recorder system is often the better tool, and we will say which fits.

Privacy, police and audio

What are the rules on recording audio with a Ring in a New York apartment or hallway?

New York follows one-party consent under Penal Law §250.00, which means a conversation can be recorded by someone taking part in it. A Ring doorbell recording you talking with a courier is one thing; one picking up neighbors' conversations in a corridor that you are not part of is riskier. One New York security firm advises turning audio recording off on cameras in shared spaces, and for corridor and hallway devices we agree. Two-way talk, where you speak to someone at your door, is a different feature from recording, and we show you how to keep one while turning the other off.

Can the NYPD get my Ring footage in 2026, and do I have a choice?

The NYPD announced it would join Ring's Neighbors app, and PIX11 reported that officials would not monitor the app around the clock but would be able to request homeowners' Ring clips. A request is a request: you decide whether to share, and we show you where Ring's request settings live. Police can also seek footage through legal process, which is separate from the app. NYCLU has argued the partnership could conflict with the city's POST Act, so the policy may change; check the Ring app's control settings after any news.

Is Ring's facial recognition on when the camera arrives, and can I turn it off?

According to Smart Home Perfected's Ring privacy guide, Familiar Faces, Ring's facial recognition feature launched in the US in December 2025, is off by default, while Search Party, the feature that uses neighbors' cameras to look for lost pets, is on by default. TechCrunch reported a class action filed in June 2026 claiming Familiar Faces stores images of passersby without consent. At handover we go through each of these settings with you, leave Familiar Faces off unless you ask otherwise, and show you how to turn Search Party off if you do not want your camera taking part.

Wiring, walls and Wi-Fi

I'm tired of charging my Ring doorbell battery every few weeks. Can it be hardwired in an old building?

Often, yes, and it is one of the best upgrades you can make. A Yelp reviewer looking for video doorbell help on the Lower East Side described monthly battery changes as a real nuisance, and that is common at busy New York doors where the camera wakes constantly. If there is an existing bell circuit, we measure the transformer and test the wire; if both are sound, the doorbell runs on it and the battery becomes a backup. If there is no circuit, a new low-voltage run from a transformer is possible in houses and some brownstones, but in a rental it needs the landlord's approval. We tell you which applies after testing, before you pay for the wiring.

Will Wi-Fi reach a Ring on the front door of a prewar building with thick walls?

Sometimes not without help. Prewar walls of 12 to 18 inches of masonry, as one real-estate guide describes them, weaken Wi-Fi far more than modern drywall. We test the signal at the exact spot before mounting. If it is weak, the usual fixes are a Ring Chime Pro or Wi-Fi extender placed between the router and the door, moving the router if you control it, or, for a house or a building owner, a Ring model that runs on a network cable. A device that drops off Wi-Fi twice a day is worse than no device, so we would rather fix the signal first.

Can a Ring doorbell or floodlight go on a brownstone in a landmark district?

It can, but the facade rules come first. The LPC's rule amendments address security cameras and intercoms on facades, the proposed rules would bar many such objects from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and a later rule lets staff approve work that does not damage significant features and is not visible. In practice, a doorbell at the door, wired through the frame, is a smaller question than a floodlight cam above the stoop, and the permit guidebook expects visible camera equipment to be finished to match. We check your address against designation before booking and, where a permit is likely, plan the work around it.

FAQ

New York City Ring Camera Installation FAQ

Why does a Ring install in Manhattan cost more than the same job in Brooklyn or the Bronx?
+

Because the work around the device costs more there, not the device. Manhattan jobs are almost all in apartment buildings, which means doorman-desk check-ins, insurance paperwork, elevator scheduling and paid parking near the building. Each borough has its own Ring price list for that reason, and the Manhattan list includes those access costs as separate lines so you can see them.

How long should a building keep its camera footage before it is overwritten?
+

We found no general New York City rule setting a retention period for private apartment building cameras. In practice it depends on the system: NYCHA has reported 21 days on its newer cameras, and residents on r/AskNYC describe buildings keeping around 30 days. With Ring, how long recordings stay available depends on your Ring plan and settings, so we set it during setup and show you how to download clips you need to keep.

Is my landlord allowed to put cameras up in my building's halls and lobby?
+

Generally yes, in common areas, as long as the cameras do not record places where people expect privacy, which Penal Law section 250.45 forbids. Posting a visible notice brings the system within the protection described for Penal Law section 250.65. A guide from one security firm advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to document their security reasons and notify tenants before installing.

Are New York apartments really more likely to lose packages than houses?
+

Nationally, yes. Security.org's 2026 package theft report found 10 percent of apartment residents had a package stolen in the previous three months, against 3 percent of single-family residents. In New York, deliveries often sit in lobbies or vestibules that many people pass through, which is why a camera on your own door, plus a building camera on the package area, helps more than either alone.

Does whoever installs my Ring need a license in New York?
+

If they do it as a paid business, yes. The city's business portal says a New York State Department of State license is required to install or maintain security systems, and the licensing course provider says that covers security cameras. You can install your own Ring yourself without a license. AESS does its low-voltage work under NYS license 12000287431.

I put up my own Ring and it keeps disconnecting. Will you fix a DIY install?
+

Yes. Most DIY Ring problems in New York come from three things: weak Wi-Fi at the door through masonry, a wired doorbell on a transformer that cannot supply enough power, or a mount position that wakes the camera all day. We test each, fix the cause, and move the device if it is in the wrong spot, rather than simply resetting it.

How long does a Ring visit take from arrival to handover?
+

It depends on the door far more than the device. A Peephole Cam on an apartment door with good Wi-Fi is a short visit. A wired doorbell on an old bell circuit takes longer because we test and sometimes repair the wiring, and a house with floodlight cams and cable runs takes longer again. When you send photos, we tell you what to expect before booking.

My Ring doorbell died. How quickly can someone come out to replace it?
+

We book you within 2 hours of your request, and estimates are available the same day. When the visit happens depends on the schedule and whether a replacement device is on hand, so we confirm the time when we book rather than promise it in advance. If the old unit is under Ring's warranty, we help you work out whether to claim it first.

Should I put up a sign saying the door is under video surveillance?
+

For a business or a building's common areas, yes. Penal Law section 250.65, as summarized by Ambient.ai, protects systems with a conspicuously posted written notice or cameras whose presence is obvious. For a house or an apartment door, a doorbell camera is usually obvious already, but a small sticker costs little and removes doubt.

Will the old mechanical chime in my hallway still ring with a Ring doorbell?
+

Often, yes. Many wired Ring doorbells can keep an existing mechanical chime working, and we test it after installation. Some older chimes or certain models do not work together, and in that case a plug-in Ring chime does the job. We check the chime type during the wiring test so you know before the doorbell goes up.

For a whole apartment building, is Ring better than a wired recorder system?
+

Usually not. Ring works well for one owner watching a door or a small building's entrance, but a building with many entrances, a package room and staff who need footage is better served by a wired recorder system that keeps footage locally under the owner's control. We install both and will tell you which fits after a look at the building.

Can you install Ring outside the five boroughs, on Long Island or upstate?
+

Yes. We install Ring across Nassau and Suffolk counties and the Hudson Valley counties as well as the city. Each area has its own Ring price list, because travel and access differ, and the same process applies everywhere.

If I move out of my rental, what happens to the Ring and the account?
+

Because we set the device up in your own account, it moves with you. A Peephole Cam comes out and the original peephole goes back in. A wired doorbell installed with the landlord's approval can stay if the landlord wants it, in which case you remove it from your account so the next tenant can register it to theirs, or it can come down and the door be restored.
