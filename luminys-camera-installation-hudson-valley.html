The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need Luminys Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley is not one place to put a camera system, it is six counties of very different buildings strung along a river. The Historical Marker Database tells the short version of how that happened: after Henry Hudson's voyage in 1609, the Dutch set up a trading post near the future site of Albany in 1614, Esopus, which became Kingston, was settled in 1653, and the great land grants of the Van Rensselaer, Livingston, Philipse and Van Cortlandt families gave the valley what that marker calls an aristocratic quality. The Hudson River Valley Heritage Area adds that by 1750 most of the region had been settled in a network of small local communities that still survives today. That is still the shape of the place. A Luminys job here can mean a colonial stone house a few blocks from the Senate House in Kingston, a brick row in Newburgh, a riverside village on Route 9W, a hamlet with one main road, an office park in Westchester or a distribution building on a former farm field in Orange County. Each one asks a different question of the camera, the cable and the recorder.

The brand itself is the second reason this page exists. Luminys came out of the sale and rebranding of Dahua USA's North American business, according to the distributor Selectum. SecurityInfoWatch describes Luminys as a U.S.-based subsidiary of the Taiwan-based electronics manufacturer Foxlink, and The Register reported in April 2024 that Dahua, which is on the U.S. entity list, sold its U.S. subsidiary for $15 million to Taiwan's Central Motion Picture Corporation, with dealer emails signed by Luminys System Corp. and Foxlink. The road since then has not been perfectly smooth. In November 2024 a U.S. House select committee wrote to the FCC raising concerns that the sale was designed to get around American restrictions and asking who develops the firmware and software. The FCC then ordered Luminys to explain why its equipment authorizations should not be revoked, and later, as SecurityInfoWatch reported, concluded that Luminys is not an entity identified on the Covered List, withdrew its earlier action and closed the proceeding. Luminys says on its compliance page that its branded products contain nothing from entities covered by Section 889 of the NDAA. For a homeowner that is background. For a Hudson Valley town, school district, contractor on a public job or business with a federal customer, it is the first thing the buyer asks, and we answer it with paperwork rather than reassurance.

The practical side of the brand change matters more on most valley jobs. Many properties still have a Dahua recorder and Dahua cameras that are working, ageing or failing. The sources we read disagree about how well the old and new mix. A St. Louis integrator writes that most existing Dahua cameras are fully compatible with Luminys recorders. A forum installer on IPCamTalk found that new Luminys cameras were not backward compatible with a Dahua recorder, apart from basic ONVIF support. Another poster on the same forum said Dahua firmware cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, and another described few problems with mixed systems except some smart-search metadata from the Dahua cameras not appearing on the Luminys recorder. So when someone in Poughkeepsie or Port Chester asks us whether the old cameras can stay, the only honest answer is that it depends on the model, and we check each one before we quote.

Then there is why people in the valley want cameras at all, and here the local news is more useful than any brochure. In September 2026, Hudson Valley One reported that Kingston police defended the city's surveillance network as residents raised privacy concerns, noting that besides the plate-reader cameras there are 71 more conventional surveillance cameras, and that private business cameras are the bullet-style cylinder type. In June 2026, WRRV reported a tip-jar theft at Alimentari Rossi & Sons at Eastdale Village in Poughkeepsie that was caught on camera, along with an incident at Plato's Closet. News 12 Westchester reported Port Chester police reminding residents to lock their cars after neighbors shared Ring footage of break-ins. AsAmNews reported in February 2025 that State Police were investigating burglaries targeting Asian American residents in Westchester, Dutchess and Putnam counties. And in 2024, WBLK reported Scarsdale police saying burglars had hidden camouflaged cameras in bushes outside homes. None of those stories involves a Luminys system that we know of, and we do not claim any of those businesses as customers. They show what valley cameras are expected to do: give a clear picture that police can use, without pointing at the neighbors.

The building stock is the fourth reason, and it is wider than in any single borough. Newburgh's East End Historic District spans about 445 acres with more than 2,000 structures, most of them brick townhouses, and the city's design guidelines say security equipment should not damage historic fabric and belongs on side or rear facades. Kingston has five historic districts, the Stockade, Rondout-West Strand, Chestnut Street, Fair Street and Ponckhockie, and each needs a certificate of appropriateness before a building permit issues for exterior work. Down in Westchester, Howard Properties reported in May 2026 that office vacancy had fallen to 19.9 percent from a recent peak of 27 percent, that a buyer had taken 660,000 square feet of 1970s office buildings on Route 287 for mixed use, and that the county's newest office building is 37 years old. In Orange County, HGAR reported in 2020 that a 1,015,740 square foot Amazon distribution center and a 1.2 million square foot Medline building were starting construction in the Town of Montgomery. The USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counts 620 farms in Dutchess and 486 in Ulster. Every one of those is a different camera job.

Weather and power shape the job too. Central Hudson serves about 315,000 electric customers from the northern suburbs of New York City to the Capital District, and Orange & Rockland about 300,000 across Orange, Rockland and Sullivan counties and northern New Jersey. The Times Union reported that thunderstorms on June 27, 2024 affected a combined 60,000 O&R and Central Hudson customers, and Central Hudson reported restoring power to more than 19,000 homes and businesses after a slow-moving nor'easter in September 2026. Earth Networks' 2020 lightning report ranked Orange County first in New York, with Ulster, Westchester and Dutchess close behind. A recorder that goes dark every time the lights flicker, or a camera cable that runs bare between two buildings on different electrical services, is a weak point the valley will find. We plan for both.

Licensing is the last piece. The New York State Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and the Mid-Hudson licensing course run for the industry states that this includes security cameras, CCTV and access control, with fines of up to $10,000 for unlicensed installers. The counties then add their own rules for electricians. Westchester requires plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade license, Rockland, Orange and Ulster have electrical licensing laws, and Putnam's Chapter 145 sets up a low-voltage technician license that covers video surveillance cabling at 90 volts or less fed from an outlet a licensed electrician installed. We could not find a county contractor license in Dutchess. What that means for a Luminys job is simple: the camera, network and recorder work is ours under NYS license #12000287431, and any new outlet high on a wall or on a pole is an electrician's job, named as such in the quote.

Why we test before we promise

An installer who tells you over the phone that "all your Dahua cameras will work" is guessing, and so would we be. The sources above were written by integrators and forum users working on their own systems, and they do not agree. The differences probably come down to which camera generations, which recorder models and which firmware each person had, but we cannot know that for your site without looking. So on a migration job we bring the new Luminys recorder to the site, plug it in beside the old one, and connect the existing cameras to it one at a time while the old system is still recording. Within an hour or two we can show you a list: these cameras come across with everything, these give basic video, these need to be replaced. Only then do we finalise which cameras come down.

Staged moves for businesses that cannot go dark

For a warehouse running shifts, a medical office or a store with high-value stock, a weekend with no recording is not an option. A staged move solves that. The new Luminys recorder goes in first and takes over the existing cameras that work with it. Cameras that do not come across stay on the old recorder until their replacements arrive, so nothing goes unwatched. Then the old cameras are replaced in batches, usually starting with the ones covering doors and cash, and the old recorder is retired last, once you have checked that the recordings you need from it are exported. The cost of a staged move is a second visit or two; the benefit is that there is never a gap in coverage.

What happens to the old recorder's footage

The video on an old Dahua recorder does not move onto a Luminys recorder; it stays on the old hard drives. If you have a reason to keep it, such as an insurance claim, an open police report or a staff dispute, tell us before the changeover. We export the clips you need to a drive you keep, or we leave the old recorder powered on a shelf, off the network, until its retention period runs out. If you do not need it, we wipe the drives or hand them to you, your choice.

Think about who you want to identify, not just what you want to see

A camera that shows "someone walked up the drive" and a camera that shows a face you could hand to the State Police are two different cameras in two different places. The first can sit high on a corner of the house and watch a wide area. The second has to be closer to where a person stops, such as the front door, the office counter or the warehouse dock door, and at a height where it looks at a face rather than the top of a cap. Before we visit, walk your property the way a visitor or a delivery driver would and note where they stop, turn or slow down. Those are the points where an identifying camera earns its place, and they are where we start the layout.

Look at the light at the hour you are worried about

Many valley properties are dark at night in a way city lots never are. A driveway with no street lights, a barn yard with one bulb, or a warehouse lot lit only at the docks will look very different on camera at two in the morning than at noon. If you can, step outside after dark and take a phone photo from the spot where you want the camera to look. It tells us whether a camera's own infrared will be enough, whether a colour night camera needs some ambient light, or whether a light needs to go in first, which on a lighting-code village street may itself have rules.

From the Tappan Zee to the edge of the Catskills, the valley holds more building types than any single borough. Each changes three things about a camera job: what the camera is fixed into, how the cable gets back to the recorder, and who has to agree before anything goes on the outside. Brick Row Houses in River Cities

Newburgh's East End, mostly two- and three-story Italianate brick townhouses, and older blocks in Kingston and Poughkeepsie. Masonry fixings, party walls, and design review for anything on the street face.

The Kingston Stockade's Dutch stone houses, including those on all four corners of Crown and John Streets, and older stone houses across Ulster and Dutchess. Thick walls, irregular stone, and commission approval for visible changes.

The houses of Westchester and Rockland villages and the suburbs further north. Wood frame, siding, attics and basements that usually give cable an easy route, with neighbors close enough that aim matters.

Dutchess and Ulster farm properties and the estates along Route 9. Buildings on separate electrical services, long distances, animals and dust, and gates far from the house.

Office Parks and Campuses

1970s and 1980s office buildings on Westchester Avenue's Platinum Mile and along I-287, and research and medical campuses. Drop ceilings, landlord rules, shared risers and decades of old cable.

Warehouses and Industrial Buildings

New distribution buildings in Montgomery, Wawayanda and Wallkill, and older industrial sites such as Kingston's former IBM campus. Tall ceilings, block walls, conduit, and lots that need pole cameras.

On historic brick and stone. In a Newburgh row house or a Kingston stone house, the wall itself is the thing being protected. Newburgh's East End guidelines say installing security features shall not damage historic building fabric. That steers us toward three habits. First, we fix into mortar joints rather than drilling the face of old brick or stone, because mortar can be repointed and a cracked brick face cannot be undone. Second, we keep the number of holes to the minimum the mount needs, and use a junction box behind the camera so the cable enters the wall in one place, hidden. Third, we look at the side and rear facades first, which is where the Newburgh guidelines want security equipment to go, and use the front only when there is no other view of the entrance. In Kingston, City Resolution 156 of 2024 says nobody may change light fixtures, signs, fences, steps or other exterior elements visible from a public street or alley in a district without commission approval. We help you prepare the application, with a photo of the wall, the camera's cut sheet and a marked-up picture of where it would go, before any hole is drilled.

On barns and far buildings. A camera on a barn 300 feet from the house is a distance problem, a power problem and often a lightning problem at once. The usual standard for network cable is a 100 metre, or 328 foot, channel, so a long run may simply be beyond what copper can carry. A barn on its own electrical service has its own ground, and a copper cable between two separately grounded buildings can carry a surge from one to the other in a storm. In a county that Earth Networks ranked first in New York for lightning in 2020, that is not theoretical. So for far buildings we usually recommend fiber between the buildings, with a small switch at the barn end, or a point-to-point wireless bridge where there is clear line of sight, and we explain which and why at the walk-through.

What a warehouse camera has to do that a house camera does not

A camera over a front door watches one person at a time, at close range, in decent light. A camera in a new distribution building watches forklifts, trailers and people moving fast across hundreds of feet, under high-bay lighting that may cycle on and off, from a mount 30 or 40 feet up that nobody wants to climb to again. That changes almost every choice. Aisle cameras need the right lens to see down a long, narrow space instead of a wide room. Dock door cameras need to read a trailer number and see inside the trailer when the door is open. Yard cameras on poles have to cope with wind, snow and the glare of headlights. And the cable has to be supported in conduit or on J-hooks above the racking so a high reach truck cannot snag it. On new builds we prefer to be in before the ceiling is full of sprinkler pipe and ducting, coordinating with the general contractor, because pulling cable around a finished high-bay ceiling from a lift takes far longer.

Office parks: working around tenants, landlords and old cable

Westchester's older office buildings bring a different problem. Howard Properties notes the county's newest office building is 37 years old, and in buildings that age the ceilings above each floor often hold cable from several generations of tenants, much of it abandoned. A tenant adding Luminys cameras usually needs the landlord's approval for anything in common areas, may need to use the building's own risers and telecom rooms, and often has to work outside business hours. We ask for the building's contractor rules up front, send insurance papers before the visit, tone out and label the cable we use so it is not confused with what was left behind, and keep the tenant's cameras on the tenant's own space unless the landlord has asked otherwise.

Rules and practical limits for business camera systems

  • State license for the installer. The NYS Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and the industry licensing course states that this covers security cameras and CCTV. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431. Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, an owner or their own employees installing a system they bought on their own property do not need a license.
  • County rules for electricians. Westchester, Rockland, Orange and Ulster license electricians at county level, and Putnam's Chapter 145 has a separate low-voltage technician license for video surveillance cabling fed from an outlet a licensed electrician installed. Any new outlet for a pole camera or a rack is left to a licensed electrician, and our quote names it.
  • Historic districts. In Newburgh's East End and Kingston's five districts, visible exterior equipment needs a certificate of appropriateness before a building permit issues. We prepare the camera details for your application.
  • Federal procurement. For towns, school districts and businesses with federal customers, we list every device by brand and model, and Luminys advises checking legacy or third-party stock individually for NDAA status.
  • Exits and sightlines. Cameras and their brackets never cover exit signs or reduce headroom on an exit path; in a warehouse they stay clear of sprinkler heads and racking flue spaces.
  • Privacy. Business cameras are aimed at the business's own doors, lots and floor. We avoid views into neighboring homes, and we do not put cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere people expect privacy.

The brand behind the box

Someone told us Luminys is Dahua with a new sticker. How much of that is really true, the hardware, the software or both?

Partly true, and the forum where installers argue about it puts the question as "100 percent rebranded or more like 85 percent." What is documented is that Luminys was formed from the sale of Dahua USA's North American business, and that installers on IPCamTalk describe the cameras as the same quality as Dahua and the Luminys phone app as almost the same as DMSS. What is also documented is that they are not interchangeable: Dahua firmware cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, and some posters found Luminys cameras would not fully work on Dahua recorders. A U.S. House committee in 2024 asked who develops the firmware and software, which is exactly the part buyers care about. For a Hudson Valley owner, the practical answer is that the hardware will feel familiar, but the software, the firmware updates and the support now come from Luminys, and the system should be treated as a Luminys system from day one.

Our Poughkeepsie office has a Dahua recorder and a dozen Dahua cameras. Can the cameras stay up while a Luminys recorder replaces the box?

Often many of them can, and this is the most common way to move from Dahua to Luminys during a migration. A St. Louis integrator writes that most existing Dahua cameras are fully compatible with Luminys recorders, and an IPCamTalk poster said the Luminys NVR runs Dahua cameras fine, with the caveat that some smart-search metadata from the Dahua cameras may not appear. We do not take that on trust for your building. On the day, we connect the new Luminys recorder beside the old one and add your cameras one at a time while the old system is still recording. You get a list of which cameras come across fully, which give video without smart features, and which do not work at all. The ones that work stay on the wall; the rest are replaced in an order you choose, usually doors and cash first.

Going the other way: will a new Luminys camera work on the Dahua recorder we already own, or show in the DMSS app?

That direction is where installers report trouble. An IPCamTalk poster found new Luminys cameras were not backward compatible with Dahua recorders apart from ONVIF support, and another said Luminys cameras would not work on a Dahua NVR even though the reverse did. ONVIF is a general standard that usually gets you live video and recording but not every smart feature. So if you add one Luminys camera to an old Dahua recorder, expect basic video at best, and do not expect the camera's people-and-vehicle detection to drive searches on the old box. If you are adding more than a camera or two, we usually suggest putting the Luminys recorder in first, then adding new cameras to it, so every new camera runs with its full feature set and the app you use is the Luminys one.

Compliance and paperwork

A school district in Rockland asked whether Luminys is NDAA compliant and what happened with the FCC. What can we show them?

We are a camera installer, not a lawyer or procurement officer, so we give buyers the facts and the paper trail and leave the compliance decision to them.

Does whoever mounts the cameras at our Nyack shop need a state license, and could we put them up ourselves instead?

Someone in the business of installing cameras for other people does need a license from the New York State Department of State. The industry licensing course page states that the license covers security cameras, CCTV and access control, that unlicensed installers can be fined up to $10,000, and the Department of State lists the training as four 15-hour modules and one 21-hour module. The exemption matters for you, though: under 19 NYCRR 195.2, no license is needed where the owner bought the system and the owner or the owner's own employees install it on the owner's property. So you can mount your own cameras legally. What you cannot do is hire an unlicensed handyman to do it. We work under NYS license #12000287431, and if you would rather mount the cameras yourself and have us set up the recorder and app, we can do just that part.

Our house is in the Kingston Stockade District. What has to happen before a camera can go on the front?

A certificate of appropriateness from the city's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission, before a building permit for any exterior work. Kingston's Resolution 156 of 2024 says no one may change a property's light fixtures, signs, fences, steps or other exterior elements visible from a public street or alley without commission approval, and the Stockade is one of five districts, with Rondout-West Strand, Chestnut Street, Fair Street and Ponckhockie. In practice that means planning before drilling. We photograph the facade, mark where the camera would sit, supply the camera's cut sheet with its size and colour, and describe how it will be fixed, usually into mortar rather than stone, with cable entering at one hidden point. Often the simplest approval is for a small camera on a side or rear wall that still sees the front path. You or your contractor file the application; we give you everything about the camera it needs.

We own an Italianate row house in Newburgh's East End. Can a camera go on the street face at all?

Possibly, but the city's guidelines push you toward the side and back first. Newburgh's East End design guidelines say security features shall not damage historic building fabric and that security or motion-detection equipment should be located on side or rear facades, with the Architectural Review Commission issuing certificates of appropriateness. On a row house there is often no side wall, so the question becomes how to see the front door from somewhere less visible: from inside a transom or a vestibule, from under the cornice, or from the rear with a camera covering the back entrance and a small, discreet one by the door. We bring options to the ARC application rather than a single fixed plan, so you have a fallback if the first location is not approved. Land, distance and old wire

Can Luminys cameras cover a wooded Putnam property where the barn and the gate are a long way from the house?

Yes, but the link to each far point is the real design question, not the camera. A normal network cable channel stops at about 328 feet, so a barn or gate beyond that needs fiber, a powered extender or a wireless bridge. Fiber is the safest choice between separately powered buildings because it does not carry electricity, which matters in storm season. A wireless bridge needs clear line of sight, and in Putnam's woods that can change when the leaves come in, so we check it with the trees in mind rather than in a bare winter. At a gate with no power, a camera may need a power supply run by an electrician, or a cloud camera with its own buffer. We walk every far point before quoting and tell you which link fits each one, rather than assuming one method works everywhere.

Our Orange County warehouse has an old analog system with coax to every camera. Do we have to rip all that wire out?

Not necessarily. Luminys's own camera wiki makes the point that for many businesses the biggest obstacle to upgrading an analog system is not the cameras but the wire. Pulling new cable to cameras 30 feet up in a working warehouse is often the most expensive part of an upgrade. There are ways to reuse existing coax for network cameras, using adapters at each end, and whether that is sensible depends on the condition of the cable, its length and what the camera needs. We test a few runs first. If the coax is sound and the run lengths suit, reusing it can save a lot of lift time. If the cable is old, damaged by heat or rodents, or has splices nobody can find, new cable is the honest recommendation, and we say so before you commit.

Can a Luminys plate-reader camera read cars coming in off Route 17K at our lot entrance?

It can, if it is placed to the manufacturer's rules, which are stricter than most people expect. Luminys's LPR installation guide says the angle between the camera lens and the edge of the farthest lane cannot exceed 30 degrees. That rules out the obvious spot, high on the corner of the building looking down across the whole entrance. A plate camera usually ends up on a pole or post close to the lane, looking along the direction of travel at a modest height, and covering one or two lanes, not five. At a wide truck entrance we may need two. Headlights, speed and the lighting at night all matter too. We measure the entrance on the walk-through and show you exactly where the camera has to go before anyone digs a footing for a pole.

Living with the system

Aim at your own ground and say so. What keeps the recorder going when Central Hudson or O&R loses power in a storm?

We test this before we leave by switching the system off and on and watching every camera return. If you have a standby generator, we check that the camera closet is on a circuit it feeds.

  • Height and access. Warehouse and parapet cameras usually need a lift; a two-story house soffit needs a ladder.
  • What can be reused. Working Dahua cameras on a new Luminys recorder, sound coax or conduit already in place all reduce the job.
  • Approvals and other trades. A certificate of appropriateness in Newburgh or Kingston, or an electrician for a new outlet, is named in the quote rather than hidden in it.

FAQ

Hudson Valley Luminys Camera Installation FAQ

Does LumiCenter software come with a yearly maintenance fee for our office?
+

According to Luminys's own LumiCenter FAQ, there are no annual maintenance fees for LumiCenter. That makes it a sensible choice for a front desk or security office in a Hudson Valley business that wants several cameras on a large screen without a recurring software bill. We install it on your computer and set up the views during handover.

Do we have to pay a monthly plan just to check the cameras on our phones while we are away from the house?
+

No, not for basic remote viewing with a Luminys recorder on site. A vendor listing for the Luminys R4 series recorder says no subscription is required for basic remote access and that LumiCloud Lite is included at no extra charge for viewing through LumiViewer Mobile. Paid LumiCloud plans are for direct-to-cloud cameras and longer cloud storage.

If our LumiCloud license runs out, are we locked out of our own cameras?
+

Luminys says no. If a LumiCloud license expires, you keep full local access to your cameras and recorders; what stops is the cloud service the license paid for. Luminys also says LumiCloud plans require registration in its MVP program, so we handle that step when we set a plan up for you.

Are Luminys cameras more expensive than the Dahua cameras we bought years ago?
+

Installers on the IPCamTalk forum say the quality is the same as Dahua but the cameras cost more, even at wholesale prices. We cannot compare against what you paid years ago, but our county Luminys price pages show the current installed figures so you can see the real number for your county before deciding.

Will the recorder slow down if we turn on all the smart detection features?
+

One IPCamTalk poster reported that once more features are turned on, the system seems to start lagging a bit. We set analytics deliberately rather than switching everything on: people and vehicle detection where it helps, such as doors, gates and docks, and simple recording elsewhere. That keeps the recorder responsive and the alerts useful.

Will smart search still find people and cars from our older Dahua cameras once they are on a Luminys recorder?
+

Possibly not for every camera. An installer on IPCamTalk reported few problems with mixed systems except some metadata targets from Dahua cameras not being picked up on the Luminys recorder. Those cameras still record, but their footage may not appear in people or vehicle searches. We tell you which ones are affected so you can replace them first.

What happens to recordings on a cloud camera at our cabin if the internet goes out for days?
+

A vendor listing for the LumiCloud direct-to-cloud camera says each camera has a built-in 256GB memory card that acts as a local buffer, holding up to 27 days of video at 720p and 30 frames per second. The same listing describes a 60-day rolling playback license for cloud storage. For a seasonal Ulster or Putnam cabin, that buffer covers most outages.

Can a PTZ camera be mounted on the parapet of our flat-roofed building?
+

Yes, with the right bracket. ADI Global Distribution lists the Luminys LUM-R01 parapet mount bracket for select P-Series PTZ cameras. We check the parapet's condition and height, confirm the PTZ model is one the bracket supports, and plan the cable route through the roof or down the wall before quoting.

Is Luminys the best replacement for Dahua, or should we look at EmpireTech too?
+

Both come up when people leave Dahua, and IPCamTalk has a thread comparing them. We are an authorized Luminys partner, so Luminys is what we install and support on this page. Its main advantage for a property with Dahua already on the walls is that a Luminys recorder can often take over working Dahua cameras, which we test on site.

Who actually owns Luminys today?
+

SecurityInfoWatch describes Luminys as a U.S.-based subsidiary of Foxlink, a Taiwan-based electronics manufacturer. The Register reported in April 2024 that Dahua sold its U.S. subsidiary for $15 million to Taiwan's Central Motion Picture Corporation, with dealer emails signed by Luminys System Corp. and Foxlink. The FCC later concluded Luminys is not on its Covered List.

Police in Scarsdale warned about burglars hiding cameras in bushes. What should we do if we find one?
+

Leave it where it is and call the police, as the Scarsdale warning reported in 2024 involved camouflaged cameras that burglars had placed to watch homes. Your own cameras can help by covering the edges of the yard and the front planting as well as the doors, so anyone placing something is recorded doing it.

Can LumiCenter show the other-brand cameras we already have next to the new Luminys ones?
+

Often, yes. Luminys says LumiCenter supports native Luminys camera profiles, generic manufacturer profiles and ONVIF, which is the standard most network cameras speak. Other-brand cameras usually give live video and recording that way, though not always every smart feature. We test each existing camera before promising it will appear.
