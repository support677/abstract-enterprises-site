The Neighborhood

Why Concourse Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Concourse was built as a boulevard of apartment houses, and that is the first fact behind any camera plan here. Wikipedia draws the neighborhood from East 169th Street in the north to East 149th Street in the south, with Webster Avenue on the east and Jerome Avenue and the Harlem River on the west, and divides it into West Concourse, East Concourse and Concourse Village. It sits in Bronx Community Board 4 and is patrolled by the NYPD's 44th Precinct. The Grand Concourse itself, the idea of the engineer Louis Risse, was begun in 1894 and opened to traffic on November 25, 1909. Once the IND Concourse Line opened under it in 1933, building took off, and by the mid-1930s almost three hundred apartment buildings lined the street. The result for a camera installer is simple to state. Very few properties here are single houses with one owner and one front door. Most are multifamily buildings where the person who wants a camera is not always the person who can approve one, and where the useful places for a camera are shared doors, lobbies, stairs and halls.

The buildings themselves are the second fact. Wikipedia describes most of the Concourse apartment houses as six-story high-class semi-fireproof elevator buildings, mixed with five-story New Law walk-ups and a handful of taller fireproof towers. The LPC's designation report notes that 1000 Grand Concourse is one of only three buildings in the historic district taller than eight stories. The Historic Districts Council dates most of the district's structures between 1916 and 1941, in styles from neo-Renaissance to the Art Deco the street is known for, and Brick Underground, citing the LPC, sets out the sequence: Tudor, Renaissance and Colonial Revival designs through the 1920s, then Art Deco and Modern from the mid-1930s. Postwar brick elevator buildings near 161st Street and the courthouse, and the taller blocks of Concourse Village, fill in the rest. For cameras, that means masonry walls, plaster corridors, original lobbies that owners want to keep, and buildings that were never designed with a cable path from the front door to a recorder. Planning the route is often the biggest part of the job.

The third fact is the historic district. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Grand Concourse Historic District on October 25, 2011, after a hearing on June 22, 2010, and CityLand describes it as covering properties along the Concourse between 153rd and 167th Streets and parts of Walton and Gerard Avenues. Inside it, most exterior changes to front and rear facades need LPC review, and the Commission's own Permit Guidebook names security cameras among the safety equipment it commonly sees, alongside window bars, lighting and intercom panels. That chapter says LPC staff can approve security cameras when the installation meets the criteria in Section 2-20 of the LPC Rules, and it illustrates dome-type cameras finished to match the facade. In 2018 the Commission wrote its practice of reviewing equipment on facades, security cameras included, into its rules. When the district was being considered, CityLand reported that Lisa Lieberman, who owned four buildings in the proposed area, supported preserving its Art Deco heritage but opposed designation. Owners' worries about approvals are not new. In our experience, the practical answer is to treat a facade camera in the district as a small design job with paperwork, not as a quick bracket on a wall.

Entry is the fourth. CBS New York reported a case near East 168th Street and Sherman Avenue, around 2 a.m. on September 23, in which police said a man followed a 50-year-old woman into her building, and she shut her door on him. Following someone through the street door is the risk a well-placed vestibule camera is meant to record. The building records show how often the entry itself is the weak point. Augrented's summary of HPD data lists 411 recorded violations at 750 Grand Concourse, against a Concourse Village average of 102.8, with a bell, buzzer and intercom complaint logged on May 12, 2026; in 2017 the city announced it was working with tenants and advocates on an action to take over operations there. 1228 Grand Concourse shows 150 violations and a similar complaint on March 5, 2026. A camera does not lock a door, and we say that plainly. What it does is show who came in behind whom, and when, which is the question a super, a managing agent or a detective will ask first.

Public cameras are part of the picture too. PIX11 reported that Borough President Vanessa Gibson's office set aside 1.2 million dollars for NYPD surveillance cameras and 500,000 dollars for Sanitation cameras aimed at illegal dumping, and that 161st Street and the Grand Concourse is one of the intersections on the list. On NYCHA property, which in Concourse is patrolled by Police Service Area 7, the City Comptroller's audit of entrance doors found cameras placed by just 53 percent of the 3,538 doors it observed, and found 24 percent of the 767 front doors at Bronx developments standing open. Those numbers describe cameras run by agencies, watching intersections and public housing entrances for their own purposes. They do not send a picture to a private building's super, they were not placed to watch your lobby, and a resident cannot add to them. A private building that wants a record of its own doors needs its own system.

Finally, the people who call. Most Concourse camera requests come from owners, managing agents and boards: a co-op like 1100 Grand Concourse, a condominium such as the 1936 building at 675 Walton Avenue, or a rental building with a lobby and two entrances. Store owners make up the next group, along the retail nodes Wikipedia names on 161st Street around Concourse Plaza and on 165th and 167th Streets, and in the big-box units of Bronx Terminal Market beside the Major Deegan. Tenants call as well, and the honest answer for them depends on paper. In a co-op, the hallway is common space under the board's control, and in a rental the lease and the landlord's policy decide, so we look at what the tenant controls before suggesting anything. Community Board 4's district needs statement puts median household income in the district at 30,900 dollars, and in many buildings the budget is tight. We plan systems that can start with the entrances and grow floor by floor, so nothing in the first phase has to be torn out later.

The owner's building: entrances first, then floors

For an owner or managing agent, the plan starts at the street. Every Concourse apartment house has at least one entrance that matters most, and many have two: the main door on the Concourse or a side street, and a service door at the rear or in the cellar. We map every way into the building first, then the lobby and mail area, then the stairs and elevator landings, then the halls on each floor. That order keeps the first phase useful on its own, because even a building that can only afford the entrances this year ends up with a record of who comes and goes. We also ask where the recorder can live: a locked room in the cellar, the super's office, or a closet off the lobby, with power, some airflow and a way to run cable to it.

The board's building: decisions on paper

Co-op and condo boards make camera decisions in meetings, and a meeting needs something to vote on. We write the plan as a document a board can read: every camera, what it looks at, what it deliberately does not look at, where the recorder sits, how many days of video are kept, and who can see live and recorded footage. If the building is in the historic district, the facade cameras get their own page, because those wait for LPC. Seneca Security's guidance on NYC buildings notes that building-wide security upgrades such as new camera systems may require board approval, and we have never seen a board regret having the details in writing before the vote.

The tenant's apartment: what you actually control

When a tenant calls, we start with the lease, not the camera. Connextivity's summary of NYC rules puts it simply: in a co-op the hallway is common space under the board's control, and in a rental the lease and the landlord's policy govern. Inside your own apartment, a camera on a shelf facing the inside of your door is your own decision. Anything on the door, the frame or the hall wall needs the landlord's or board's written yes. We will not install in common areas without that, and we will tell you so on the first call rather than after a visit.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Installation for Concourse's Architecture

Concourse was built over about half a century, from the first apartment houses after World War I to the postwar elevator buildings near the courthouse. The building types below are the ones the sources describe, and each changes how cameras are mounted, wired and aimed.

Six-Story Elevator Apartment Houses

The street's most common building: semi-fireproof masonry, an elevator, a lobby and one or two stairs. Cable has to climb six floors through a building that was not built for it.

Five-Story New Law Walk-Ups

Built under the Tenement House Act of 1901, with stairs as the only way up. Every floor's landing is a camera position, and the stair is the cable path.

Art Deco Showpiece Buildings

Buildings like 888 Grand Concourse, the 1937 Emery Roth design with its original geometric lobby floor, or 930 Grand Concourse with its wraparound corner windows, where owners protect the original finishes.

Taller Fireproof Buildings

A handful above eight stories, including 1000 Grand Concourse, and the 12-story 1936 condominium at 675 Walton Avenue. Longer runs, more floors, more landings.

Postwar Brick Elevator Buildings

1960s and 1970s buildings near 161st Street and the courthouse, and the taller Concourse Village blocks, with plainer lobbies and often better shafts for cable.

Storefronts in Apartment Houses

Ground-floor stores that, Welcome2TheBronx notes, were not permitted on the Concourse before the 1970s and appeared later, so the store's wiring often shares a building never planned for retail.

Masonry and plaster: where anchors go and where they do not

A Concourse apartment house is mostly brick behind plaster inside and brick, limestone or cast stone outside. The rule we follow on these walls is to drill into mortar joints, not into the face of stone, brick or ornament, wherever a mount allows it. A joint can be repointed if the camera ever comes down, which leaves the building as it was; a hole through a carved stone or a glazed tile cannot be undone. Inside, old plaster on wood or metal lath is often harder and more brittle than it looks, so we drill slowly, use anchors meant for plaster, and put a mount where it lands on solid backing. In lobbies with original decorative ceilings, we prefer an upper-wall position near the corner, which usually gives a better view of faces anyway, over a ceiling mount that would cut into the design. Before any drilling starts, we show the owner or super exactly where each hole will be, with a pencil mark and a photo for the file.

Getting cable from the lobby to the sixth floor

The question we expect most from owners is how cable reaches the upper halls without tearing open corridor walls. There are usually three answers, and we look for them in this order. The first is a path the building already has: an old intercom or cable TV chase, a pipe shaft, or a dumbwaiter that has been closed off, which can sometimes carry low-voltage cable from cellar to roof with only small openings at each floor. The second is the stairwell, where cable can run in a slim paintable raceway along the wall or stringer, painted to match and kept clear of the handrail and the exit route. The third, for a building that genuinely has neither, is a camera network that uses one cable up the building to a small switch on an upper floor, which then feeds the cameras on nearby floors with short runs. Every opening we make between floors is sealed when the cable is through, with rated fire-stopping where the floor or wall is rated.

Landmark-district facades: designing a camera LPC can approve

Inside the historic district, a camera on the front or rear facade is reviewed by LPC, and its Permit Guidebook tells applicants the Commission's staff can approve security cameras when they meet the criteria in Section 2-20 of its Rules. We read those criteria for each job rather than paraphrasing them from memory, because they matter in the details. What we can tell you from the guidebook itself is the direction of travel: equipment that is small, placed where it does not compete with the architecture, and finished to match the surface, with the guidebook illustrating dome cameras treated that way. In practice that pushes us toward a compact dome tucked under an entrance soffit, a lintel or a canopy rather than a large bullet camera on a pilaster, with its cable entering the building directly behind the mount instead of running across the face of the stone. We prepare the photographs, the camera's dimensions and finish, and a marked-up elevation showing each position, so the owner or their filing representative has what the application needs.

Stores cut into apartment houses

Many storefronts on the Concourse's side streets were carved out of ground floors that were built as apartments, so the store's ceiling may be an old plaster ceiling with a dropped grid under it, and the back wall may be the building's original masonry. That changes where the recorder can sit and how cable reaches the door. We run cable above the store's ceiling, not through the building's apartments, take any pass-through to the back room through a single planned opening in the block or brick, and seal it. Outside, the storefront's sign band and the building facade belong to the landlord, and inside the district, to LPC review as well. We will plan the inside of the store around the hours you are closed and leave the outside until the landlord has answered in writing.

A store camera that names someone, not just shows them

NBC New York reported on August 25, 2026 that security video captured the whole 30-second incident in a Bronx bodega shooting; the report's link refers to the Grand Concourse, though we could not confirm the cross street, so we cite it only as an example of what store video is asked to do. A wide camera high in the corner of a sales floor is good at showing what happened and in what order. It is poor at showing a face, because a person in the middle of the room fills only a small part of the frame. So every store plan has two layers. Overview cameras cover the floor, the aisles and the stock-room door so nothing happens out of view. Identification cameras sit at the entrance, just inside the door at about head height and aimed so people walk toward them, and over the counter, where they see hands, cash and the face across from the register. On 161st Street, where the door often looks onto a bright, busy sidewalk, we choose cameras with strong wide dynamic range for the door and test them with the door open at midday, because a bright doorway turns faces into dark shapes unless the camera can handle it.

Recorders, staff access and the network in a small store

Most stores have an owner who is not always there, a manager who opens and closes, and staff who need to see the door but should not be able to delete anything. We set the owner as the only account that can change settings or erase video, create separate users for managers and staff with only the cameras and rights they need, and set the owner's phone to get alerts after closing. The recorder goes in the back office or a locked cabinet, on a battery backup, out of sight from the front door. Where the store's internet connection is shared by the cameras, the card terminal and an office computer, we put the camera system on its own network segment, change every factory password and use the maker's app for remote viewing rather than an open port on the router.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras in Concourse

  • The landmark district covers storefronts too. If your building is inside the Grand Concourse Historic District, a camera on its facade, including above your store, goes through LPC review, and the building owner usually files.
  • The landlord decides outside your unit. We ask for the landlord's or center management's written answer before any camera goes on a facade, sign band, walkway or shared corridor.
  • No general camera mandate. Seneca Security's guidance on NYC buildings notes there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings, and most businesses install them because they choose to.
  • Light comes before the camera. A camera aimed at a dark rear door or a dim stock room gives no usable picture. We check lighting at every position and tell you where light is needed.
  • Fire-rated walls stay rated. Where cable passes through a rated wall, floor or ceiling, we seal the opening with rated fire-stopping material.
  • New circuits are an electrician's job. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable powered from the recorder or a switch. If a store or building needs a new outlet or circuit, the quote says so and names that work as an electrician's.
  • Private places stay private. No cameras in restrooms, changing rooms, staff break areas where people change, or anywhere people reasonably expect not to be watched.
  • Keep cash and card numbers off camera. Counter cameras are aimed to see hands and faces, not the keypad of the card terminal, so the video does not become a record of customers' PINs.

Then we go through the street door and stand in the vestibule with the outer door closed, to see whether a camera there would face people coming in or catch only the backs of their heads. In the lobby we look up first, at the ceiling and mouldings, and down, at the floor, because those are the finishes owners most want left alone. Next comes the cellar, which tells us more than any floor upstairs: where the old intercom and cable TV wires come in and go up, whether a shaft runs the height of the building, where a recorder could live with power and a lock. Then we walk the stairs to the roof door, landing by landing, noting where a camera would see the stair without looking into an apartment. Only after that do we sit down with the owner or super and talk about cameras.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Concourse: Questions Answered

Cameras on landmark-district facades

What goes into the LPC paperwork for a camera on a Grand Concourse Historic District facade?

LPC wants to understand exactly what will be added and where, so the package is mostly specifics: photographs of the facade showing each proposed position, the camera's make, size and finish, how it will be fastened, and how its cable gets into the building. The Permit Guidebook says staff can approve cameras that meet the criteria in Section 2-20 of the LPC Rules, so we design each position against those criteria before anything is drawn. We prepare the camera details, photos and marked-up elevation; the building owner or their representative files, because the permit belongs to the property.

Is a camera on the back of the building, facing the rear yard, treated more loosely than one on the Concourse side?

Not automatically. LPC's own permits page says most exterior changes to the front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require its review, so a rear camera inside the district still goes to the Commission. What often changes is how easy the position is to design, because a plain rear wall rarely has the carved stone and metalwork a front entrance does. We plan both sides with the same care and tell you plainly which positions we expect to be straightforward and which may need more back and forth.

Our 1930s lobby still has its original patterned floor. Does LPC have any say over cameras inside it?

Usually not. LPC's permits page says interior work needs its permit only when the work requires a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself has been designated an interior landmark, as the Bronx General Post Office lobby at 558 Grand Concourse was in 2013. Most apartment lobbies on the Concourse are not interior landmarks, so a lobby camera is the owner's or board's decision. We still treat original finishes as untouchable: no anchors in the floor or decorative ceiling, mounts on plain plaster at the walls' upper edge, and cable kept out of sight.

The front door and the vestibule

After the reported case near Sherman Avenue where a man followed a woman inside at 2 a.m., which camera would show the follower's face?

The one inside the vestibule or just inside the inner door, facing the street door at about head height, so anyone coming in walks toward it. A camera over the outside of the entrance looking down sees the tops of heads, and a lobby camera at the far wall sees two people at a distance. The vestibule camera needs strong low-light performance for the small hours and wide dynamic range for daylight behind the door. We test it by having someone walk in behind a second person while you watch the screen.

Our entrance has an outer door, a small vestibule and an inner door. Where does one camera do the most good?

If the budget covers only one, we usually put it inside the inner door, facing back toward the vestibule. From there it sees everyone who gets in, how they got in, whether the inner door was buzzed or forced, and whether someone slipped in behind them, all with the face toward the lens. A camera in the vestibule itself is a close second and catches people who never make it through. The second camera, when there is one, goes on the lobby side covering the mail area and elevator or stair.

HPD shows open intercom complaints on our building. Should we fix the entry before adding cameras?

Ideally both are planned together, and the door comes first if you must choose. A camera on an entrance whose lock or intercom does not work records strangers walking in, which is useful evidence but not protection, and the HPD records for buildings such as 750 and 1228 Grand Concourse show how often the entry is the complaint. We can plan the camera around the door and intercom you are fixing, so the camera position, power and cable route do not have to change when the new entry hardware goes in.

Wiring old buildings

Is there a way to reach hallway cameras on the upper floors without opening up our plaster corridor walls?

Usually, yes. We look first for a path the building already has, such as an old intercom chase, a pipe shaft or a closed-off dumbwaiter, which can carry cable from the cellar to the top with small openings at each floor. If there is none, the stairwell can take cable in slim paintable raceway. Where both are poor, one cable up to a small switch on an upper floor can feed the cameras on nearby floors with short runs. We show you the route before we start, and seal every opening.

Our building is set back behind a courtyard and a gate. Should the camera watch the gate or the front door?

Both, if you can, because they answer different questions. A camera at the building door facing out across the courtyard shows who walked from the gate to the entrance and lets a face come toward the lens over a long approach. A camera at the gate shows who came in from the sidewalk and when, including people who never reached the door. If you must pick one, we usually choose the building door, because the courtyard gives the camera time to see a face. We keep the gate camera's view off the apartment windows facing the courtyard.

Picture quality in old lobbies and halls

Our Art Deco lobby has large mirrors on the walls. Will they confuse the cameras?

They can, and they can also help. A camera aimed straight at a mirror sees its own reflection and the bright doorway behind it, which throws off exposure and wastes part of the picture. Angled correctly, a mirror can give a second look at someone's face as they pass. We set each lobby camera so the mirror sits off to one side of the frame, check for glare from the entrance at different times of day, and adjust motion detection so reflections do not trigger false alerts.

The hallway video flickers and rolls under our old fluorescent lights. Is the camera faulty?

Usually not. Older fluorescent fixtures pulse with the power supply, and a camera's shutter can catch that pulse as bands or flicker. Most current cameras have an anti-flicker or power-frequency setting that fixes it, and we set it on every hallway camera at installation. If a fixture is failing and flickering visibly, the camera cannot correct that, so we tell the super which fixtures to replace. Brighter, steadier hallway light improves every camera in the building.

Stores and owners with more than one building

How do we keep the counter camera in our 161st Street store from recording customers' card PINs?

By choosing the angle on purpose. The counter camera is placed to see the customer's face and the cash drawer from across the counter, not to look down on the card terminal's keypad. Where a camera cannot avoid the keypad, many recorders let us black out that part of the image with a privacy mask, so it is never recorded. We check the view with your terminal in its usual place, and if you move the terminal later, call us to recheck it.

We own two adjoining walk-ups on Gerard Avenue. Can one recorder serve both buildings?

Often it can, if there is a practical path for cable between them, such as a shared cellar wall or a route at roof level. One recorder in one locked room is easier to manage, and footage from both buildings is searched in one place. Each building can also have its own recorder linked in the same app, which is sometimes simpler when the buildings are separately financed or may be sold separately. We look at the path and the ownership before recommending one or the other.

Our managing agent works from another borough. Can they get alerts only after hours, while the super handles the daytime?

Yes. Most recorders let each user have their own alert schedule. We set the super's account to get alerts during working hours for the areas they manage, and the managing agent's account to get alerts at night and on weekends, perhaps only for the street door, the cellar and the roof door. Both see live video when they need it. Alerts are tuned so a normal stream of residents does not drown out the events that matter.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Concourse

Our build environment could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask boxes for Concourse, so we do not invent quotes from them. These are claims we found repeated in installer blogs and quick online answers about cameras in New York buildings, set against what the record says about this neighborhood and what we see in the field. Cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; none of them knows whether your building is in a historic district or how cable gets to your sixth floor.

"Wired security cameras in New York City must be installed by a licensed electrician."

One NYC installer's cost guide says exactly this. Camera systems run on low-voltage cable, with power carried from the recorder or a switch over the same cable as the picture, and the licensing question that actually applies is a security one, not an electrical one.

New York General Business Law §69-m bars anyone from the business of installing or servicing security or fire alarm systems without a license, while the Department of State's installer application says installing passive, non-detecting security devices does not require licensure. The two descriptions do not line up neatly, and online answers usually pick one.

We stay out of the argument by being licensed: AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and is insured. Where a job needs a new circuit or outlet, that part is an electrician's work, and our quote says so.

"You can't put cameras on a landmark building."

Many owners in the Grand Concourse Historic District assume this, and some quick answers repeat it. The Commission's own Permit Guidebook says otherwise: it lists security cameras among safety equipment it commonly reviews and says its staff can approve them when the installation meets the criteria in Section 2-20 of its Rules.

The guidebook illustrates dome cameras finished to match the facade, which tells you the kind of installation that fits. Since 2018, the review of facade equipment including cameras has been written into LPC's rules rather than left to practice.

So the real question on these blocks is not whether, but which camera, where, finished how, and with its cable hidden how. That is a design job, and it is the part we do before anything goes on a wall.

"In a historic district, only the street front is regulated."

This is a natural guess, because the Concourse front is what people photograph. LPC's permits page says most exterior changes to both front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require its review.

For a Concourse apartment house, that means a camera on the rear wall over the service door or the yard is also an LPC matter if the building is inside the district. It may be easier to design, because rear walls are usually plainer, but it is not exempt.

Interior cameras are different. LPC's review reaches inside only when the work needs a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or touches a designated interior landmark.

"NYC landlords must post camera warning signs under Local Law 50."

One installer's blog states this as settled law, and says NYC does not require tenant consent for common-area cameras. We could not confirm a "Local Law 50" camera-sign rule from a city source, so we do not publish it as a requirement.

What we can say is that a plain notice that an area is recorded is good practice in any building, and many owners post one. It tells residents what to expect and tells visitors the camera is real.

If a firm legal answer matters for your building, ask an attorney or the city before relying on any blog, this page included. We install visible cameras either way.

"NYCHA tenants are only legally allowed wireless cameras."

A competitor page makes this claim. We could not verify it from any NYCHA or city source, and we do not repeat it as a rule.

What the sources do support is narrower. Common areas in NYCHA developments belong to NYCHA, and the Comptroller's audit found cameras at 53 percent of the 3,538 entrance doors it observed citywide. Those are NYCHA's cameras, and a resident cannot commission more of them.

For anything a NYCHA resident wants inside their own apartment, the lease and NYCHA's rules decide. We will tell a resident what we can and cannot do on the first call.

"New city cameras on the Concourse mean buildings don't need their own."

PIX11 reported Borough President funding for NYPD cameras, with 161st Street and the Grand Concourse on the list of intersections, plus separate money for Sanitation cameras aimed at illegal dumping. That is real public investment.

Those cameras watch intersections for the agencies that run them. They do not look into your vestibule, your lobby, your stairs or your store, and they do not send video to your super or managing agent.

If something happens at your door, a detective will ask whether the building has video of it. The answer to that depends on your own system, not the camera on the corner.

"Camera installation for an apartment building costs a set amount per camera."

Cost guides quote per-camera labor ranges and building package averages, and AI answers repeat them as if every building were the same. In Concourse, two buildings with the same camera count can be very different jobs.

A postwar building with an open riser is a short cable pull. A 1920s walk-up with no shaft needs stairwell raceway on five floors. A building in the historic district adds facade design and LPC material before any outside camera can go up.

FAQ

Concourse Security Camera Installation FAQ

Is every building in Concourse inside the Grand Concourse Historic District?
+

No. The district runs along the Grand Concourse between 153rd and 167th Streets and takes in parts of Walton and Gerard Avenues, so many buildings in East Concourse, on the side streets toward Webster Avenue and in Concourse Village are outside it. Some individual buildings, such as the courthouse and the post office, are landmarks on their own. We check your address against LPC's district map before planning anything for the outside of the building.

Does it matter whether our building is in West Concourse, East Concourse or Concourse Village?
+

It matters because the building stock differs. The Concourse and its western side streets hold most of the prewar elevator houses and the historic district. East Concourse has more walk-ups and side-street buildings outside the district. Concourse Village includes taller and postwar buildings with longer cable runs and more floors to cover. We plan around the building in front of us, but the subsection tells us roughly what to expect.

How is a camera plan different for a five-story walk-up than for a six-story elevator building?
+

In a walk-up, the stair is the only way up, so every landing is a natural camera position and the stairwell is usually the cable path. In an elevator building, people bypass the stairs, so the elevator landings on each floor and the lobby in front of the elevator become the key views, and the stairs are covered as secondary routes. Elevator buildings also tend to have shafts or risers that make cabling easier.

Tenants keep propping our vestibule door open. Can the camera system tell us when that happens?
+

Often, yes. A door contact wired to the recorder can trigger an alert when the door stays open past a set time, and many recorders can also alert when a camera detects a door left open in its view. The super or managing agent gets the alert with a clip of who propped it. The Comptroller's audit of NYCHA found a quarter of Bronx front doors it observed standing open, which shows how common the problem is.

Our entrance is framed in ornamental stone or mosaic. Where can a camera go without touching it?
+

Usually under the entrance soffit or lintel, or on a plain stretch of wall beside the ornament, with anchors set in mortar joints rather than the decorative surface. A compact dome in that kind of spot sees the approach well and stays out of the design. Inside the historic district, the position also goes to LPC review. Buildings like the Fish Building at 1150 Grand Concourse show how much owners here value that detail, and we plan accordingly.

On a landmark facade, should the camera be obvious to deter people or kept discreet?
+

On these buildings we lean toward discreet but visible. A compact dome finished to blend with the facade suits the direction of LPC's guidance, and it is still plainly a camera to anyone at the door. The real deterrent and evidence come from the cameras inside the vestibule and lobby, which can be more prominent. A sign at the entrance saying the building is recorded does more for deterrence than a large camera on the stone.

In a law office near the Bronx County Courthouse, where should cameras never point?
+

Not into meeting rooms or private offices where clients talk, not at desks where files or screens are open, and not into restrooms. We cover the street door, reception, the hall to the offices and any file room door, so you have a record of who came and went without recording private conversations or documents. If the office shares a building entrance, we keep the cameras on the office's own space.

Can a camera watch our store's roll-down gate overnight?
+

Yes, usually with a camera mounted inside or just above the storefront that sees the gate and the sidewalk directly in front of it, on low-light settings with steady light from the store or the street. We aim it at the gate and its lock, not across the street at apartment windows. If the storefront is in the historic district or on the landlord's facade, the outside mount waits for their answer.

Our store is on 165th Street, not the big 161st Street strip. Do we need the same system?
+

Probably not the same size, but the same logic. A small store still needs an identification camera at the door, one over the counter, and enough overview to see the floor and the back door. Fewer cameras, a smaller recorder and a shorter cable route keep the job smaller. We size it to your floor, hours and stock rather than to what a large store down the street has.

We are buying a building on the Concourse. What should we check about its camera system before closing?
+

Ask who holds the recorder's administrator password and the app accounts, and get them handed over at closing. Check whether the recorder is actually recording and how many days it keeps, whether every camera works day and night, and whether the equipment is still updated by its maker. If facade cameras are on a historic district building, ask for any LPC approval paperwork. We can survey the system and give you a written report.

How often do NYCHA building entrances in the Bronx actually have a camera?
+

The City Comptroller's audit of NYCHA entrance doors found cameras placed by just 53 percent of the 3,538 doors it observed citywide, and found 24 percent of the 767 front doors at Bronx developments open. Those cameras are NYCHA's to install and run. Residents cannot add to them, so questions about coverage at a NYCHA entrance go to the development's management.

Do you take jobs in buildings that have open HPD violations?
+

Yes. Cameras are often part of how an owner or a new manager starts putting a building right. We will tell you if a violation affects our work, for example a broken entry door that a vestibule camera would only record, and suggest fixing that first. Our camera work does not clear violations itself; that is between the owner and HPD.
