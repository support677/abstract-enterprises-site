The Neighborhood

Why New York City Properties Need Tapo Camera Installation

Tapo cameras are sold as cameras you set up yourself in ten minutes, and in a suburban house with a router in the next room that is often true. New York City is not built like that. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council's housing stock visualization counts roughly 3.7 million homes in the city, more than 80 percent of them built more than fifty years ago, and about 2 million units, over half the total, put up before 1947. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report puts the pre-1947 share at 54 percent of units. The same council analysis finds about 62 percent of units in buildings of six or more apartments, and only just over a quarter in one- and two-family houses. In plain terms, the typical place a New Yorker wants a camera is an apartment door on a shared hallway, inside a building with masonry walls, a building owner or board, and a lot of other people's Wi-Fi in the air. Every one of those details changes how a Tapo camera should be chosen, mounted and connected.

The most common reason people call is the door, and in particular what arrives at it. Security.org's annual package theft report found that apartment residents were more than three times as likely as other people to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent, and noted that apartment dwellers may not be allowed to put doorbell or security cameras outside their units, depending on their landlord's rules. A delivery company blog on New York package theft says many older city buildings lack both cameras and a designated package drop-off area. Gothamist, writing about missing Amazon packages, reported a SafeWise estimate that the total value of stolen packages in New York City is close to $1 billion, and described the "package shuffle" New Yorkers do, including installing doorbell cameras to watch the stoop. The New York Attorney General's 2024 consumer tips on package theft specifically recommend using a doorbell camera or video surveillance to help deter thieves or help police identify them.

The door matters for more than packages. On November 3, 2026, amNY reported that robbers dressed as Amazon workers forced their way into a home near Bainbridge Avenue and East 199th Street in the Bronx, after the victims heard their doorbell ring and went to answer it, believing it was someone they knew. We are not claiming a camera would have changed what happened in that case, and we have no connection to it. We mention it because it shows the question a door camera is really bought to answer: who is on the other side before the door opens. A Tapo doorbell or door camera that sends a live picture to your phone the moment someone rings gives you that answer from the couch, the kitchen or the office. The same idea showed up in a citywide participatory budgeting proposal for the Gowanus Houses, where residents suggested smart door systems that send notifications to residents' phones when someone rings or approaches their door. And because a camera on a door is a camera within arm's reach, mounting matters too: a West Village neighbor was arrested, according to a news report, for allegedly ripping a Ring video doorbell off the wall and walking away with it.

What makes a city install different from a suburban one is that the rules mostly come from the building, not from Albany or City Hall. Connextivity's guide to New York City apartment camera law puts it simply: no New York statute bans a door camera that views a hallway. The co-op and condo law firm Guzov LLC says the same thing about the city, and adds that a co-op's own rules may prohibit a shareholder from placing a video doorbell in a common area. In Brick Underground's Ask a Condo/Co-op Lawyer column, a lawyer said a board resolution banning video doorbells is "certainly an option," especially where the cameras sit in common areas the board controls. Renters face a stricter view from at least one law firm, Outerbridge Law, which says tenants can place cameras inside their units as long as they do not point them outside, and cannot aim them at public hallways or other tenants' doors. These sources do not fully agree, which is exactly why we ask who owns the wall, who controls the hallway and what the lease or proprietary lease says before we mount anything.

The other side of hallway cameras is worth knowing too. A guide to apartment camera laws by Safe and Sound Security notes that landlords have used hallway camera footage to build eviction cases arguing that tenants were not present often enough to count as primary residents, and says the strategy has worked in New York rent-stabilized housing disputes. That is about building-owned cameras, not your Tapo, but it is a reminder that a camera in a shared space records everybody, including you. We set up every camera so it watches what you have a reason to watch and as little else as possible.

Then there is the network, which is where most do-it-yourself Tapo setups in the city go wrong. TP-Link's support FAQ says most Tapo cameras connect over 2.4GHz Wi-Fi, with only selected models, including the Tapo C260, C460 and C840, also supporting 5GHz. Tapo's own setup guide lists network types that are not supported at all: WEP, captive portal networks, WPA or WPA2-Enterprise, and WPA3-only networks. In a building where the landlord supplies Wi-Fi with a login page, or where a tenant's new router defaults to WPA3 only, the camera simply will not join. And internet at home is not a given. The New York City Council Data Team, using Census survey data for 2021, found that 8.1 percent of city households had no internet access, 12.8 percent relied on a cellular data plan only, and 24.5 percent had no cable, fiber or DSL broadband subscription. A Tapo camera can record to a memory card without the internet, but it cannot send you an alert or a live picture without a connection, so the network question is part of the install, not an afterthought.

Finally, there is the brand question, which customers raise with us directly. In 2024 Senator Joni Ernst and colleagues wrote to the Department of Commerce asking it to investigate TP-Link, which the letter notes sells security cameras under the TP-Link and Tapo names. A 2026 review on Smart Home Perfected reports that regulators moved toward broader rules on foreign-made consumer routers rather than a single TP-Link ban, and that in early 2026 the White House reportedly shelved the targeted action. We give customers that context plainly, and we install Tapo cameras the way security-minded people recommend for any internet-connected device: on a separate guest or IoT network so the cameras cannot reach your laptop, phone or work computer.

We also want to be plain about what our research could not find. We looked for Reddit threads from New York City residents about installing Tapo cameras, and for Patch, BKReader and Bronx Times coverage of Tapo installs, and found nothing specific. We found no HPD violation or 311 complaint data about tenant cameras. We found one installer's blog claiming that a New York City "Local Law 50" requires residential buildings to notify residents of common-area cameras, but no other source confirmed it, and we could not verify it against the law itself, so we do not repeat it as fact here. Where this page describes what we do in a building, it describes our practice; where it states a rule or a number, it names the source.

Manhattan: the apartment door is the whole job

In a borough where nearly every home is in a building of six or more apartments, a Tapo install usually means your own door, your own entry hall and your own windows. The hallway outside belongs to the building, and in co-ops and condos the board controls it. Many Manhattan buildings also sit inside historic districts, where the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules discourage cameras on a primary, street-facing façade unless there is no feasible alternative. Old elevator buildings and doorman buildings tend to have long corridors and thick walls between the apartment door and the router, which is why we test the signal at the door before we choose between a battery doorbell, a plug-in camera inside the door or a wired option. The Manhattan price list is higher than Brooklyn's and the Bronx's, because access, parking and building logistics take longer.

Brooklyn: stoops, party walls and walk-ups

Brooklyn has the most even mix of building sizes in the city, so in one week we might mount a Tapo doorbell on a brownstone's vestibule door, put two outdoor cameras on the brick rear wall of a row house, and set up a plug-in camera behind the door of a fourth-floor walk-up. Brownstones and row houses raise the masonry question: brownstone, brick and stucco all take masonry anchors drilled into the stone or brick face, not the mortar joint. Brooklyn Heights, which the Historic Districts Council notes was designated on November 23, 1965 as the first New York City historic district, is one of many places where façade cameras may need Landmarks approval. Walk-ups raise the Wi-Fi question, because a router at the back of a long apartment often cannot reach a camera at the front door.

Queens: attached houses, garden apartments and glass towers

Queens mixes some of the oldest planned housing in the city with some of the newest towers. Forest Hills Gardens, laid out by Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. and Grosvenor Atterbury, was built partly with what the Cultural Landscape Foundation describes as Atterbury's standardized precast concrete panels, a wall that needs masonry fixings and blocks Wi-Fi. In Long Island City, 6sqft reported a 1,194-apartment development of two towers, 46 and 56 floors, on Center Boulevard at Hunter's Point South; glass, steel and concrete towers like those often have plenty of bandwidth but crowded airwaves and doors you are not allowed to drill. In between are the attached and semi-detached houses where driveway and side-door cameras are the usual request.

The Bronx: big complexes and brick corridors

Some of the largest apartment complexes in the country are in the Bronx. Wikipedia describes Parkchester as 171 brick buildings of 8 or 13 stories built by Metropolitan Life between 1939 and 1942, later converted to condominiums. Co-op City, according to its own history page, has 15,372 units in 35 high-rises and seven townhouse clusters. In buildings like these, a Tapo camera is for one family's door, set up under rules written for thousands of families. Across the borough there are also prewar apartment buildings with long, echoing corridors and one- and two-family houses with porches and yards. The amNY report of the November 2026 home invasion near Bainbridge Avenue is a Bronx example of why people want to see the person at the door first.

Staten Island: the house-and-yard borough

With about 83 percent of its units in one- and two-family houses, Staten Island is where Tapo cameras are most often used the way TP-Link designed them: on a porch, over a driveway, at a side gate or above a garage. That brings different questions: weather exposure, how far the router is from the far corner of the yard, and whether a floodlight camera needs a junction box an electrician must wire. The St. Paul's Avenue–Stapleton Heights Historic District, designated in 2004 with about 92 freestanding houses according to the Historic Districts Council, is a reminder that even on Staten Island some front façades fall under Landmarks rules. The Staten Island price list includes the travel over the bridge.

The Network First

Before the Camera: The Wi-Fi a Tapo Has to Live On in a New York Building

Most of the "my Tapo keeps going offline" calls we get are not camera faults. They are network problems that were there before the camera was unboxed: a router at the far end of a long apartment, a building's shared Wi-Fi that the camera cannot join, a new router set to a security mode the camera does not support, or a 2.4GHz band so crowded with neighbors' networks that the camera's video stutters and drops. We sort the network out first, because a camera mounted in the wrong place on the wrong band will keep failing no matter how well it is screwed to the wall.

Why 2.4GHz is both the problem and the answer

TP-Link says most Tapo cameras use 2.4GHz Wi-Fi. That band travels further and through walls better than 5GHz, which helps in a building with masonry partitions. It is also the band every neighbor's router, every older smart plug and many baby monitors use, so in a dense building it can be crowded. In a walk-up where you can see a dozen other networks from your sofa, we pick the least crowded channel the router allows and keep the camera close enough to the router or a mesh node that it holds a strong signal even when the neighbors are busy. For the few spots where crowding is severe and the camera supports it, a dual-band model on 5GHz may be the better choice, provided the 5GHz signal actually reaches the door.

Thick walls and long apartments

One New York renovation company describes prewar buildings as having exceptionally thick walls, often 12 to 18 inches of masonry. A wall like that can stop a Wi-Fi signal almost completely, and a railroad apartment can put several such walls between the router at the back and the camera at the door. We measure the signal at the camera's planned spot with the router in its real place, at the time of day the building is busiest if we can. If it is weak, the fix is usually one of three things: move the router to a more central spot, add a mesh node or an access point near the door, or, in a house or a building you own, run a network cable and use a wired PoE camera instead of Wi-Fi.

A separate network for the cameras

Common advice for any internet-connected device, repeated in coverage of the TP-Link router investigation, is to put cameras, smart plugs and TVs on a separate guest network so they cannot reach your more sensitive devices. We do that as standard on Tapo jobs where your router supports it. The cameras get their own network name and password, your laptops and phones stay on the main network, and the app still works from anywhere because it reaches the cameras through the internet. If you run a business, the same idea applies with more force: the cameras should never share a network with the card terminal or the office computer.

Ask the board or the landlord the right question

If any part of the camera sits in a hallway, on a building door or on the outside of the building, ask in writing and be specific. "Can I put a Tapo video doorbell on the hallway side of my apartment door, attached with adhesive, with the microphone recording turned off and the view limited to the area in front of my door?" gets a clearer answer than "Can I have a camera?" Boards and managers are more likely to agree to a small, reversible device with audio off and a narrow view. If they say no to the hallway, the fallback is a camera inside your apartment looking at the door, which in most buildings is entirely your own business.

Check the router before you buy more cameras

If you already own a Tapo camera that has trouble connecting, do not buy a second one hoping it will be better. Look at the router's settings page or the provider's app, and check two things: that the 2.4GHz band is switched on, and that the security mode is not WPA3 only. Then stand at the camera spot with your phone and check how many bars of Wi-Fi you have. If the signal is weak where you stand, it will be weaker inside a camera housing. Send us a screenshot and we can usually tell you over the phone whether the fix is a setting, a router move or more equipment.

Think about the people the camera will see

A camera at your door will record your neighbors coming and going, the super, delivery workers and your own family. Decide before the install what you want it to watch, how long you want recordings kept and who in the household can see them. Those choices drive which camera we recommend, where we aim it and how we set it up, and they are much easier to get right at the start than to fix after a neighbor complains.

New York packs more kinds of building into one city than almost anywhere, and each changes three things about a Tapo job: what the camera or doorbell can be fixed to, how it gets power and a network connection, and who has to agree before it goes up. These are the building types the public record describes across the five boroughs, and how we handle each.

Prewar Walk-Up Tenements

Four- to six-story walk-ups built before 1929 under the earlier multiple-dwelling laws, as one real estate guide describes them. Brick walls, long narrow apartments, a router at the back and a door at the front. Usually rentals, so the hallway belongs to the landlord.

Prewar Elevator Apartment Houses

Eight- to twelve-story elevator buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, and the larger doorman buildings of the 1920s and 1930s. Many are now co-ops. Thick masonry, long corridors, board rules and contractor sign-in.

Brownstones and Row Houses

Stone, brick and stucco fronts, often in historic districts. The owner usually controls the building, but the street façade may fall under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Stoop doors, vestibules and rear yards.

Postwar Co-op Towers and Large Complexes

Brick and concrete towers and planned complexes such as Co-op City and Parkchester. One family's door inside a building with thousands of neighbors, rules written for everyone, and corridors full of other people's Wi-Fi.

One- and Two-Family Houses

Frame, brick and attached houses in every borough, and the great majority of homes on Staten Island. Porches, driveways, side gates and garages, where Tapo cameras are used much as TP-Link designed them.

New Glass and Concrete Towers

High-rises like the Hunter's Point South towers in Long Island City. Strong internet, crowded airwaves, concrete and steel that weaken Wi-Fi, apartment doors you may not drill, and management rules on anything in the corridor.

Walk-ups: the long apartment problem

The classic New York walk-up apartment runs front to back, with the front door at one end of a hallway and the windows, and usually the internet provider's router, at the other. Between them may be three or four rooms divided by brick or plaster walls. A Tapo doorbell or a camera just inside the door can easily end up at the edge of the router's range. Before we choose a camera, we measure the signal at the door with the router where it actually lives. If the signal is weak, a mesh node in the middle room or a small access point near the door usually fixes it without any drilling, and the node also helps phones and laptops in the front rooms. The landlord question comes next: in most walk-ups the door is the tenant's to use but the landlord's to own, so we keep anything on the hallway side reversible and only with the landlord's agreement.

Prewar elevator buildings: metal doors and long corridors

Apartment doors in many older elevator buildings are heavy, metal-clad and self-closing; HPD's fire safety guidance says buildings with three or more apartments must have self-closing apartment doors. Metal blocks radio signals, so a camera mounted on the hallway side of a metal door has the door itself between it and the router. Battery cameras in that position also work harder to hold a connection. We test before mounting, and in many cases the better answer is a camera inside the apartment aimed at the door, or a doorbell placed where the frame rather than the door carries it. Never mount anything that stops a self-closing door from closing fully or blocks the peephole.

Brownstones and row houses: stone, brick and the Landmarks question

On a brownstone or row house, the owner usually controls the whole building, which makes the job simpler, but the materials are harder. Brownstone is a soft sandstone that can flake when drilled carelessly; brick varies from hard to crumbly; stucco hides what is underneath it. We drill slowly into the face of the stone or brick, not the mortar joint, with anchors sized for a small camera, and we seal around the holes so water does not get behind the mount. If the house is in a historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules apply to the street façade. The LPC's 2018 rules amendments list security cameras among the objects whose installation on façades the commission regulates, and its Permit Guidebook says staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria, with cameras kept off the primary façade where there is another option and kept as small as possible. In practice we often put the front camera inside the vestibule or under the stoop rather than on the stone front, and save the outdoor cameras for the rear, where the rules are usually looser.

Large co-ops and complexes: one door among thousands

In a complex the size of Co-op City or Parkchester, a Tapo camera is one family's device inside a system of rules written for everybody. Many of these buildings are cooperatives or condominiums, and the board or management office decides what can go on apartment doors and in hallways. Brick and concrete construction and hundreds of nearby networks make the 2.4GHz band busy. We ask for the building's rules up front, send whatever insurance paperwork the management office wants, and design around an inside-the-door camera unless the board has clearly allowed a hallway device.

Houses: where wiring options open up

In a one- or two-family house, Tapo's full range comes into play. Wired outdoor cameras can plug into an outdoor or garage outlet; battery and solar models can go where no power reaches; PoE cameras can run on network cable from a basement switch to the front porch, back yard and driveway. Floodlight cameras are different: TP-Link's own instructions mount them on a junction box on a wall or under an eave, which is line-voltage electrical work. The NYC Department of Buildings says an electrical permit is required for most electrical work, including handling electrical wires, in a home or business. We mount and set up the camera; a licensed electrician handles the junction box and the wiring. In two-family houses, we also ask which unit the camera serves and whose router it will use, because a camera on the upstairs network covering the downstairs door is a recipe for arguments.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Audio is a legal question, not a setting. New York's eavesdropping law, Penal Law §250.00 and the sections that follow, is built around consent to record conversations. Recording customers' and staff conversations with a camera microphone carries risk, so on business jobs we leave microphone recording off unless the owner has taken advice and decided otherwise.
  • Private spaces are off limits. Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent. No camera goes in a restroom, a changing area or anywhere similar, whatever the reason.
  • Biometric signs are a different law. The city's biometric identifier law, Local Law 3 of 2021, requires certain businesses that collect biometric information from customers to post notices at entrances, but the city's rules say the signage provision generally does not apply to information collected through photographs or video recordings such as security cameras. Facial recognition features are a different matter, and we do not set them up for businesses.
  • Sharing with the NYPD is voluntary. NYC 311 says businesses with security cameras can register camera locations with the NYPD through NewYorkCityConnect.org and can share live video during emergencies. A Tapo system can be registered as a location; registration does not give anyone access to your account.
  • City help for small retailers may be coming. In June 2026 the City Council announced Int. 553-A, which would require Small Business Services to set up a program giving small retailers financial help to buy and install security technology, and Int. 623-2026 would create a pilot installing cameras and plexiglass in certain small businesses. Both were proposals, not programs, when we checked; ask SBS before you count on them.
  • Storefront gates and sightlines. A 2009 city law set a July 1, 2026 deadline for roll-down gates on business and retail buildings to be at least 70 percent transparent. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the Council unanimously voted to repeal that deadline, with the bill going to the mayor. Either way, a camera behind a solid gate sees nothing at night, so we plan night views around the gate you actually have.
  • Cords stay out of walls. National Electrical Code §400.12, as EC&M explains it, does not allow flexible cords to be hidden inside walls or run through holes in walls. A plug-in camera's cord stays on the surface, in a raceway or cord cover; where power is needed inside a wall, that is electrician work.

Field Notes: Standing in the Doorway With a Phone Before Anything Goes on the Wall

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's apartment, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at the start of a Tapo job in the kind of home New York is full of: a long apartment, the router in the back bedroom, a metal door onto a shared hallway, and a new Tapo box on the kitchen counter.

We leave the camera in its box. We ask who rents or owns, and whether the building has said anything about door cameras. We stand where the camera would go and run a Wi-Fi test on a phone, first with the door open, then with it closed, because a metal door can cut the signal in half. We look at the router: which bands are on, what security mode it uses, whether the provider has locked it. We hold the phone's camera where the Tapo would sit and look at the picture together, so you can see exactly whose doors and windows it would catch. We look for the nearest outlet and how a cord would reach it without crossing a doorway. Only when the signal, the view, the power and the permission all check out do we open the box and pair the camera, on your own phone and your own account. That routine is what keeps a Tapo working in a city apartment a year later, not just on the day it goes up.

Your Questions Answered

Tapo Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Hallways, neighbors and building rules

Can I mount a Tapo camera in the hallway outside my apartment, or just outside my front door?

Under city and state law it is not banned outright: Connextivity's guide says no New York statute bans door cameras that view a hallway, and Guzov LLC says the city has no law prohibiting doorbell cameras in apartment hallways. The hallway itself, though, belongs to the building, and co-op rules may prohibit video doorbells in common areas, while a landlord can treat a device on a hallway wall as an alteration it never approved. A JustAnswer legal Q&A on a Brooklyn apartment put it plainly: for common areas the landlord owns, you need permission, and a camera installed without consent can be removed. So the honest answer is that a camera on your own door is often possible with the building's agreement, and a camera on the hallway wall almost always needs it. We ask you to get that agreement in writing, and if it is not given we set up a camera inside your door instead.

My Tapo doorbell will see the apartment door across the hall. Does that put me on the wrong side of the law?

Connextivity's reading is that no New York law dictates how a camera on your own door has to be aimed, and that it is a building-rules question rather than a criminal one. Outerbridge Law takes a stricter view for renters, saying tenants cannot point cameras at public hallways or other renters' doors. Because the sources disagree, we set every door camera up to see as little of your neighbors' doors as the model allows: the narrowest useful angle, the camera tilted toward your own doormat, and privacy masking over the neighbor's door where the model supports it. That approach keeps you on the safe side of both views and gives your neighbors and your building far less to object to.

Our co-op board is talking about banning video doorbells. Can a board actually do that?

Yes, it appears so. In Brick Underground's Ask a Condo/Co-op Lawyer column, a lawyer said a board resolution banning video doorbells is "certainly an option," especially where the cameras are in common areas the board controls, and Guzov LLC notes that a co-op's rules may prohibit shareholders from placing video doorbells in common areas. If your board is considering a ban, the most useful thing you can do is propose a narrower rule: devices allowed on the shareholder's own door, audio recording off, a limited field of view, and no permanent alteration. If a ban passes, a camera inside the apartment watching the door is usually still yours to install, and we can set that up so you still get an alert and a picture when someone knocks.

I rent a rent-stabilized apartment. Is there any downside to having cameras in or near the hallway?

There is one worth knowing about, although it concerns building cameras rather than yours. Safe and Sound Security's guide to apartment camera laws says landlords have used hallway camera footage to build eviction cases arguing that tenants were not present often enough to qualify as primary residents, and that the strategy has worked in New York rent-stabilized disputes. Your own Tapo camera records to your own memory card and your own account, so nobody else sees it unless you share it. Our advice is simply to keep your cameras aimed at your door, keep the recordings in your control, and remember that any footage you hand over to anyone, including a landlord, can be used for more than the reason you shared it.

Should the Tapo microphone stay on for a camera that faces a shared hallway?

We set it to off. Connextivity notes that whether a camera passively capturing hallway audio counts as unlawful eavesdropping under New York law has never been settled cleanly, which is why audio is usually left off. Video of your door is one thing; recordings of your neighbors' conversations as they walk past are another, and they are the part most likely to cause trouble with a board, a landlord or a neighbor. Two-way talk, where you press a button to speak through the doorbell to a courier, is a different feature from recording, and we explain on the day which settings on your model control which. Inside your own apartment, audio is your choice.

Wi-Fi, walls and the network

Which Wi-Fi band will my Tapo use, and why does it matter in a crowded building?

TP-Link's support FAQ says most Tapo cameras connect over 2.4GHz Wi-Fi, and that selected models, including the Tapo C260, C460 and C840, also support 5GHz. In a city building, 2.4GHz is the band that gets through walls best, but it is also the band every neighbor's router is using, so it can be crowded. If your router has 2.4GHz switched off, most Tapo cameras will not see it at all. Before mounting, we check that 2.4GHz is on, pick a less crowded channel where your router allows it, and confirm a strong signal at the camera spot. For a dual-band model in a spot that a strong 5GHz signal actually reaches, we may use 5GHz instead.

Our building gives us Wi-Fi with a sign-in page. Can a Tapo camera join it?

Very likely not. Tapo's setup guide lists captive portal networks, the kind that show a login or welcome page before you can browse, as not supported, along with WEP, WPA and WPA2-Enterprise and WPA3-only networks. That rules out most building-supplied Wi-Fi in new rentals, many dormitory and office networks, and hotel-style setups. The fix is to give the cameras their own network: your own router or access point on your own internet service. In a building where you cannot get your own service, we talk through whether the cameras will be useful recording to a memory card without alerts, before you spend more money on them.

Will Wi-Fi from the back bedroom reach a camera at the front door through prewar masonry walls?

Often not well. One New York renovation firm describes prewar buildings as having walls often 12 to 18 inches of masonry, and a long apartment can put several of them between the router and the door. We test the actual signal at the door with the door closed, because metal-clad doors weaken it further. If it is weak, the usual fixes are moving the router to a more central room, adding a mesh node or small access point halfway along the apartment, or, in a house you own, running a network cable and using a wired PoE camera. We would rather add one mesh node than mount a camera that drops every evening when the building gets busy.

Wiring and power

What cable does a wired PoE Tapo camera need, and do I have to buy a recorder too?

Tapo's own PoE installation guide says to use Cat5e or Cat6 network cable. We use Cat6 for new runs, terminated and tested end to end, because a cable that tests clean on day one saves a lot of guessing later. The same Tapo guide addresses whether a PoE camera can be installed without a recorder; a PoE camera can take its power and data from a PoE switch or injector, and on many models it can record to its own memory card, so a recorder is a choice rather than a must. A recorder makes sense when you want all cameras recording to one place, longer storage, or footage that survives a camera being stolen. We check which recording options your chosen models support and put the recorder, if any, on the written quote.

Can a Tapo floodlight camera go over my garage or back door without an electrician?

Not in most cases. TP-Link's instructions mount its floodlight cameras on a junction box on a wall or under an eave, which means connecting to the house's line-voltage wiring, not plugging into an outlet. The NYC Department of Buildings says an electrical permit is required for most electrical work, including handling electrical wires, in a home or business. Our work is the camera, the mount position, the network and the app; the junction box and the wiring belong to a licensed electrician. If there is already a working outdoor light fixture on a box in the right spot, the electrician's part may be small. If there is not, a plug-in outdoor camera or a battery or solar model avoids the electrical work entirely.

Permits, licenses and landmarks

Do I need a Department of Buildings permit to put Tapo cameras up in my New York building?

For a typical home camera that plugs in, runs on a battery or uses low-voltage network cable, one installer's guide states that most residential camera installations need no permit, and we found no source saying otherwise for that kind of work. Permits come in when the job touches electrical wiring: the Department of Buildings says an electrical permit is required for most electrical work, which is why floodlight and hardwired fixtures go to a licensed electrician. A separate low-voltage rule, AC 28-105.4.7, is cited by one New York low-voltage contractor as taking effect on December 21, 2025. Building rules, board approvals and Landmarks permits are separate questions from DOB permits, and in practice they are the ones that stop more city camera jobs.

Does whoever installs my Tapo cameras need a New York State license?

If they do it as a business, yes. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a license from the Department of State to engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems, and the city's business portal describes the same state license for anyone who installs or maintains security systems. A homeowner putting up their own camera does not need a license; a paid installer does. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431, and we are happy to send the license number and a certificate of insurance to your board or managing agent before the visit. Ask any installer you are considering for theirs.

Our brownstone is in a historic district. Does a Tapo camera on the front need Landmarks approval?

It may. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2018 rules amendments list security cameras among the objects whose installation on façades and areaways the commission regulates, and the proposed rules would prohibit many such objects on a primary façade unless there is no feasible alternative, with the device kept as small as possible. The LPC Permit Guidebook's health-and-safety chapter says staff can approve cameras that meet the criteria in the rules. The commission does enforce this: its July 16, 2013 calendar lists an application to legalize security cameras and an intercom installed without permits at a rowhouse at 52 West 76th Street. We usually suggest a camera inside the vestibule or under the stoop, or on the rear, which avoids the primary façade, and if the front is the only option we help you prepare the application details.

The TP-Link brand question

Is TP-Link being banned in the US, and would my Tapo app stop working if it were?

There was no ban when we reviewed this page. A 2024 letter from Senator Joni Ernst and colleagues asked the Commerce Department to investigate TP-Link, noting that it sells security cameras under the TP-Link and Tapo names. A 2026 review on Smart Home Perfected reports that regulators moved toward broader rules on foreign-made consumer routers rather than a single TP-Link ban, and that in early 2026 the White House reportedly shelved the targeted action. Nobody can promise what any government will do in future, and we found no reliable source on what would happen to the app under a hypothetical ban. What we can do is install the cameras so they record locally to a memory card where the model allows, sit on their own separate network, and use a strong, unique password on your account, so your setup is as resilient and as contained as possible whatever happens.

"Tapo is plug-and-play, so nobody in the city needs an installer."

For one indoor camera on a shelf a few feet from the router, that is often true, and we tell people so. Tapo cameras are designed to be set up from a phone.

The installer's value is not pressing the pairing button. It is knowing whether the spot is allowed, whether the signal will hold at 9 p.m. when the building is busy, and how to fix the mount without damaging stone, brick or a self-closing door.

"Any home Wi-Fi network will work with a Tapo camera."

In New York, that hits two common situations hard: new rentals and dorm-style buildings where Wi-Fi comes with a sign-in page, and new routers that default to the newest security mode or have the 2.4GHz band switched off.

We check the band and the security mode before we mount anything, and where the network cannot be changed we set up a separate camera network rather than weakening your main one.

Our Installation Process

Every Tapo job we do in the five boroughs follows the same eight steps, whether it is one doorbell on a Bronx apartment door or a dozen PoE cameras on a Staten Island house and garage. The order matters most in apartments, where skipping the permission or network step is the usual reason a camera comes down a month later.

1. Tell us the building and the door

The building type, the floor, whether you rent or own, which door, window or wall the camera is for, and photos of that spot and your router. We match the job to your borough's Tapo price list before you book.

2. Check whose wall it is

Lease, proprietary lease and house rules for anything outside the apartment, board or landlord agreement in writing, and Landmarks status for any façade camera.

3. Test the network

The Wi-Fi band and security mode, a check for unsupported login-page, enterprise or WPA3-only networks, and a signal test at every camera spot with doors closed.

4. Choose models, power and mounts

Plug-in, battery, wired, doorbell or PoE for each spot, and the right mount: adhesive, screws, masonry anchors or no drilling at all.

5. Mount and power

Mounts fixed into the stone, brick or framing face; Cat5e or Cat6 to PoE cameras; plug-in cords kept on the surface; junction-box work handed to a licensed electrician.

6. Set up the app on your own account

Every camera added under your own TP-Link account, microSD cards formatted in the camera, and the cameras placed on a separate guest or IoT network.

7. Aim, mask and set audio

Each view agreed with you, privacy masking over neighbors' doors and windows where the model supports it, and microphone recording off on any camera facing a shared hallway.

8. Test and hand over

Alerts tested with your phone off the home Wi-Fi, a recording played back, cables labeled, and a written sheet of models, locations and settings left with you.

FAQ

New York City Tapo Camera Installation FAQ

As a renter, can I put a Tapo doorbell or camera on my own apartment door?
+

Often, with the landlord's agreement. Tapo says its apartment doorbells mount with the included adhesive or screws, so a reversible adhesive mount is usually possible on a door. The door and hallway still belong to the building, and Outerbridge Law takes the view that renters should keep cameras inside their units and not aim them at hallways or other doors. Ask your landlord in writing first. If the answer is no, an indoor camera just inside your door gives you a picture and an alert when someone knocks, without touching anything outside your apartment.

What happens if one of my cameras ends up looking into a neighbor's window?
+

Fix it as soon as you notice. New York Penal Law 250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent, and a view into a neighbor's home is exactly what invites a complaint or a police call. Re-aim the camera, narrow its view, or use the privacy masking feature where your model has it. When we install, we check every view on a phone together with you and mask or re-aim anything that shows a neighbor's window before we leave.

Can my neighbors complain about my door camera, and what usually happens if they do?
+

Yes, they can complain to the board, the landlord or management, and in a building those complaints carry weight because the hallway is a shared space. The usual outcomes are a request to re-aim the camera, turn off audio or remove a hallway device. Building management can regulate hallway-facing cameras that interfere with other residents' privacy. Setting the camera up narrowly from the start, audio off, view on your own door, nothing permanent, is the best way to avoid the complaint altogether.

Is a co-op allowed to put outdoor cameras around the building that see shareholders' doors and windows?
+

A Justia legal Q&A from April 2026 on New York co-ops answered that building video surveillance is generally legal as long as the camera is not aimed into the apartment when the apartment door is open. That is about the building's own cameras, which the board controls, not a shareholder's Tapo. If a building camera looks into your home, raise it with the board in writing. For your own cameras, the same principle applies in reverse: aim them at what you have a reason to watch, not into anyone else's home.

Are NYC apartment buildings required to have security cameras?
+

We found no general city or state rule requiring private apartment buildings to have cameras. What the law does require in many buildings is an entrance intercom: under Multiple Dwelling Law Section 50-a, apartment buildings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have an intercom at the main entrance. That is why many tenants add their own Tapo camera for their own door. If your building has cameras, they belong to the owner, and access to their footage is the owner's call.

How much does it cost to have Tapo cameras installed in New York City?
+

It depends on the number and type of cameras, the wall and mount, how the cameras get power, whether the Wi-Fi needs help to reach them, and which borough you are in. We price every job from our published Tapo price list for that borough, line by line, and the full NYC list is on our Tapo prices page. Send a photo of each camera spot and your router label and we will tell you which lines apply before you book.

Our building uses a smart intercom app and key fobs. Does that add any NYC rules for my own camera?
+

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to building owners who use smart access systems such as key fobs, phone apps and biometric entry, and requires them to give tenants a data retention and privacy policy. HPD's description applies it to those building entry systems, not to a tenant's own Tapo camera. Your camera is yours and its footage is on your account. The building's app and fobs remain the owner's system, and we do not connect your Tapo to them.

How does a Tapo doorbell work on an apartment door that has no doorbell wiring?
+

Tapo makes battery-only and wired-and-battery models, and says its apartment doorbells mount with the included adhesive or screws. A battery doorbell needs no wiring: it sits on the door or frame, connects to your Wi-Fi, and sends a live picture and alert to your phone when someone rings or approaches. The battery needs recharging from time to time. Because it relies on Wi-Fi, we test the signal at the door before mounting, especially through a metal-clad door.

Can a Tapo camera be set up without the Tapo app or a TP-Link account?
+

TP-Link's support FAQ addresses this question directly, and in our experience setup is done through the Tapo app with a TP-Link account. On our jobs we set the cameras up on your own account, under your own email and password, never ours, so you control who can see them. If you want to share access with family or a manager, we use the app's sharing feature rather than handing out your login. Some models offer extra options for connecting to other recording equipment; we check your model before promising any.

If the building's internet or my Wi-Fi goes down, does the camera keep recording?
+

On models with a microSD card and power, the camera can generally keep recording to the card, but you will not get alerts or live view on your phone until the connection comes back, and anything relying on cloud storage stops. That is one reason we fit memory cards as standard. If the power goes out too, a plug-in camera stops completely; battery models keep running for a while, and wired PoE cameras keep running if the switch has battery backup.

Do Tapo cameras need a monthly subscription to be useful?
+

Not for basic recording on most models. Many Tapo cameras record to a microSD card in the camera, which costs nothing per month, and you can watch live and play back recordings in the app. TP-Link also sells an optional cloud plan for features such as cloud storage of clips. We set the cameras up to record locally by default and explain what the optional plan adds for your model, so you can decide whether it is worth paying for.

Is it legal to use my own camera to catch package thieves in my apartment building?
+

A camera on your own door or inside your unit watching where packages land is common, and the New York Attorney General recommends doorbell cameras and video surveillance to help deter package thieves or help police identify them. The same building rules apply as for any door camera: the hallway and lobby belong to the building, so a camera there needs permission. If something is stolen, report it to the police, save the clip from the app or card, and give it to the investigating officer.
