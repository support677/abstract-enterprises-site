The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Properties Need Alarm Installation

Park Slope runs down the western side of Prospect Park, from Prospect Park West to Fourth Avenue and from Flatbush Avenue south to the Prospect Expressway. Wikipedia's article on the neighborhood describes it in three parts, North Slope, Center Slope and South Slope, with Fifth and Seventh Avenues as the main shopping streets and the east–west side streets lined with brownstones and apartment buildings. Most of the eastern half sits inside the Park Slope Historic District, which the article calls the largest landmark neighborhood in New York, with 2,575 buildings across about forty blocks.

That housing is also what makes the neighborhood a particular kind of alarm job. A Park Slope row house is tall and narrow. The parlor floor, where the front windows are biggest and the valuables usually sit, is one floor up from the sidewalk. The bedrooms are two or three floors higher. The back of the house faces a long run of gardens, rear extensions and fire escapes that nobody on the street can see. Many houses were split into flats decades ago; many still have a family upstairs and a tenant on the garden floor. Apartment buildings on Prospect Park West and the side streets, and newer ones on Fourth Avenue, add lobbies, staff on some shifts and key fobs.

The break-ins reported here follow the shape of the day. In one series the Brooklyn Paper covered, a burglar forced apartment door locks near Seventh Avenue and Lincoln Place at about 3:30 in the afternoon, on President Street near Fifth Avenue at ten past noon, and tried and failed on Park Place near Flatbush Avenue at 8:45 in the morning. In another week the paper listed a Union Street apartment entered through a bedroom window between noon and seven, and a 12th Street apartment burglarized between 10:30 in the morning and 1:45 in the afternoon. Those are weekday, empty-house break-ins.

The other clock runs at night. In April 2015, DNAinfo reported that a couple near Eighth Street and Prospect Park West woke to find their back door open after a burglar came in between midnight and four in the morning; the husband had heard a thump and gone back to sleep. In May 2016 the same site reported a man standing on the ledge outside a third-floor bedroom window on Eighth Avenue near Carroll Street at about 5:30 a.m., where a 61-year-old woman woke up and saw him. In April 2026, Hoodline described NYPD video of a man smashing the glass of a building's front door on Seventh Avenue at 3:33 a.m., reaching in to unlock it and walking inside. Those are break-ins into houses that were full of sleeping people.

Park Slope facts that shape an alarm design

  • The largest historic district in the city. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Park Slope Historic District on July 17, 1973 with 1,948 buildings, extended it on April 17, 2012 with 613 more and on April 12, 2016 with another 292, according to New York YIMBY's report on the 2016 vote. Wikipedia gives the combined district as 2,575 buildings; the two sources count differently, and we quote each as published.
  • Row houses and mid-size apartment buildings. Wikipedia describes most of the neighborhood as row houses and six-to-eight-story apartment buildings, with Fourth, Fifth and Seventh Avenues carrying apartments over ground-floor shops.
  • Families in tall, narrow houses. NYC Health's 2018 community profile, quoted in the same article, counted 18 percent of Community District 6 residents aged 0 to 17 and 46 percent aged 25 to 44: a lot of working parents and school-age children, which is why so many houses really are empty on weekdays.
  • One precinct. Park Slope is patrolled by the 78th Precinct, at 65 Sixth Avenue. The precinct recorded 181 burglaries and 553 grand larcenies in 2019, the year Wikipedia's crime summary cites.
  • Rises and spikes. In April 2017, CBS New York reported a more than 50 percent rise in Park Slope burglaries that year and a building off Sterling Place entered by fire escape; a month later News 12 put the year-to-date count at 61, against 39 at the same point in 2016.
  • Two ZIP codes. 11217 north of Union Street and 11215 south of it, which matters when a monitoring center is given the address.

Park Slope households that ask us for an alarm almost always ask about the street door next, because that is where both the delivery problem and the 3 a.m. problem begin. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Park Slope's Architecture

Park Slope was built mostly between the Civil War and the First World War, then partly rebuilt over the last fifty years. Wikipedia credits the Prospect Park era with the mansions and row houses of the eastern blocks, and the 1960s and 1970s with the renovation wave that turned rooming houses back into one- and two-family homes. Each building type below takes an alarm differently.

Italianate and Neo-Grec brownstone rows

CityLand's report on the 2012 extension describes an Italianate row from 1869 to 1875 and more than two hundred simpler Neo-Grec row houses between Seventh and Eighth Avenues. Stoop to a parlor floor, garden floor below, two or three bedroom floors above.

Prospect Park West houses and the Gold Coast

Wikipedia dates the large Victorian mansions on Prospect Park West to the 1880s and 1890s. Wide houses with several street-facing windows per floor, deep cellars and service doors at the side or back.

Prewar apartment houses

From the Emery Roth building at 35 Prospect Park West, opened in 1929, to the six-to-eight-story buildings on the side streets: elevator lobbies, staff on some shifts, and apartments whose only door is on an inside corridor.

Houses divided into flats

Brownstones converted to two, three or four apartments, with a shared vestibule, one stair and separate doors on each landing. Each household can have its own system, or the owner's floors can be one area of a split panel.

Newer buildings on Fourth Avenue

Glass-and-panel buildings with lobbies, package rooms, management-run fobs and apartments on many floors. The apartment alarm starts at your own door and works with whatever the building controls.

Apartments over Fifth and Seventh Avenue shops

Walk-ups above storefronts, with a street door beside the shop and rear windows facing the gardens of the side streets. The store and the apartments keep separate systems and separate sirens.

Method notes by building type

Historic district houses. Inside the district nothing we install has to show from the street; the AI reality check below explains how the woodwork is handled.

Tall row houses and the distance to the top floor. A four-story brownstone puts the roof hatch and the top-floor windows forty feet or more above a panel in the cellar, through brick and plaster. We put the panel on the parlor or second floor, near the middle of the house, and test every wireless device from its final spot before the cover goes on. Where an earlier alarm left wire in the walls, we use it.

Rear extensions and back windows. Many Park Slope houses have a one- or two-story rear extension whose flat roof sits just below a back bedroom window, and many have a fire escape across the back of an upper floor. Both put windows within reach that the street never sees. Those windows get contacts, and the rooms behind them get glass-break detection, because they are the ones that matter most while you sleep.

Divided houses. In a brownstone with a rental floor, the owner's floors, the tenant's apartment and the shared vestibule are three different problems. We usually split the panel into two areas, give the tenant control of their own door and windows only, and leave the street door to the household that pays for the alarm, with the tenant's agreement written down.

Park Slope owners who open walls for an alarm often run data cable at the same time, to a home office on the top floor or a camera at the back door. Our structured cabling in Park Slope can go in on the same visit, so the plaster is opened once.

Two Clocks

Why a Park Slope Alarm Is Planned Around the Time of Day

Most alarm quotes begin with a floor plan: count the doors, count the windows, add a motion detector. Ours in Park Slope begin with a clock. We ask when the house is empty and when it is asleep, because the reported break-ins here fall into those two windows and each one asks something different of the system.

The chart below places every Park Slope break-in we could pin to a time from published reports. It is a small sample, and it is not police data; it is the reporting we found, plotted honestly. But the shape is clear enough to design around. The daytime entries cluster between late morning and mid-afternoon, on weekdays, through doors whose locks were forced or windows left open. The night entries cluster between midnight and dawn, through a back door, a ledge outside a bedroom and the glass of a front door.

The two groups also differ in what the burglar expected to find, as the comparison below sets out.

The empty-house clock: weekdays from the school run to pickup

Park Slope's weekday pattern is predictable to anyone who stands on a corner for a morning. Strollers and scooters head toward the schools, commuters head toward the F and G on Ninth Street or the 2 and 3 under Flatbush Avenue, and by nine many side streets are quiet. NYC Health's community profile counts 18 percent of the district's residents as children and 46 percent as adults between 25 and 44, so the household that leaves together and comes back in the evening is common here.

For those hours the alarm needs to be fully armed, with no gaps for convenience. That means every door, every reachable window, interior motion on every floor and the roof hatch, in away mode, with an entry delay only on the door the family actually uses to come home. It also needs a reliable way to get armed in the first place, because the most common daytime failure we see is not a sensor that misses a burglar. It is a system nobody switched on because the last person out was rushing for the train.

We handle that with the household's own schedule. The app sends a reminder if the house is still disarmed at an hour you choose after the last phone has left the area, and many panels can arm themselves in away mode at a set time on weekdays if nobody cancels. We set that up only where it fits the family.

The sleeping-house clock: midnight to dawn, with the family upstairs

The night pattern is harder, because the people the alarm is protecting are inside and moving around. Most systems answer that with a stay mode that arms the doors and windows and switches the motion detectors off, so that nobody trips the alarm walking to the bathroom. That is a good start. In a Park Slope row house it often leaves the wrong things off.

Look at where the night entries in the chart happened. A back door on the garden floor, with the couple asleep above. A ledge outside a third-floor bedroom, the floor the household was sleeping on. A building's front door, at the bottom of a stair the residents would have to come down to check. In a house four floors tall, the household is far from most of the doors, and close to some of the windows that a burglar on a fire escape, an extension roof or a ledge can reach.

So the night setting we build for a Park Slope house has three layers. The perimeter is armed, every door and every window, including the ones on the bedroom floor, because a bedroom window should not open at 3 a.m. without someone in the room knowing. The floors nobody uses at night, usually the cellar, the garden floor and the parlor floor, keep their motion detectors armed too, which most panels allow through a night or "night-stay" option that leaves chosen interior zones active. Only the bedroom floor's hall and the stair between bedrooms stay quiet.

Alarm Grid's guide to Honeywell's arming modes describes that difference: in stay mode the interior zones are bypassed; in night mode they are bypassed unless programmed to stay active at night. Most current panels have the same idea under other names.

What each clock asks of the equipment

The two clocks lead to different equipment choices, and a good Park Slope system carries both, sharing one set of contacts. The chart shows which items serve which clock.

The entry-delay question deserves a minute. A delay of thirty or forty-five seconds on the front door lets you come in and disarm. During the day it is necessary. At night it is a gift to anyone who opens that door, because the alarm waits before it sounds. Most panels allow the delay to be dropped to zero when the system is armed at night, so the front door becomes instant until morning. We set it that way unless someone in the house routinely comes home after the others have gone to bed, and then we give that person a fob and their own way in instead.

The morning gap

There is a third, quieter hour that catches families out: the stretch between the first person getting up and the last person leaving. Someone disarms at six to make coffee, the house stays disarmed while everyone gets ready, and the back door is left unlocked when the last person runs for the train. In April 2015 the couple near Eighth Street found their back door standing open in the morning; nobody is helped by an alarm that was switched off at six and never switched back on.

The fix is a routine rather than a device. The early riser switches the night setting to stay, not off, so the doors stay armed while the house wakes up. The last person out arms away, and the app reminds them if they forget. We write that routine on a card by the keypad in the words the family uses, and we walk through it with everyone at handover.

The House at Night

Floor by Floor: Arming a Four-Story Row House While the Family Sleeps

A typical Park Slope brownstone or limestone has a cellar, a garden floor half a level below the sidewalk, a parlor floor at the top of the stoop and two or three floors of bedrooms above, with a roof hatch or bulkhead on top. Here is how we treat each level in the night setting, starting from the bottom. The particular house you live in will differ, and the walkthrough decides the details.

The cellar

The cellar usually has a door under the stoop, sometimes a rear door into the yard, and small windows at the front and back. Nobody goes down at night except to the boiler or the laundry. Every door and window gets a contact, a motion detector covers the stair, and all of it stays armed in the night setting. A water sensor by the boiler and the washer is worth adding while the technician is down there; it costs little and it reports at any hour, armed or not.

The garden floor

The garden floor carries the back door to the yard, which is the door named in the 2015 report near Eighth Street, and often a front door under the stoop as well. In a house with a garden-floor rental, this floor belongs to the tenant and becomes its own area. In a single-family house it is usually the kitchen and family room, empty at night. The back door gets a contact with no entry delay in the night setting, the rear windows get contacts, and a motion detector covers the room. If the back door has glass, a glass-break detector covers it.

The parlor floor

The parlor floor is the one with the tall front windows beside the stoop, the main entrance and, in many houses, the most valuable things in the house. The front door gets a contact; the vestibule door, if there are two, gets its own contact so the panel knows the difference between someone in the vestibule and someone in the hall.

The front windows get contacts and the room gets glass-break detection. At night the motion detector here stays armed: nobody needs to be on the parlor floor at 3 a.m., and if somebody is, the household should hear about it.

The bedroom floors

This is where Park Slope houses differ from the one-story houses most alarm advice assumes. The windows on these floors face the street at the front and the gardens at the back, and the back windows are the ones that matter: windows onto a fire escape, onto the flat roof of a rear extension, beside a ledge, or within reach of the next house's roof. Every one of them gets a contact that stays armed while you sleep. Where a window is opened in summer for air, a second magnet set a few inches up the frame lets it stay armed while open a hand's width. Motion detectors on these floors stay off at night, because the household is here, and a keypad or a fob with a panic button goes beside the main bed.

The top floor and the roof

The roof hatch or bulkhead door gets a contact that is armed in every mode, day and night, because nobody in the family uses it after dark. If there is a skylight over the stair, a glass-break or shock detector covers it. On blocks where the roofs run level from one house to the next, the roof can be a path from the end of the row to your hatch. One resident's account on Reddit describes someone climbing onto a neighboring building's roof, crossing to theirs and coming down the fire escape; we treat that route as real.

Who hears the siren, and what happens next

The panel's own sounder is often in the cellar or the hall, the worst place for a household asleep three floors up, so we add an indoor siren on the bedroom floor, programmed to stop well inside the city's fifteen-minute limit.

When the alarm trips at night, the plan we write with every household is the same: stay upstairs, use the keypad or the phone to see which sensor tripped, take the monitoring center's call or call 911 yourself, and let the police come up the stoop. The point of an alarm that wakes you at the first open door is that you never have to go downstairs to find out what it was.

Apartments

Floor-Throughs, Prospect Park West Co-ops and the New Buildings on Fourth Avenue

Many Park Slope households live in an apartment rather than a whole house. The two clocks still apply; the building's own arrangements matter more.

Floor-throughs in divided houses. The apartment door opens off a shared stair, the front windows face the street and the back windows face the gardens. In the daytime series the Brooklyn Paper reported, the burglar's method was to break the apartment door lock; so the apartment door gets a contact and the hall inside gets a motion detector, which together catch someone who gets past the lock. At night the windows matter more, especially a rear window onto a fire escape, which CBS New York's 2017 report named as the way into a building off Sterling Place.

Prewar co-ops and rentals. Buildings like 35 Prospect Park West, which Wikipedia describes as opening in 1929 with penthouses, duplexes and plain apartments, usually have staff in the lobby for part of the day. The hours the lobby is covered, and the hours it is not, become part of your schedule. We also give the co-op board or the managing agent what they ask for before work starts: the license number, a certificate of insurance, and a list of what will be fixed to which surface.

Newer buildings on Fourth Avenue. The Department of City Planning rezoned much of Park Slope in 2003 and set special rules for Fourth Avenue in 2011, including ground floors kept for non-residential use and curb cuts generally limited to the side streets, according to its published summaries. The buildings that followed have fob-controlled lobbies, package rooms and, in many cases, a smart lock on every apartment door run by management. Your alarm starts at your own door, works alongside whatever lock the building controls, and never ties into the building's fire system or network without management's written permission.

An apartment system is small: a contact on the door, contacts on the windows that a fire escape or a roof can reach, one motion detector in the hall, a smoke detector the panel watches, and an indoor siren. It is wireless, removable when you move, and set up so that the hours you are out and the hours you are asleep each have their own setting. The access control we install for Park Slope buildings is the separate job of controlling the lobby and street doors, for boards and owners who want it.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Park Slope

Park Slope's businesses sit on a few long avenues and a handful of cross streets, mostly in the ground floors of buildings with apartments above. Their clocks run the other way from the houses: busy by day and into the evening, empty from closing until the first delivery. In January 2022, CBS New York reported that the Park Slope Fifth Avenue Business Improvement District was seeing near-nightly burglaries, that one restaurant owner's places had been hit four times in two months with windows costing $750 each to replace, and that in another case a man walked in behind an employee during the morning and went into the kitchen.

Fifth Avenue

The longer of the two main shopping streets, running the length of the neighborhood with restaurants, bars and shops under apartments, and its own business improvement district. Glass fronts, back doors onto yards and cellars below the sidewalk.

Seventh Avenue

The shopping street that runs through the historic blocks, one of the two Wikipedia names as the neighborhood's main commercial streets. Wikipedia also notes that the stretch south of Ninth Street is zoned largely for low-density commercial use, and CityLand lists the 1879 Ansonia Clock Factory on the avenue.

Flatbush Avenue and the north edge

The northern boundary, with the 2 and 3 trains beneath it and Grand Army Plaza at the top, where the Central Library stands across the plaza from the neighborhood's northeast corner.

Fourth Avenue

The western edge, with newer apartment buildings over ground-floor commercial space, and the F and G at the Fourth Avenue station on Ninth Street.

Ninth Street

The cross street under which the F and G run, with the Fourth Avenue and Seventh Avenue stations; Wikipedia calls it a main shopping street.

Union Street and the side-street businesses

Union Street is home to the Park Slope Food Coop, which Wikipedia puts at about 17,000 members. Studios, schools and small offices also sit on the side streets in converted buildings.

What a Park Slope shop alarm needs

Something that reacts to broken glass, not only to an opened door. The two Brooklyn burglary sprees NYPD Crime Stoppers flagged in 2026 both included Park Slope or the 78th Precinct. In the July series, Hoodline reported, the suspect came in through basement-level doors. In the August and September series the two men threw an object through a window. A contact on the front door does nothing about the second method. Glass-break detectors behind the display windows and a motion detector that sees the floor inside them do.

Basement doors on their own zone. Cellars under Fifth and Seventh Avenue stores are reached from outside steps, a sidewalk hatch or a back yard, out of sight of the street. That door gets a contact that is armed whenever the store is closed and that reports even during the day if it opens when it should not.

A closing routine that works when the last person is tired. Gate down, back door locked, cellar door checked, one code, and a confirmation the closer can see. If the store is not armed by an hour after closing, the owner's phone is told.

Personal codes for every staff member. So the event history shows who opened and who closed, and so a former employee's code can be deleted the day they leave.

Separate from upstairs. Where there are apartments above, the store's system, keypad and siren belong only to the store, and the upstairs tenants' street door is never part of it.

Rules a Park Slope business alarm has to respect

Sirens that end on their own. Under section 24-238 of the New York City Administrative Code, a building's audible burglar alarm must be able to stop, and must stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of going off. On a block with apartments over every shop, that limit is the difference between an alarm and a complaint.

Who may install it. Anyone installing alarms for paying customers in New York State needs a security or fire alarm installer license from the Department of State, which requires qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting of employees. Ours is #12000287431, printed on every quote.

Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm with a smoke detector on it is not a code fire alarm. Buildings that need one, such as many mixed-use buildings with apartments over a restaurant, need it designed and filed as its own system; our fire alarm installation for Park Slope buildings covers that work.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your landlord, board, insurer or the city may have requirements of their own, and they take priority.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Park Slope: Questions Answered

Weekday hours, when the house is empty

The burglar who worked near Seventh Avenue and Lincoln Place broke the apartment locks. If someone forces our front door, does he get the same entry countdown we get when we come home?

Yes, if that door is programmed with a delay and the system is in away mode: the panel cannot tell a forced door from a key. That is why we keep the delay short on the door you use, why the keypad sits where it cannot be seen or reached from the doorway, and why the panel reports over cellular as soon as the delay starts, so a burglar who smashes the keypad still leaves a "door opened, not disarmed" signal behind. At night the same door goes to zero delay.

Two of us work from home on different days of the week. How do we keep the house armed on the days it really is empty without arming it around someone on a call upstairs?

Give the schedule days, not just hours. The app's reminder and any automatic arming follow a weekly pattern you set: Monday, Wednesday and Friday at a set time if nobody is home, nothing on the days one of you stays. The person at home arms stay mode instead, so the doors and the garden floor are covered while they work on the top floor, and switches to away if they go out for the afternoon.

The app tells us at work that a parlor window opened at one in the afternoon. What should we do in the next five minutes, and what should we not do?

Look first: the app shows which sensors tripped and in what order, and a camera, if you have one, shows the room. Take the monitoring center's call or call 911 yourself if there is any sign of a real entry. Call a neighbor only to ask them to look from the street, not to go in. Do not rush home and walk in alone. When you get there, wait outside for the police if the alarm history shows movement inside after the window opened.

After midnight, with the family upstairs

In 2015 a couple near Eighth Street and Prospect Park West slept through a burglary. The husband heard a thump and went back to sleep, and the back door was open in the morning. What would an alarm have done differently at that moment?

If the back door had been on a contact armed for the night with no entry delay, the siren would have started the second it opened, on the floor where they were sleeping, and the monitoring center would have been calling within moments. A thump is easy to dismiss. A siren and a phone ringing with "rear door" on the screen are not. Most night entries are short; the point is to end them at the door.

A man was found on the ledge outside a third-floor bedroom window on Eighth Avenue at 5:30 in the morning. Should the windows of the room we are sleeping in be armed while we are in it?

Yes, any of them that can be reached from outside. A closed bedroom window opening at 5:30 a.m. is never the household, so it can safely be armed all night. The contact sounds the alarm the moment the sash moves, and a glass-break detector in the room covers a window that is broken rather than opened. If you sleep with a window open in summer, a second magnet lets it stay armed open a few inches.

Our parlor floor holds the things we would least like to lose, and nobody is on it after we go up to bed. Can its motion detector stay live at night while the floors above stay free to walk?

On most current panels, yes. The motion detector on the parlor floor is set to stay active in the night setting, while the detectors on the bedroom floors are left out. The stair between the parlor floor and the bedrooms is the boundary: if someone in the family needs to go down at night, they switch the panel to stay or disarm from the bedroom keypad or the app first.

When the alarm goes off at three in the morning and we are two floors up, should one of us go down to look?

No. The keypad by the bed or the app tells you which door or window tripped. If it is a door or a window on a lower floor, stay in the bedroom, keep the phone in hand, take the monitoring call or call 911, and let the police come in. If the history shows a false trip you recognize, such as a window you left unlatched in the wind, cancel with your passcode. Nobody in the house should be the first person to find out what opened.

Apartment buildings

Our building on Prospect Park West has a doorman until midnight and nobody in the lobby after that. Should our apartment alarm behave differently once he goes off shift?

It helps. While the lobby is staffed, a stranger is more likely to be stopped at the street door. After midnight the building is a set of locked doors and a stair, so your night setting matters more: apartment door on zero delay, fire-escape and courtyard windows armed, hall motion live if nobody walks it at night. We set the night setting to come on at the hour the lobby empties, with a reminder if it has not been armed.

Shops on Fifth and Seventh Avenues

Our shop on Fifth Avenue is a short walk from the 78th Precinct station house on Sixth Avenue. Is a monitored alarm still worth paying for?

Yes. Being close to the station house does not mean anyone there knows your store has been broken into at 3 a.m. Officers respond to calls, and an unmonitored siren on a closed shop may simply ring out its fifteen minutes. A monitored alarm turns the broken window into a call within moments, with the zone that tripped, and police can be sent while the person is still inside. CBS New York's 2022 report of near-nightly Fifth Avenue burglaries is a reminder that proximity alone does not stop them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Park Slope

We could not open Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches from our research environment, so the statements below are not quotations of any AI answer. They are claims we hear repeated by homeowners, by brochures and by generic online articles, and each is followed by what we see on Park Slope jobs.

"Burglars strike when nobody is home, so arm the alarm when you leave."

Half true. Many Park Slope break-ins in the reports we found did happen in empty houses on weekdays. But the 2015 back-door entry near Eighth Street, the 2016 ledge outside a third-floor bedroom and the 2026 front-door glass on Seventh Avenue all happened between midnight and dawn, with people asleep inside.

An alarm that is only ever armed in away mode protects the house during the hours it is easiest to protect, and does nothing during the hours when a break-in is most frightening. The households most at risk of that gap are the ones who think of the alarm as something for vacations.

We set up every Park Slope system with both a weekday away schedule and a night setting, switchable in one step from the bedroom.

"Stay mode protects you at night."

Stay mode arms the doors and windows and switches the interior detectors off. In a one-story house that may be enough. In a Park Slope house with four floors, it leaves the cellar, the garden floor and the parlor floor unwatched inside, which is where an intruder who gets past a window would walk.

Most current panels have a night or night-stay setting that keeps chosen motion detectors armed, as Alarm Grid's description of Honeywell's arming modes explains. It needs to be programmed per detector, and many installers never do it.

Ask any installer which of your motion detectors will be live at 3 a.m. If the answer is "none," the system is doing less than it could.

"Upper-floor windows don't need sensors."

This advice comes from suburban houses where the second floor is out of reach. A Park Slope row house has fire escapes on the back of many divided houses, rear extensions with flat roofs below the back bedrooms, ledges and cornices on the front, and roofs that run level along the row.

CBS New York's 2017 report of a fire-escape entry off Sterling Place and DNAinfo's 2016 report of a man on a third-floor ledge on Eighth Avenue are two reminders that the top of the house is not out of reach here.

We look out of every back window before deciding which ones need contacts; on most houses the answer includes the bedroom floors.

"A historic district means you can't have a proper alarm."

Being inside the Park Slope Historic District affects what can be added to the outside of a building. It does not stop an alarm. Panels, keypads, contacts, motion detectors and sirens all go inside, and a cellular communicator needs no antenna on the facade in most row houses.

The careful part is the woodwork. Original doors, sashes and frames get contacts recessed from the hinge or meeting-rail side, or fixed out of sight on the inside, so nothing new shows and nothing original is cut where it can be seen.

If a family wants a siren or a camera on the front, we suggest the back instead, because the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules apply to the facade.

"Alarm installation costs $1,000 to $2,000, wherever you live."

National averages hide the things that set a price: how many doors and reachable windows, how many floors the signal has to cross, whether there is old wire to reuse and whether the house needs two areas for a rental floor.

On our Brooklyn list, which Park Slope uses with no surcharge, a basic wireless apartment system runs $600 to $1,200, complete home protection starts at $2,500, and the Brooklyn page puts a typical three-story brownstone at $2,500 to $5,000 installed.

The four-story houses with garden-floor rentals land toward the top of that range, because two areas, a bedroom-floor keypad and contacts on the upper back windows add lines. We write each one down so you can choose.

"A loud outdoor siren scares burglars away."

A siren helps, but in a row house the people who most need to hear it are inside, often three floors above the panel. An outdoor siren on a landmarked front can raise its own questions, and on a block of attached houses it mainly wakes the neighbors.

The city also limits how long it can sound: section 24-238 of the Administrative Code requires a building's audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes.

We put the second siren indoors on the bedroom floor and rely on the monitoring center, not the noise, to bring the police.

"Anyone handy can install an alarm."

A homeowner can put a kit into their own house. A business that installs alarms for paying customers in New York State needs a Department of State security or fire alarm installer license, with qualifying coursework, an exam and fingerprinting for employees.

That license matters to the people around the job as well. Co-op boards on Prospect Park West ask for it before they approve work, and insurers may ask for proof of a professional installation.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start the Survey at the Roof Hatch

This describes how we run a Park Slope survey; it is our method, not an account of a particular job. The technician does not start at the front door. He climbs to the top of the house first, opens the roof hatch or the bulkhead door if the family allows it, and looks along the row: whether the roofs run level, whether there is a parapet between houses, where the fire escapes are. Then he comes down one floor at a time and looks out of every back window, because the back of a Park Slope row house is the side the street never sees.

On each floor he asks one question: who is in this room at three in the morning? The answer sorts every sensor into the empty-house group, the sleeping-house group, or both. Bedroom windows that face a fire escape go into both. The parlor floor's motion detector goes into both. The stair between the bedrooms goes into neither at night. By the time he reaches the cellar door under the stoop, the night setting is already drawn on the survey sheet.

Can you schedule the installation during school hours, so the technicians are not working around the children?
+

Yes. Most of our Park Slope installs start after the morning drop-off and are planned so the noisy work, drilling frames and running cable, is finished before pickup. A longer job on a four-story house may need a second morning. The handover, which everyone who uses the alarm should attend, is booked for an evening or a weekend when the whole family is home.

Our teenager often gets home after the rest of us are asleep. How does she come in without disarming the whole house or waking everyone?
+

Keep the front door instant at night and let her disarm from the app on her phone before she opens it, or give her a fob that works from the top of the stoop. Her code or fob can be set to switch the system from night to stay rather than off, so the back door and the lower floors stay armed after she is in, and the event history shows the time she got home.

Should the weekday schedule follow the school calendar, so the house does not arm itself on snow days and holidays with the kids at home?
+

If the panel arms itself on weekdays, yes, or it will arm around children home on a snow day. For families with school-age children we prefer a reminder to automatic arming: the app tells you the house is still disarmed after everyone has left, and you arm it with one tap. Where automatic arming is used, it skips the school holidays you give us and can be cancelled from the keypad or the app that morning.

What is the difference between stay mode and night mode, and which should a Park Slope row house use?
+

Stay mode arms the doors and windows and switches the interior motion detectors off. Night mode, on panels that have it, does the same but keeps the motion detectors you choose armed, usually those on the floors nobody uses after bedtime. For a house of three or four floors, night mode is the better choice when you go to bed, and stay mode is for the evening, while people are still moving around the parlor floor and the kitchen.

We would rather have the police sent at once if the alarm trips while we are asleep. Can the monitoring center skip its verification call at night?
+

Ask your monitoring provider, because practices differ. Most centers call the house or the first person on the list before dispatching on a burglary signal, and dispatch if nobody answers or the passcode is wrong, so a real night break-in usually still brings the police quickly. What you can change is the order of the list and the instruction you give when they call: answer, confirm it is real, and ask them to send help.

Does a panic button by the bed call the police, or only make noise?
+

It does what it is programmed to do. A monitored panic button sends a signal the center treats as an emergency, with or without the siren; a silent setting asks for help without sounding anything in the house. Without monitoring it sounds the siren and sends an alert to everyone in the app. We set it up the way you choose and test it with you at handover.

Do window contacts still work on replacement double-hung windows whose sashes tilt in for cleaning?
+

Yes, with the right placement. A contact can drift out of line after a sash is tilted in and put back, so on tilt-in windows we place the magnet and the contact where tilting does not move them apart, often at the meeting rail or the head of the frame, and we test each one after tilting the sash in and out. That way cleaning day does not end with a trouble light on the keypad.

The first person up in our house starts at 5:30. How do we stop the night setting from tripping when she walks down to the kitchen?
+

There are two ways, and we usually set both. The night setting can step down to stay mode on a schedule, at a time you choose, so the parlor-floor motion detector is off before she comes down; or she switches it from the bedroom keypad or the app before leaving the room. Either way the doors stay armed until the last person leaves the house.
