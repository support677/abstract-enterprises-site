The Neighborhood

Why Mount Eden Properties Need Security Cameras That Produce Usable Evidence

Mount Eden takes its name from Rachel Eden, who according to NYC Parks bought the sloping eastern part of the old Anderson farm in 1820. The slope never went away. The neighborhood climbs from Jerome Avenue, where the 4 train runs overhead, up to the Grand Concourse, and some of its cross streets are steep enough that pedestrians use stairs instead of sidewalks. On those slopes sit mostly large apartment houses: city tax-lot data for the eight census tracts at the core of the neighborhood counts roughly 10,491 homes on about 270 residential lots, and more than half of those homes are in elevator buildings. Most were built between 1901 and 1929. A camera plan here is usually a plan for a five- or six-story building with a lobby, a mailbox panel, an elevator, a long stair, a cellar and a roof door, home to dozens of households who all walk past the same few doors.

The reason people call us is rarely abstract. In September 2024 News 12 Bronx reported on an apartment building on Shakespeare Avenue, on the western edge of what the station called Mount Eden, where a panel of eight mailboxes did not lock and had been broken for months. A resident said a debit card and checks worth $400 were taken from his box, and he told the station the building had no security cameras, which made it hard to file a police report or identify anyone. That is the pattern we hear most from Mount Eden: something small and expensive goes missing in a shared space, and the building has either no camera at all or a camera that cannot show a face at the spot where it happened.

Then there are the days that are not small. On the afternoon of July 25, 2026, gunfire at the corner of Walton Avenue and Elliot Place killed a 12-year-old boy and wounded two men. ABC7 reported that surveillance video from outside a bodega on that corner showed a fist fight in the street just before the shots, and police made an arrest the next day. We mention it with care, and only because it is the clearest recent example of what residents here already understand: when something terrible happens on a Mount Eden block, investigators go looking for video, and the cameras that matter are the ones whose owners can find and hand over the right minutes quickly.

Ownership shapes the work as much as the buildings do. A sizable share of Mount Eden's housing is owned by nonprofits and HDFC co-ops. New Settlement Apartments, opened in 1990 after the renovation of 14 abandoned buildings, accounts for more than a thousand homes on its own, according to the Settlement Housing Fund. Tax-lot owner names suggest HDFC corporations hold dozens of other lots. Those owners answer to boards, funders and tenants, and they tend to ask the same careful questions we ask: who will see the video, how long it will be kept, and how a tenant asks for a clip. Most of this page is about answering those questions well, because a camera system that nobody trusts or nobody can operate is just hardware on a wall.

  • Video at a corner bodega. ABC7's July 27, 2026 report said the video from Walton Avenue and Elliot Place "shows a group of men engaged in a fist fight in the street just before 5 p.m. on Saturday."
  • Burglary up slightly, car theft down sharply. NYPD CompStat for the 44th Precinct, for the year through November 1, 2026, shows 205 burglaries against 200 a year earlier (up 2.5%) and 199 grand larcenies of autos against 279 (down 28.7%).
  • Retail theft down but still frequent. The same report lists 440 retail thefts and 1,403 petit larcenies year to date, both lower than 2025 but still more than one a day.

The Clip Test

Planning Mount Eden Cameras Backward From the Morning After

Most camera plans start with the building: count the doors, count the floors, pick a number of cameras. We start a Mount Eden plan from the other end. Picture the morning after a theft or an assault on your block. Someone, usually the super or the managing agent, has to answer five questions in order: which camera saw it, what time it happened, whether the recorder still has it, whether the picture is good enough to show a face or a plate, and how to get it off the recorder as an original file. If any one of those answers is "we don't know," the system failed, however many cameras it has. Everything below is how we design so each answer is yes.

Question one: which camera saw it?

A recorder with sixteen channels named "Camera 01" through "Camera 16" is the most common thing we find when we take over a system in an older building. Nobody remembers which number covers the mailbox panel and which covers the rear stair. On every job we name each channel in plain words before we leave: "street door from vestibule," "mailbox panel," "elevator landing, lobby," "4th floor west stair," "cellar door to yard," "roof door." The cable behind each camera carries the same label at both ends, and the owner gets a one-page camera list with a photo of each view. In a six-story elevator building that list is often the single most useful thing we hand over, because it turns a search through a whole recorder into a search through one channel.

Question two: what time did it happen?

A clip with the wrong time is a weak clip. Recorders drift, lose their clock after a power cut, or were never set to the right time zone and daylight-saving rules in the first place. We set every recorder to sync its clock automatically from a time server and check it again before we leave. For a building that loses power often or sits behind an internet connection that drops, we also note the recorder's time against a phone at the handover, so the owner knows the system was correct on the day it was installed. When residents can tell you roughly when something happened, motion markers on the timeline narrow it to the minute.

Question three: is it still on the drive?

Recorders keep video until their drives fill, then write over the oldest footage. How long that takes depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, whether they record all the time or only on motion, and the size of the drives. FirstService Residential, which manages co-ops and condos across New York, says footage is generally kept for 30 to 90 days depending on building size, storage and legal requirements. For a Mount Eden building we size storage so the cameras that matter most, usually the street door, the vestibule and the mailbox panel, keep enough days for a tenant to notice a stolen check, report it and ask for a clip. Quieter cameras can keep less. The quote lists the drives and the expected number of days in writing.

Question four: is the picture good enough?

The difference between "a person" and "that person" is placement, light and distance, not megapixels. A camera high in a lobby corner records the tops of heads; the same camera at the inner vestibule door, a little above face height and looking back toward the street door, records faces as people come in. At a mailbox panel the camera has to see the face of someone standing at the boxes and their hands at the doors at the same moment, which usually means mounting to one side rather than straight overhead. Outside a garage, a narrow view aimed at the bay door shows a plate or a face; a wide view of the whole block shows neither. We check every view after dark, because the Mount Eden lobbies and landings we see range from bright to almost unlit.

Question five: can you get it out properly?

When police or an insurer ask for video they want the original file exported from the recorder, with its time stamp, not a phone recording of a monitor. We show the super, the managing agent or the shop owner how to export a clip to a fresh USB drive the day something happens, and we leave a printed one-page guide beside the recorder. We also recommend keeping a copy of anything you hand over. You do not need to give anyone the recorder, the master password or remote access to the system to cooperate with an investigation, and a detective who asks for footage can tell you which camera and which window of time they need.

Who Gets to Watch

Footage Rules for Mount Eden's Rental, Nonprofit and Co-op Buildings

The question that decides whether tenants welcome a camera system or resent it is almost never about the cameras. It is about the video: who looks at it, who can copy it, how long it exists and how a resident who has been robbed gets to see it. In a neighborhood with as many nonprofit owners, HDFC co-ops and tenant organizations as Mount Eden, that question gets asked early, and it deserves a written answer before installation day. We help owners write that answer and then build the system so it actually works that way.

A landlord does not have to share footage, and that is why a policy helps

Brick Underground put the tenant's side plainly in a column on package thefts: attorney Jennifer Rozen said "your landlord isn't obligated to share surveillance footage with you," though a landlord would have to give access if police requested it or a court ordered it. That is the law as most New York renters meet it. It is also why a building with a clear, posted process gets fewer arguments. When tenants know that a request goes to the managing agent, names the camera and the time window, and gets an answer within a set number of days, they stop wondering whether the cameras are there to protect them or to watch them.

What a good footage policy says

FirstService Residential advises co-op and condo boards to "establish clear policies that define who can access footage and under what circumstances," to route requests through the property manager with the reason, timeframe and cameras involved, and to keep a log of requests and of everyone who accessed footage. That advice works just as well for a rental or a nonprofit building. A one-page policy for a Mount Eden building usually covers who has live viewing (often the super and the managing agent), who can export (usually fewer people), how long each camera keeps video, how a resident requests a clip, how requests from police are handled, and when old video is deleted. We do not write legal documents, but we build the logins, retention settings and export permissions to match whatever the owner and the owner's lawyer decide.

Building the policy into the recorder

Most modern recorders let us give each person a login with only the cameras and the rights they need. A super can watch the lobby, the mailbox panel and the cellar but cannot export or delete. The managing agent can export. The owner keeps the master account and the only right to change settings. Where a building has a board, a board member can be given view rights for a defined period without becoming the day-to-day viewer. Many recorders also keep their own log of who signed in and what was exported, and we turn that on and show the owner where to find it. When someone leaves the job, their login is removed that day.

Nonprofit owners and tenant organizers

New Settlement, which has long supported tenant organizing in the southwest Bronx through its Community Action for Safe Apartments project, is one example of how close owners and residents here can be to these decisions. For nonprofit and HDFC owners we often suggest showing tenants the camera list and the footage policy before cameras go up, at a building meeting or on a notice in the lobby. Residents can then point out a spot that worries them, such as a dark stair turn or a back door that never closes, and the owner can decide whether to add it. A system built with that conversation tends to be one residents defend rather than one they tape over.

When a tenant wants a camera of their own

A renter who wants a camera at their own apartment door is in a different position. In May 2024 News 12 reported that management of a building in nearby Mott Haven gave residents a deadline to remove cameras from their apartment doors, and a tenant took down her doorbell camera for fear of a lease violation. A representative of a Bronx tenant rights group told the station to "review the lease, see if they're anything that explicitly says you can't have a camera on your door." Our advice is to read the lease, ask management in writing before mounting anything on the hallway side, and in the meantime use a small indoor camera aimed at the inside of your own front door, which touches no common area at all.

Slopes, Stairs and the Expressway

Aiming Cameras on Mount Eden's Hills, Stair Streets and Cut Above the Cross Bronx

Mount Eden's geography changes how cameras are aimed in ways that a flat-block installer can miss. Streets climb steeply between Jerome Avenue and the Concourse, some blocks end in public stairs, and the Cross Bronx Expressway runs below grade along the neighborhood's southern side. Each of those features changes what a camera at a building's door can and cannot see, and each one shows up on our walk-throughs.

Doors on a grade

When a building's entrance sits on a steep sidewalk, people approach from above or below rather than straight on. A door camera aimed level, as it would be on a flat street, records the top of the head of someone walking up the hill and the knees of someone coming down. We set the camera's tilt for the direction most people actually arrive from, usually the subway side, and on a corner building we sometimes use two narrow views instead of one wide one. Where the entrance steps rise from the sidewalk, the camera sits inside the vestibule looking back out, so it meets faces at the top of the steps.

The stair streets

Michael Cairl's guide to the city's stair streets describes the Mount Eden Avenue stair, 74 steps leading to East Mount Eden Avenue at the northeast corner of Claremont Park. Public stairs like that are city property, and a private camera may not be mounted on them or aimed to watch the whole flight as if it belonged to the building. A building beside a stair can still cover its own entrance and the part of the sidewalk directly in front of it, and we draw a privacy mask over the rest of the frame so the recording does not become a log of everyone who uses the stair. If residents want the stair itself watched, that is a request for the city or the local council member, not something a private owner should install.

Above the expressway

In June 2024 News 12 described a street in Mount Eden that runs above the Cross Bronx Expressway, between Nelson Avenue and West 173rd Street, as a hot spot for illegal dumping; the Sanitation Department said it had cleaned it nine times that year, and officials said about 280 cameras are deployed in dumping hot spots around the city. Owners whose buildings back onto that kind of edge often ask whether their own cameras can help. A camera aimed at your building's own frontage, side yard and the curb directly in front of it can record a vehicle stopping there, and a narrow view on your own curb gives a better chance at a plate than a wide view of the street. It cannot police the whole block, and we would never aim a private camera across a fence onto the expressway's right of way.

Wind, grit and vibration

Buildings close to the expressway and along Jerome Avenue under the elevated 4 train live with steady vibration, road grit and wind funneled along the cut. Mounts that would hold on a quiet side street can work loose over a year here, so we use anchors rated for masonry, add a thread-locking compound to camera brackets on exposed walls, and choose housings that can be wiped clean without being knocked out of aim. We also tell owners plainly that a camera exposed to traffic grime needs its dome or lens cleaned a few times a year, or the night picture slowly turns to haze.

Six-story elevator buildings: one lobby, many landings

Among the larger residential buildings in Mount Eden's core tracts, city tax-lot data shows about as many six-story buildings as five-story ones, and the six-story elevator building is the one we plan most often. The lobby is the heart of the system: a camera at the inner door for faces, a wide view of the lobby and mailbox panel, and a tight view of the elevator door. Upstairs, the stair is the weak spot, because residents who skip the elevator, and anyone who wants to avoid the lobby camera, use it. We usually cover the stair door on the lobby floor, the cellar and the roof bulkhead first, then add landings floor by floor as the budget allows. Cable rises in an existing riser or a stair corner, every floor penetration is fire-stopped, and the recorder sits in a locked room in the cellar with its own battery unit.

Five-story walk-ups: the stair is the building

In a walk-up, every resident climbs the same stair, so the stair cameras carry more weight than they do in an elevator building. We place cameras at the top of each flight looking down, where they meet faces coming up, and keep them aimed along the stair rather than at apartment doors. On narrow landings where doors face each other, a privacy mask blacks out the part of the frame that would show into an apartment when a door opens. The vestibule camera, the mailbox camera and the cellar door complete the core. Because walk-ups rarely have a chase from cellar to roof, cable often rises in painted conduit in a stair corner, which we price as its own line so the owner can see the cost.

Rehabilitated nonprofit buildings: work with what was upgraded

Many of Mount Eden's older buildings were rehabilitated by nonprofit owners, some of them during the wave of renovations that produced New Settlement Apartments in 1990, others later. Renovated buildings often have newer electrical service, a managed entry system and sometimes old camera cable left from an earlier project. We trace what exists before we propose anything new, because an old run that tests clean can be reused and save the owner money. Nonprofit owners also tend to want a clear paper trail, so our quote, camera list and handover notes are written so a board or a funder can read them without a technician in the room.

HDFC co-ops: phased plans that fit a reserve

A limited-equity co-op run by a resident board often has to pay for cameras out of a small reserve, and every shareholder may have an opinion on where the money goes. We break the plan into phases on the quote: the street door, the vestibule, the mailbox panel and the cellar door first; the stair, the roof door and the laundry room second; extra landings last. The recorder and switch are sized for the whole plan from the start, so later phases add cameras without replacing anything. Board approval comes before any order, and we can present the plan at a shareholder meeting if the board wants residents to see it.

New buildings near the 4 train: wire it before the walls close

The 2018 Jerome Avenue rezoning has brought new buildings to the avenue, including the 11-story building at 1351 Jerome Avenue that Bronx Times reported in January 2025 had 131 homes, 98 of them affordable, in its housing lottery. New construction is the cheapest time to get cameras right. While walls are open, each camera spot gets a box and a run back to a head-end room with its own circuit, and spare pathways are left for later. The usual list is every entrance, the lobby, the mail and package room, each stair door, elevator landings, the trash and bike rooms, the roof door and the building corners. Cameras go on after paint, and the system is labeled and tested before residents move in.

Small houses and mixed-use corners

Mount Eden's few dozen small houses and two-to-four-family buildings cluster on a handful of side streets. Their plans center on the front door, the curb and the back: the recorder in the basement, cable through the basement ceiling to the front and along the joists to the yard. Where a store sits under apartments at a corner, the store and the residential entrance get separate recorders and separate logins, so the shopkeeper is not watching tenants' mailboxes and the landlord is not watching the register. Anything on the shared facade is agreed between the two in writing before it goes up.

Inwood and Cromwell Avenues, just to the east, have more repair shops and small industrial spaces. Auto shops under the 4 train

City Limits reported in 2021 that roughly 150 auto businesses were still operating along the Jerome Avenue corridor after the rezoning, with dozens displaced. The ones that remain keep customers' cars, tools and parts on site, often with the bay doors open all day and the lot packed at night. In April 2026 the Bronx District Attorney announced indictments in Operation Pit Crew, a case involving about 250 thefts of vehicles and parts across the Bronx, which is exactly the kind of crime these shops worry about. A useful shop system has a narrow camera on each bay door, a view of the lot from a high corner of the building, a camera on the counter and the key board, and one inside the bays for the cars left overnight. The elevated tracks cast hard shadows and flicker across the street by day, so we check every outdoor view at noon and after dark before setting exposure.

Plates and faces at the curb

Owners often ask for a camera that can "read every plate on Jerome Avenue." No single camera can. Plate capture needs a narrow view at a known spot, a shutter fast enough to freeze motion and lighting that does not flare off the plate at night. The recorder and the clip

Our recorder lists sixteen cameras by number only, and nobody knows which one covers the mailbox panel. Can the channels be renamed without replacing the system?

Almost always, yes. Nearly every recorder lets an administrator give each channel a name, and the names appear on the live view, the playback timeline and every exported clip. The work is finding out which number is which: we step in front of each camera, watch which tile changes, trace any cable whose camera is not obvious, and then rename every channel in plain words like "mailbox panel" or "5th floor east landing." If you have the administrator password this is a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour on our Bronx rate, then 30-minute blocks; if nobody has the password, regaining access comes first and depends on the brand.

The super recorded the monitor with his phone and texted that video to the detective. Is that good enough, or does the precinct need something else?

It is better than nothing for a quick look, but it is not what investigators usually want. A phone video of a screen loses detail, can blur faces that are clear on the recorder, and does not carry the recorder's own time stamp and file information. What helps most is the original export from the recorder for the camera and time window the detective names, copied to a fresh drive, with a copy kept by the building. Write down the detective's name, the case number and exactly what was exported. If nobody in the building knows how to export, that is the single most useful thing to fix before the next time.

Rules for who sees the video

Our HDFC board wants a record of every time someone watches or downloads video. Can the recorder keep that by itself?

Many modern recorders keep a log of sign-ins, playback and exports, and we turn it on and show the board where to read it. How detailed that log is depends on the model, so if a full audit trail is a requirement, tell us before we choose equipment and we'll pick a recorder that records it. Pair the recorder's log with a simple written log kept by the managing agent, as FirstService Residential recommends for co-ops and condos, so every request has a reason, a date range and the name of the person who handled it.

Our managing agent wants residents to fill out a written request before any clip is reviewed. What should that form ask for?

Keep it short so people actually use it: the resident's name and apartment, the date and approximate time, the place in the building (mailbox panel, vestibule, a particular landing), what happened, whether a police report was filed and its number, and how to reach the resident. Add a line saying who will review the video and when the resident will hear back. A clear form protects the building as well as the tenant, because it shows requests were handled the same way for everyone. We set up export rights so the person named on the form can actually do the review.

Our nonprofit's lawyer asked us to choose how long video is kept. What range do New York buildings use, and what happens if something may end up in court?

FirstService Residential says buildings it works with generally keep footage for 30 to 90 days, depending on size, storage and legal requirements, and that boards should talk to their attorney about keeping footage longer when an incident could lead to litigation. Your lawyer sets the policy; we build it. That means sizing drives so the chosen number of days actually fits, setting retention per camera, and showing staff how to export and set aside a clip before it is overwritten when a lawyer or the police ask for it to be preserved.

Stairs, slopes and two-level buildings

Our building stands beside the Mount Eden Avenue stair, and people gather on the steps at night. Can our camera watch the stair?

Not as a private surveillance post. The stair is public property, and a building camera should not be mounted on it or aimed to record the whole flight. Your camera can cover your own entrance and the sidewalk directly in front of your door, with a privacy mask over the rest of the frame. If the stair itself needs lighting or city cameras, residents can raise it with the community board, the precinct and the local council member, who can request city action. We set your view so that anyone who comes off the stair and up to your door is recorded clearly.

Because of the hill, our building has its main entrance on one street and a second entrance a full floor lower on the side street. How do we cover both without doubling the system?

One recorder can serve both doors; what changes is the cable route and where the recorder sits. We often place the recorder near the lower entrance, where the cellar meets the street, run one cable to the lower door and the cellar, and rise in a riser or stair corner to the upper lobby. Each entrance gets a face-height camera at its inner door and, if there are mailboxes, a camera at the panel. If the lower door is the one residents use less, it is also the one an intruder would choose, so we treat it as a full entrance rather than a back door.

The district's text in the Zoning Resolution regulates signs, display windows and new door and window openings, and it does not mention security cameras. A customer lounge is an ordinary public area of the business, and a video camera there is common and lawful, as long as it does not see into a restroom or changing area and does not record conversations. We are building near the Mount Eden Avenue station. Should cameras be in the general contractor's scope, or under a separate contract?

Either can work, but the cable and the boxes have to be planned before the walls close no matter who signs the contract. Aim the camera at the people approaching the window, from the waiting-room side, rather than over the receptionist's shoulder at the desk. Where a monitor or a sign-in sheet still shows in the frame, a privacy mask can black that area out in both live view and recordings. The Law in Plain Words

New York Rules Behind a Mount Eden Camera Job

Mount Eden buildings and businesses run into the same short list of New York rules. This is a plain summary of how we work within them. It is not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Cameras are a choice, not a mandate

Owners sometimes ask whether the city requires cameras in an apartment building. Security sources on New York building rules state there is no general city law requiring them. What the law does require at the entrance of many buildings is working door hardware and, in many cases, an intercom, which is a separate job. Cameras are installed for the building's own reasons, and the owner decides where and how, within the privacy rules below.

Private places stay off camera

Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people without consent in places where they reasonably expect privacy, such as bedrooms, bathrooms and changing areas. A stair camera may watch the stair; it may not look into an apartment when a door opens. A camera over a store door may watch the sidewalk; it may not look into a window across the street. Privacy masks black out those parts of the frame in live view and in recordings.

Sound is treated differently from video

Recording a conversation without the consent of at least one person in it can be eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. Talking through a door camera to a visitor is a conversation you are part of; a lobby camera quietly recording residents' conversations is not. We leave continuous audio off on lobby, hallway, stair, garage and outdoor cameras.

Neighbors' back yards

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. Behind Mount Eden's houses and walk-ups the yards are small and often step down the hill, so a yard camera is aimed at your own yard and rear door and masked over the rest.

Who may install

Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, a New York State license is required to install CCTV or video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. The Department of State exempts an owner who installs a system personally, or through their own employee, on their own property. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance; ask any installer for their license number before work starts.

Landmarks and the Concourse

The Grand Concourse Historic District stops at 167th Street, so Mount Eden's Concourse buildings are generally outside Landmarks review on that basis, though any individually designated building would still need it. The Special Grand Concourse Preservation District is a zoning district with its own rules on signs and openings; it does not address cameras, and we treat its facades with the same care anyway.

FAQ

Mount Eden Security Camera FAQ

Our stair lights are on motion sensors and stay dark until someone walks in. Will the landing cameras miss the first seconds?
+

They can, if the cameras rely on that light. A camera in a dark stair switches to infrared night mode, and when a motion light snaps on it needs a moment to switch back, which can blur the first second or two. We usually set landing cameras to stay in night mode, which keeps a steady black-and-white picture whether the light is on or off, or we ask the owner whether a low, constant light can stay on at each landing. We test the stair both ways on the night visit before deciding.

Will the camera list you leave in the building name tenants or apartment numbers?
+

No. The camera list names places, not people: the street door, the mailbox panel, the lobby, the third-floor west landing, the cellar door. It shows a still from each camera so staff know what each one sees. Where a view passes an apartment door, the list notes that a privacy mask is in place rather than naming whose door it is. The owner keeps the master copy, and a version without stills can be posted for residents if the owner wants tenants to see where cameras are.

Can the recorder warn us when someone covers a stair camera or twists it toward the wall?
+

Many recorders and cameras have a tamper or scene-change alert that fires when a lens is covered, sprayed or turned away from its view. We turn it on where the equipment supports it and send the alert to the super or managing agent, with quiet hours set if the owner prefers. Vandal-resistant domes mounted out of easy reach make tampering harder in the first place, and a camera aimed at the tampered one will usually show who did it.

When you take down the old cameras, do you patch and paint the holes?
+

We remove the old cameras, brackets and loose cable we are replacing, pull or cut back abandoned anchors and seal any opening to the outside so water and drafts can't get in. We don't do plastering or painting, so finished patching on interior walls and facade touch-up are left for the owner's painter or mason. The quote says exactly what we remove and what is left for others, so nobody is surprised by a wall full of small holes.

If we add a camera at the roof bulkhead next year, does a new cable have to come all the way up from the cellar?
+

Not if we plan for it now. When we run cable up a riser or stair corner to the top landing, we can leave one or two spare runs coiled and capped at the top, labeled for the bulkhead and the roof. Next year's camera then connects to a cable that is already there and already tested. Spare runs cost far less while the path is open than a separate pull later, and we list them as their own line on the quote.

Our building's internet comes in through a provider box in the cellar that the provider controls. Do the cameras need their own internet line?
+

Not to record. The cameras and recorder work on their own network inside the building and keep recording with no internet at all. Internet only matters for watching from a phone and for alerts.
