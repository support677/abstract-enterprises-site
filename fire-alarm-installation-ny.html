The Projects

Why New NYC Buildings and Fit-Outs Need Fire Alarm Installation Planned Early

A new fire alarm system installation in New York City is decided long before anyone mounts a device. The occupancy group, the height of the building, the number of people on each floor and whether the floors above have dwelling units all decide what the Building Code asks for, and those facts are fixed in the architectural drawings months before the electrician arrives. When a project treats the fire alarm as a late trade, the usual result is a ceiling that has to be reopened, a riser that has no room left, or a TM-1 that cannot be filed because the floor numbers on the fire alarm plans do not match the Schedule A. When it is treated as part of the design, the drawings, the filing and the installation move together and the acceptance test lands near the date the owner needs to open.

The city keeps building, and every one of those projects that houses a business, an assembly space, a school, a care facility or enough apartments crosses a fire alarm threshold. Fire alarm installation for commercial buildings is not optional detail on these jobs. It is one of the systems the FDNY must accept before the building or the floor can be used, alongside sprinklers, standpipes and, in taller buildings, elevator recall and smoke control. On a ground-up tower the fire alarm talks to almost every other life-safety system in the building; on a single-floor office fit-out it has to talk to a base-building system that somebody else owns and that already has its own Letter of Approval.

The numbers explain why the FDNY treats this so seriously. The city's Mayor's Management Report for fiscal 2025 counted 20,180 structural fires in residential buildings and 4,887 in non-residential buildings, and the Department's civilian fire prevention staff carried out 205,294 inspections in the same year. A building that opens with a properly designed, properly filed and properly tested system is a building that starts its life on the right side of that inspection workload.

For owners, developers, general contractors and construction managers, the practical question is timing: when to bring in the fire alarm installation contractor, what that contractor needs from the design team, and what has to be true on site before the FDNY will schedule the test. This page is written around those questions, for new construction fire alarm installation specifically. If you are dealing with an existing system that is in trouble, our NYC Existing Buildings covers violations and old panels; for the citywide overview by borough and building type, see NYC.

Six facts every NYC project team should have in front of them

  • Who prepares the plans. The FDNY's filing guide says a New York State registered design professional, a Professional Engineer or a Registered Architect, prepares the fire alarm plans and files the documents (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).
  • Who may install. The installer must be a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY-issued Fire Alarm Company Certificate (same source).
  • How long it takes. Plan submission to final inspection and approval averages about twenty to twenty-four weeks, and the initial plan review alone can take up to eight weeks (same source).
  • When work may start. Installation starts only after plan approval, and testing follows NFPA 72 as modified by Appendix Q of the NYC Building Code (same source).
  • Who leads a fire alarm company. At least one principal must hold an S-97 Certificate of Fitness, which requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certification FAQ).
  • How early to ask for the test. The FDNY recommends 40 to 70 business days of lead time on a fire alarm test request (FDNY, document application requirements by type).
Need Repair?

Need Repair on a New NYC System Before or After Turnover?

New systems have problems too, and they usually show up at the worst moment: the week of the pre-test, the morning a tenant moves in, or the first time the building engineer silences a panel and cannot clear it. A trouble light on a brand-new panel almost always traces back to construction itself. Drywall screws through a cable, a device head left off after painting, a module address entered twice, a ground fault from a cable crushed against a stud track, or a NAC power supply on a temporary circuit that someone switched off. Fire alarm troubleshooting on a building that is still under construction is detective work, and the clues are on the as-built drawings and in the panel's event history.

When a GC or owner calls with a fire alarm system trouble condition, we book the visit, usually within 2 hours of the call, and come with the shop drawings and the point list. We do not advertise 24 hour fire alarm repair or promise that every call becomes a same day fire alarm repair, because nobody honest can promise that in this city; what we do promise is a booked time, a technician who reads the panel before touching it, and a written note of what was found. Most construction-caused faults on a new system are found and fixed in a single visit, and the ones that are not get a clear plan.

If the system has already been accepted and the trouble is on an occupied floor, remember that work on an approved system is still work on an FDNY-approved system: replacing a damaged device with the same model at the same address is maintenance, but moving devices, adding a room or changing the sequence is a modification that may need its own filing. We tell you which is which before the work starts.

Some calls cannot wait for a scheduled visit: a fire alarm system down repair on an occupied floor, a fire alarm panel failure repair the night before an inspection, or a fire alarm system not communicating with the central station. For that kind of emergency fire alarm repair, emergency fire alarm service or urgent fire alarm repair, call rather than fill in a form, tell us whether the panel is in alarm, supervisory or trouble, and we book an emergency fire alarm technician at the first open slot. After hours fire alarm service is booked the same way. Commercial fire alarm emergency repair and fire alarm system emergency service start with emergency fire alarm troubleshooting by phone: what the panel display says, which light is lit, and whether the trouble is new. A fire alarm panel trouble light or fire alarm panel beeping repair is often fixed in one visit; fire alarm trouble emergency service, fire alarm trouble signal repair, smoke detector circuit troubleshooting, fire alarm communication failure emergency repair and fire alarm system malfunction repair take longer when the cause is buried in a closed ceiling. When the system cannot be restored, the owner follows the FDNY's rules for an out-of-service system, which may include a fire watch.

Many owners ask at this stage: what is a commercial fire alarm system, and how far does it reach on their project? In plain terms, it is the network of initiating devices that sense fire or water flow, the control panel that decides what each signal means, the notification appliances that tell occupants to leave, and the outputs that make other building systems react. How does a commercial fire alarm system work on a new building? A detector or switch reports to the panel over a signaling line circuit, the panel matches that input against the programmed matrix, and then it sounds the notification circuits, sends the signal to the central station and triggers the relays for elevators, fans and doors. Commercial fire alarm system design is the engineer's job; building it so it behaves exactly as designed is ours, and that is what professional fire alarm installation means on a construction site.

Ground-up high-rise: one network, many trades, a long schedule

On a new tower the fire alarm is the coordinator of the building's emergency behavior. The panel, or a network of panels, receives every smoke detector, duct detector, waterflow switch and tamper switch, and it drives the fire alarm voice evacuation installation, the fire alarm elevator recall integration, stair pressurization and the smoke control interface the mechanical engineer designed. The riser carries the signaling line circuits and the speaker circuits, and their circuit class and survivability level must be shown on the riser diagram, as the FDNY's filing guide points out under Building Code section 907.1.1. Survivability is not an abstract term on a high-rise: it decides whether the cable runs in a rated shaft, in a two-hour enclosure or as a rated cable, and that decision belongs in the design before the core walls go up.

The schedule is long because the systems arrive in pieces. The fire alarm installers set boxes and pull cable as each floor is framed, but programming and full testing cannot happen until the elevators, fans and sprinkler system are actually working, and the FDNY expects elevator automatic recall, Fireman's Service and fan shutdowns to be operational before it will test. On a ground-up building we plan the fire alarm work in waves that follow the other trades, and we keep a running list of every interface that cannot be tested until somebody else finishes.

Mixed-use with apartments: two systems on one lot

A large share of what the city builds is retail or community facility space at the base with apartments above. The FDNY's guide is direct about this: R-2 occupancy systems are filed separately from the other occupancy groups. That means the multifamily building fire alarm installation for the apartment floors and the commercial system for the ground floor are two filings, often two panels, with defined points where they talk to each other. An apartment building fire alarm installation in New York City also has its own device rules, such as smoke detection in the mechanical, electrical, transformer and telephone rooms the guide lists, and the square footage of those rooms has to appear on the drawings for R-2 buildings up to 75 feet. For condominium building fire alarm installation the same logic applies, with the added step that the condo board or sponsor needs to own the documentation once the building is turned over.

Office floor fit-out: working inside someone else's system

Most office work in Manhattan and Downtown Brooklyn is not a new building at all; it is a tenant taking a floor and building it out. The tenant's devices connect to the base-building system, and the FDNY expects to see the Letter of Approval for that base-building system with the filing for the new devices. Tenant improvement fire alarm installation therefore starts with the landlord: which panel and protocol the building runs, whether there are spare addresses and power on the floor's loop, who holds the base-building service contract, and what the building's rules are for silencing and testing on an occupied tower. A tenant's contractor that skips this step can install perfect devices that the base-building panel cannot read.

Restaurants, warehouses, schools and clinics: the special cases

A restaurant build-out brings the kitchen hood. The suppression system belongs to its own licensed trade, but fire alarm kitchen hood suppression monitoring reports its discharge to the building panel, and commercial kitchen fire detection installation uses heat detectors in places where cooking would set off smoke detectors every lunch. A new warehouse or storage facility fire alarm installation turns on ceiling height: projected beam smoke detector installation across the bays or aspirating smoke detection installation with sampling pipe, a fire detection device installation method that keeps the detector at floor level, can cover areas that would otherwise need lift work for every test. Schools, hospitals, nursing homes and assisted living facilities sit in occupancy groups where the code requires a fire alarm in every case, and the design pays close attention to how notification reaches people who cannot simply walk out on their own.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Ceiling Closes Before the Panel Is Ready

The most common problem we see on New York City fit-outs is not a code question. It is sequencing. The drywall and ceiling trades are on a tight schedule, the fire alarm devices are on back order or the shop drawings are still with the engineer, and the ceiling grid goes in with empty boxes above it. Weeks later someone has to pop tiles and lift ladders through a finished space to pull the last runs, terminate the heads and find the box that was buried by the duct crew.

Our habit on every construction job is simple. Before any wall closes, the fire alarm crew walks it with the drawings, confirms each box and each cable home run, labels the conductors at both ends and photographs the open wall. The photos go in the job folder next to the shop drawings, and when the as-built drawings are prepared at the end, they are built from what was photographed, not from memory. It costs an hour per floor while the walls are open. It saves days when a conductor needs to be found after the ceilings close, and it is how a ground fault on a finished floor gets traced in an afternoon rather than a week.

On a tenant fit-out, the tenant's architect or project manager calls once the lease is signed and the landlord's base-building requirements are known.

The industries we see on new projects across the five boroughs follow the city's construction activity. Industrial building fire alarm installation and manufacturing facility fire alarm installation come up in the city's industrial business zones and in converted factory buildings where light manufacturing, food production and maker spaces return. Retail store fire alarm installation and shopping center fire alarm installation follow the ground-floor retail in new mixed-use buildings and the larger shopping developments, where Group M thresholds on fire area and number of stories decide whether the system is required. Hospital fire alarm installation, nursing home fire alarm installation and assisted living fire alarm installation follow the city's health care construction, where every Group I occupancy needs a system. Hotel fire alarm installation follows the hotel construction that clusters around Midtown, Long Island City and Downtown Brooklyn, where R-1 occupancies need a system in nearly every case. Parking garage fire alarm installation, storage facility fire alarm installation and data center fire detection installation each bring their own detection problems: exhaust in garages, high racks in self-storage, and the need in a data hall to detect smoke very early without discharging anything onto running equipment.

Industrial fire alarm systems in New York City deserve a note of their own. An industrial fire alarm contractor on these projects is as much a detection specialist as an installer: knowing when a fixed temperature heat detector installation is the right answer for a dusty room, when a rate of rise heat detector installation suits a space that warms gradually but should alarm on a sudden spike, and when industrial fire detection system installation calls for air sampling because nothing else will see smoke early enough in that environment.

Compliance notes for NYC new buildings, gut renovations and fit-outs

Every NYC fire alarm project answers to two agencies, whichever commercial fire alarm installation company builds it. The Department of Buildings governs the building and the work permit; the FDNY reviews the fire alarm plans, approves the system and witnesses the test. Three compliance points come up on nearly every construction job:

  • The paperwork the TM-1 needs. The FDNY's guide lists certified fire alarm plans, the TM-1, and a Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A; when neither exists, which is common on new buildings and changes of use, the applicant obtains a Letter of No Objection from DOB. These fire alarm permit requirements are handled by the design professional, who prepares the fire alarm installation permit filing; we make sure the installation can match what was filed.
  • Changes after approval. If the design changes, the FDNY expects a Post-Approval Amendment to be opened under the DOB PW-1 before an amendment is filed with the FDNY. On a fit-out that discovers a new room or a moved wall, that amendment is the difference between a clean test and a Notice of Defect.
  • Who signs what. The A-433 form is signed and sealed by the licensed electrical contractor, and the FDNY lists an amended A-433 among its top ten tips to avoid defects. The approved plans, the TM-1, the A-433, the monitoring contract and the as-built riser all have to be on site on test day.

The National Fire Protection Association standard, NFPA 72, is the installation and testing standard underneath all of this, but New York City adopts it with its own changes in Appendix Q of the Building Code. That is why a NFPA 72 fire alarm installation that would pass in another state can still draw objections here: the city's version and the FDNY's rules sit on top of the national standard. Fire alarm code compliance on a New York City project means building to the approved drawings, to NFPA 72 as the city modified it, and to the FDNY's own filing and testing rules.

Design to Filing

What FDNY Fire Alarm Drawings for a New NYC Building Must Show

Fire alarm construction drawings for a New York City filing are not a sketch of where detectors go. The FDNY's own guide lists what the plan examiner expects, and almost every item on that list is something an installer will later have to build or prove. When we are asked in during design, this is the list we review with the engineer, because a drawing that misses one of these items comes back with an objection and costs weeks. Fire alarm system design requirements in the city start here:

What the plans must showWhy the installer cares
A legend defining every fire alarm device symbolThe crew builds from symbols; a missing symbol is a device nobody orders.
Every device on the floor plans: panel, central office connection, data gathering panels, power supplies, initiating devices, notification appliances and power componentsPower supplies and transponders need space, power and a home run, and they are the items most often left off.
Device counts on the riser diagram that match the floor plansMismatched counts are a classic objection, and the as-built riser at the end has to match what was actually installed.

An input/output matrix specific to the designed systemThe matrix becomes the panel program and the script for the acceptance test.

The same guide lists the device rules the FDNY sees violated most often at the test. Smoke detectors must be at least 36 inches from supply diffusers and return openings and out of direct airflow, which is easy to draw and easy to get wrong once the mechanical contractor moves a diffuser. Carbon monoxide detection is required at CO-producing equipment and must be arranged to shut that equipment down, and if CO detection is added, the job description needs a DOB Post-Approval Amendment to include it. Fans over 2,000 cfm must shut down on any automatic alarm, and elevators must recall on waterflow or on activation of the smoke or heat detectors associated with the elevator. Each of those lines becomes a relay, a module and a test on the matrix.

There are also documents that live in different worlds and get confused. The fire alarm construction drawings are the sealed design filed with the FDNY. The fire alarm shop drawings are the installer's detailed sheets built from that design: device by device, with addresses, wire paths and termination details. The fire alarm as built drawings are the final record of what was installed, including the as-built riser diagram the FDNY's guide asks for under 3 RCNY 105.01. All three exist on a well-run project, and the as-built is the one the building will rely on for the next twenty years.

Plan review timing, objections and resubmission

The FDNY says the initial plan review can take up to eight weeks, and that resubmitting within six months of an objection date avoids a new filing fee. In practice that means two things for a schedule. The fire alarm design should be filed as soon as the architectural layout is stable enough to support it, not when the ceilings are framed. And objections need an owner: the design professional answers them, but the installer is often the one who knows whether the answer can be built. We stay on the objection thread on our jobs so a response does not create a field problem three months later.

Every project also needs a plain answer to one of the most practical questions in the industry: does my building need a fire alarm permit, and when does a building need a fire alarm system in the first place? In New York City the occupancy classification in Building Code Section 907 decides it, with thresholds such as an assembly occupant load of 300 or more, business occupancies with 500 or more occupants or more than 100 above or below the level of exit discharge, every educational and institutional occupancy, mercantile fire areas over 12,000 square feet, and residential R-2 buildings with dwelling units three or more stories above the lowest exit discharge or 16 or more units (as summarized from BC 907.2 by New York Engineers). The design professional makes the final call from the code itself; if a system is required, the FDNY filing follows, and fire alarm system approval comes only after the witnessed test. Fire alarm system inspection requirements after that are set by the Fire Code and NFPA 72.

Your Questions Answered

NYC New Construction Fire Alarm Questions, Answered

Before the drawings are sealed

Our architect has not drawn the fire alarm yet and we are already pricing the job. Can an installer give us a budget without drawings?

Not a number we would stand behind, and we will not pretend otherwise. Before drawings exist, the honest help an installer can give is a list of the drivers: occupancy, height, number of floors and tenants, whether voice evacuation is required, how many interfaces the building has and how hard the cable routes are. A fire alarm installation estimate built on those drivers belongs in the owner's budget only as a placeholder until the sealed design exists; the real fire alarm installation quote comes from the drawings.

Should the fire alarm installer be hired before or after the engineer files the TM-1?

Either works, but earlier is usually better on complex buildings. On a design-build arrangement the installer reviews the design for buildability before it is filed, which avoids objections and field changes. On a traditional bid, the installer is hired after approval and prices exactly what was approved. What does not work well is hiring the installer after the ceilings are framed and hoping the approved drawings and the field conditions agree.

Who decides between voice evacuation and horns and strobes on a new NYC building?

The design professional, reading the Building Code for the occupancy and height. Fire alarm emergency voice communication is required in some occupancies and buildings and is a choice in others. Our part is practical: speaker circuits, amplifier space and power, and the fire command location all need room early, so we raise the question at the first coordination meeting rather than at rough-in.

During construction

The mechanical contractor moved diffusers after our detectors were laid out. Does that matter?

It can matter a great deal. The FDNY lists smoke detectors too close to supply diffusers and returns among the defects it sees most, and the minimum is 36 inches and out of direct airflow. When ductwork moves, we check every detector near it against the new layout, and anything that has to move goes back to the design professional, because relocating a device on an approved plan may need an amendment.

Can fire alarm cable share a raceway or sleeve with other low-voltage cabling on a fit-out?

Fire alarm wiring installation follows NFPA 72 and the electrical code as the city adopted them, and the design shows the pathway and survivability required for each circuit. In general we keep fire alarm circuits in their own identified pathway, red cable and labeled raceway where the design calls for it, so nothing installed later by another trade can be confused with a life-safety circuit. Where the drawings require a rated pathway, it is not shared.

Our building has no Certificate of Occupancy yet because it is under construction. Can the fire alarm still be filed?

Yes. The FDNY's guide anticipates this: when there is no Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A, the filing includes a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings. Your expediter or design professional obtains it; the floor numbers on the fire alarm plans then have to match what DOB recognizes.

How early must elevators, fans and sprinklers be finished for the fire alarm test?

Before the FDNY test, not after it. The FDNY's pre-inspection list requires elevator automatic recall, Fireman's Service and fan shutdowns to work and the sprinkler system to be operational. On a ground-up building that makes the fire alarm test one of the last milestones, and it is why we track every interface on a list from the first week of rough-in.

Tenant fit-outs in existing buildings

We are a tenant building out a full floor. Do we need our own fire alarm panel?

Usually not. In most NYC office buildings the tenant's devices connect to the base-building system through the floor's loop or a sub-panel, and the filing references the base-building Letter of Approval. Whether there is enough address capacity and power on that loop is the first thing we check with the landlord's fire alarm vendor.

The landlord says only their own fire alarm company may touch the base-building panel. Can our installer still do the floor?

Often yes, with a clear split. Our crew installs the devices, cable and modules on the tenant floor to the approved drawings, and the landlord's vendor programs the base-building panel and joins the test. We agree on that split, the point list and the test date in writing before any work starts, so nobody discovers the boundary on test day.

Our fit-out adds a server room and a pantry. Do those change the fire alarm design?

They can. A server room may call for early-warning detection or a suppression interface, and a pantry with cooking equipment may need heat detection instead of smoke detection to avoid unwanted alarms. Both are design questions for the engineer of record, and both are easier to settle before the ceiling grid goes in.

Testing, approval and turnover

What has to be on site the day the FDNY Fire Alarm Inspection Unit comes to test?

The FDNY's list is specific: at least one technician holding an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness, enough staff to run the test, radios, a licensed plumber or knowledgeable staff to flow water for the waterflow devices, an elevator mechanic, a ladder, tools and canned smoke, access to every room, and the A-433, TM-1, monitoring contract, as-built diagram and approved plans. A missing elevator mechanic alone can end a test before it starts.

Does the central station monitoring have to be live before the acceptance test is booked?

Yes. The FDNY requires a central station monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60 application, and the monitoring connection with the approved central station must be active before the inspection is scheduled. Fire alarm central station communicator installation is therefore one of the first finish items, not the last.

What does the owner receive at turnover of a new fire alarm system?

On our jobs: the approved plans, the as-built drawings and riser, the battery calculations, the panel program and point list, the device test records, the manuals and the Letter of Approval once the FDNY issues it. That package is the fire alarm system certification record the building keeps on site, and it is what the next inspector and the next service company will ask for.

Addressable or conventional on a new NYC job

What is an addressable fire alarm system, in practice? It is a system in which every detector, pull station and module has its own address on a signaling line circuit, so the panel knows exactly which device in which room is in alarm or trouble. So what is the difference between addressable and conventional fire alarms on a new building? It is not mainly about cost; it is about information. A new building with interfaces, tenants and a long life is almost always designed addressable, because maintenance, troubleshooting and future expansion all depend on knowing which device is speaking. Conventional fire alarm system installation, or conventional fire alarm installation on a single small tenant space, still has a place where the space will stay simple; later conventional fire alarm repair or conventional fire alarm replacement on such a system is straightforward.

Addressable fire alarm installation, which some manufacturers market as intelligent fire alarm installation for an intelligent fire detection system, brings tasks that conventional jobs do not have: fire alarm device addressing during installation, addressable fire alarm device installation with each address labeled, and addressable fire alarm system programming and fire alarm device programming that turn the engineer's matrix into panel logic. Years later the same address map makes addressable fire alarm troubleshooting, addressable fire alarm system repair and any addressable fire alarm system upgrade far quicker. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation on NYC Construction Projects

AI answers and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to describe fire alarm installation as if it were one national process with one national price list. New York City is not that market. These are the claims we hear repeated most, and what actually happens on a construction project in the five boroughs.

"A licensed electrician can design and install the fire alarm"

In New York City the design and the installation are two different roles. The FDNY's filing guide says a New York State registered design professional, a Professional Engineer or a Registered Architect, prepares the plans and files the documents. The installer, a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor with an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, builds what was approved.

An installer can contribute a great deal to the design, from product data to cable routes, but the seal on the drawings is the design professional's. A proposal that says the installer will "design and file" without naming an engineer or architect is missing a step the FDNY requires.

This matters for scheduling as much as compliance. The design professional's work, the plan exam and any objections take weeks before installation can legally begin, so the engineer belongs on the project calendar first.

"Fire alarm installation takes a few days"

The physical work on a small fit-out can be short, but the project is not. The FDNY puts the average from plan submission to final inspection and approval at about twenty to twenty-four weeks, with the initial review alone taking up to eight weeks.

How long does fire alarm installation take, then? On a new building the honest answer has three parts: the design and filing time, the installation time that follows the other trades, and the lead time to get the FDNY test scheduled, for which the Department recommends 40 to 70 business days.

Project teams that plan around the installation days alone are the ones who end up with a finished floor that cannot be occupied because the fire alarm has not been accepted.

"You can get a price per square foot online"

Directory sites publish national averages for fire alarm installation pricing, and AI answers repeat them. Those numbers describe nothing about a New York City project, where the sealed drawings alone can run into the thousands before a single device is ordered, and where the FDNY filing, the interfaces and the test day crew all sit on top of the installation.

How much does commercial fire alarm installation cost in New York City? It costs what the approved drawings, the building and the schedule require, and the only reliable figure is a written proposal built from those drawings after a review of the site. That is why we do not publish ranges.

Government fees are the exception that can be stated: for example, the FDNY lists a 105 dollar application fee for the Fire Alarm Company Certification itself and for a Notice of Defect document submission. Everything else depends on the building.

"Any fire alarm company can do the work"

The FDNY says any company that installs, inspects, tests or services any component of a fire alarm system other than smoke detectors alone must hold company certification, and at least one principal must hold an S-97 Certificate of Fitness backed by NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license.

Company certification is renewed every year, and the FDNY states that servicing must stop if it expires, even if individual technicians' certificates are still valid. Who installs commercial fire alarm systems in the city is therefore a question with a checkable answer.

How do I find a licensed fire alarm contractor, then? Ask for the company's FDNY approval letter and number, the name of its S-97 principal, a certified fire alarm technician's Certificate of Fitness for whoever will test, and its insurance, and check them before signing. Fire alarm contractor licensing in the city is layered, and an experienced fire alarm contractor will hand those documents over without being asked twice. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems tells every client to do this with every contractor on the job, including us. The best commercial fire alarm installer for a project is the one whose paperwork checks out and whose commercial fire alarm installers have built the same kind of building before, not the one with the biggest advertising budget; a local fire alarm installation company or a fire alarm service company that will still answer the phone in year five matters as much as the first install.

"The inspector just checks that it works"

What happens during fire alarm acceptance testing in New York City is far more structured. The FDNY expects the installation to be 100 percent complete, the panel permanently installed and free of alarm, supervisory and trouble conditions, permanent primary and secondary power, and the elevator recall, Fireman's Service and fan shutdowns working.

The test itself walks the input/output matrix: devices are operated, waterflow is flowed by a plumber or knowledgeable staff, the elevator mechanic is present for recall, and the paperwork, including the A-433, TM-1, monitoring contract and as-built diagram, is checked against what is on the walls.

A fire alarm acceptance inspection that finds problems ends with a Notice of Defect rather than a Letter of Approval, and the correction and resubmission add weeks. A thorough pre-test is the cheapest insurance a project can buy.

"Wireless devices avoid all the wiring"

Listed wireless fire alarm devices exist, and there are places in existing buildings where they solve a real problem. On new construction with open walls, they are rarely the right answer, because the walls are open now and wired circuits are what most designs and most buildings are built around.

Every wireless device still reports to a listed panel, still needs to appear on the approved drawings, and still needs batteries that become a maintenance item for the life of the building. The decision belongs to the design professional, and it has to be accepted by the FDNY like any other device.

When a project wants to skip wiring to save a few days on rough-in, we usually point out that the wiring is the shortest part of the schedule; the filing and the test are the long parts.

"Monitoring can be set up after the building opens"

The FDNY requires a central station monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60 application, and the monitoring connection must be active before the inspection can be scheduled. Monitoring is part of getting the system accepted, not an add-on for later.

That means the communicator, its phone or cellular path and the central station account need to be ordered during construction. A project that leaves monitoring for the last week usually loses a test date.

It also means the central station has to be one the FDNY approves. Changing central stations later is possible, but the FDNY treats a changeover as its own filing, with its own recommended lead time of 10 to 20 business days.

When a young system develops a fault

Why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble, owners ask, when the system is only a year old? A trouble signal means the panel has found something that could stop it working: an open circuit, a short, a ground, a missing device, a communication problem or low battery. What causes a fire alarm ground fault on a newer building is usually physical: a nicked conductor, a screw through a cable, water in a box, or a device base installed against bare metal. Fire alarm ground fault troubleshooting and fire alarm ground fault repair start at the panel's ground fault indicator and work out by isolating circuits until the fault moves.

Other faults have their own paths. Fire alarm circuit troubleshooting has a method for each fault. Fire alarm open circuit repair follows the as-built drawings to the end-of-line device or the break. Fire alarm short circuit troubleshooting looks for crushed or mis-terminated cable. Fire alarm SLC troubleshooting and fire alarm signaling line circuit repair on an addressable loop start with the device the panel says is missing and the ones next to it on the loop. Fire alarm battery trouble repair and fire alarm power supply repair check the charger, the battery date and the load. A fire alarm supervisory trouble usually means a closed valve, a tamper switch or a supervised function, not a fire. A fire alarm intermittent trouble that clears itself is the hardest kind, and it is where the panel's event history and the photographs taken during construction pay off.

FAQ

NYC New Construction Fire Alarm FAQ

When should a general contractor bring the fire alarm installer onto an NYC construction schedule?
+

Ideally during design, and no later than the week the fire alarm drawings are approved. The installer needs time for shop drawings, equipment lead times and rough-in before framing closes, and the FDNY estimates about twenty to twenty-four weeks from plan submission to final approval, so a late start usually pushes the acceptance test past the opening date.

Can Abstract Enterprises Security Systems seal or file our fire alarm drawings with the FDNY?
+

No. In New York City the fire alarm plans are prepared and filed by a New York State Professional Engineer or Registered Architect. We work with your design professional, or one you choose, and provide product data, layouts and field knowledge, but the seal and the TM-1 filing belong to the engineer or architect of record.

What should we check before hiring a fire alarm contractor for a New York City project?
+

Ask for the company's FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate approval letter, the name of its S-97 principal, proof of insurance and the licensed electrical contractor who will sign the A-433. The FDNY renews company certification every year, so check that it is current on the day you sign, and ask every bidder for the same documents, including us.

Is a tenant fit-out on one floor filed with the FDNY separately from the base building?
+

A fit-out that adds or changes fire alarm devices is filed as its own job by the tenant's design professional, and the FDNY expects the filing to include the Letter of Approval for the existing base-building system the new devices connect to. The landlord or its managing agent usually supplies that letter.

Why did our fire alarm drawings come back with an objection, and what happens next?
+

Common reasons are device counts on the riser that do not match the floor plans, floor numbers that do not match the Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A, missing circuit classes or survivability, or no input/output matrix. The design professional answers the objection and resubmits; the FDNY says resubmitting within six months of the objection date avoids a new filing fee.

Can fire alarm rough-in start before the FDNY approves the plans?
+

The FDNY's guide says installation should take place only after plan approval. On real schedules, teams sometimes stage material or coordinate locations earlier, but anything installed before approval is at the owner's risk if the plan examiner changes it, so we discuss that risk in writing before any device goes in.

Who books the FDNY acceptance test for a new fire alarm system in NYC?
+

The test is requested with the B-45 form, supported by the sealed A-433, the PW-1 or work authorization, the TM-1 and approved plans, the TB-60 for new systems and the central station contract. The FDNY recommends 40 to 70 business days of lead time, so the request should go in while the system is still being finished.

What makes an FDNY fire alarm acceptance test fail on a new building?
+

Most failures come from things that were not ready: a panel still showing trouble, temporary power, elevator recall or fan shutdown not working, the sprinkler system not operational, a missing elevator mechanic or plumber, or paperwork that does not match the walls. A full pre-test against the input/output matrix removes most of them.

Does a new restaurant in an NYC mixed-use building need its own fire alarm panel?
+

It depends on the building and the occupancy, and the design professional decides it from the code. Many restaurants connect to the building's system; others with assembly occupant loads or separate filings get their own panel. Either way, the hood suppression system is monitored and heat detection is used where cooking would cause smoke detector alarms.

What documents should the owner keep after a new fire alarm system is accepted?
+

Keep the Letter of Approval, the approved plans, the as-built drawings and riser, the TM-1 and A-433, the central station contract, battery calculations, the panel program and point list, and every test and inspection record. Future inspections, repairs and modifications all start from these documents, and they belong on the premises.
