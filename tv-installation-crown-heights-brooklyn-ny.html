The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need TV Installation

Crown Heights is older than its houses. The Brooklyn Eagle's history of the neighborhood notes the land was first settled in the 1600s and farmed by enslaved Africans for their Dutch owners, and that after emancipation some families bought property in Weeksville and Carrville, two of the earliest free Black communities in the country. The Weeksville Heritage Center on Buffalo Avenue still keeps the Hunterfly Road houses from that settlement. The move from farmland to suburb began, by 6tocelebrate's account, in 1854, when the Lefferts family, the area's main landowners, auctioned off parcels. Olmsted and Vaux conceived Eastern Parkway in 1866, and NYC Parks records that it was built between 1870 and 1874 and runs 2.2 miles from Grand Army Plaza to Ralph Avenue; it became a National Scenic Landmark in 1978. The name came from Crown Hill and, according to cityneighborhoods.nyc, came into common use in the 1890s. CityLand notes that rapid building followed the opening of the Kings County Elevated Railway in 1888. All of that matters to a TV installer for one reason: the houses went up in a short, intense burst, using the materials and methods of that moment, and those materials are still in your walls.

Brownstoner's history of the area puts most of Crown Heights North's row houses in the last decade of the nineteenth century and the first decade of the twentieth, and real estate guides describe most of the neighborhood's housing as limestone and brownstone row houses built between about 1890 and 1915. The oldest house still standing, the George B. and Susan Elkins House at 1375 Dean Street, dates from the 1850s. By the 1930s, many of the area's mansions had been replaced by large Tudor and medieval-inspired apartment buildings along St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues, and 6tocelebrate notes that when the subway reached the area in 1920, apartment building picked up speed. The Brooklyn Eagle adds that walk-up apartments went up in the 1940s for the large numbers of Lubavitch Hasidim who had emigrated from the Soviet Union. South of Eastern Parkway, the developer Charles Goell built Colonial Revival houses, and many Crown Heights South row houses have English-style stoops that rise only a few steps above the sidewalk. Each of those generations used a different wall. A row house of 1895 has plaster on wood lath on its partitions and plaster laid on brick along its party walls. A 1930s apartment house often has plaster over hollow tile or block between rooms. A 1940s walk-up can be different again. A TV bracket that holds perfectly in one of these can fail quietly in another if the anchor is chosen for the wrong material.

Then there are the landmark districts. Gothamist reported that the Landmarks Preservation Commission protected 472 buildings in Crown Heights North in 2007, built from the 1860s to the 1930s, in an area running from Pacific Street to Dean Street, Prospect Place and St. Mark's Avenue and from Bedford to Kingston Avenues. The second phase, designated on June 28, 2011, covers 630 buildings and sites by the commission's own count, and the third, designated on March 24, 2015, sits mostly between Brooklyn and Albany Avenues from Pacific Street to Lincoln Place, taking in the short stretches of Hampton, Revere and Virginia Places. CityLand reported that the third phase brought the total in the Crown Heights area to 1,752 landmarked buildings and sites. Sources even disagree about how many districts there are: the commission's walking tour counts three historic districts, while one commercial brokerage counts four, likely because it includes the small Park Place district designated in 2012. For most TV jobs inside a row house none of this changes the drilling, because the commission's permits concern a building's exterior. Where it does matter is anything that would show from the street: a new cable line run up the front of the house, a dish or an antenna on the roof, a box fixed to the facade. We cover those cases in the questions further down.

The newest layer is still going up. Real estate guides note mid-rise condos built near Franklin and Nostrand Avenues since 2015, and one agency describes the new mid-rise buildings near the subway hubs as bringing floor-to-ceiling glass to the neighborhood. New York YIMBY reported in June 2026 that six new buildings in Crown Heights would add a combined 230 rental and condominium units, among them The Eve, a seven-story, all-electric building at 827 Sterling Place between Nostrand and Rogers Avenues whose amenities include a virtual doorman. A separate YIMBY roundup the same month listed Prosper Brooklyn, a 17-story building at 1042 Atlantic Avenue, a ten-story building at 1070 Pacific Street and an eight-story building at 311 Eastern Parkway. 6sqft has covered The Arch, the 16-story, 355-unit rental at 1101 President Street on the Bedford Union Armory site, Loden at 54 Crown Street with 569 units, and a ten-story rental at 962–972 Franklin Avenue. And 6sqft reports that the Atlantic Avenue Mixed-Use Plan, a rezoning of 21 blocks in Bed-Stuy and Crown Heights, allows roughly 4,600 new homes and buildings up to 18 stories. For a TV, those buildings mean drywall instead of plaster, often on light-gauge steel rather than wood, fewer solid walls because of the glass, and management offices that want paperwork before a contractor arrives.

Living here also shapes the visit itself. Eastern Parkway carries the West Indian Day Parade each year, and the avenues have their own rhythms: Brick Underground describes Nostrand Avenue as having a West Indian feel and Kingston Avenue as the street of stores, shuls and schools serving the Hasidic community. Lobbies are another concern. News 12 Brooklyn reported a man who got himself buzzed into 240 Crown Street, a 135-unit building, and took packages from the lobby, and another case in which a thief walked into the foyer of 1492 Union Street and left with an e-scooter. In October 2026 crownheights.info reported an arrest in a package theft after Shomrim volunteers retrieved video, and COLlive counted more than two dozen arrests over the summer of 2026, including package thefts at Lincoln Place, Carroll Street and President Street near Albany Avenue. None of that is unusual for Brooklyn, but a 65-inch TV is the most tempting box a lobby ever holds, so we plan deliveries around the install. Some buildings bring other problems: News 12 described a Sterling Place building with 435 open HPD violations after years of leaks, and public records show a March 2026 HPD violation for an unsafe electrical wiring condition at 1367 Dean Street. Water-softened plaster and loose outlets are things we find and point out, not things we mount over.

Same-Day Repair in Crown Heights

Need Repair in Crown Heights? A Bracket Pulling Out of Lath, a Screen Sagging Over the Mantel, or a Power Cord Fished Through the Wall

The urgent calls we get from houses of Crown Heights' age tend to be one of three things. The first is a screen that has started to droop on one side or tip forward. On a row-house partition, that usually means the screws went into the wood lath strips rather than the stud behind them, or that plastic plugs were pushed into the plaster alone. Lath is thin and plaster is hard on the face and crumbly underneath, so a fixing that felt tight on the day loosens a little every time the TV is pulled or bumped. The second is a TV over a mantel that was hung on the chimney breast with anchors driven into the mortar between the bricks instead of into the bricks themselves, which works loose as the softer mortar crumbles around it. The third is what an earlier installer left behind: the TV's own power cord pushed into a hole above the screen and pulled out of another near the baseboard. That shortcut is not allowed under the electrical code, because a flexible cord hidden inside a wall can be crushed, overheat in a bundle and never be inspected.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back up. If two people can safely lift it, take the weight off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV then goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, the remount is priced as a new mount on that wall, from $179.99 with the plaster or brick line added as it applies. A hidden power cord comes out and is replaced either by an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) on a framed wall or by a paintable cord cover ($59) on masonry. If we find that the real problem is the wall itself, such as plaster that has gone soft from a leak, a crack running through the chimney breast or a loose outlet box, we tell you plainly so you can bring in the owner, the super or an electrician, and we do not hang anything on it until it is fixed.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Crown Heights' Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every TV call we get from Crown Heights. They differ in what the walls are made of, how far the screen has to travel to reach the room, and who besides you has a say over the wall. Those three things settle the plan long before anyone talks about brackets or brands.

The limestone or brownstone row house

Three- and four-story houses from about 1890 to 1915, many on blocks facing Eastern Parkway and inside the Crown Heights North districts. Plaster partitions, brick party walls and fireplaces on the parlor floor.

The row house split into apartments

The same houses divided into flats over the decades, where a TV wall may be a later partition, a filled-in doorway or the original brick, and the owner often lives on another floor.

The freestanding and Colonial Revival house

From the 1850s Elkins House on Dean Street to the Colonial Revival houses Charles Goell built south of the parkway, often with low English-style stoops in Crown Heights South.

The 1930s apartment house

Tudor and medieval-inspired buildings on St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues, with elevators or long stairs, mixed masonry and partition walls, and a super who knows every riser.

The walk-up and the brick two-family

1940s walk-ups and the brick two- and three-family houses toward Weeksville and the east side, where stairs, shared walls and a landlord downstairs shape the job.

The new mid-rise

Buildings near Franklin and Nostrand since 2015 and the newest along Atlantic Avenue, Sterling Place and President Street: drywall, often on steel, lots of glass and an office with rules.

The limestone or brownstone row house: two kinds of wall in one front parlor

The front parlor of a Crown Heights row house is where most TV conversations start, and it nearly always offers two very different walls. The long side walls are usually party walls shared with the houses next door, and in a row of brick-built houses they are solid masonry under a coat of plaster. The wall between the front and back parlors, or the one along the hall, is usually a framed partition with plaster on wood lath. The party wall is the strongest place in the house for a heavy screen, but it means a hammer drill, masonry anchors set into the brick itself rather than the mortar, and the brick or stone line of $65 on our list. The partition is quieter to drill and can take an in-wall cable kit, but the bracket has to land on studs that, in a house of this age, are rarely on modern spacing, so we find them one at a time. Plaster on lath adds $45. We show you both walls and both prices before you choose.

Ceiling height is the other parlor issue. Parlors in these houses are tall, and owners often want the TV high, above the wainscot or in line with the mantel. A screen hung high on a tall wall is comfortable only if it tilts down toward the sofa, so a tilting bracket is the usual choice on a party wall and a full-motion arm is better saved for long rooms where the dining table also wants a view. We mark the outline with painter's tape and ask you to sit where you watch before a single hole is drilled, because moving tape costs nothing and moving a bracket on old plaster costs a patch. Where the house has its original plaster moldings or a picture rail, we keep fixings clear of them and route any cord cover so it runs below the molding line rather than across it.

The row house split into apartments: a later wall, a filled doorway and an owner upstairs

Many Crown Heights row houses were divided into flats over the twentieth century, and a few have since been joined back together. Each conversion left walls behind that are not the house's original construction. A partition added in the 1950s may be drywall on wood studs; a doorway between the parlors may have been filled with framing and patched with plaster; a kitchen may have been put against what used to be a bedroom wall. These later walls look like the rest of the plaster from the front, which is why we never price a wall from its color. The pilot hole tells us what it is: white plaster powder followed by wood means lath, a quick break into a void means a later framed wall, and red grit means brick. A filled doorway gets special care, because the framing in it may not line up with the studs either side, and a bracket that straddles the patch needs a fixing pattern that reaches solid framing on both edges.

In a split house, the owner is often a neighbor. If you rent the parlor-floor apartment, the owner may live on the garden floor below or on the top floor above, may hear the drilling, and decides whether the TV can go on the wall at all. We suggest a short text before the visit naming the wall, the rough number and size of the holes, and whether the wires will go inside the wall in a rated kit or on the surface in a cord cover. If you own the house and are setting up a rental floor, mounting one TV yourself on a wall you chose, with the cables in an in-wall kit, saves a succession of tenants each drilling their own set of holes over the years.

The freestanding and Colonial Revival house: wider rooms, older framing

Crown Heights has houses that do not fit the row-house pattern. The George B. and Susan Elkins House at 1375 Dean Street dates from the 1850s, older than almost everything around it, and Brownstoner's history of the area describes the Colonial Revival houses Charles Goell built south of Eastern Parkway in the twentieth century. In Crown Heights South, 6tocelebrate notes, many row houses have English-style stoops that rise only a few steps above the street. For a TV, the difference is mostly about rooms and walls. Freestanding and semi-attached houses have exterior walls on more sides, more windows, and fewer long solid walls, so the best TV wall is often an interior partition, which in houses of these periods is usually framed and plastered.

The low stoops of Crown Heights South are a small mercy on install day: a big box goes in at nearly street level instead of up a full flight of brownstone steps. Inside, these houses often have living rooms that open to a dining room through a wide archway, and the wall beside the arch is a favorite spot for a screen. That wall usually hides a header above the opening and doubled framing at its edge, which is excellent for a fixing if we can find it and a problem if a bracket lands half on the header and half on lath. We scan for it, confirm with a pilot hole, and set the bracket so all its fixings bite into the same kind of material.

The 1930s apartment house: elevators, long corridors and walls that fool scanners

The Tudor and medieval-style apartment buildings that replaced mansions along St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues in the 1930s hold a large share of Crown Heights' apartments. Some have elevators and some have long stairs; most have a super who knows exactly what runs through each wall. Interior partitions in buildings of this era were often built of hollow clay tile or gypsum block with plaster over them, and the outer walls, the stair walls and the shafts are masonry. Hollow tile is the wall that catches out handymen: it sounds like solid masonry when you knock, but a standard sleeve anchor can break through its thin face into the void behind, and a plastic plug simply spins in it. The answer is an anchor designed for hollow masonry, set with a slow drill so the face does not shatter, and the full set of fixing points on the bracket plate so no single anchor carries too much.

These buildings also carry steam risers, gas lines to the kitchens and electrical conduit added during later upgrades, usually in the corners and along the walls nearest the kitchen and bathroom. A scanner finds metal pipe, but on plaster over metal lath it can read metal everywhere, so we combine the scan with a look at where the radiators and outlets sit and with the super's knowledge of the building. Where an apartment's best wall carries a riser, we move the screen along it or choose another wall rather than drill near it. When it comes to wiring, the masonry and tile walls of these buildings do not allow a hidden in-wall kit, so the cables usually run in a paintable cord cover or drop straight down into a console below the screen.

The walk-up and the brick two-family: stairs first, then shared walls

The 1940s walk-ups and the brick two- and three-family houses toward Weeksville and the east side are the buildings where the stairs are half the job. A TV over 55 inches in its box is long and awkward, and in a narrow stair with a turn at each landing it may have to go up on its edge, which is fine for a screen still in its original packing and risky for one that has been unboxed and wrapped in a blanket. Keep the box until we arrive. Larger screens are priced from the full Brooklyn list at booking, and for the largest sizes the list calls for two technicians because one person cannot safely carry and lift them. Tell us the floor, the stair width and the turns when you book, and we plan the carry before we plan the wall.

Inside, a brick two-family or three-family often puts the living room TV on a wall shared with the house next door or on an interior wall with the stair behind it. The shared wall is solid; the stair wall is framed and plastered. In a two-family where both households want a TV on the same interior wall, one on each side, the brackets need offset fixings so that neither set of anchors runs into the other. And because the owner often lives in the other unit, the same short note we recommend in split row houses applies here: tell them which wall, roughly what the holes will look like and where the wires will go, before the drill is heard downstairs.

The new mid-rise: drywall, steel, glass and a building office

The buildings finished in Crown Heights since 2015, and the ones going up now on Atlantic Avenue, Sterling Place, Pacific Street, Crown Street, Franklin Avenue and the Bedford Union Armory site, are where we most often find drywall on light-gauge steel studs. A lag bolt that bites a wood stud strips straight out of a thin steel one. The fixing that holds is a toggle or steel-stud anchor rated for the load and spread across at least two studs, and on full-motion arms we often add a backer behind the bracket so the arm has something solid to pull against. Steel studs are a separate line on the full Brooklyn list, and we tell you the figure at booking once your photos show the wall is likely steel. Drywall on wood studs is the base price of $179.99.

The two other things these buildings bring are glass and paperwork. A living room with a wall of windows leaves fewer places for a screen, and the one solid wall may be shared with the corridor or with the apartment next door. Many building offices ask for a certificate of insurance naming the building and its managing agent, set working hours for contractors, and want to know whether the wall will be opened. An in-wall power and cable kit goes through the drywall between studs and puts a recessed outlet behind the TV, which most offices accept when it is installed correctly; a cord cover needs only a few small screws. We check which one your building allows before the visit, and we send the insurance certificate ahead of time so nobody is turned away at the desk.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Quarter-Hour in a Crown Heights Parlor

These notes describe how our technicians work in the kinds of houses Crown Heights is made of. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Read the room before the wall. The technician looks at which walls are shared with the houses next door, where the chimney breast sits, where the radiators are, and whether the outlets are original baseboard boxes or surface-mounted boxes in conduit added later. Conduit runs somewhere, and we want to know where before we drill.

Knock, then scan, then distrust the scan. A row of knocks at screen height maps hollow and solid. The scanner confirms framing and metal, but on plaster the reading is a hint, not an answer. A magnet will not find a stud through thick plaster at all, so we do not rely on one.

One pilot hole, behind the plate. The technician drills a single small hole where the bracket plate will cover it. White powder then wood means lath; white powder then red grit means brick; a sudden drop means a void. That one hole decides the anchor. If it changes the line we quoted, you hear about it before any more drilling.

Tape, sit, decide. Painter's tape marks the screen. You sit on the sofa or lie in the bed where you watch, and we check height, glare from the front windows, the mantel shelf and the swing of the parlor doors.

Drill once, clean as we go. Masonry dust travels, so a vacuum nozzle sits under the bit on brick and the drilling happens in one short burst after we warn anyone resting in the next room.

Wall by Wall

Lath, Brick, Stone or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Crown Heights Wall and What It Adds

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, bracket separate. Very little in a Crown Heights row house is drywall on wood studs, so we explain the wall lines before anything else. Each exists because that wall needs different tools, different anchors and more time; none exists because an old house is an excuse to charge more.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The original partitions of houses from the 1890s and 1900s: narrow wooden strips nailed across studs, covered in two or three coats of plaster that can add up to close to an inch. Plaster is strong when pressed and weak when pulled. A screw into a lath strip, or a plug in plaster alone, can feel solid on the day and let go months later. The fixing that lasts goes through plaster and lath into the stud behind, with the pilot hole drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward around it. Where the bracket cannot reach a stud, we use a toggle rated for the load that spreads its grip behind the lath rather than in the plaster.

Plaster laid on brick (+$65). Party walls, chimney breasts and many exterior walls in Crown Heights are brick with plaster applied straight onto them, sometimes thick and uneven. One installer's guide to Brooklyn notes that brownstones and Crown Heights row houses often have brick hidden behind layers of plaster, and it is the most common surprise on a wall that was booked as plain plaster. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor goes into the brick itself and not the mortar joint, which is softer and can crumble around a fixing. The anchor also has to be long enough to pass through the plaster coat and still seat its full depth in the brick, so a thick plaster coat changes the hardware even though it does not change the line on the list. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and on older, softer brick we use more fixings at a lighter load each.

Stone (+$65). The cellars and garden floors of many row houses have foundation walls of stone or rough brick, and owners finishing a family room downstairs often want a TV there. Stone is irregular, so the bracket is fixed to the soundest stones with the plate shimmed level where the face is uneven, and the dust is controlled because stone dust in a finished cellar goes everywhere. The same $65 line covers brick and stone.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). Later partitions in split row houses, renovated kitchens and some newer buildings. This is the wall the $179.99 base price describes, with lag bolts into two studs and, where the wall allows it, a clean path for an in-wall kit.

Steel studs, concrete and hollow tile (quoted at booking). New mid-rises often use steel studs; some apartment buildings have poured concrete or hollow tile partitions. Each needs its own anchors and each is a separate line on the full Brooklyn list. When your photos suggest one of these, we give you the exact figure before you book.

Hiding the wires the legal way in an old house

After "mount it," the most common request is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole above the screen and pull it out of another near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls, floors or ceilings, because a cord hidden in a wall can be pinched by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and cannot be checked. The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit: a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, rated cable inside the wall, a recessed outlet behind the TV and a separate pass-through for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit.

The kit needs a hollow, framed cavity between its two openings, so it suits plaster-and-lath partitions and drywall, and it does not suit brick, stone or concrete. On a Crown Heights party wall or chimney breast, the honest choices are a paintable cord cover at $59, run neatly down the wall and painted to match the plaster, or placing the TV directly above a console so the cables drop behind the furniture and are never seen. On lath walls we also check for blocking between studs, the horizontal pieces old framers used, because a fishing path that is blocked partway down needs a second small opening, which we tell you about before cutting the first.

In a rental, an in-wall kit means two neat openings that will need patching if you move and take the TV with you, while a cord cover means a few small screw holes. Both are simple to restore, and both are the kind of thing an owner may want to agree to first, which is where the next section starts.

Row houses and the historic districts

Our row house in the Crown Heights North district has no cable line on the parlor floor. Can a new line be run up the front of the house to reach the TV?

It is better not to, and in a historic district it may not be allowed without approval. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules cover alterations that affect a designated building's exterior, and the commission issues Certificates of No Effect, Permits for Minor Work and Certificates of Appropriateness for that kind of work, even when no Department of Buildings permit is needed. A new cable stapled up a limestone or brownstone facade is exactly the kind of visible change those rules are written for. The usual route is inside: from where the line enters the cellar, up through a closet, a back corner or the space beside a chimney breast, to the parlor. Your provider runs its own line to the house, and we handle the run from the entry point to the TV within the house. If you want something on the exterior anyway, talk to the commission first.

We would like a satellite dish or an antenna on the roof of our landmarked limestone for the TV. What rules apply?

Several, and they point in the same direction. The federal FCC rule that protects people's right to install dishes allows restrictions where they are needed to preserve a recognized historic district, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission treats satellite dishes as rooftop structures, alongside bulkheads and skylights, that are reviewed for visibility. The Department of Buildings separately limits rooftop antennas to no more than six feet above the roof or parapet. In practice, a small dish or antenna set back from the street edge and out of sight from the sidewalk has the best chance of approval, and anything visible needs the commission's sign-off first. We mount TVs inside; for a dish, start with the provider and the commission, then we connect the line once it reaches the room.

The plaster in our 1890s house goes straight onto the brick and is more than half an inch thick in places. Does that change what holds the bracket?

It changes the hardware, not the principle. The bracket is held by the brick, not the plaster, so the anchor has to pass through the full plaster coat and still seat its whole working depth in the brick behind. A short anchor that would be fine on bare brick can end up gripping mostly plaster on a thick coat, and plaster crumbles under that kind of load. Our pilot hole tells us how deep the plaster is before we choose anchor lengths, and we drill the plaster slowly so it does not spall around the hole. Thick plaster also tends to be uneven, so we check the bracket plate for rock against the wall and shim it if needed so the screen hangs flat. The wall is priced on the brick or stone line, $65.

Our limestone house has a tall, narrow strip of wall between the two front windows of the parlor. Is that a sensible place for the TV?

Sometimes, but it is rarely the best one. That strip, the pier between the windows, is usually solid masonry and strong, but it has two drawbacks. A screen there faces into the room with the daylight behind it, so on bright afternoons you are looking at a dark screen framed by two bright windows. And on a parlor-floor front wall facing the street, the light from the TV at night is visible from outside. If the pier is wide enough for the screen with some margin on each side and you mostly watch in the evening with the curtains drawn, it can work, on a fixed bracket. More often we tape the screen outline on the pier and on the opposite party wall, you sit in both directions, and the party wall wins.

The parlor fireplace has not worked in decades. Could the TV's cables travel down inside the old chimney instead of crossing the wall?

One competitor advertises running cables down through the chimney chase in Crown Heights houses, and it can be possible, but only after two questions are answered. First, is the chimney truly dead? In many row houses one flue in the stack still serves a boiler or water heater, or a neighbor's, and nothing goes into a working flue. Second, what goes down it? Low-voltage cables such as HDMI can, in the right conditions, follow a dead chase. Power cannot, because the TV's own cord may not be concealed inside a wall or chase under the electrical code. So the usual result is low-voltage cables down a confirmed-dead chase or the wall beside the breast, and power from an outlet placed properly by an electrician or carried in a paintable cord cover at $59. The above-fireplace line is $130, plus $65 when the breast is brick.

Prewar apartment buildings and walk-ups

Can the cable for an antenna, or a line from the roof, be run along the fire escape outside our apartment window?

No. The Department of Buildings' code notes are explicit that no radio, antennas or other wires may be attached to fire escapes, and a cable along an escape is also a trip hazard for anyone using it in an emergency. We will not run anything there, and a reputable installer should not either. If the line has to come from the roof, it belongs in a path the building approves, usually inside a shaft or along a route the super already uses for other lines. If that is not available, the simpler answer for many prewar apartments is streaming over the apartment's internet connection, with the cable box or provider app on the TV and no roof line at all.

Behind the spot where the TV should go there is an old outlet that wobbles in the wall. Will you just plug the TV into it?

Not until it is fixed. A loose outlet usually means the box behind it has come free of the plaster or the device has worked loose in the box, and either can pull on the wiring every time something is plugged in. Electrical repair is not part of TV installation and we do not open outlets, so we point it out, photograph it for you and suggest the super, the owner or an electrician look at it first. In a rented apartment it is the owner's responsibility, and the city's 311 system has a category for defective or exposed wiring if it is not addressed; HPD records show the kind of violation that follows, such as the unsafe-wiring violation issued at 1367 Dean Street in March 2026. Once the outlet is sound, we can mount the TV and, on a framed wall, take power from it through an in-wall kit.

The super in our 1930s building told us not to drill into the wall that backs onto the stairwell. Which walls in an apartment like ours are usually off limits?

Listen to the super; he knows what is inside. In apartment houses of that era, the walls that most often carry things you do not want to hit are those beside the kitchen and bathroom, where gas lines and plumbing run; the corners and outer walls where steam risers rise to the radiators; and walls added during electrical upgrades with conduit in them. Stair and corridor walls are often fire-separation walls and are commonly built of masonry, and a building may simply not want them touched. That usually leaves an interior partition between two rooms of the same apartment or the wall opposite the windows. We scan, look at where the radiators and outlets sit, and confirm with a pilot hole before any bracket goes up, and we will move the screen rather than drill near a riser.

"A brownstone is a brick building, so every wall takes masonry anchors"

Generic advice about Brooklyn row houses often treats the whole house as masonry and recommends brick anchors for any wall you pick.

Inside a Crown Heights limestone or brownstone, only the party walls, the chimney breasts and the outer walls are brick. A masonry anchor driven into a lath partition grips nothing; a drywall toggle driven into plaster on brick cannot even open.

The only reliable answer is to find out which wall you have. We do it with a look at the room, a scan and one small pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate, and we price plaster at $45 and brick at $65 only once we know.

"Limestone houses are stone inside, so they are the hardest walls to drill"

The limestone on blocks facing Eastern Parkway is mostly the front face of the house. Stone does show up indoors, but usually in the cellar or garden-floor foundation walls, not in the parlor.

Painter's tape marks the screen; you sit where you watch; window glare, the mantel shelf and door swings are checked before drilling.

5. Fix the bracket

Anchors matched to the wall, set into framing or into the brick itself, never the mortar, and leveled to true, not to a century-old floor.

6. Hang and route cables

The TV goes up and is leveled; cables go into an in-wall kit, a paintable cord cover or straight down behind the console.

7. Set up sound and devices

Soundbar placed or wall-mounted, cable box and streaming devices connected, picture and sound adjusted for the room.

8. Test, clean, hand over

Every input and the remote tested with you, dust and packaging cleared, and a plain account of what holds the TV to the wall.

FAQ

Crown Heights TV Installation FAQ

Is Crown Heights South, below Eastern Parkway toward Empire Boulevard, billed the same as the north side?
+

Yes. Every Crown Heights address, in ZIP codes 11213, 11216, 11225, 11233 and 11238, is billed at the same Brooklyn rate, from $179.99 for the base mount. The blocks near Lefferts Avenue and Empire Boulevard are priced exactly like the blocks near Atlantic Avenue.

Why does mounting on the brick party wall cost more than the drywall wall across the same room?
+

Brick needs a hammer drill, masonry bits and anchors set into the brick rather than the mortar, which takes more time and different hardware. On our list, drywall on wood studs is the $179.99 base and a brick or stone wall adds $65, while plaster on lath adds $45.

How many test holes will you drill to find out what is behind my wall?
+

Normally one small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it once the TV is up. Together with a look at the room, a knock test and a scan, that single hole tells us whether the wall is lath, brick, stone or drywall, so there is no searching with large holes across the wall.

Will the technician show me where the bracket goes and which anchors are being used before drilling?
+

Yes. The screen outline is marked in painter's tape so you can check it from where you sit, and the technician tells you what the wall is and what will hold the bracket before the first fixing hole is drilled. If you want a different height or position, that is the moment to say so.

Is there a TV size where your $179.99 starting price no longer applies?
+

Yes. The $179.99 base covers TVs up to 55 inches. Larger screens are separate lines on the full Brooklyn list, and the largest sizes need two technicians. Send the model number and we quote the exact figure for your screen at booking.

Should the soundbar go on the wall under the TV or sit on the console in a row-house parlor?
+

A wall-mounted soundbar, $129, keeps sound and picture together and frees the console. A soundbar placed on the console, $79, is the better choice when the TV hangs on a party wall shared with a neighbor's bedroom, because a bar fixed to that wall passes more bass through it.

How is a setup-only visit billed if it runs past the first hour?
+

The first hour is $195, and time after that is billed in 30-minute blocks. Most visits that only cover a smart TV, Wi-Fi, a cable box and a streaming device stay within the first hour, and the technician tells you before starting another block.

Do you mount screens in school classrooms or social halls along Kingston Avenue?
+

Yes. Classrooms, halls and community rooms are priced from the same Brooklyn list by wall, mount and extras. We work with whoever manages the building, schedule outside class or event hours, send a certificate of insurance, and mount screens high and secure enough for a busy room.

Is a TV in the bedroom priced differently from one in the living room?
+

No. The room does not change the price. Each TV is priced by the wall it hangs on, whether a fireplace is involved, how the cables are handled and any soundbar, so a bedroom TV on a plaster partition costs the same as a living room TV on the same kind of wall.

How much dust does drilling into old plaster and brick make in the room?
+

Some, but it is contained. Drop cloths go down under the work area, a vacuum nozzle is held under the bit when drilling plaster and brick, and the area is cleaned before we leave. Moving fragile things off nearby shelves beforehand is still a good idea.

Our finished cellar has an exposed stone foundation wall. How is a TV on that wall priced?
+

Stone falls under the same line as brick on our list, so it is the $179.99 base plus $65. The bracket goes onto the soundest stones and is shimmed level where the face is uneven, and because nothing can be hidden inside a stone wall, cables usually go in a cord cover or drop to a console.

Who in the household needs to be home for the installation?
+

Someone who can approve the final position and any change in the wall line, ideally the person who watches the TV most, so the height is set for them. If your building needs you to let us in or the owner wants to see the wall first, plan for them to be there at the start.
