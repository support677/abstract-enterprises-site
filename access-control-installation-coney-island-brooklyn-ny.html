The Neighborhood

Why Coney Island Properties Need Access Control

Coney Island is a thin strip of land, and that shapes almost every door on it. Wikipedia describes the peninsula as roughly four miles long and half a mile wide, with a highest point of about seven feet, running from Brighton Beach in the east to the private community of Sea Gate at the western tip. It is one ZIP code, 11224, inside Brooklyn Community District 13, and the 60th Precinct at 2950 West 8th Street covers it along with Brighton Beach, West Brighton and Sea Gate. Three long avenues carry the neighborhood from end to end, Surf, Mermaid and Neptune, crossed by numbered streets from West 1st to West 37th. Within that small grid sits one of the densest collections of large apartment complexes in the borough, a beachfront business district that half-closes every winter, and a gated enclave of houses. Each has a different idea of who should be allowed through a door, and a different person who gets to decide.

The biggest doors here belong to co-ops. Wikipedia lists Trump Village, Warbasse and Luna Park among the cooperative complexes built in the 1960s, and their scale is hard to picture until you read the numbers. Trump Village Section 4, at 2940 West 5th Street, is a single 23-story building of 572 apartments completed in 1964, and its building record already describes “a secure keyless door entry system” and 24-hour security patrolling the grounds. Luna Park Housing is larger still: the State Senate’s 2009 release describes a Mitchell-Lama co-op of 1,600 apartments and more than 5,000 residents, with $21 million committed that year to major exterior and structural repairs, $15 million of it from the city’s housing department. At that size, a fob system is not a gadget. It is a membership list for thousands of people, and the board has to be able to show who holds a credential and why.

That last point is not theoretical in Coney Island. In May 2019, Habitat reported that the Brooklyn District Attorney and the city’s Department of Investigation had charged Luna Park’s board president, treasurer and office manager in a 78-count indictment, alleging $874,000 in bribes to move 18 households into apartments ahead of the waiting list using falsified documents. We mention it only because it shows how much rides on a co-op’s records of who lives where. A well-run access system ties every fob to a named resident of a named apartment, records when it was issued and by whom, and makes it hard for anyone, staff included, to hand out credentials quietly. That is a governance feature, not just a security one, and it is the first thing we discuss with any Coney Island board.

The public housing on the peninsula has already been through a large access control rebuild, and it is worth knowing what was done. When NYCHA broke ground on the $90 million recovery and resiliency project at Coney Island Houses in May 2017, the release listed security doors with “Layered Access Control,” new cameras, standby generators, new electrical systems and flood-protected boilers and equipment annexes for over 1,100 residents in five buildings. A year earlier, the $41 million project at Coney Island Sites 4/5 promised the same layered access doors and boilers raised above projected future flood levels for more than 1,000 residents. And The NYCHA Journal reported in February 2025 that Haber Houses had completed a roughly $55 million FEMA-funded project that included door security access systems. Those doors are NYCHA’s, maintained by NYCHA, and we do not work on them. But private owners on the surrounding blocks see them every day, and they ask us for the same thing on a scale they can afford.

The smaller residential buildings cluster along Mermaid Avenue, where apartments sit over storefronts. When the new Coney Island Business Improvement District moved forward in 2026, News 12 described Mermaid Avenue as a corridor with vacant storefronts, litter, limited security and few streetscape upgrades such as lighting, and quoted the BID’s executive director, Kalvis Mikelsteins: “It’s been 10 years in the making.” In these buildings, the street door is shared by the store’s customers, the tenants upstairs and every courier on the block. A Brooklyn Paper 60th Precinct report described two people taking a delivery driver’s food order inside the lobby of an apartment building on West 31st Street between Mermaid and Surf Avenues by claiming it was theirs. A lobby door that stays locked until the right resident answers, and a separate place for deliveries, takes that kind of encounter out of the hallway.

Then there is the beachfront, which runs on a calendar. Brooklyn Paper reported that Luna Park opened its 2026 season on Saturday, March 28, with Deno’s Wonder Wheel open weekends until Memorial Day. A Brooklyn News Service piece from December 2025 described the other half of the year: hours ending by 10 p.m., businesses without a brand name closing early or shutting down, and Ruby’s closed for the winter. A business that opens for a season hires a new crew each spring and leaves its back door alone for months each winter. Brooklyn Paper’s archive shows why the second part matters: a boardwalk boutique between West 12th Street and Stillwell Avenue whose roll-down door was pried open in 2010, and a store on the boardwalk near West 12th Street entered through its security gate at about 3:10 a.m. in June 2018. Access control will not stop a crowbar, but it can make sure last season’s keys and codes are dead before the new season starts, and report a door that opens when nobody is supposed to be there.

Water is the condition behind all of it. Amusing the Zillion’s photographs after Hurricane Sandy put the flood line at about five feet at Surf and Stillwell, and its follow-up noted that boardwalk businesses had no structural damage but had electrical problems from flooded basements. DNAinfo reported that the boiler room at Surfside Gardens took at least ten feet of water. That is why NYCHA’s Coney Island Houses project included flood panels stored on site and fitted over doors and doorways before a storm, and why the city’s flood-resistant construction rules treat new equipment below the design flood elevation as an increase in noncompliance. For access control it means two practical things: the controller and power supply go above the level the cellar has ever flooded, and every lock is chosen for what it does when the power is cut, because in a storm it will be.

At the western tip, Sea Gate asks a different question. Wikipedia describes about 850 single-family houses behind a private gate, a privately funded Sea Gate Public Safety Department, and Association dues and charges that have averaged about $3,000 a year, with about 54 percent of the housing rented. The Association decides who comes through the gate. The owner decides who comes through the house door, and in a two-family house with a tenant upstairs or a summer rental, that is a real decision with its own credentials. On the newest blocks the question is speed: Hoodline reported in June 2026 that 1709 Surf Avenue, across from Maimonides Park, had topped out at twelve stories with about 420 affordable apartments, due in 2028. A lobby that fills with hundreds of new households needs credentials issued, changed and withdrawn without a line at the management office.

Reader Offline After a Storm, Fobs Rejected at the Laundry, or a Back Door That Won’t Relock: Repair in Coney Island

What the materials mean for the install

The co-op towers are concrete and masonry, which is good news for hardware and slower news for cable. A reader fixed into a mortar joint leaves only a joint to repoint if it is ever moved. But a cable route from a security room to a lobby door in a 1960s building usually means finding existing sleeves and conduit rather than drilling new holes through structure, and in a campus of several towers it often means fiber or network links between buildings. We survey the routes first and price them honestly, because in a big co-op the cable, not the reader, is most of the labor.

The houses are mostly wood, and wood near the ocean moves. Sea Gate porches, side doors and older front doors swell and shrink with the seasons, so a strike that fits perfectly in August can bind in February. We set strikes with a little more adjustment than inland, fit stainless or coated hardware where the door faces the beach, and check the latch alignment again on the follow-up visit. The beachfront businesses are the opposite: steel doors in steel frames, often dented, sometimes filled with grout, and next to a roll-down gate that takes the beating. There the work is cutting a clean pocket for a heavy-duty strike, fitting a closer that can win against the wind off the boardwalk, and mounting a contact so the owner knows when the door was opened in the off season.

One Credential List for Thousands of Households: Fob Governance in Coney Island’s Co-ops

A co-op the size of Luna Park Housing, 1,600 apartments and more than 5,000 residents by the State Senate’s count, does not have an access control problem so much as a records problem that shows up at the door. Every fob is a statement that a particular person is entitled to walk into a particular building. If the list behind the fobs is wrong, out of date, or open to anyone who works in the office, the hardware faithfully lets the wrong people in. That is why the first meeting we have with a Coney Island board is about who will run the list, not which reader to buy.

We set the system up so that each credential belongs to one named person in one apartment, linked to the co-op’s own record of shareholders and authorized occupants. Issuing a fob takes a name, an apartment and the staff member who issued it, and that entry stays in the log. Board officers and the managing agent get separate administrator accounts with different powers: the office can issue and cancel, the board can see reports, and only a small number of people can change the rules themselves. When an apartment is sold or a shareholder dies, the credentials for that apartment come up for review automatically rather than waiting for someone to remember. In a Mitchell-Lama co-op, where the supervising agency has its own rules about occupancy, that trail also helps the board answer questions quickly.

The same records serve residents. Habitat’s 2013 account of a 32-door fob project at a Manhattan co-op described exactly the features shareholders ask for here: groups, so that only bike owners open the bike room and dog walkers and cleaners open only the doors they need; a lost fob cancelled and replaced without touching anyone else; and a console that shows each entry by the name programmed into the fob. That same article is also a fair warning about time. The installer told Habitat, “[We] underestimated the amount of time needed for the wire,” and older basement doors had to be replaced before magnetic strips would fit. In a campus of 1960s towers we plan for both before the board votes: we survey every cable route between buildings and every door that will take a lock, so the price and the schedule the board approves are the ones it gets.

Privacy the board has to put in writing

Because a co-op is a multiple dwelling, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to its smart-access system. HPD’s summary of the law requires a written privacy and data retention policy, consent, collection of only the data the system needs, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and no use of the data to track residents outside the building or to harass them. We hand the board a draft policy that matches the system as installed, set log retention to match it, and keep entry reports limited to the people the policy names. A board that can show residents exactly what is kept, and for how long, finds the fob rollout far easier to pass.

What the Public Housing Rollouts Teach the Private Building Next Door

Few neighborhoods have seen as much access control go in at once as Coney Island’s public housing. NYCHA’s Coney Island Houses, Coney Island Sites 4/5 and Haber Houses projects all included security doors or door access systems, part of a citywide effort that, according to NYCHA’s 2023 testimony to the City Council, put layered access controls on “more than 400 buildings across 80 developments.” NYCHA’s own fact sheet describes what that involves: key fob access points, intercoms, magnetic or electronic locks on doors and gates, a central low-voltage hub, power supplies and backups, and network lines, delivered over a timeline of about three years from planning to the end of construction.

The lessons for a private owner are mostly about what happens after installation day. The same testimony said NYCHA had to issue protocols for monitoring and repairing damaged doors quickly, and called that work costly and time-consuming. In April 2025, CBS New York reported from Queensbridge South in Queens, not Coney Island but the same kind of rollout, that tenants found the new fobs did not work, old keys had stopped working, some doors had jammed, and neighbors were propping doors open with duct tape. The north side of the complex logged 78 door complaints in one month. A propped door is the end of any access system, and it is almost always a sign that the system made the honest resident’s life harder than the old key did.

So for a twenty-unit walk-up on Neptune Avenue or a co-op tower on West 5th Street, we plan the rollout as carefully as the hardware. Old keys keep working at a door until every household has tested its new fob there. Every resident gets a second way in, a code or a spare fob, so a dead phone or a lost key ring does not lock anyone out. The super gets a short list of what to check when a door complains, and a direct line to us. And every controlled door is adjusted to close and latch by itself before any reader goes on it, because a door that has to be pulled shut will be propped. None of that is expensive. All of it decides whether residents trust the system in its first month.

What NYCHA residents can and cannot ask us for

Residents of Coney Island Houses, Haber, O’Dwyer, Surfside, Carey Gardens and the other developments sometimes call us when a lobby fob or a building door stops working. Those doors, readers and fobs belong to NYCHA, and repairs go through NYCHA’s own channels. We do not modify, bypass or add to them. What we can do for a NYCHA household is limited to equipment the resident is allowed to own, and we will say plainly when a request belongs to the housing authority instead.

When the Power Goes Out With the Tide: Choosing How Each Coney Island Door Fails

Every electric lock does one of two things when its power disappears. A fail-safe lock unlocks, so the door opens freely. A fail-secure lock stays locked from outside, while its inside handle still lets people out. On most inland jobs the choice comes up once, in the design meeting. On a peninsula where a storm surge has already put five feet of water at Surf and Stillwell, it is the most important decision on the door schedule, because the day the power fails is also likely to be the day people are leaving the building, coming back to check it, or sheltering inside it.

Exit comes first, and the code settles it. The NYC Building Code requires an electrically locked door on a way out to release when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock to be released by hardware built into the door that works with one hand. So lobby and stair doors that people leave through either use strikes and panic hardware that always open from inside, or magnetic locks that drop on power loss. The choice that remains is about entry. A lobby door on a fail-safe lock stands open to the street during a long outage; a fail-secure strike keeps it locked to outsiders while residents use their keys or the key override. For most Coney Island residential lobbies we recommend fail-secure strikes with a mechanical key override, a battery that runs the reader for hours, and no magnetic lock on the main entrance unless the door hardware leaves no other option.

The battery and controller have to survive the same storm, which is where the city’s flood rules come in. The Department of Buildings’ guidance on flood-resistant construction treats installing new equipment below the design flood elevation as an increase in noncompliance, with exceptions for like-for-like replacement and for spaces that already hold similar older equipment. Whatever the exact rule for a given building, the practical answer for access control is the same: mount the controller, power supply and battery on an upper floor or high on a wall above any level the building has flooded, run cable down to the door, and keep the only things below that level the parts that must be at the door. A reader and a strike can be replaced in an afternoon. A controller full of salt water takes the whole system with it.

Doors that get flood panels or barriers

The NYCHA Journal’s account of Coney Island Houses describes flood panels stored on site and fitted over windows, doors and doorways before a storm. More private buildings and shops on the peninsula now do something similar, with slide-in barriers at a cellar door or panels at a storefront. If yours does, the access hardware has to stay out of the way: no reader or exit button in the panel’s channel, no strike or contact that a barrier presses against, and cable entering the door frame above the barrier height rather than through the sill. We ask to see the panel or barrier on the site visit and lay the hardware out around it, so fitting it before a storm never means unscrewing a reader first.

A season of keys: access for businesses that close for the winter

A beachfront business has a problem most Brooklyn shops never face: every spring it hands out access to a new crew, and every fall it walks away from its doors for months. With keys, that means a ring of copies that grows each year and never quite comes back. With one shared code, it means a number that everyone who ever worked there still knows. Access control solves both with dates. Each worker gets a personal code or card with a start date when they are hired and an end date at the close of the season, so last summer’s staff are locked out before this summer starts without anyone remembering to do it. Managers who return every year keep their credentials, and the owner can extend everyone’s end date with one change if the season runs long.

The winter is the second half of the job. Brooklyn Paper’s reports of a boutique’s roll-down door pried open and a store entered through its security gate at about 3:10 a.m. are reminders that an empty beachfront unit is a target. An access system cannot hold a gate against a crowbar, but it can lock out every staff credential for the closed months, report any door that opens while the business is shut, and show the owner which credential was used if one was. Paired with a door contact on the personnel door and a camera on the gate, that turns a break-in discovered in March into one you hear about the night it happens. We also ask owners whether they cut the power at the breaker for the winter, because a controller with a flat battery cannot report anything; where the power has to go off, we size a battery for the gap or put the controller on a small circuit that stays live.

Deliveries before the doors open

Food businesses on Surf and Stillwell Avenues take deliveries early, often before the first employee arrives. A delivery code for a regular supplier, valid only for the delivery window and only at the receiving door, lets a driver leave product in a back corridor or a locked receiving room without reaching the sales floor or the cash room. Every use shows in the log with the time, which settles the usual argument about when a delivery arrived. When the supplier changes drivers, the code is changed once in the app rather than by cutting a key.

Rules that apply to Coney Island business doors

  • Free exit. The NYC Building Code requires electrically locked doors on a way out to release on power failure and when the fire alarm or sprinklers operate, and magnetic locks need a one-handed release built into the door hardware. Customers and staff must always be able to leave without a key, code or card.
  • Listed hardware. Controllers and electric locks on exit doors should carry a UL 294 listing for access control equipment. We install listed products and keep the listings with the job paperwork.
  • Apartments over shops. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling to be self-closing and self-locking, and an intercom with voice and remote release once there are eight or more apartments. A store’s schedule cannot leave the residential street door unlocked during business hours unless an attendant is on duty.
  • Residents’ data. Where a door serves apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to fobs, apps and other smart access: a written policy, consent, minimum data, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A shop’s staff-only door is outside it; a shared residential door is not.
  • Flood rules. In a building subject to the city’s flood-resistant construction rules, new equipment below the design flood elevation can count as an increase in noncompliance. We keep controllers and power supplies above that level and talk to the owner’s architect or engineer where the building has a flood-resistance filing.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install or service security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Coney Island: Questions Answered

Co-op campuses and their boards

Our board wants every fob tied to the shareholder records, so that nobody in the office can hand one out without a trace. How is that set up?

The system is built around the co-op’s list of apartments and authorized occupants rather than around a box of loose fobs. Each credential is enrolled to one named person in one apartment, and the software will not issue one without both. Every issue, cancellation and change records the staff account that made it and the time, and that history cannot be edited by the same staff account. Board officers get a reporting account that shows, for example, every credential issued in the last month and to which apartments, or every apartment holding more fobs than it has occupants.

We also separate powers. The office can issue and cancel; only two or three named administrators can change door rules or create new staff accounts; and the board can ask for a report at any meeting without going through the office. In a co-op where questions about who lives where have reached the courts before, as Luna Park’s did in 2019, that separation protects honest staff as much as it protects the board.

The parking lot behind our tower fills with beachgoers every summer weekend. Can the lot entrance be controlled with the same fobs as the lobby?

Yes. A gate or barrier arm at the lot entrance can read the same encrypted fobs residents already carry, or windshield tags for registered cars if the board prefers drivers not to lean out of the window. The lot gets its own group, so only residents with a registered space or a parking permit open it, and guest passes can be issued for a single day. The gate log shows which credential opened it and when, which helps the board enforce its own parking rules without a guard standing at the entrance.

The work is mostly at the gate itself: a post-mounted reader at driver height, a detector so the gate closes behind each car rather than staying open for the one behind it, and safety sensors so the arm never comes down on a person or a vehicle. If the lot is reached through an opening that also serves pedestrians, we keep a separate pedestrian gate on its own reader so people are not walking through a vehicle lane.

Our campus has a security patrol at night. Should the guards carry a master fob, a key, or something else?

A personal credential for each guard, not one shared master. Each guard gets a card or fob in a “security” group that opens the doors patrols need, such as stairwells, roof doors where the code allows, mechanical rooms and the perimeter, during the hours of their shift, and the log shows which guard opened which door. If a guard leaves the company, that one credential is cancelled. A shared master fob, by contrast, becomes the most valuable thing on the campus and the one that can never be traced.

For the moment when the power or the network fails, the security office keeps a small number of mechanical override keys in a locked key cabinet with a sign-out sheet. Trump Village Section 4’s building record mentions 24-hour security patrolling the grounds; patrols like that work best when their credentials are managed like any employee’s, by the same office and with the same end dates.

Salt, water and the beach side

The strike on our beach-block lobby door still clicks when a fob is read, but the door does not release. Is the strike worn out or is something else wrong?

The click tells you the reader, the controller and the power are doing their jobs, so the fault is mechanical. On doors near the water the two usual causes are corrosion inside the strike, where salt and grit stop the keeper from swinging freely, and a door that has dropped or shifted so the latch presses hard against the keeper. A door that presses against its strike will not release even when the strike unlocks, because the strike cannot move under the load. We check by pushing the door closed firmly and reading the fob: if it opens then, the door needs adjusting.

If it still will not release, the strike comes out for inspection. A corroded strike is replaced with a model rated for exterior use with sealed or stainless internals, and we fix the door alignment at the same visit so the new strike is not loaded the same way. Spraying lubricant into a strike usually buys a few weeks and then attracts more sand.

Our general contractor wants the access power supplies and controllers in the cellar telecom room of the new building we are putting up near Surf Avenue. Should we push back?

Yes, unless the engineer can show that room sits above the building’s design flood elevation or is protected to the standard the city’s flood-resistant construction rules call for. On this peninsula a cellar telecom room is the most natural place for low-voltage equipment and the most likely place to fill with water. A controller, its battery and its power supplies are exactly the components that do not survive immersion, and losing them takes every door down at the moment residents most need them.

The better design puts the access head-end in a room on an upper floor or a raised mechanical level, with cable running down to the doors, and keeps below-flood equipment to the parts that must be at the door. We can mark this up on the hardware schedule and the low-voltage drawings early, while moving a room is still cheap, and coordinate with the electrical contractor so the circuits feeding the head-end come from a panel above the flood level too.

Walk-ups over the stores

A delivery was taken from a rider inside a lobby a few blocks from us. How can our street door let tenants collect food without the rider, or a stranger, waiting in the hallway?

The Brooklyn Paper report about a food order taken inside a West 31st Street lobby describes the weak point exactly: a hallway where a rider waits, and anyone else who walked in can claim the order. The fix is to keep the handoff outside or in a locked vestibule. A video station at the street door lets the tenant see the rider, talk to them and come down, without buzzing anyone in. Where the building has an outer and an inner door, the station can release only the outer door, so the rider steps out of the weather into the vestibule but cannot reach the stairs.

For a building with a lot of deliveries, a small locked shelf or locker in the vestibule, opened by the rider with a one-time code and by the tenant with their own fob, removes the face-to-face moment entirely. Either way the inner door stays locked to anyone who does not hold a credential for the building, which is what Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a expects of the street entrance in the first place.

Our Mermaid Avenue store and the apartments above share one cellar, with the boiler and the meters down there. Who should be able to open the cellar door?

The building owner decides, and the answer is usually three groups on one reader. The super and the owner’s own staff get access at all hours. The store gets access only if its lease gives it storage space down there, and then only during its business hours. Utility workers and boiler technicians get a temporary code for the day of their visit. Residents usually get no access to the cellar at all unless the laundry is there, in which case they get the laundry door and not the boiler room.

If the store’s storage and the building’s mechanical space are in the same open cellar, a second door or a locked cage around the boiler and meters is worth the cost, because once the cellar door is open, a reader cannot tell the difference between someone fetching stock and someone at the electrical panel. The cellar controller, if it is the building’s, belongs upstairs, not on the cellar wall.

People on summer nights keep trying our building’s door handle on the way back from the amusement area. Can the system tell us how often it happens?

Partly. A reader logs every credential it rejects, so if people are trying old fobs or random cards, that shows as a list of failed reads with the time. A door that is pulled while locked does not register on most systems, because nothing electrical changes. If the attempts are hard enough to rattle the door, a door contact may register brief openings if the latch has play in it, and that by itself is a sign the door needs adjusting.

To actually see what is happening, a camera covering the entrance, set to record on motion at the door, answers the question better than any lock. Most buildings that ask this want reassurance that the door held, and the honest answer is a strong strike, a closer that pulls the door fully shut, and a latch guard over the gap on an out-swinging door.

The beachfront season

We hire about twenty new people for our Surf Avenue food stand every spring. What is the fastest way to set them up, and to cut them all off when the season ends?

Enroll them in a batch with a shared end date. Before opening, the manager enters the new crew in the app, or sends us a list, and each person gets a personal code or card valid from their first shift to the last day of the season. Returning managers keep their credentials from last year with a new end date. On the last day every seasonal credential expires on its own, and if the season runs long, extending the date for the whole group is one change.

Two habits make it work. Hand out credentials only on the day someone starts, not the day they are hired, so no-shows never hold a working code. And give each person their own, never a shared crew code, so that when the cash room or the walk-in is opened at an odd hour, you know who did it.

Our ride’s maintenance crew starts hours before the park opens to the public. Can mechanics reach the workshop and the ride while the visitor gates stay shut?

Yes. The workshop, the parts room and any service gate onto the ride area go on one group for the maintenance crew, valid from early morning, while the public entrances stay on their own opening schedule. Inspectors or the manufacturer’s technicians get dated credentials for the days they visit. Because each mechanic has their own card, the log shows who opened the workshop before dawn on the day something went missing, without anyone keeping a paper book.

Ride areas have their own safety rules and operators’ procedures, and those come first. We do not wire anything into a ride’s controls or its safety interlocks; our work stops at the doors and gates people walk through.

Sea Gate and the west-end houses

Our house was raised after Sandy, and the front door now sits at the top of a long flight of stairs. Where should the keypad and the call button go?

Usually in two places. The lock and its reader or keypad stay at the door at the top, because that is the door being controlled. A call button or a small video station goes at the bottom, at the gate or the foot of the stairs, so a visitor can ring without climbing to the door and you can see who it is before anyone comes up. Family members and caregivers who have a code use it at the top; everyone else is answered from the bottom.

If an older relative lives in the house, a station at the bottom that rings a phone and an indoor screen lets them answer and release the door without going down the stairs at all. The cable between the two runs inside the stair structure or in conduit along it, kept above the level that the yard has flooded, with the controller inside the house on the main floor.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Coney Island

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. We do not attribute any specific claim to those sites; where we quote a figure, we name the vendor that published it. Each claim is set against what a Coney Island door actually needs.

“A fob system is a fob system.”

Generic answers treat every fob as equally secure. They are not, and the difference is invisible to the person holding one.

Swiftlane’s cost guide states plainly that legacy 125 kHz proximity fobs “aren’t encrypted” and can be copied by a cheap duplicator, and that older MIFARE Classic cards have been broken too. Many buildings across the city still run on those formats.

In a co-op campus with thousands of residents, a copyable fob turns the access list into a suggestion. We specify current encrypted credentials and, where a building already has readers, check whether they can read them before anyone orders new fobs.

“A magnetic lock is the simplest lock for a lobby.”

Magnetic locks are easy to mount on almost any door, which is why quick answers recommend them. They hold only while powered.

On a block where outages come with storms, a lobby on a maglock stands unlocked whenever its battery runs down. The NYC Building Code also requires a maglock to be released by hardware built into the door, and to release on power loss, so it is never as simple as it looks.

For most Coney Island lobbies we use fail-secure strikes or electrified panic hardware with a mechanical key override, and keep maglocks for doors where nothing else fits.

“Put the equipment in the basement, out of the way.”

Installation guides written for inland buildings send controllers and power supplies to the basement or the telecom closet by default.

Here, that is where the water goes. Sandy put about five feet of water at Surf and Stillwell, boardwalk businesses were left with electrical problems from flooded basements, and the city’s flood rules now treat new equipment below the design flood elevation as an increase in noncompliance.

We place the head-end above the building’s flood history and run cable down to the doors, so a flooded cellar costs a reader, not the whole system.

“Fobs cost $500 to $5,000 a door.”

ButterflyMX’s guide to NYC access control gives roughly $500 to $5,000 per door for fob and card systems, without a source. Swiftlane’s national guide gives $1,500 to $5,000 per standard door.

Ranges that wide do not help a board vote. Our published Brooklyn list puts a single controlled door with reader or keypad, strike, wiring and ten fobs at $1,500 to $2,500, and a full building system at $15,000 to $40,000.

Coney Island pays the Brooklyn base list, with the figure fixed in writing after the visit.

“Install it once and it runs itself.”

Quick answers describe access control as a one-time purchase. NYCHA’s 2023 testimony to the City Council said the opposite about its own doors: repairing damaged doors quickly is costly and time-consuming, and the systems need funding to maintain, not just to install.

CBS New York’s 2025 report from Queensbridge South showed what happens without that care: fobs that did not work, jammed doors and doors taped open by residents.

We hand over a simple maintenance routine, keep closers and latches adjusted, and treat the first month after go-live as part of the job.

“A gated community does not need locks on the houses.”

Some answers suggest that a private gate makes house-level access control unnecessary.

Sea Gate’s gate and its privately funded public safety department control the perimeter. They do not decide who comes through a house door, and with about 54 percent of the housing rented, many houses have tenants, summer renters, caregivers and contractors coming and going.

A keypad or reader at the house door, with a separate credential for each of those people, covers what the gate cannot.

“Phone apps have replaced fobs.”

Online answers often treat phone keys as the modern default and fobs as legacy.

A Coney Island lobby serves older shareholders who may not carry smartphones, seasonal workers who do not want an employer’s app, and anyone whose phone dies on the beach. NYCHA’s own move to fobs in Queensbridge left some residents locked out even with physical credentials, which is a warning about removing options, not adding them.

We set up phone keys as an option alongside fobs and codes, never as the only way in.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find the Waterline Before You Choose the Lock

This describes how we approach a building on the peninsula in general, not a particular customer. Before we talk about readers, we go down to the cellar with the super and look for the highest water the building has seen. It is usually written on the walls if you know where to look: a tide line of staining on the masonry, rust that stops at the same height on every steel post, a replaced electrical panel sitting a little higher than its conduit expects, or a newer drywall patch with an obvious bottom edge. Supers who were here in 2012 can usually point to the step on the cellar stairs where the water stopped.

That line decides the design more than any catalog. Everything that must not get wet, the controller, the power supplies and the batteries, goes well above it, usually on the first floor or higher. Only the parts that have to be at a cellar door go below it, and those are chosen so that losing them costs an afternoon’s repair, not the building’s whole system. Then we go back upstairs and check the lobby door the way we would anywhere: does it close and latch on its own, is the strike side of the frame sound, and does the closer win against the wind? Only then do we pick the reader.

Sea Gate is a private community with its own gate and its own public safety department, so we ask the homeowner to tell the gate in advance that a contractor is coming, with the date and the company name, and we follow whatever check-in the gate asks for on the day. The work itself is on the homeowner’s property and the homeowner’s door. We do not connect anything to the Association’s gate equipment unless the Association asks us to.

The city has secured money to rebuild the boardwalk. Should a boardwalk shop wait before adding a reader to its back door?
+

Usually not. Brooklyn Paper reported in December 2025 that about $1 billion had been secured to rebuild the 2.4-mile boardwalk from the piles up, with funding running through 2032 and construction still years away. A reader and strike on your own door are not tied to the boardwalk deck, and they can be removed and refitted if your frontage is affected later. What is worth avoiding is burying new conduit under boardwalk-side paving, so we keep cable inside the building wherever possible.

Does the new Coney Island business improvement district require storefronts on Mermaid or Surf Avenue to change their doors?
+

Not as far as the announcements go. The city’s February 2026 release and News 12’s July 2026 report describe the district’s work as sanitation, public space, marketing, business support and public safety, with a budget of around one million dollars. Nothing in them sets rules for a business’s own doors or locks. The building code, the Multiple Dwelling Law for apartments above stores, and your lease still decide what goes on your door.

How early should a beachfront business book access work before the season opens?
+

In the winter, well before the first warm weekend. Luna Park opened its 2026 season on March 28, and the weeks just before opening are when every business on the boardwalk and Surf Avenue is doing repairs at once. Booking in January or February leaves time to order a strike or a reader that is not in stock, to fix the door first, and to enroll the returning managers before the new crew is hired.

What is NYCHA’s layered access control, and is it the same thing you install?
+

It is NYCHA’s name for its own building security program. NYCHA’s fact sheet lists key fob access points, intercoms, magnetic or electronic locks on doors and gates, a central low-voltage hub, power backup and network connections. The technology is similar to what we install in private buildings, but NYCHA’s systems are designed, owned and maintained by NYCHA and its contractors. We do not service, change or connect to them.

Can a resident use an OMNY card or a bank card as the building fob?
+

We do not set that up. Some readers can pick up an identifying number from a contactless payment or transit card, but those cards were not designed as building keys, the number may not be stable, and a resident who replaces a bank card would lose access to the building. A purpose-made encrypted fob or a phone key does the job properly and keeps payment cards out of the building’s records.

Our building’s fobs have a brand name printed on them. Does that tell us whether they can be copied?
+

Not on its own. The same manufacturers sell both old proximity fobs and modern encrypted ones, often in similar shapes and colors. The format matters, not the logo. On a site visit we read a sample fob with a test reader, which tells us its frequency and format, and check the existing readers to see whether they can accept an encrypted credential. That tells the board whether it needs new fobs, new readers or both.

Sand gets tracked across our lobby floor all summer. Does that cause trouble for the door hardware?
+

It can, mostly at the bottom of the door. Sand collects in floor closers, threshold-mounted contacts and the bottom pivot of heavy lobby doors, and grit in a strike’s keeper makes it stick. We mount door contacts in the frame header rather than the floor where we can, choose strikes with sealed internals, and add cleaning the threshold and closer to the twice-yearly check. A good entrance mat outside the door helps more than people expect.

Can our co-op keep resident entry logs for less than the 90 days the city allows?
+

Yes. The Tenant Data Privacy Law sets an outer limit, requiring authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized, but a shorter period is allowed and often wise. Many boards keep detailed entry records for 30 days and only anonymized counts after that. We set the retention period in the software to match whatever the board writes into its privacy policy, so the two always agree.

Does a Coney Island co-op need to change its house rules or proprietary lease before fobs replace keys?
+

That is a question for the co-op’s attorney, and the answer depends on what the current documents say about keys and entry. Many boards adopt a house rule covering how fobs are issued, what a replacement costs, who may hold one and how long records are kept, alongside the privacy policy the city requires. We supply the technical details the attorney needs and draft a plain description of how the system works for shareholders.

Can the fob that opens our co-op laundry room also pay for the washers and dryers?
+

Usually not, and it is better kept separate. In most buildings the machines belong to a laundry operator with its own card or app payment system, while the laundry room door belongs to the co-op. We put the room door on the residents’ fobs, with laundry hours if the board wants them, and leave payment to the operator. That way a dispute over a laundry account never locks a resident out of the room, and the access records hold no payment data.
