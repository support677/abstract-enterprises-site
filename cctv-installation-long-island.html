The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need CCTV

Most of the CCTV work we do in the city happens inside buildings that share walls: a lobby, a hallway, a basement, a storefront with one door. Long Island is a different job. Nassau and Suffolk are counties of detached houses on their own lots, strip centers with parking fields in front and service alleys behind, office parks set back from the road, and waterfront properties where the thing you most want to watch is a hundred feet or more from the house. The distance between the recorder and the camera is the first thing we plan on almost every Long Island job, because it decides whether a camera will be wired, how it gets power, and whether it will still be recording after the first ice storm.

The second thing is what people are actually worried about. Island residents rarely call us about strangers in a hallway. They call because a car was gone from the driveway in the morning, because packages keep disappearing from the front step, because the shed or the detached garage was opened during the night, because a boat engine walked off the dock, or because a summer house in the Hamptons sat empty from October to May and nobody knew a pipe had burst or a window had been forced until spring. A business owner on Jericho Turnpike calls after a smash-and-grab, after an employee dispute over a register, or because the insurance carrier asked what the cameras recorded and the answer was "nothing usable." Those calls all ask the same question: where should the cameras go, and how do we make sure the footage exists when we need it?

The third thing is weather and power. Long Island sits between the ocean and the Sound. Salt air corrodes cheap connectors and steel mounts, wind-driven rain finds every unsealed cable entry, and the island's overhead power lines come down in tropical storms and nor'easters. When Tropical Storm Isaias hit in August 2020, a LIPA task force report described more than 646,000 outages, with more than 500,000 customers in the dark over a summer week. A CCTV system that goes blind every time PSEG Long Island loses a feeder is not a security system. That is why we put the recorder, the PoE switch and the modem on a battery backup (UPS) on most Long Island installs, and why we use outdoor-rated cable, sealed connectors and drip loops at every exterior entry.

Crime numbers on the Island have been falling, and that is good news, but they also show why recorded video matters. Nassau County Police data reported by the New York Post in March 2025 counted 710 major crimes in the first two months of 2025 against 951 a year earlier, with car thefts down nearly 18% and non-residential burglaries down from 87 to 45. Fewer incidents means each one gets more attention, and the cases that get solved are the ones where someone can hand detectives a clear clip of a face, a vehicle and a direction of travel. In Suffolk County the police department goes further and asks residents and businesses to tell it where their cameras are.

“Video surveillance is one of the best methods for apprehending criminals and convicting suspects who are caught in the act of committing a crime.”

— Suffolk County Police Department, SCPD SHIELD Camera Registry Program

Long Island facts that shape a CCTV design

  • Nassau County, January–February 2025: 710 major crimes versus 951 in the same months of 2024, car thefts down nearly 18%, commercial robberies down from 32 to 24 (Nassau County Police Department data, New York Post).
  • Suffolk County camera registry: the SCPD SHIELD program lets residents and business owners register where their cameras are, so detectives can ask them to check a specific date and time; footage is collected at the owner's convenience (SCPD SHIELD).
  • Package theft: in November 2025 the Suffolk County District Attorney charged 14 men in a porch-pirate ring that targeted 31 communities over two years, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, and stole more than 200 phones and tablets (CBS New York).
  • Storm outages: Tropical Storm Isaias caused more than 646,000 outages on Long Island in August 2020, per the LIPA task force report (Newsday via NYSenate.gov).
  • Empty winter houses: in Bridgehampton and Water Mill, more than 60% of housing units are seasonal, according to the Town of Southampton Housing Plan (2022). Those homes sit unwatched for months at a time.

Put together, the Long Island brief is simple to say and harder to do well: cover the approaches to the property, not just the front door; record locally so footage survives an internet outage; keep the system running through a power outage; and build it with parts that hold up outdoors near salt water. If you only want the general picture of camera systems on the Island, our security camera installation guide for Long Island covers it. This page is about CCTV specifically: the recorder-based, wired systems that most Long Island businesses and a growing number of homeowners choose when they want video they can rely on.

Field Notes

Field Notes: A Four-Camera CCTV Job at a Nassau County Showroom

This job is a good picture of what a small Long Island business actually needs. The client runs a window-and-door showroom with a working office in Nassau County: a front walk off the street, a side lot along a brick wall, an office floor where staff and customers meet, and a back storage room full of trim, glass and boxed paperwork. They wanted four cameras, local recording, and a screen in the office so they could glance up and see the whole property at once.

Inside, we mounted compact turret dome cameras high in the corners, tight to the drop ceiling, so each lens looks across the room instead of down at the tops of heads. In the office, the dome sits in the corner beside the flag, above the alarm motion sensor, where it sees the desks, the counter and the door in one frame. In the storage room the ceiling tiles were old and water-stained, so we kept the mount on the solid corner and ran the cable above the grid rather than trusting a sagging tile. Outside, one camera watches the front walk and entrance door and another runs along the brick side wall toward the lot, so anyone walking or driving in is on video before they reach the building.

The recorder feeds a wall-mounted monitor in the office in a four-way split: office floor, storage room, front walk and side lot, each labeled and time-stamped. We set motion recording with a pre-record buffer, set the owner up on the phone app for remote viewing, and printed a one-page sheet showing which camera covers what. Nothing about it is exotic, and that is the point. Four well-placed cameras on a wired recorder answer the questions a Long Island business owner actually gets asked: who came in, when, from which direction, and what they did.

The building decides the design. Here is how we approach the property types we see most often across Nassau and Suffolk, and what changes from one to the next.

Detached Houses with Long Driveways

Driveway CCTV installation is the most common Long Island request. A camera on the garage corner looking down the driveway, aimed so a car's front plate and the driver's door land in frame, does more than a wide view of the street. Long driveways may need a second camera near the curb.

🛖

Detached Garages, Sheds and Pool Houses

Garage CCTV installation on a detached garage means either trenching cable in conduit, running it overhead on a messenger, or a point-to-point wireless bridge. We price the options separately so you can choose; a camera on Wi-Fi at the edge of the router's range is the one that drops first.

🏠

Ranches, Capes and Split-Levels

Most postwar Levittown-era capes and ranches have an attic or crawlspace that makes concealed wiring straightforward, and split-levels often let us drop cable from the attic to soffits on several faces of the house. We plan cable paths before we drill, so exterior walls stay clean.

Waterfront and Canal Homes

South Shore canal homes in places like Massapequa, Freeport and Lindenhurst want the bulkhead and the boat covered, plus the street side. We specify cameras rated for outdoor exposure, use stainless or coated mounts and sealed junction boxes, and keep the recorder inside and above flood level.

❄️

East End Seasonal Homes

A Hamptons or North Fork house that sits empty all winter needs CCTV that works without anyone on site: wired cameras, a recorder on a UPS, remote viewing and alerts for doors and driveway activity, and settings that keep weeks of footage so a problem found in March can be traced back.

Condo, Co-op and Garden Apartment Complexes

Condominium building CCTV installation and HOA CCTV installation cover mailrooms, entrances, parking fields and dumpster corrals across several buildings. We plan where the recorder lives, how cable or fiber links buildings, and who on the board or management team gets access.

New Construction and Renovations

CCTV installation for new construction is cheapest before the drywall goes up. We pre-wire Cat6 to each camera location, leave service loops and labeled ends at a central point, and come back to mount cameras and set up the recorder after the painters are gone.

How we run cable on Long Island houses

Most of the time we can hide nearly all of the cable. On a house with an accessible attic, we run Cat6 or coax across the attic, drop it into the soffit and mount the camera on a junction box under the eave, so the only thing you see outside is the camera. Where there is no attic access, such as a flat-roof extension or a finished cathedral ceiling, we run along the basement rim joist and come up through the sill plate, or we use paintable exterior raceway along trim lines. Every exterior penetration is sealed and every outdoor connection sits inside a weatherproof box. For a detached garage, we price trenching in conduit, an overhead aerial run, and a wireless bridge side by side, because the right answer depends on how far it is, what is in the way and whether you are planning landscaping work anyway.

Salt air, sun and wind

Within a mile or two of the ocean or the Sound, cheap zinc-plated screws and unsealed RJ45 connectors corrode within a few seasons. We use stainless or coated hardware outdoors, weatherproof connector boots or junction boxes at every camera, and drip loops so water runs off the cable before it reaches the entry hole. South-facing cameras on beach houses deal with low winter sun and glare off sand and water, so we angle them down slightly and pick models with good wide dynamic range. On exposed waterfront poles and dock pilings, we use mounts designed for the load and check that wind won't make the image shake on a gusty night.

Wi-Fi reach versus wired PoE

Long Island lots are wider than city lots, and a house's Wi-Fi rarely reaches the back fence, the far end of the driveway or a detached garage with solid signal. Wireless cameras on weak Wi-Fi drop frames, miss motion events and go offline after a router reboot, and battery cameras slow down in cold weather. A wired PoE camera has none of those problems: Ethernet carries video and power on one cable up to 100 meters (328 feet) per run, and the recorder keeps working whether or not the internet is up. Where a wired run truly is not practical, we use a dedicated point-to-point wireless bridge to a PoE switch at the remote building, which behaves like a cable instead of like a phone on the edge of your Wi-Fi.

A typical commercial CCTV installation for a single storefront or office on Long Island runs eight to sixteen cameras on an NVR with enough drive space for two weeks or more of footage, a PoE switch or PoE recorder, a UPS, and a monitor at the counter or in the office. Multi-site owners can view several stores in one app. For more on larger systems see our commercial security camera installation page for Long Island.

Rules every Long Island business should know before adding cameras

This is general information, not legal advice. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from placing video recording devices in restrooms, toilets, showers, locker rooms and changing rooms, and we will not install a camera in any of those spaces. New York's eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.00 and §250.05) treats recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party as a crime, so we leave audio recording off on business cameras unless you have a clear reason and posted notice, and we explain the trade-off before turning it on. Penal Law §250.45 makes it unlawful surveillance to record someone in a place where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as a bathroom or bedroom. Signs that say video recording is in use are a good practice for customers and staff. If you lease, check your lease before mounting cameras on the building exterior or in shared areas; many Long Island landlords want to approve camera locations and cable paths.

Working with your insurer and the police

Insurance carriers increasingly ask what cameras a business has and whether footage is retained. We document camera locations, retention days and the recorder model so you can answer that question in writing. In Suffolk County, you can also register your camera locations with the police department's SCPD SHIELD Camera Registry, which lets detectives ask you to check a specific date and time after an incident nearby. In Nassau, the police department runs an information-sharing network for businesses called SPiN; ask your precinct how to join. In both counties, the most useful thing we can do is make sure you can export a clean, time-stamped clip to a USB stick or a phone in a couple of minutes, because that is what investigators ask for.

Upgrades & Maintenance

Upgrading an Older CCTV System on Long Island

A lot of Long Island businesses and houses already have a CCTV system from ten or fifteen years ago: grainy analog cameras, a DVR in a closet that nobody has logged into since the installer left, and a hard drive that may or may not still be recording. Before you replace everything, it is worth knowing what can be saved.

Existing coax in good condition can usually carry modern HD-analog video, so an older analog system can often be upgraded to sharp 5MP or 8MP 4K images by replacing the cameras and the DVR and keeping the cable. Where the coax is damaged, the runs are too long, or you want features like PoE power and AI detection, an analog to IP CCTV upgrade with new Cat6 is the better long-term choice. A DVR to NVR upgrade can be done in stages, too: we can install a hybrid recorder that accepts both old and new cameras so you replace cameras as budget allows.

Recorders fail quietly. The most common call we get on an older system is "the cameras show live, but nothing was recorded," which almost always means a failed or full hard drive or a recording schedule that was changed. CCTV hard drive replacement, a CCTV storage upgrade, resetting the recording schedule and a firmware update fix most of these. We also handle CCTV camera relocation and repositioning when renovations or new landscaping block a view, CCTV cable replacement for runs damaged by weather or pests, CCTV power supply replacement on older analog systems, and CCTV mobile app setup when the old remote access stopped working after a router change.

For systems that matter to a business, a CCTV maintenance plan is cheap insurance: a visit to clean lenses salted over from the shore, check every camera day and night, verify retention days and drive health, update firmware and adjust aiming after the trees leaf out. Details are on our camera maintenance page.

Placement Guide

Where CCTV Cameras Belong on a Long Island Property

Camera placement is the part of CCTV system design that decides whether you ever get a usable clip. The plan we draw for a Long Island house or business starts with the ways a person or a vehicle can arrive, then the things worth stealing, then the places where a face will be close to a lens. Here is how that plays out on the properties we see most.

Front door and porch

Front door CCTV installation should show a face, not just a figure. We mount the porch camera at about head-and-a-half height under the porch ceiling or soffit, angled so it sees a visitor's face as they reach the door and the package spot on the step. A doorbell camera alone sees the top of the head of anyone standing close, and many miss the package left to one side. If package theft is the problem, a second camera that looks back toward the walk and the street captures the vehicle that stopped, which is usually the detail police want.

Driveway and street approach

On most Long Island houses the driveway is the main approach for both cars and people. A wide camera on the garage corner shows the whole driveway; a narrower camera aimed at the point where a car slows down to turn in captures the plate and the driver's side. On long driveways we add a camera near the curb or on a post, powered over PoE. We keep driveway cameras out of direct headlight glare where we can, because a bright headlight at night washes out the plate on cheaper cameras.

Backyard, patio, pool and side gates

Backyard CCTV installation covers the rear door, the patio slider, the pool gate and the side gates between houses. Side gates are the approach burglars use most on suburban blocks because they are hidden from the street. A turret camera on the rear corner of the house can often see both a side gate and the back door; a second one on the opposite corner covers the other side and the pool. Patio CCTV installation also helps with the less dramatic problems: a gate left open, a dog that got out, a delivery left at the back.

Detached garage, shed and boat

Outbuildings hold bikes, tools, mowers, outboard motors and sometimes a second car. We aim one camera at the outbuilding's door from the house, which needs no cable to the outbuilding, and, when the outbuilding is far or has a side door, a second camera on the outbuilding itself. On a waterfront lot, the boat and the dock get their own camera, aimed along the dock toward the land so anyone stepping onto it is facing the lens.

Inside a store, office or showroom

Indoor CCTV installation for a business follows the money and the doors: the register from the side, at an angle that shows hands, the cash drawer and the customer's face; a camera inside the entrance at about seven feet, aimed at faces coming in; the stockroom and its door; and the back door from inside. Office CCTV installation adds the reception desk, any server or records room and, where it matters, the hallway between public and private areas. We never put cameras in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms, and we keep indoor audio off unless there is a clear and legal reason.

Parking field, dumpster corral and loading area

Behind most Long Island strip centers is a service lane with dumpsters, grease bins, back doors and loading docks. That is where after-hours trouble starts, and it is often the darkest part of the property. We use color night vision or cameras with built-in white light on the rear, mount them high enough to discourage tampering, and add a camera at the lane entrance to capture vehicles. In the front parking field, pole or building-mounted cameras cover the rows nearest the doors, and a narrow camera at each driveway catches vehicles coming and going.

Seasons on the Island

CCTV Through a Long Island Year

The Island's calendar shapes what people ask us for. Most of our seasonal calls fall into a few patterns, and planning for them ahead of time costs less than reacting after an incident.

Holiday shopping season and Black Friday

From late November through December, stores in strip centers and around the malls carry more stock, take more cash and stay open later. CCTV installation for the holiday shopping season should be finished before Thanksgiving week, not during it. For retailers that means the register and entrance cameras tuned, the stockroom covered, the rear door and the parking field on video, recording retention checked so the busiest weeks are not overwritten, and staff shown how to pull a clip without calling anyone. For homeowners, the same weeks bring the heaviest package deliveries of the year, so a front step camera that sees the package and the street pays for itself in December.

Winter on the East End and the North Fork

CCTV installation for winter property security matters most for houses that close after the season. When the summer residents leave the Hamptons, Montauk, Shelter Island and the North Fork, many homes sit empty until Memorial Day. Remote viewing lets you check the driveway after a snowfall, see whether a caretaker came by, and confirm that doors and garage doors are closed. Pair it with a UPS, a recorder set to keep a long stretch of footage, and an internet connection that stays on through the winter, and you will know about a problem in days instead of months.

Summer rentals and before vacation

In summer the pattern reverses: many houses are full of guests or renters, and year-round residents leave for vacations. CCTV installation before vacation should be done a week or two ahead so you can check that alerts reach your phone and that every camera looks right at night before you leave. If you rent a house out, aim cameras only at the exterior approaches, never inside the living space, and tell renters in writing that exterior cameras are in use.

Hurricane season

From late summer into fall, tropical storms and nor'easters bring wind, rain and outages. Before the season, check that exterior cameras are tight on their mounts, that cable entries are sealed, that the UPS battery still holds a charge and that the recorder is above any flood level in a basement. After a storm, a quick look through the cameras from your phone can show downed trees, standing water and whether the house is secure, which is useful if you evacuated or live elsewhere.

New business openings and construction

Footage

Recording, Retention and Getting a Clip When You Need One

A CCTV system is only as good as the footage you can find and hand over. These are the settings we take care of on every Long Island installation, and the questions to ask any installer about them.

Continuous or motion recording

Recording all day and night on every camera gives you the most complete record but fills the drive fastest. Motion recording saves space but can miss the moments just before something happens. Our usual setup is continuous recording on the cameras that matter most, such as the driveway, entrance and register, with motion recording and a pre-record buffer on quieter cameras. AI detection that separates people and vehicles from leaves and rain keeps the motion recording and the alerts useful.

How long footage is kept

Our packages include 2 weeks of recording. Many homeowners find that enough, because they notice a problem within days. Businesses, HOAs and seasonal homes often want 30 days or more so footage still exists when a problem is reported late or an insurance claim takes time. Retention is a drive-size question, and we can size the drive to the number of days you need. The recorder overwrites the oldest footage automatically, so the only way to keep an important clip longer is to export it.

Exporting and sharing a clip

Before we leave, we show you how to find an event by date and time, play it back, and export a clip to a USB stick or your phone. Detectives and insurers want a clip with the date and time stamp visible and the original file, not a phone video of the monitor. We also make sure the recorder's clock is correct and set to sync automatically, because a recorder that drifts by an hour makes footage harder to use. If you register with the Suffolk County camera registry, this is the skill that makes the program work for you.

Who can see the cameras

Every person who views the system gets their own login, with permissions to match their role. An owner sees everything, a manager may see live video and playback, and staff may see only live video. For condo and HOA systems, the board decides who can view and export footage, and we set it up that way. Default passwords are changed on every device, remote access goes through the recorder's secure app, and firmware is updated at installation.

Glossary

CCTV Terms in Plain English

  • CCTV (closed circuit television): cameras wired to a recorder you control, rather than broadcasting or streaming to a public service. Today the term covers both analog and IP systems.
  • DVR (digital video recorder): the recorder for analog and HD-analog cameras on coax. Our A, F and C Series use DVRs.
  • NVR (network video recorder): the recorder for IP cameras on a network, often with built-in PoE ports. Our E and D Series use NVRs.
  • PoE (Power over Ethernet): power and video on a single Cat6 cable from a switch or NVR to the camera, up to 100 meters per run.
  • HD-TVI, HD-CVI, AHD: HD-analog formats that send high-resolution video over coax, letting older cable carry modern images.
  • H.264 and H.265: video compression standards. H.265 stores similar quality in less space, so the same drive holds more days.
  • Retention: how many days of footage the recorder keeps before it overwrites the oldest.
  • Varifocal lens: a lens we can zoom and focus on site to frame a distant gate, plate or dock.
  • Active deterrence: a camera with a light and siren that reacts to a person or vehicle in a set zone.
  • UPS (uninterruptible power supply): a battery that keeps the recorder and switch running through short outages and allows a safe shutdown in long ones.
  • VLAN: a separate network segment that keeps cameras apart from your computers and phones for security.
Your Questions Answered

CCTV on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island homeowners, landlords and business owners ask us on the phone before they book, answered the way we answer them on the call.

How many CCTV cameras does a typical Long Island house need?

For a detached house on a standard suburban lot, four cameras cover the basics: front door and walkway, driveway, backyard, and one side yard or the detached garage. Corner lots, long driveways, pools and waterfront yards often push it to six or eight. We count approaches, not walls: every way a person or a car can reach the house should cross at least one camera at a distance where a face or a plate is readable, and the side gates that only neighbors know about deserve a camera too.

Can CCTV cameras reach a detached garage or a shed at the back of the lot?

Yes. The three ways are a buried cable in conduit, an overhead aerial run, or a point-to-point wireless bridge between the house and the outbuilding. Buried conduit is the most durable and the most disruptive; the bridge is fast and clean when there is line of sight. We avoid relying on the house Wi-Fi for a camera that far out, because that is the camera that stops recording first. All three options are priced after we confirm the layout and distance.

Will my CCTV keep recording when PSEG Long Island loses power?

Only if the recorder and the switch are on battery backup. Without a UPS, the system goes dark the moment power drops, which is also when some burglars choose to act. We install a UPS sized to your camera count so recording continues through short outages and the recorder shuts down safely during long ones, protecting the hard drive. Outdoor cameras powered over PoE run as long as the switch they connect to has power, which is another reason we prefer PoE on Long Island.

Does CCTV installation require internet at our property?

No. A CCTV system records to a DVR or NVR on site, so the cameras record with or without internet. Internet is only needed for remote viewing on your phone and for push alerts. That matters on the East End, where some seasonal homes shut off service in winter, and at boatyards with weak connections: the footage is still on the recorder when you arrive. If you want remote viewing at a place with no wired internet, a cellular router can provide it.

How long does CCTV installation take on a typical Long Island home?

Most four-camera house jobs with attic access are done in one visit. Six to eight cameras, a detached garage, or a house without an accessible attic can take a full day. Commercial systems with ten or more cameras, ceiling lifts or long outdoor runs can take two days or more. We tell you the expected time when we confirm the layout, and we book within 2 hours of your request, with jobs scheduled 7 days a week first come, first served once the deposit is paid.

Should a Hamptons or North Fork summer house have CCTV while it sits empty?

It is one of the best uses of CCTV on the Island. An empty house can go months without anyone checking it, and in the hamlets where most homes are seasonal there may not be a neighbor home all winter either. A wired system with remote viewing lets you or your caretaker look in from anywhere, check the driveway after a storm and confirm the doors are shut. Set retention long enough that footage from a break-in found weeks later still exists.

Can you put a CCTV camera on a dock or a bulkhead?

Yes, with the right parts. Dock and bulkhead cameras need weatherproof housings, corrosion-resistant mounts, sealed connections and a cable route that does not sit in standing water. Distance is the main question: a camera at the end of a long dock may need a cable in conduit along the dock, a PoE extender or a wireless bridge. We aim cameras to avoid glare from lights on the water at night and to show the boat, the slip and the approach from land.

What is the difference between CCTV and a Wi-Fi camera from a big-box store?

CCTV means closed circuit: the cameras are wired to a recorder you own, and the video stays on that recorder unless you choose to view it remotely. Most consumer Wi-Fi cameras send video to a cloud service, depend on your Wi-Fi to work, and charge a monthly fee to keep recordings. Wi-Fi cameras are fine for a single door; for a whole property, a wired CCTV system is more reliable, records continuously, and has no subscription.

Can I register my cameras with the police in Suffolk or Nassau?

In Suffolk County, yes: the police department's SCPD SHIELD Camera Registry Program lets residents and businesses register camera locations so detectives can ask them to check footage for a specific date and time. Joining is voluntary and you decide when and how video is handed over. Nassau County Police run SPiN, an information-sharing network aimed at businesses. We can help you note camera locations and field of view so registering is easy.

How far from the recorder can a wired CCTV camera be?

A standard Ethernet run for a PoE camera is limited to 100 meters (328 feet). HD-analog cameras on good coax can usually go farther. Beyond those limits, we add a PoE extender, a remote switch or a fiber link. On large Long Island properties and business parks we often put a small network switch in an outbuilding or a second building and connect it back, which keeps every camera run short and every camera powered.

Do I need permission from my HOA or village to install outdoor cameras?

Usually not for cameras mounted on your own house, but some homeowners associations and condominium boards have rules about anything on the exterior, and a few villages regulate structures like poles. If you are in an HOA or condo, check the rules before we mount. Aim cameras at your own property and approaches rather than into a neighbor's windows or yard; it avoids disputes, and recording inside a neighbor's home where they expect privacy can be illegal.

Can a CCTV installer hide the wires on a finished house?

In most Long Island houses, yes. Attics, basements, crawlspaces and soffits give us paths to most exterior camera spots without opening walls. Where a run has to be exposed, we use paintable raceway along trim or downspout lines so it blends in. Fully concealed wiring through finished interior walls takes more time and is priced after we confirm the layout. We will tell you up front which runs can be fully hidden and which cannot.

Is professional CCTV installation worth it for a small Long Island shop?

For most shops, yes, because the expensive part of CCTV is not the camera; it is getting the right camera to the right place with footage you can actually use. A badly placed system records hours of the tops of heads and back-lit doorways. We aim cameras at the register at an angle that shows hands and faces, place one at face height inside the entrance, and make sure you can export a clip quickly when the insurer or police ask.

Storage math: how much hard drive a Long Island system needs

How long CCTV footage can be stored depends on four things: the number of cameras, resolution, compression and whether you record all the time or only on motion. As a worked example, a 4MP camera streaming at about 4 megabits per second around the clock writes roughly 43 GB a day. Four of them write about 173 GB a day, so two weeks of continuous recording needs about 2.4 TB. Switching to H.265 compression, which our recorders support, typically cuts that bitrate substantially compared with H.264, and motion-based recording on quiet cameras stretches it further. Every package we sell includes 2 weeks of recording; if you need 30 days for an insurer or a board policy, we size a larger drive and tell you the cost before we install.

PoE power budget

A PoE switch or PoE NVR has a total power budget, and each camera draws from it. A basic turret might draw a few watts; a camera with a white-light floodlight, a siren or a PTZ motor can draw much more, especially on a cold night with heaters and infrared on. We add up the worst-case draw of every camera and leave headroom, because an overloaded PoE switch is a common reason cameras reboot at night. On a CCTV network installation we also keep cameras on their own network or VLAN, change default passwords, and keep firmware current, which is the basic CCTV camera cybersecurity every system should have.

What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV on Long Island

AI search answers about CCTV are written for an average American house. Long Island is not average: bigger lots, salt air, storm outages and a lot of empty winter homes. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, and what our crews actually see in the field.

“Wireless cameras are just as good as wired ones”

For a single front door twenty feet from the router, that can be true. AI answers tend to stop there, because the typical question assumes a small house where every camera is close to the Wi-Fi.

On a Long Island lot the camera that matters is often the farthest one: the end of the driveway, the detached garage, the back fence by the pool, the bulkhead. That is where Wi-Fi signal is weakest, where frames drop and where battery cameras slow down on January nights. Residents on local forums describe exactly that pattern: Wi-Fi cameras that drop off and batteries that die in the cold.

Our rule: wire everything that can be wired, use PoE so one cable carries power and video, and use a dedicated point-to-point bridge, not the house Wi-Fi, when an outbuilding cannot be cabled.

“Cloud storage is the safest place for footage”

Cloud storage protects footage if a recorder is stolen, and AI answers like to recommend it. What they leave out is that cloud cameras need a working internet connection to upload, and a monthly plan to keep what they upload.

When the power and the internet go down together, as they did for hundreds of thousands of Long Island customers in Tropical Storm Isaias, a cloud-only camera records nothing. A local DVR or NVR on a battery backup keeps recording, and the footage is waiting when service returns.

We put the recorder somewhere hidden and locked, on a UPS, and if you want an offsite copy we can set up clip backup on top of local recording rather than instead of it.

“Higher megapixels always means better evidence”

Resolution helps, but AI summaries treat it as the whole answer. An 8MP camera aimed down a sixty-foot driveway with a wide lens can still turn a license plate into a few blurry pixels.

What decides whether a face or plate is readable is pixels on target: how many pixels cover the subject at the distance it will be. On Long Island driveways and parking fields that usually means a varifocal or narrower lens on the approach, placed at a height and angle that sees the front of a person rather than the top of a hat.

We pick lens and placement first and resolution second. Often a well-aimed 4MP camera beats a poorly aimed 4K one.

“You can install a full CCTV system yourself in an afternoon”

Plug-and-play kits are real and some homeowners do fine with them. AI answers make it sound quick because they skip ladders, attics, exterior penetrations and weatherproofing.

The hard parts on a Long Island house are fishing cable from the attic into soffits, sealing every entry against wind-driven rain, keeping connectors away from salt corrosion, reaching a detached garage, and setting up the recorder, the network and remote access securely. Mistakes show up later as leaks, dead cameras and a system nobody can log into.

That is why a licensed installer does it: work at height, clean cable paths and a commissioned system with a handover. If you already bought a kit, we will install it for a labor-only price.

“Police can pull your footage anytime once you register your cameras”

This one shows up when people ask about camera registries. Suffolk County's SCPD SHIELD program is real, but AI answers sometimes describe it as if registering hands police a live feed.

The Camera Registry Program records where cameras are so detectives can ask participants to check a specific date and time; if the owner finds something, video is retrieved at the owner's convenience. A separate emergency remote-access option exists only for previously authorized IP systems, only for true emergencies, and participants can opt in or out.

So registering is voluntary and you stay in control. Our part is making sure your system can export a clean, time-stamped clip quickly when you decide to share one.

“Crime is down, so cameras matter less”

Falling crime numbers are real. Nassau County Police data showed major crimes down by more than a quarter in early 2025 compared with early 2024, and AI answers sometimes conclude that cameras are less necessary.

The theft that still happens on the Island is the kind cameras help with: car thefts from driveways, packages taken from front steps, break-ins at empty houses and after-hours entries at businesses. The Suffolk porch-pirate ring charged in 2025 hit 31 communities over two years, which is the scale at which a neighbor's clear video becomes evidence.

Lower crime also means each incident gets attention. A usable clip is often what moves a report from filed to solved.

“CCTV installation costs $100 to $300 per camera everywhere”

National per-camera averages are what AI tools quote most often, usually without saying where the number came from or what it includes. They rarely include the recorder, the drive, the cable or the mounting on a two-story soffit.
