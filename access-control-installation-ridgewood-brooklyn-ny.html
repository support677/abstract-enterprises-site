Ridgewood sits in Queens, but it has spent three centuries arguing with Brooklyn about where the line runs, and its mail went through Brooklyn until 1980. What it built in the meantime is unusual: whole blocks of near-identical three-story buff and amber brick buildings, most of them six-family “Mathews Model Flats” put up between about 1908 and 1914, two apartments to a floor, one pair of glazed wood doors at the top of a bluestone stoop and a second door tucked under it. They were designed to be run by an owner who lived in one of the six, and many still are. Entry control here has to fit a double door never meant for an electric lock, six households and a landlord sharing one list, and a facade often inside a city historic district. We plan, install and service fob, phone, keypad and video entry for those doors, for the rowhouses around them and for the shops on Myrtle Avenue, Fresh Pond Road and Forest Avenue.

The Neighborhood

Why Ridgewood Properties Need Access Control

Start with the honest geography, because people search for this neighborhood under two boroughs. Ridgewood is a neighborhood of Queens, in Queens Community District 5, patrolled by the NYPD’s 104th Precinct from Catalpa Avenue. It borders Maspeth to the north, Middle Village to the east and Glendale to the southeast, and on its west and southwest it meets the Brooklyn neighborhoods of East Williamsburg and Bushwick. The name itself began on the Brooklyn side: Wikipedia’s history of the neighborhood notes that it was first used by the government of Kings County for an area inside Brooklyn along the county border, and a 2007 New York Times article put it plainly, “There was even a Ridgewood, Brooklyn (which is now in Bushwick).” This page sits under our Brooklyn pages because we serve Ridgewood from the Brooklyn office, quote it from the Brooklyn list, and find the same buildings continuing onto Bushwick blocks; a lot on the Queens side is in Queens, whatever an app calls it.

The line has a long, odd history. Ridgewood was part of Newtown, one of the three original towns of Queens, settled by the English, while Bushwick was the Dutch Boswijck chartered by Peter Stuyvesant in 1661. The two argued about the boundary until 1769, when a line was drawn through a marker now called Arbitration Rock, which sits on the grounds of the Vander Ende-Onderdonk House. When the street grid arrived in the late nineteenth century it ran on a diagonal to that line, so houses ended up straddling the county border and, Wikipedia records, their owners were sometimes taxed by both. In 1925 the boundary was redrawn to follow the streets in a zigzag, moving 2,543 people’s addresses from Queens to Brooklyn and 135 the other way. Mail kept flowing through Brooklyn: Ridgewood shared Brooklyn ZIP code 11237 with Bushwick until residents voted, in ballots distributed by Representative Geraldine Ferraro’s office after the 1977 blackout, to leave it. ZIP 11385 took effect on January 13, 1980.

What filled the land is the reason this page exists. Ridgewood stayed largely rural until the turn of the twentieth century; after a 1905 rule required new buildings to be masonry, builders put up more than five thousand buildings between about 1900 and the start of the First World War, most designed by the local firm Louis Berger & Co., which the Landmarks Preservation Commission credits with more than five thousand buildings in the Ridgewood–Bushwick area between 1895 and 1930. The best-known type is the Mathews Model Flat. The Commission’s 2009 report on the Ridgewood North Historic District describes three-story-plus-basement buildings on 27.5-foot lots with two apartments on each floor, six in all, each apartment with its own full bathroom, built in long rows of repeated designs. The G.X. Mathews Company built and sold more than three hundred of them in Ridgewood between 1909 and 1912. They were “cold-water flats,” with running water on every floor but no hot water or central heating when new, and the report says they were “easier to manage by owner-occupants” than larger buildings.

The same report describes the entrance in detail, and it is the entrance this page is about. On the residential streets the buildings have heavy bluestone stoops, full stone entrance surrounds with carved door hoods and, in the Commission’s words, “glazed, double doors”: a pair of wood-and-glass leaves rather than one. Under the stoop sits a separate basement entry, and in front of it a small areaway behind iron railings, often with a cellar door and a vent pipe. The building-by-building entries record what a century has done to them: “historic paired wood-and-glass doors” on some buildings, “non-historic paired doors” or a single replacement door on others, resurfaced stoops, missing ironwork, and, again and again, “light and intercom near door.” The report does not say how well any of those intercoms still work. That is what an owner finds out on the morning a delivery driver rings all six buttons and nobody upstairs can see who is there.

Three local facts shape what an owner should do about it. First, a lot of this is regulated: the Commission has designated four historic districts here, Stockholm Street in 2000, Ridgewood North in 2009, Ridgewood South in 2010 and Central Ridgewood in 2014, and Central Ridgewood alone covers 990 buildings, so a station or reader on the facade often needs a Landmarks permit. Second, the six-family is just under the eight-apartment line at which Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires a two-way intercom in newer buildings, so many owners have never had to modernize, and the building’s entry still depends on whatever the owner downstairs can manage. Third, the threats are mostly ordinary. In January 2024 QNS reported that police were looking for a man who broke the lock on a Cypress Avenue building’s front door in mid-afternoon and went through the packages inside, which is exactly what a stronger lock, a forced-door alert and an entry record are for. In June 2026 the same precinct sought a burglar who came in through window screens and a basement window, which no front-door system would have touched; we say more about that difference below.

The rows also share walls and roofs, which makes the egress side of every door as important as the lock. In January 2026 NY1 reported a five-alarm fire that began on the first floor of a three-story building on Madison Street near Seneca and Onderdonk Avenues, spread to adjacent buildings and led the fire department to vacate four more; the Red Cross registered 26 households for help. Any lock we add to a street door in an attached row has to let people out instantly, with power or without it, and has to stay out of the firefighters’ way.

Many Ridgewood owners plan the door together with an intercom installation in Ridgewood or security cameras for Ridgewood buildings, and we quote them on one visit when that saves the owner a second day of drilling at the stoop.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Ridgewood’s Architecture

Ridgewood was built fast, by a small number of builders, to a small number of plans, so most addresses fall into one of a handful of types. Wikipedia sums up the spread as six-family buildings near the Brooklyn border giving way to two-family and single-family row houses deeper into Queens.

Six-family Mathews flats

Three stories over a basement, two flats a floor, a stoop up to glazed double doors and a basement entry under the stoop. The type the Commission’s Ridgewood North and Ridgewood South districts were drawn around.

Flats with a store on the ground floor

On the avenues the same buildings were built with a shop at street level and apartments on the second and third floors. The residents’ door sits beside the storefront, and the store’s back door and the apartments often share a cellar.

Two-story brick rowhouses

Long rows of brick houses built between about 1906 and the First World War, some with bow fronts, porches or steep stoops. Stockholm Street’s 36 two-story rowhouses, on the neighborhood’s last brick-paved street, are the Commission’s best-known example.

Curved two-family rows and houses with stores

Builders such as Paul Stier, who the Commission says put up more than two thousand houses here, produced curved-front two-family rows; the Ring-Gibson houses mixed two- and four-family houses with stores. Owner downstairs, one tenant upstairs, one shared front door.

Pre-1905 frame houses and later houses with garages

Wikipedia notes that most houses built before the 1905 masonry rule were wood frame, and that some later houses were single-family homes with garages. A frame house takes a different strike and a different cable route from a brick flat.

The same buildings across the Bushwick line

The Commission’s report says Mathews began building in both Bushwick and Ridgewood, and the brick rows do not stop at the county line. Across Wyckoff Avenue the doors, stoops and owners’ questions are often the same, under a Brooklyn address.

What the materials mean for the install

The fronts are the first thing an owner worries about, and rightly. The Commission’s report describes buff- and amber-colored brick, much of it what the report calls Kreischer brick, laid with corbelled, recessed and patterned courses; carved limestone door hoods and surrounds; heavy bluestone stoops; and pressed-metal cornices. None of that is replaceable from a catalogue. Our rule on these fronts is that nothing is fastened into carved or patterned stone, and anchors go into mortar joints where a hole can later be repointed invisibly. That is also what the Commission’s permit guidebook asks for in its own words: attach equipment at mortar joints or plain masonry, not decorative features, and keep conduit concealed.

The doors are the second worry. A hundred-year-old wood-and-glass pair has seen several sets of hinges, at least one lock change and a lot of slamming. Before anything electrical goes on, we check that each leaf hangs true, that the hinge screws bite, that the bottom rails are sound and that the doors meet without daylight at the center. An electric strike only works on a door that closes the same way every time, and a pair that sags will defeat the best hardware within a winter.

The cable is the third. The under-stoop entry that every Mathews flat has is a gift here: it gives a route from the basement up into the first-floor hall without breaking any part of the facade, and the controller and battery can live in the basement, inside the building and out of the weather. Where the basement is damp, the equipment goes on a board well off the floor and the cable comes up through the hall rather than along a cellar wall that sweats.

The Paired Doors

Two Leaves, One Lock: Electrifying the Doors at the Top of the Stoop

Most entry hardware sold for apartment buildings assumes a single door. Ridgewood’s flats were built with two narrow leaves that meet in the middle, and that changes almost every decision. One leaf, the active one, is the door people use every day. The other, the inactive leaf, is meant to stay shut, held by bolts at the top and bottom, and opened only to move furniture or a stroller wider than one leaf. A lock on the active leaf only keeps the building closed if the inactive leaf is properly bolted, and in a building where six households and their movers have been opening both leaves for a century, it may well not be.

So the first part of a Ridgewood job is the inactive leaf. We check whether its bolts still reach their keepers in the head of the frame and in the sill or threshold, replace them if they do not, and fit a door contact so the system knows when that leaf is open. The second is the meeting edge, where the two leaves come together: if the strip that covers the gap is missing or worn, the latch can be slipped with a blade from outside. The third is the lock itself. On a pair, the strike usually goes on the inactive leaf’s edge, which takes the active leaf’s latch, or an electrified mortise lock goes into the active leaf with a power transfer through the hinge. Which one suits a given door depends on how thick the stiles are and how much original hardware the owner wants to keep.

Glass is the next question. The Commission calls these doors glazed, and the glass that makes them handsome also lets someone outside see whether anyone is in the hall, and in principle break a pane and reach the inside handle. The Building Code does not let us fix that by making the door hard to open from inside: a street door has to let people out with one motion, without a key, and an electrically locked door has to open when the power fails. What we can do is keep the latch beyond reach of any pane, fit laminated or filmed glass where the owner and, in a historic district, Landmarks staff agree, and make the system report a forced door the moment it happens rather than when someone notices.

Then the reader and the call station. Landmarks’ guidebook lets its staff approve an intercom or doorbell placed within the door opening or right beside it, no more than one intercom and two lights per entrance, fastened at mortar joints, with no more than a short run of visible conduit. On a Ridgewood entrance that usually means a slim station on the plain brick or the reveal of the opening, not on the carved surround and not on the wood. Many fronts already have a hole and a box where an older intercom sat; reusing that position, where it is sound and fits a modern station, is often the least visible choice.

The last piece is the under-stoop door, and here the honest answer is often “less.” It is used by the owner, the boiler technician, the meter reader and whoever takes the trash out, not by visitors. A good mechanical deadbolt or a keypad deadbolt with a short list of codes may be all it needs, plus a contact so the owner knows it was left open. The areaway gate in front of it is a separate question: a lock there stops people sheltering in the areaway but also stops the trash from getting out, and we only fit one when the owner has decided who keeps a key.

Six Flats, One Owner

Six Apartments and a Landlord Downstairs: Who Runs the Door

A Mathews flat was sold, as the Commission’s report puts it, to provide “affordable home-ownership through multi-family occupancy”: the buyer lived in one apartment and rented the other five. Plenty of Ridgewood buildings still work that way, and it changes the design more than any piece of hardware. There is no super, no managing agent and no front desk. The owner is the person who gets the call when the door sticks, who hands out keys, who notices that a former tenant’s boyfriend still has one, and who is woken when someone is locked out. A system for this building has to be one that person can run from a phone in a few minutes a month, and that keeps working on the weeks they cannot.

In practice that means a short credential list with clear names: each of the five rented apartments gets a set number of fobs or phone keys, the owner’s household gets its own, and trades get codes that expire. When a tenant moves out, the owner removes that apartment’s credentials on the day the keys come back, and the new tenant’s are issued on move-in, with the record showing both. We set up a second administrator, usually a grown child or a trusted relative, so the system is not stuck if the owner is in the hospital or abroad for the summer.

The law treats a six-family as a multiple dwelling, and that brings duties. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, says owners of multiple dwellings who use keyless entry must give tenants a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use the system to track tenants outside the building or to harass them. The Multiple Dwelling Law’s §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, and a two-way intercom with remote release in those with eight or more apartments; buildings older than that, like every Mathews flat, have to provide them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. The Housing Maintenance Code’s §27-2043 still requires a key lock and a key for each apartment door. None of that stops an owner from adding fobs; all of it shapes how we set them up.

Being the landlord who lives downstairs also means being careful with what the system shows. Most door platforms can log every entry by every credential. In a six-family that log sits on the phone of a neighbor who sees the tenants in the hall every day. We set the record to what the building needs, entries at the street door and forced or held-open alarms, keep it for a short, written period, and leave out what the owner does not need to know. We draft the required privacy policy in plain English for the owner to adapt.

Rows and Landmarks

A Row Built Alike, a District Reviewed Alike

Walk down a block in Ridgewood North and the doors repeat: the same stoop, the same surround, the same pair of leaves, building after building, because one company built the row from one set of plans. That has practical consequences. A solution worked out on one building usually fits its neighbors, the same strike and the same station sit at the same height on the same brick, and a row that does its doors together can look as uniform as it did in 1912 rather than collecting six different boxes from six different years. When several owners on a row ask together, we survey them on one visit and design one standard entrance for the row, while each building still gets its own price, its own credentials and its own permit paperwork.

The paperwork matters because so much of the neighborhood is regulated. The Commission designated the Stockholm Street Historic District in 2000, the Ridgewood North Historic District on September 15, 2009, the Ridgewood South Historic District in 2010 and the Central Ridgewood Historic District on December 9, 2014, when its announcement described “one of the most harmonious and architecturally-distinguished enclaves of working-class dwellings built in New York City during the early twentieth century” and noted that many original stoops, cut-glass and wood doors and iron railings remain. Inside those lines, the Landmarks Law requires a permit before work that affects the exterior, and the Commission’s own page says owners or members of the owner’s team, including commercial tenants, apply through its online system, Portico.

Most door-station and reader work falls under the staff-level permits the guidebook describes, which are issued by Commission staff without a public hearing when the work meets set criteria. We prepare what such an application usually needs: photos of the entrance, a marked-up elevation showing where each device goes and how it is fastened, the device’s dimensions and finish, and the route of any visible conduit. The decision is the Commission’s; we do not promise an outcome, and we do not install on a district facade before the permit is in hand.

Much of Ridgewood is also inside one of the ten National Register districts listed in 1983, which is not the same as a city district: the city districts are the ones with a permit process for exterior work. We check which map a building sits on before the survey.

The Brooklyn Line

Blocks on Both Sides of the Bushwick Border

The 1925 boundary is a zigzag, and Wikipedia’s note on it reads like directions: from Newtown Creek along Onderdonk Avenue, then Flushing Avenue, Cypress Avenue, Grove Street, St. Nicholas Avenue, Palmetto Street, Wyckoff Avenue, Covert Street and Irving Avenue, before it enters the cemeteries. A building’s borough depends on which side of that line its lot falls, and owners, couriers and city agencies do sometimes get it wrong. For a door job the practical differences are smaller than people expect. The Department of Buildings and the Landmarks Preservation Commission are citywide agencies with the same rules on both sides; the Multiple Dwelling Law and the tenant data privacy rules apply in both boroughs; and we quote both sides from the same Brooklyn list with the same crew.

One difference shows up on the door itself: the address. Queens numbers its buildings with a hyphen, the cross street first and the house number after it, and Brooklyn does not. In the Ridgewood North report you can see both on one block: the buildings on Palmetto Street and Woodbine Street carry plain Brooklyn-style numbers, while those facing Forest Avenue carry hyphenated Queens numbers. Couriers navigating by app and visitors from out of town are guided by whatever the door says. A call station whose directory, labels and on-screen text match the number painted on the building, with no stray “Brooklyn” or “Queens,” is a small thing that saves a lot of rung bells next door.

The other is transit. The Myrtle–Wyckoff Avenues station sits right on the border with the L and M trains and the Ridgewood bus terminal beside it, and the M runs on its 1915 elevated structure through the neighborhood. Buildings near those stations see more strangers on their stoops at odd hours, which argues for doors that latch every time and a record of forced or held-open events, not for anything more dramatic.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Ridgewood

Ridgewood’s shops sit in ground floors built as stores or later cut into the flats above. Who operates on each corridor below is described from the sources at the foot of this page, not from any client list.

Myrtle Avenue, Wyckoff Avenue to Fresh Pond Road

The Myrtle Avenue Business Improvement District has covered this stretch since 1988; its site describes close to or more than three hundred businesses. The BID describes a balanced mix of traditional and contemporary businesses; landmarks on the stretch include the 1929 Ridgewood Savings Bank headquarters at Forest Avenue and the 1916 Ridgewood Theater building.

Fresh Pond Road

The neighborhood’s other main shopping street, served by the M at Fresh Pond Road. QNS reported in October 2025 that the BID’s new leadership is continuing work to bring Fresh Pond Road businesses into the district, a process its president put at about two years.

Forest Avenue

Shops at the ground floor of flats built with storefronts, according to the Ridgewood North report, and a cluster of businesses Wikipedia associates with the area’s Serbian community. Where a shop has a back door, it usually opens onto a side street or a yard.

Wyckoff Avenue and Myrtle–Wyckoff

The border street and the transit hub: the L and M station, the Ridgewood bus terminal and shops on both sides of the county line.

Fairview and Grandview Avenues

The Commission’s report notes that the flats facing these avenues inside Ridgewood North were built with commercial storefronts on the first floor and apartments above, and that the historic storefronts are the district’s main alterations.

Metropolitan Avenue and the north

One of Ridgewood’s three oldest roads, with Engine 291 and Ladder 140 at its firehouse. Wikipedia notes that the northern part of the neighborhood toward Newtown Creek was historically given over to factories and knitting mills.

Rules that apply to Ridgewood shop and building doors

  • Free egress. Building Code §1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9 allow electrically locked egress doors only with a release that works without a key: a sensor on the inside, a push-to-exit button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, unlocking on power loss and on a fire alarm signal. A shop’s back door that is also an exit is planned around that first.
  • Landmarks. Storefronts inside a city historic district need a Landmarks permit for exterior changes. The Commission’s page says commercial tenants can apply as part of the owner’s team, and the city’s business portal tells applicants to get the building owner’s permission and signature.
  • Apartments above the store. Where the residents’ door shares a building with the shop, the residential side falls under the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Tenant Data Privacy Law; the shop’s credentials and records are kept separate from the tenants’.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Ridgewood: Questions Answered

Running a six-family yourself

Tenants knock on my door downstairs whenever a visitor needs letting in or someone is locked out. Can the system take me out of that loop?

Mostly, yes, and in a six-family that is often the real reason to do it. With a video station, each apartment answers its own callers on a phone or an indoor screen and releases the door for its own guests, so the owner stops being the building’s doorman. A tenant locked out can open the street door from the phone app, or with a code the owner set for that purpose that can be changed afterward, instead of ringing the owner at midnight. Deliveries can have a daytime code. What stays with the owner is the short list of decisions only an owner should make: who gets a credential, when a former tenant’s is removed, and what happens after a forced-door alert. The routine changes take a few minutes in the app; if you would rather not do them, we set up a second administrator or make them for you as a paid service call.

Should my own apartment door be on the same system as the street door my five tenants use, or kept apart from it?

We usually keep it apart. The street doors are shared space and belong on the building’s system, where the record and the rules apply to everyone. Your own apartment door is your home, and putting it on the same list means a mistake in that list, or a person with administrator access, can affect who gets into your rooms. A good keyed lock or a standalone keypad deadbolt on your own door, with no link to the building’s app, is simpler and more private. The exception is an owner who wants one phone app for everything; then we put the owner’s door in its own group that no tenant or trade credential can ever be added to.

Our six-family is below the eight-apartment line in the state intercom rule. What does the law still expect of us at the front door?

More than some owners assume. The intercom requirement in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies automatically to buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968; a Mathews flat is neither. But the same section says older multiple dwellings must install self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and a two-way intercom when a majority of tenants request them in writing, so a six-family can be asked. The Housing Maintenance Code still requires a key lock and a key for each apartment door. And if you add any keyless entry, the Tenant Data Privacy Law’s duties apply because a six-family is a multiple dwelling. None of this is legal advice; HPD and a housing lawyer have the final word, and we make sure what we install meets the rules as written.

The stoop doors themselves

On moving day both leaves of our front doors stand open for hours. How does a controlled door get through a move without being propped open and forgotten?

Plan the move as an event in the system. The owner or tenant tells you the date, and on that morning you open both leaves with the inactive leaf’s bolts drawn deliberately rather than kicked loose. The contacts on both leaves report that the doors are open, and instead of an alarm every few minutes, the system is set to a move mode with an end time. When the end time passes and either leaf is still open, or the inactive leaf’s bolts were not thrown back, the app tells you. It is the forgotten bolt at the end of a long day, not the open door during it, that leaves a building unlocked overnight, so that is the alert we care about.

Our old intercom sits in a box recessed into the brick beside the door. Can a new video station go into that same opening?

Often, yes, and it is usually the least visible choice. We measure the existing opening and the box behind it, check that the brick around it is sound, and compare it with the stations that fit the building. Many modern stations come with flush backboxes or adapter plates, and a station chosen to fit the old opening avoids cutting new brick. If the old opening is larger than any suitable station, a plain filler plate in a matching finish closes the gap. If it is smaller, we would rather surface-mount a slim station at mortar joints than enlarge the cut. In a historic district the reuse still goes into the permit application, with photos of the existing box.

We plan to strip and restore our original wood-and-glass doors next year. Should the access work wait until the restoration is finished?

Do the planning now and the hardware afterward. The restorer needs to know where the strike, the bolts, any electrified lock and its hinge-mounted power transfer will go, because those mean mortises and holes in the wood they are refinishing. We can survey now, agree the hardware positions with you and the restorer, and if the restorer is willing, have them cut the mortises while the doors are off. The cable, controller and station can go in beforehand; the station works on its own for calls, and the electric lock is fitted the week the doors are rehung. That way the old wood is cut once.

Rows, permits and the shops

The building next door is identical to ours and already has an approved door station. Can our Landmarks application simply copy theirs?

You can use it as a model, but each building needs its own permit. Landmarks permits are issued for a specific property and a specific scope of work. What a neighbor’s approval gives you is a tested design: the same station, the same position on the same brick, the same fastening and conduit route, which Commission staff have already found acceptable on an identical front. We draw your entrance the same way, cite the neighbor’s approved work if they agree, and flag any difference between the fronts; the decision is still the Commission’s.

Several owners on our row want to do their front doors at the same time. Does a row-wide job change the plan or the price?

It changes the plan more than the price. We survey all the buildings on one visit and design one standard entrance for the row, so the stations sit at the same height in the same place and the row keeps its uniform look. Each building gets its own written price from the Brooklyn list, its own controller, its own credentials and its own permit application where one is needed; nobody’s tenants can open a neighbor’s door. The savings that do appear come from sharing travel and survey time and from ordering identical parts together, and we show them on each owner’s quote rather than promising a discount in advance.

We rent a storefront in a landmarked row. Who applies for the Landmarks permit for a reader on our shop door: us or the building owner?

Either can file, but not without the owner. The Commission’s applications page says owners or representatives of the owner’s project team, and it names commercial tenants as an example, apply through its Portico system. The city’s business portal tells applicants to get permission and a signature from the building owner before they submit. So a shop tenant can run the application, but needs the landlord’s written consent first. We give both of you the same drawing and photos so the application and the lease agree on what is going on the front.

Parcels, buzzing and the border

Couriers photograph parcels inside our hall and some are gone by evening. Will a camera behind the door or the door’s own entry record do more to stop it?

The entry record tells you who came in; a camera tells you who took the parcel. With fobs on the street door, the log shows which credentials opened the door between the courier’s photo and the moment the parcel was missed, and which visitors were buzzed in by which apartment. A camera inside the hall, aimed at the parcel shelf and not into anyone’s apartment, settles who. Neither stops a thief who breaks the lock, as police say one did on Cypress Avenue in January 2024; a strong lock and a forced-door alert do that.

Some tenants buzz the door open without asking who is there. How do we stop one careless buzz from opening the building to anyone?

Change what a buzz can do. With a video station, a tenant can only release the door during a call made to their own apartment, so an unrequested buzz does nothing, and they see the caller before they press. The station can show the visitor which apartment let them in, and the record notes it, which on its own changes habits. Couriers can get a delivery code that works in daytime hours only, so tenants are not buzzing strangers to save themselves a trip downstairs.

Our block sits right on the Brooklyn–Queens line. Does it change the permit, the price or the crew whether the building is in Queens or Brooklyn?

Very little. The Department of Buildings and the Landmarks Preservation Commission are citywide agencies, the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Tenant Data Privacy Law apply on both sides, and we quote both from the same Brooklyn price list with the same technicians from our Troy Avenue office. What does change is the paperwork: the borough and the address on a permit application must match the city’s records for the lot, and the precinct and community board are different on each side. We check the lot on the city’s property records before the survey so the drawings carry the right borough and the right number.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Ridgewood

Google’s AI Overview could not be opened from our build environment, so nothing below quotes it. These are claims that keep appearing in quick online answers, consumer guides and national cost pages such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what Ridgewood doors actually need.

“Ridgewood is a Brooklyn neighborhood.”

Apps and listings often file Ridgewood under Brooklyn. It is in Queens, Community District 5, ZIP 11385, though its Brooklyn history is real.

For a door job the mistake matters only on paper. A permit application, a Landmarks filing or an insurance certificate has to carry the borough and address the city has for the lot, and a mismatch slows things down.

We check the lot before we draw anything, and we serve both sides of the line the same way, from the same office and the same price list.

“Any electric strike fits any front door.”

Online guides usually picture a single door in a metal frame. A Ridgewood pair has two leaves, an inactive one held by bolts, a meeting edge where the latch can be attacked, and wood stiles that may be thinner than the strike assumes.

On a pair, the strike goes into the inactive leaf, which only works if that leaf is solidly bolted at head and sill. Fit a strike without fixing the bolts and the building is locked on one leaf and open on the other.

That is why our quotes for paired doors list bolts, contacts and door repair as separate lines before the electronics.

“Historic buildings can’t have modern entry systems.”

Some answers suggest landmark rules forbid intercoms or readers. The Commission’s own guidebook does the opposite: it sets out criteria under which its staff can approve an intercom or doorbell at an entrance, with rules on placement, the number of devices and how they are fastened.

What it asks for is restraint: one intercom and up to two lights per entrance, beside or within the opening, fastened at mortar joints, with conduit hidden.

A slim station on the brick reveal of a Mathews flat fits that description. A large panel bolted to the carved surround does not.

“Access control prices are the same everywhere.”

National cost pages quote one range for a door. Prices really vary with the door, not the zip code: a sound single door with a metal frame is the cheapest kind of job, and a century-old wood pair that needs bolts, a meeting-edge repair and a permit drawing is not.

Ridgewood is priced on this page from our Brooklyn list, the same figures as the rest of the borough, even though the neighborhood is in Queens.

“One consumer smart lock can run a six-family’s street door.”

A consumer smart lock is built for one household. Put it on a six-family’s street door and every tenant needs an account on one person’s app, the record belongs to that person, and the lock’s batteries become the building’s single point of failure.

It also does nothing for visitors. Somebody still has to come down, or buzz blind, to let a guest in.

A small building system with a door station, a strike and a credential list run by the owner solves both, and meets the privacy duties the law puts on owners of multiple dwellings.

“Fobs stop burglaries.”

They stop some. The January 2024 Cypress Avenue case, where police say a man broke the front-door lock in daylight to get at packages, is exactly what a stronger lock and a forced-door alert are for.

The June 2026 burglaries near Central Avenue are a different story: the burglar came in through window screens and a rear basement window. No front-door system would have touched them.

“Install it and forget it.”

Every door system degrades. Closers lose their adjustment, hinges sag, batteries age, and tenants come and go. In a building with a resident owner and no super, nobody else will notice.

On a pair, the failure that matters is the inactive leaf drifting out of its bolts, which no reader can see unless it was fitted with a contact.

2. Survey of both leaves and the basement

3. Map check, permit drawing and written price

5. Install from the basement up

Controller and battery in the basement, cable up through the hall, strike or electrified lock, contacts on both leaves, then the station on plain brick at mortar joints.

6. Program and test with the power off

Each apartment’s calls and credentials are set, the move mode and delivery codes configured, and the door is opened from inside with the lock power switched off before we leave.

7. Enroll the tenants and hand over to the owner

We enroll each household, show the owner and a second administrator how to add and remove credentials, and leave a one-page sheet and a draft privacy policy.

After handover: a five-minute check each season

Once a season, and always after a move in or out, stand inside the front doors and watch the active leaf close behind someone: it should finish its swing and latch without a push. Pull on the inactive leaf; it should not move. Open the app and confirm that both doors show closed. Call one apartment from the station and confirm the picture and the release work. Switch off the lock power for a moment and make sure the door still opens from inside. Then look down the credential list and remove anyone who has moved out or no longer works for you.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Pull the Inactive Leaf Before You Price the Lock

The first thing we do at a paired front door is not look at the old intercom. It is to stand outside, let the active leaf close and latch, and then pull on the inactive leaf. On a pair whose top bolt no longer reaches its keeper, the whole door moves, latch and all, and no amount of electronics on the active leaf will keep the building shut. The second thing is to look at the meeting edge from outside in good light. If daylight shows between the leaves, or the strip that once covered the gap is gone, a thin blade can reach the latch.

Only then do we look at the wall beside the door for a place the station can go without touching the carved stone, and down at the areaway for a way into the basement. On a Mathews flat the under-stoop entry nearly always gives one. The notes we leave an owner start with the door, then the route, then the electronics, in that order, because that is the order things fail in.

This is how AESS surveys a door, not a story about any particular building.

A tenant who is hard of hearing never hears the buzzer. What can a door station do for her?
+

Let her see the call instead of hearing it. With a video station, a visitor’s call can ring her phone, which vibrates and shows the caller’s picture, and an indoor answering screen can be chosen with a visible call indicator and a large display. She releases the door from the screen or the phone after seeing who is there. For deliveries, a daytime courier code means she does not have to answer at all. We set this up for her apartment only, so the other five flats keep whatever ring they prefer.

Will the cable to our door station have to be cut into the bluestone stoop or the brick front?
+

No. On a building with an under-stoop entry, and almost every Mathews flat has one, the cable runs from the controller in the basement up through the floor of the hall just inside the doors, and from there through the wall to the station at a mortar joint. Nothing is chased into the stoop, the stone surround or the brick face. Where a short piece of conduit has to show outside, it is kept to a few inches and finished to match, which is also what the Landmarks guidebook asks for.

We own two adjoining flats in the same row. Can one controller run both front doors?
+

It can, but we usually advise against wiring one building’s door through the other. Each building is its own lot, may be sold or refinanced separately one day, and should keep working if the other has a problem. The better arrangement is a small controller in each basement, both managed from one account, so you see both doors in one app with separate tenant lists. The cost difference is modest, and nothing has to cross the party wall.

How should the call buttons be laid out in a building with two flats on each floor?
+

The way people find the apartments: by floor and by side. A six-button station reads 3 left and 3 right at the top, then 2 and 1, matching the labels on the apartment doors inside, and a store, if there is one, gets its own button set apart. Names are optional; many tenants prefer only the apartment label, and the Tenant Data Privacy Law is a good reason not to put more on the street than the building needs. On a directory screen the same order applies.

A tenant mounted her own video doorbell on the building’s front door. Should it stay once the new station goes in?
+

Usually not on the shared door. A personal doorbell on the front of a six-family records everyone who comes and goes, including the other tenants, and the owner has no say over what it keeps. Once the building station is in, she can answer her own calls on her phone through the station’s app. We remove the doorbell with her agreement, patch the screw holes, and hand it back to her. In a historic district, an extra device on the facade may also need the Commission’s approval.

A tenant uses a wheelchair that is wider than one leaf of our front doors. Can both leaves open together on a fob?
+

Yes, with more hardware. Where the Ridgewood Facts Come From

We read each source below in October 2026 while writing this page, and every local date, count and news report above comes from one of them. Two kinds of statement do not: prices, which are our own Brooklyn list, and descriptions of how we survey and install, which are AESS practice rather than claims about any building in Ridgewood.

  • Wikipedia, “Ridgewood, Queens” — borders, CD5, precinct, name, 1769 and 1925 boundaries, ZIP history, 1905 masonry rule, building types, districts, landmarks, transit
  • Landmarks Preservation Commission, Ridgewood North Historic District designation report (September 15, 2009) — Mathews flats’ plan, entrances, stoops, alterations, storefront avenues, owner-occupants and street numbering
  • Landmarks Preservation Commission, “Central Ridgewood approved as a New York City historic district” (December 9, 2014) — 990 buildings, 1906 to the First World War, Louis Berger & Company’s 5,000-plus buildings, Paul Stier’s 2,000-plus houses, original stoops and cut-glass and wood doors, 112th district
  • CityLand, “Commission Approves District Encompassing almost 1000 Buildings” — designation vote of December 9, 2014 (search excerpt)
  • Wikipedia, Seneca–Onderdonk–Woodward Historic District (Ridgewood South) — 211 contributing buildings on the National Register (1983), city landmark district October 26, 2010, Mathews flats of 1911–1912
  • Landmarks permit guidebook, health and safety chapter — what staff can approve at an entrance: one intercom, two lights, mortar-joint fastening, hidden conduit
  • Landmarks Preservation Commission, Applications — permits for exterior work; owners or the owner’s team, including commercial tenants, apply through Portico
  • NYC Business, “Performing Work on a Landmarked Property” — get permission and a signature from the building owner before applying
  • QNS, “Man wanted for stealing packages from Ridgewood building: NYPD” (January 12, 2024) — front-door lock broken at about 2 p.m. on January 8 at a Cypress Avenue building complex; items taken from packages
  • QNS, “Burglary suspect targeted three residences along same Ridgewood block in one day” (July 7, 2026) — entries through window screens and a rear basement window near 70th and 72nd Streets and Central Avenue on June 17; 81 burglaries in the 104th Precinct through July 5, 2026, against 85 a year earlier
  • NY1, “Six hurt in five-alarm fire at Queens apartment building” (January 6, 2026) — three-story building on Madison Street near Seneca and Onderdonk Avenues; spread to adjacent buildings; four buildings vacated; 26 households registered with the Red Cross
  • Myrtle Avenue Business Improvement District — founded 1988, Wyckoff Avenue to Fresh Pond Road, “over 300” businesses on the home page and “nearly 300” on its Discover page
  • QNS, “Myrtle Ave. BID under new management after longtime president’s retirement” (October 2025) — leadership change and the planned extension to Fresh Pond Road businesses
  • Wikipedia, “Myrtle–Wyckoff Avenues station” — L and M on the Brooklyn–Queens border, 2004–2008 rebuilding, Ridgewood bus terminal
  • Wikipedia, “Ridgewood Reservoir” — 1856–58, decommissioned 1989, National Register 2018, Highland Park, Queens side in Glendale
  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a — self-closing, self-locking doors and intercoms; eight-apartment threshold; older buildings on a majority tenant request
  • HPD, Tenant Data Privacy Law — duties of owners of multiple dwellings that use keyless entry
  • NYC Building Code, egress door sections — §1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9: free exit from locked doors, push-to-exit height, release on power loss
  • Housing Maintenance Code, article 11 (§27-2043) — key lock and key for each dwelling-unit door

Where sources disagree, we say so. Wikipedia’s article on the neighborhood gives the Onderdonk House as built in 1709 in one passage and in the mid-to-late eighteenth century in another. Ridgewood South is counted as about 210 buildings in the city designation summarized by Wikipedia’s Ridgewood article and as 211 contributing buildings in the National Register district of the same area. The Myrtle Avenue BID describes itself as “over 300” businesses on one page and “nearly 300” on another. Wikipedia places the Ridgewood Reservoir in Highland Park, Brooklyn, in one article and on the Queens side, in Glendale, in another. And this page’s address files Ridgewood under Brooklyn because we serve it from our Brooklyn office, while every official source places the neighborhood in Queens; the text follows the official sources.
