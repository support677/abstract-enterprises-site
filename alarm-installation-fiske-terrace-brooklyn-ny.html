Fiske Terrace is a handful of blocks between Foster Avenue and Avenue H, east of the Brighton subway line, where the houses stand apart from one another on lawns, the way they were built in the first decade of the twentieth century. The Landmarks Preservation Commission described about 250 of them, here and in neighboring Midwood Park, as large freestanding houses with big porches behind generous lawns, and in March 2008 it made them a historic district. An alarm for that kind of house is a different job from one for a Brooklyn row house. Every side of the building has doors and windows. Porch roofs put the bedroom windows within reach. The garage is often a separate little building at the end of a long concrete driveway. Phone lines reach the houses across the back yards. And anything mounted outside has to satisfy the Commission. This page explains how we design and install burglar alarms for these houses, for the apartment buildings along the edges of the district, and for the shops on Avenue H, Foster Avenue and Ocean Avenue.

The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Alarm Installation

On a map Fiske Terrace looks like part of Flatbush, and in most reference works it is. Wikipedia describes it as a planned community on the southern edge of Flatbush and north of Midwood, bounded by Foster Avenue, Ocean Avenue, the Long Island Rail Road’s Bay Ridge Branch and the Brighton subway line that carries the B and Q trains. Walk in from Ocean Avenue, though, and the place feels like a small suburb dropped into the borough. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 2008 designation report calls it “a quiet oasis in Flatbush,” sharply set off from the city around it by Foster and Ocean avenues, the Brighton line and the old Manhattan Beach Railroad tracks. The Wall Street Journal, writing about the enclave in December 2014, described large detached houses with driveways, garages, gardens and backyards, hundreds of curbside trees, and streets that dead-end at the subway tracks.

The name has a more precise meaning too. According to the Commission, the historic district joins two early suburbs. Fiske Terrace proper lies generally south of Glenwood Road; its main developer was the T.B. Ackerson Company, which bought the land from the oil merchant George P. Fiske and his family in 1905 and boasted of turning it “from woods into city in eighteen months.” Midwood Park, generally north of Glenwood Road, was largely built by the John R. Corbin Company, which shipped houses to the site as precut parts made in its own factory. Because the old farm lines ran diagonally across the street grid, the Commission notes, Glenwood Road is not a clean dividing line. Neighbors use “Fiske Terrace” loosely for both halves, and so do we on this page, but the distinction shows up inside the houses, as we explain below.

What matters for an alarm is the building type. The Commission counted about 250 houses in the district, nearly all built and occupied by 1914, most of them in three national forms of the period: the boxy foursquare under a hipped or pyramid roof, the front-gabled “temple” house, and the low bungalow with a deep porch. Styles run from Arts and Crafts and Colonial Revival to gambrel-roofed Dutch Colonial Revival, with a few Tudor Revival houses and stucco-and-half-timber fronts. The report singles out generous front lawns, old-growth trees, the planted malls on Glenwood Road and East 17th Street, concrete driveways with grass center strips, and freestanding garages built from about 1910 onward, many of them catalog designs. Every one of those features changes where an intruder can approach unseen and where the alarm has to look.

Security worries are not new here. The designation report records that in the 1950s Fiske Terrace residents grew concerned about crime after the neighborhood’s “dog patrol” ended; police officers had walked the streets with trained dogs from about 1920 to 1945, and afterwards, residents said, “several houses have been broken into.” Today the area is policed by the NYPD 70th Precinct at 154 Lawrence Avenue, whose own page lists Fiske Terrace by name along with Midwood, Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South. In May 2016 the precinct issued a burglary alert, reported by Bklyner, about break-ins through unlocked fire-escape windows and intruders walking apartment hallways trying every door; that pattern belongs to the apartment buildings on the district’s edges rather than to the freestanding houses, and we treat the two differently. The neighbors themselves organized early: the Commission records that residents of the southern part formed the Fiske Terrace Association in 1907, a group once called “the oldest civic group in Brooklyn,” and that by 1910 the Midwood Park Property Owners Association had committees on fire and police services among its other concerns. And in September 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported an NYPD search for two people behind 19 commercial burglaries between August 3 and September 10, entering businesses by throwing an object through a window, with Midwood, Flatbush and Ditmas Park among the neighborhoods hit.

There is a practical side as well. A freestanding house is exposed on four elevations instead of two, its rear yard and side passages are hidden from the street by porches, hedges and the houses next door, and many of these houses sit empty for long stretches of the day. Owners here also live inside a landmark district, which rewards an alarm that is invisible from outside. A camera over the driveway is often part of the same conversation, and we cover camera work on our Fiske Terrace security camera installation page; most households here pair the alarm with that, or with a keypad lock on the side door through access control in Fiske Terrace.

Fiske Terrace facts that shape an alarm design

  • About 250 freestanding houses, nearly all built and occupied by 1914, designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on March 18, 2008 (Commission designation report and press release).
  • District limits: Foster Avenue to Avenue H, and the Brighton subway line to East 19th Street; buildings fronting Ocean Avenue and the area between Avenue H and the Manhattan Beach tracks were left out because of demolition and alteration (designation report).
  • Streets inside it: East 17th, 18th and 19th Streets, Glenwood Road, Avenue H, Foster Avenue, and the short courts and places, DeKoven Court, Waldorf Court, Wellington Court and Irvington Place (building profiles in the designation report).
  • One apartment house: a six-story building at 800 East 17th Street, completed in 1936 on the site of a freestanding house; it is the district’s only apartment house (designation report).
  • Police: NYPD 70th Precinct, 154 Lawrence Avenue, Brooklyn 11230, which names Fiske Terrace among the neighborhoods it covers (NYPD).
  • Transit: Avenue H on the Brighton line, opened around April 26, 1897 under the name Fiske Terrace; its northbound station house began life in 1906 as the Ackerson company’s sales office and was designated a city landmark in 2004 (Wikipedia; designation report).

A Dead Dialer, a Porch Door That Won’t Arm, or a Bell Box Ringing on the Clapboard: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Fiske Terrace

Alarm repairs in Fiske Terrace have a pattern of their own because so many of the systems are old and were installed, extended and abandoned by different companies over decades. The most common call is a system that still sounds but no longer reports: its dialer depended on a copper phone line, often one that reached the house across the back yards on a utility pole, and that line was cancelled or moved when the family switched to fiber or cable. The second is a door that will not let the system arm, usually a side or porch door whose contact worked loose when the old frame shifted with the seasons, or a French door whose magnet was painted over. The third is the exterior bell box, a grey metal siren high on the siding that starts ringing in a storm because water got into it or its tamper switch corroded.

Before anyone drives over, a photo of the keypad screen sent to (347) 934-8335 usually tells us which part has failed. A bell box that will not stop deserves a same-day visit: the City’s Administrative Code, § 24-238, says a building burglar alarm must be able to shut itself off within fifteen minutes of going off, and on a quiet block of freestanding houses a bell ringing longer than that is heard by every porch on the street. Panels from long-gone installers are fine with us, and when a system from the 1980s is past saving, you hear that from us on the phone, not after an invoice.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Fiske Terrace

Each service is a separate line on the written quote, but a typical freestanding Fiske Terrace house uses five or six of them on one panel.

Whole-house perimeter on four sides

Contacts on the front, side, back and cellar doors and on every window a person can reach from the ground, a porch floor, a porch roof, a fence or a garage roof, planned elevation by elevation rather than front and back only.

Porches, sunrooms and porch roofs

Glass-break detection for enclosed porches with long runs of windows, contacts on the door between porch and house, and sensors on the second-floor windows that open onto a porch roof.

Detached garages and outbuildings

Wireless contacts on swing-out or overhead garage doors and on the side door, linked back to the house without digging a trench across a lawn inside the historic district, with the garage as its own zone.

Cellular and dual-path reporting

A cellular communicator, with internet as the second path where wanted, replacing dialers that depended on a copper line strung across the back yard.

Interior motion for big open floors

Pet-tolerant detectors placed where the front hall, the stair and the passage to the kitchen meet, so wide first-floor openings and pocket doors do not leave gaps.

Two-household houses

Separate alarm areas where a house has been divided into apartments, each with its own codes and app logins, with shared doors protecting both households.

Shop and office alarms

Glass-break and interior motion for storefronts on Avenue H, Foster Avenue and Ocean Avenue, back-door and roof-hatch contacts, hold-up buttons and opening and closing reports.

Takeovers of old systems

We meter every old cable run first; the runs that still read clean feed a modern panel, and the rest, with any dead keypads and bells, are cut out and labeled.

Repairs and false-alarm work

Doors that swelled until their magnets no longer line up, contacts lost under layers of paint, bell boxes full of rainwater, and detectors that see a radiator warm up and call it a burglar.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Fiske Terrace’s Architecture

The Commission’s report sorts the district’s houses into a few forms and styles. Each one puts its weak points in a different place, and these are the ones that decide the sensor list.

Foursquares

Box-like two-and-a-half-story houses under hipped or pyramid roofs, usually with a full-width front porch. Windows on all four walls, a side door near the kitchen, and a porch roof that reaches the second-floor front windows. Simple framing makes them the easiest houses here to wire.

Front-gabled “temple” houses

Narrow, deep houses with the gable facing the street. The long side walls carry most of the windows, and those windows face the driveway on one side and a neighbor’s yard a few feet away on the other.

Bungalows

Low houses with deep porches, thick tapered columns and broad eaves, such as the bungalow cottages the report describes on East 19th Street and Glenwood Road. Most rooms are on one main floor, so every window is a ground-level window.

Dutch Colonial Revival houses

Gambrel roofs with long dormers that hold a full third floor of bedrooms. Dormer windows are rarely reachable, but the rooms behind them are where children sleep, which matters for siren placement and night settings.

Stucco, half-timber and Tudor Revival houses

Stucco-covered fronts with false half-timbering, and a few Tudor Revival houses near Avenue H and Irvington Place. Stucco and masonry walls push us toward wireless contacts and toward fixing anything external through mortar joints, never through the decorative face.

Corbin precut houses in Midwood Park

Standard models “planned and practically made” in John R. Corbin’s factory and assembled on site, according to the Commission. Models repeat, though the builders avoided putting identical houses side by side, so one house’s layout often predicts another’s.

Method notes by building type

Wood frame with open cellars. Most of these houses have unfinished or partly finished cellars with the floor joists exposed, which is where the first-floor wiring goes. A cable reaches any first-floor door or window from below in minutes, and a recessed contact drilled into the bottom of a door frame disappears completely. Second and third floors are another matter: plaster walls, original trim and finished attics, so we use wireless contacts upstairs rather than open walls nobody wants opened.

Original windows, storm windows and replacements. Many houses still have original double-hung wood sash, often behind aluminum or wood storm windows; others have replacement units. The contact goes on the sash a person would have to open, which is the inner window, and a storm window in front of it does not change that. Where both sashes of a double-hung window can be lowered, each gets its own magnet.

Porch doors and French doors. Doors onto porches, sunrooms and back decks are often glazed French doors or old doors with large glass panels. A contact tells the alarm the door opened, but not that a pane was broken; for those rooms we add a glass-break detector or make sure an interior motion detector sees the inside of the door.

Four Elevations

Every Side Is a Front: Porches, Side Doors and Upstairs Windows on a Freestanding House

An alarm installer trained on Brooklyn row houses thinks in two directions, the street and the yard. A row house has two short walls with openings and two long party walls with none. A Fiske Terrace house has four walls with openings, and the two side walls are often the longest and least watched. One faces the driveway, which a stranger can walk down at any hour looking as if he belongs; the other faces a strip of side yard and the neighbor’s house a few feet away, screened by hedges. When we survey, we walk all four elevations in order and count the openings on each, and the side elevations nearly always carry more reachable windows than the front.

The porch is the feature that changes the count the most. The Commission’s report describes wraparound porches with grouped columns and deep bungalow porches across the district. A porch does three things to an alarm. Its roof turns second-floor windows into windows a person can reach by climbing a porch post or a railing, so those upstairs windows go on the perimeter. Its floor shelters ground-floor windows from the street, so somebody working at a parlor window under a porch roof is hard to see from the sidewalk at night. And when the porch has been enclosed as a sunroom, it adds a long run of glass with a door into the house behind it. We decide case by case whether the enclosed porch is inside the protected area, with glass-break coverage, or outside it, with the inner door as the perimeter.

Side and back doors deserve the same attention as the front door, and usually more, because they are the doors the family actually uses. In houses with a driveway, the side door near the kitchen is often the everyday entrance, so that is where the main keypad goes, and the front door, opened mostly for guests and deliveries, gets a contact and a longer entry delay only if the household asks for one. Back doors onto decks, cellar doors in the side yard, and the occasional door from a rear porch into a pantry all go on the system as separate zones so the app tells you which one opened.

The Back of the Lot

The Garage at the End of the Driveway

The designation report treats the district’s freestanding garages as historic features in their own right. They began to appear around 1910, many were catalog designs, and some survive with their original details; the report mentions pyramid-roofed garages on East 18th and East 19th Streets, one with a gambrel roof matching its house, and one on East 19th Street that keeps its swing-out wood doors with cross-bracing. Most stand at the far end of a long concrete driveway, sometimes the original two strips with grass between them, behind the house and out of sight from the street.

For an alarm, that garage is a separate building with its own doors, often holding bicycles, tools, a car, or a household’s overflow. Wiring it the old way meant trenching a cable from the house across the yard or the driveway, and in the historic district that is not a casual decision: the Commission’s permit guidebook says permits are required for work affecting the exterior of a building in a historic district, “even on parts of the building that are not visible from the street, as well as work in front, side, and rear yards.” We avoid the question by linking the garage wirelessly. A long-range wireless contact on each garage door, and on the garage’s side door if it has one, reports to the panel in the house; where the distance or a masonry wall weakens the signal, a small repeater inside the house near a rear window closes the gap.

Garage doors in this district come in three kinds, and each takes a different contact. Old swing-out doors get a contact at the top of each leaf, mounted where the door closes against the frame, with enough tolerance for wood that swells in August and shrinks in January. Overhead sectional doors get a heavy-duty contact near the floor at one side. And a garage that has become a studio or a home office, which is common, gets a motion detector inside as well. We set the garage as its own zone, and most owners choose to have it send a phone alert and sound the siren only when the house is armed for the night.

Two details are easy to miss. The first is the garage roof. A flat or low-pitched garage roof against a fence or close to the back of the house can put a rear bedroom window within reach, so the survey always looks back at the house from the garage, not only at the garage from the house. The second is batteries. A long-range transmitter on a garage door that is opened every day works harder than any sensor in the house, and in a cold garage its battery drains faster, so we use transmitters with long-life batteries and set the panel to report a low battery weeks before it fails.

How the Alarm Reports

Phone Lines on Backyard Poles, Fiber on the Street, and Why the Alarm Now Talks by Cellular

One local detail matters more than most people realize. In Fiske Terrace the telephone wires have long run to the houses across the back yards. A Brooklyn Paper article on a Verizon dispute in the district quoted Fiske Terrace Association member Fred Baer: “We have phone lines in the backyards of our homes.” The same article reported that Verizon had installed 20-foot poles on East 18th Street between Foster Avenue and Glenwood Road to guard access points to its underground fiber network, the only place in the city where it had done so, and that residents objected to them. The article’s print edition carries no year that we could confirm, so we cite it only for those facts.

The consequence for alarms is simple. Many older systems in the district sent their signals over a copper phone line that came from a pole in the yard, ran along a fence or a garage, and entered the house at the back. A line like that can be cut by anyone standing in the yard, and plenty of those lines have since been cancelled by owners who moved to fiber or cable service, leaving an alarm that sounds locally but tells nobody. The fix is a cellular communicator inside the panel enclosure, with its antenna inside the house, so there is nothing in the yard to cut. Where the household has reliable internet, we add it as a second reporting path, and the system can be set to warn you if either path drops.

Inside a Historic District

What the Landmarks Commission Reviews, and What an Alarm Can Do Without It

Owning a house in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District does not mean an alarm needs a hearing. It does mean knowing where the line falls. The Commission’s permit guidebook says that, with few exceptions, the Landmarks Law requires owners to get Commission permits before doing work that affects the exterior and, in some cases, the interior of a landmark property, and it lists interior work that needs a permit: projects that need a Department of Buildings permit, and work that may be thought of as interior but affects the exterior. An alarm built entirely inside the house, with contacts on doors and windows, interior motion detectors, a keypad and a panel in a closet or the cellar, and a cellular antenna inside, normally touches neither category. That is the design we start from in Fiske Terrace, and it is the reason we rarely put anything on the outside of these houses at all. Owners should confirm their own situation with the Commission, which is the only body that can say for certain whether a given piece of work needs a permit.

Exterior equipment is different. The Commission’s rule on health, safety and utility equipment, 63 RCNY § 2-20, lets its staff approve small installations such as security cameras, lighting and intercom panels on buildings in historic districts if they meet a list of criteria. Among them: the installation will not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative; it will be attached at mortar joints or through plain brick, stone or wood rather than through significant architectural features; its finish will match the surrounding material or not call attention to itself; visible conduit will be avoided, or limited to about ten inches, painted to match and kept off decorative surfaces; and the installation will be either not visible from a public thoroughfare or as minimally visible as possible, for example beneath a band course, under a stoop, or behind plantings. For security cameras, the rule adds that they must be as small and as few as feasible, with no more than two cameras for every twenty-five feet of street frontage on small buildings of six stories or less with forty feet of frontage or less.

For a Fiske Terrace house that translates into a short list of habits. An exterior siren, if the owner wants one at all, goes on a secondary wall under an eave, finished to match the siding, not on the front gable. Any camera follows the camera rule and stays off porch columns, brackets, shingled gables and decorative trim. Cable runs inside the house, through the cellar and up through closets, so there is nothing to paint along the outside walls. And when we take down an old exterior bell box left by a previous system, which is common here, we patch the screw holes in the clapboard or shingle and leave the surface ready for paint rather than leaving a pale square on a landmark facade. The rule does not mention alarm sirens by name; we treat a siren as the same kind of small equipment and recommend that owners who want one outside ask the Commission’s staff before it goes up.

Ackerson and Corbin Houses

Houses Built in Series: What the Ackerson and Corbin Methods Mean for Sensors and Cable

The two builders who made most of the district built in ways that still help an installer. The Commission quotes the local history Flatbush of Today on the Ackerson company’s change of policy: instead of building houses to order, it began building them “in series from ten to 20 … from carefully selected plans, all containing general features,” with a uniform standard of quality and finish. Ackerson’s houses went up mainly along the courts, East 17th Street, Glenwood Road and the east side of East 19th Street. North of Glenwood Road, John R. Corbin’s company sold complete precut houses; by 1908, the report says, its factory could produce the parts for 1,000 houses a year in various standard models, and many Midwood Park houses are Corbin models assembled on site.

For alarm work, series building means that houses of the same model tend to share window counts, stair positions, cellar layouts and the route a cable can take from the cellar to the second floor. When we have already wired one model, the next house of the same model is quicker to plan, and the survey turns into confirming what has changed: an enclosed porch, a new rear addition, a cellar finished into a playroom, a kitchen moved to the back. The builders deliberately avoided putting identical houses next to each other, the report notes, so “the same model” rarely means “the house next door.” We never assume; the walk around the house is always done in full.

The other half of this inheritance is a century of alterations. Many houses have had windows replaced, porches enclosed, attics finished, kitchens extended and wiring redone, sometimes more than once. Old alarm cable is often still in the walls from two or three earlier systems. Before we reuse any of it, each run is traced and tested, and anything that goes nowhere is cut back and labeled so the next person to open the cellar ceiling is not confused.

The Edges of the District

Dead Ends at the Subway, the Freight Cut Below Avenue H, and the Busy Avenues

Fiske Terrace’s edges are unusually hard. On the west, the Brighton line runs along the ends of the blocks, and, as The Wall Street Journal noted, some streets dead-end at the tracks. On the south, the old Manhattan Beach Railroad, now part of the Long Island Rail Road’s freight-only Bay Ridge Branch, passes below Avenue H; the designation report notes that it stopped carrying passengers in 1924. On the north and east, Foster Avenue and Ocean Avenue carry steady traffic and the apartment buildings and stores that the district’s early residents tried to keep out.

Each edge changes the alarm in small ways. A house near a dead end at the tracks gets very little through traffic, which makes the block quiet and also means fewer passers-by who would notice someone in a side yard after dark; interior motion and contacts on the windows facing the tracks matter more than any outdoor device. Houses backing onto the Brighton line hear and can feel the trains, so we set shock and glass-break detectors on that side with care and test them while a train goes by before leaving. And on the Foster and Ocean Avenue sides, where houses face busier streets, the issue is noise and light, which affect outdoor motion detectors far more than the indoor sensors we prefer.

The apartment buildings along Ocean Avenue and Foster Avenue are a separate case again. They sit just outside the district’s protected boundary, and the 70th Precinct’s May 2016 alert, as Bklyner reported it, described exactly the risks of buildings like these: burglars reaching fire escapes from a roof and entering through unlocked fire-escape windows, and others loitering in hallways to try each apartment door. For a tenant in one of those buildings, the alarm protects the apartment rather than the building. The entry door gets a contact and the hall inside it a motion detector; any window on a fire escape gets a contact and, because the window must still open freely as an exit, never anything that blocks it; and the panel and keypad go inside the apartment, out of sight of the hallway. Wireless parts come down cleanly when the lease ends.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Fiske Terrace

The historic district itself is residential. Its commercial life sits on its edges, on streets the Fiske Terrace and Midwood Park associations spent decades trying to keep at arm’s length, according to the Commission’s account. These are the business settings we alarm around the neighborhood.

Avenue H near the station

The shops around the Avenue H subway stop, just west of the district’s southwest corner. In 1916, the designation report says, the Fiske Terrace Taxpayers Association opposed a row of two-story store-and-flat buildings on Avenue H between East 16th and East 17th Streets. Storefronts with apartments above need the shop and the flat on separate alarm areas.

Foster Avenue

The northern edge. Around 1910, the Eagle reported, a block of stores with apartments above went up on Foster Avenue, which prompted Midwood Park’s residents to put up the entrance posts that still stand at East 17th, 18th and 19th Streets. Ground-floor shops here have glass fronts, back doors onto service passages and tenants overhead.

Ocean Avenue

The eastern edge, left out of the historic district because of demolition and alteration. Apartment buildings, medical and professional offices and ground-floor businesses that need opening and closing reports and codes for each staff member.

Home offices and studios

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Fiske Terrace: Questions Answered

Porches, side walls and the upper floors

Our house has a wraparound porch whose roof sits right under the second-floor bedroom windows. Do those upstairs windows need contacts too?

Yes, every window that opens onto a porch roof or sits within easy reach of one. A porch post, a railing or a downspout is a ladder for anyone determined, and once on the porch roof the person is out of sight of the street behind the porch cornice. We put a contact on each of those sashes and leave out the second-floor windows that face open air with nothing below them to climb.

We enclosed the front porch as a sunroom with windows on three sides. Is it inside the alarm or outside it?

Either can work, and it depends on how you use the room. If it holds things you care about and you want it protected, it goes inside, with one glass-break detector covering the run of windows and a contact on the outer door. If it is a mudroom full of boots and recycling, we leave it outside and treat the door from the sunroom into the house as the perimeter, which is cheaper and avoids false alarms from a room that gets hot and drafty.

Our house stands alone on its lot with windows on all four sides. How do you decide which of thirty-odd windows actually get sensors?

We walk each side and ask one question per window: can a person reach it and open it without a ladder? Ground-floor and cellar windows, windows over porch and garage roofs, and windows reachable from a fence or a rear addition get contacts. Upper windows facing open space do not. Interior motion detectors then cover the rooms, so a missed window still trips the alarm once someone moves inside. Most freestanding houses here end up with somewhere between a third and a half of their windows on contacts.

Our first floor flows through wide openings with pocket doors between the parlor, the dining room and the hall. Where does a motion detector go in a layout like that?

At a point every route crosses, usually where the front hall meets the stair and the passage to the kitchen. One detector there sees anyone going from the parlor, the dining room or the side door toward the stairs or the back of the house. A second detector at the rear covers the kitchen and back door in larger houses. Pocket doors left open or closed do not matter, because the detector watches the hall, not the rooms.

Our third floor is a finished attic under the gambrel roof, with dormer windows. Is anything up there worth protecting?

Usually the windows are not, because nobody reaches a gambrel dormer without a tall ladder, unless the dormer sits just above a lower roof. What the third floor needs is a sounder, because sirens in the front hall can be muffled two floors up, and a night setting that lets the family move around upstairs while the ground floor stays armed.

Garages, yards and the wires behind the house

Our garage still has its original pair of wooden doors that swing outward on hinges and never close quite flush. Can a contact work on doors like that?

Yes, with the right hardware and placement. We fit a wide-gap contact at the top of each leaf, where the door meets the header and moves least as the wood swells and shrinks, rather than at the bottom where it sags. Each leaf gets its own transmitter, so the app shows which side opened, and the signal travels by radio to the house, with no cable across the yard. On doors that rattle in wind we set a short delay before a reading counts, which filters gusts without missing a door that is actually pulled open.

Bikes, tools and a lawn mower live in that garage. Should it set off the siren in the house, or only send a phone alert?

Most owners choose a quiet daytime setting, a phone alert when the garage opens while the house is armed for the day, and a full alarm, siren included, when the house is armed at night. It is a programming choice, not a hardware one, and it can be changed from the app. If the garage holds a car or someone works in it, we can give it its own code so it is armed and disarmed separately from the house.

Our 1990s alarm still shows a monitoring sticker, but we dropped the landline for fiber years ago. How do we find out whether it reports anywhere at all?

Call the monitoring company named on the sticker, if it still exists, and ask for a test: put the system in test mode, trip a door, and ask whether the signal arrived. If the company is gone or nothing arrives, the dialer is talking to a dead line, which is common in houses here, where the old copper ran in from a pole in the back yard. A cellular communicator added to the existing panel, where the panel is still serviceable, restores reporting without replacing the sensors.

Our street dead-ends at the subway tracks, so almost nobody drives past. Is a quiet dead end safer, or does it need anything extra?

It is quieter, which is a mixed blessing: fewer strangers, but also fewer witnesses after dark. We would not add outdoor sensors because of it. What helps is making sure the side and rear windows are on contacts, that interior motion covers the back of the house, and that the alarm reaches your phone and, if you choose, a monitoring center, rather than relying on a neighbor hearing a siren.

Doors, glass and the old building itself

Our original front door has a large oval glass panel. Does a door contact protect it, or can someone reach through the glass and get past it?

A contact only notices the door opening. Someone who breaks the glass and reaches in to turn the lock would trip it the moment the door swings, but not before. If that worries you, a glass-break detector in the front hall reacts to the pane breaking, and the hall motion detector sees anyone who climbs through. On a historic door we never drill the glass or the carved face; the contact goes into the hinge-side or top edge of the frame where it cannot be seen.

Our house was assembled from factory-cut parts, like many Corbin houses in Midwood Park. Does that make wiring easier or harder?

Mostly easier. Factory-made houses of the same model tend to repeat their cellar layout, stair position and the bays where a cable can rise from the cellar, so once we know the model the route is predictable. A century of alterations is the bigger variable: replaced windows, finished cellars and closed-up chases. We still trace every route on site before drilling.

Several neighbors on our block want alarms at the same time. Can you schedule the surveys together?

Yes. We can walk several houses on one block in a single visit and then book the installations back to back, which saves everyone waiting. Each house is still surveyed and priced on its own, because a foursquare with a detached garage and a bungalow with an enclosed porch need different systems, and we do not publish a group price.

Apartments and shops at the edges

Burglars got into shops in Midwood and Ditmas Park this summer by throwing something through the front window. Does a glass-break detector react before they are inside, or only after?

Before. An acoustic glass-break detector listens for the particular sound of a pane shattering, so it trips at the moment of the break, while the person is still outside. A motion detector inside then confirms that someone entered. We set the siren and the monitoring call to start at the first of those two events, which is the fastest response a shop alarm can give.

We live in the one apartment house inside the district, the six-story building on East 17th Street. Is an apartment alarm there any different from one in a house?

The approach is the one we use in any small apartment building: the apartment’s entry door is the main perimeter, windows are covered only where a fire escape, a low roof or a ground-floor position puts them in reach, and nothing goes in the shared hallway. Because the building is inside the historic district, nothing is mounted on its exterior either; a wireless, interior-only system leaves both the building and the landlord’s walls alone.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Fiske Terrace

We had no way to load Google’s AI Overview while writing this page, so none of the lines in quotation marks below comes from it word for word. They paraphrase what automated answers, cost guides and contractor listings on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat, and each is checked here against the district’s houses and its written record.

“Fiske Terrace is just part of Flatbush, so a Flatbush alarm plan fits.”

Administratively, yes: Wikipedia places it on the southern edge of Flatbush, and the 70th Precinct covers it with Midwood and Ditmas Park. Architecturally, no. Much of Flatbush is apartment buildings and attached houses. Fiske Terrace is about 250 freestanding houses on lawns, built mostly between 1905 and 1914, inside a landmark district.

The difference changes the plan from the first minute. An apartment alarm starts at the entry door; a row-house alarm starts at the front and back. A Fiske Terrace alarm starts by counting four elevations, a porch roof and a garage.

So when an online answer offers a “Flatbush” price or package, read it as a starting point for an apartment or a two-family, not for a three-story freestanding house with a wraparound porch.

“A historic district means you need a permit for any alarm.”

Not according to the way the Commission describes its own process. Its guidebook ties permits to work that affects the exterior, or interior work that needs a Department of Buildings permit or affects the exterior. An interior-only alarm normally does neither.

What does need care is anything outside: a siren, a camera, conduit, or a trench across the yard. The Commission’s equipment rule, 63 RCNY § 2-20, sets criteria for those, and its guidebook says yard work is covered too.

That is why our default design here keeps everything inside the house and links the garage wirelessly. Owners who want exterior equipment should check with the Commission; we do not decide permit questions for it.

“Wireless alarms are only for apartments; a real house should be hardwired.”

In these houses the best answer is usually both. First-floor openings are easy to hardwire from the open cellar ceiling, which is cheap and permanent.

Upper floors are plaster and original trim, and fishing cable through them means opening walls an owner of a landmark-district house rarely wants opened. Wireless contacts upstairs avoid that entirely.

The garage is the clearest case: a wired link means trenching across a yard, while a wireless one means two contacts and possibly a repeater.

“Any alarm will call for help when someone breaks in.”

Only if it still has a working way to report. Older systems in this district often used a copper phone line, and Brooklyn Paper quoted a Fiske Terrace Association member saying the phone lines run through the residents’ back yards.

If that line has been cancelled or cut, the system may still sound its siren while telling no one. We find this regularly on repair calls in old houses.

The check takes a minute: put the system in test mode and see whether the monitoring center or your phone gets the signal. If not, a cellular communicator fixes it.

“A basic home alarm costs a few hundred dollars.”

For a starter kit in a small apartment, our own Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic system, and labor-only installation of a kit you already own is $200 to $400.

A freestanding Fiske Terrace house with four exposed sides, a porch roof and a detached garage is a complete-home job, which starts at $2,500 on the same list, with hardwired full-property systems ranging from $2,000 to $6,000.

The difference is not a premium for the neighborhood; Brooklyn is our base price everywhere in the borough. It is simply more openings and more distance.

“Cameras make an alarm unnecessary.”

Cameras record; an alarm decides. A camera over the driveway shows who walked to the garage, but without a sensor on the garage door it does not know whether the door was opened, and without monitoring nobody acts until someone watches the clip.

In a district where the Commission limits the number and placement of cameras on houses, an alarm’s interior sensors also do much of the work that a ring of exterior cameras would do elsewhere.

The strongest setup here is a modest number of well-placed cameras that can show what happened when an interior alarm sensor trips.

“Anyone can install an alarm.”

A homeowner can install a kit in his own house. A business that installs alarms for others in New York State needs a license from the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law.

The license matters for more than paperwork: insurers ask for it when a monitored alarm is used to support a claim or a discount, and it requires qualifying training.

So the first question for anyone offering to wire your house is simply: what is your license number? You will find ours, #12000287431, on each written quote.

Our Installation Process

A bungalow and a three-story Colonial Revival house go through the same seven stages. What makes the job local happens early, in the full circuit of the lot and in the choice to keep every device indoors.

1. Photos from the street, the driveway and the yard

You send four photos and tell us about the porch, the third floor and the garage, so we arrive knowing roughly how many openings to expect.

2. Walk all four sides and the garage

A technician walks the house clockwise from the sidewalk, counting reachable windows and doors on every elevation, then checks the garage doors and their distance from the house.

3. Cellar first, then every floor

Inside, he checks the cellar joists, any old alarm cable and where the phone line enters, then confirms upstairs what he counted outside, and plans an interior-only system.

4. Written price the same day

Openings are listed by elevation, plus the garage, and priced from the Brooklyn list in writing, usually before the technician leaves.

5. Cellar wiring and wireless upstairs

First-floor and cellar openings are wired from below; upper floors and the garage get wireless contacts; nothing is mounted on the facade unless you have asked for it.

6. Panel, communicator and signal test

The panel goes in a closet or the cellar with a cellular communicator, the signal is read at that spot, and every zone and sounder is tested, including a train passing for houses by the tracks.

7. Hand over and return visit

Each household member gets a code and the app, the arming habits are explained, and a return visit a few weeks later adjusts entry delays and any contact on a door that has moved with the season.

Each spring and fall: a fifteen-minute alarm check

Twice a year, put the system in test mode and open every outside door, including the garage doors, watching each one register. Walk past each motion detector. Check that the test signal reaches every phone that should get it and, if you have monitoring, the central station. Look at the battery report, especially for the garage contacts, which work the hardest. Then delete the codes of anyone who no longer needs one: a former tenant, a contractor who finished, a babysitter who moved on. Fall is also the time to check that the porch-roof window contacts still line up after a summer of swelling wood.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk the House Clockwise Before You Count Anything

This is how we survey a freestanding house in Fiske Terrace; it is not the story of any particular address. The technician starts on the sidewalk and looks at the house the way a passer-by would, noting what can and cannot be seen from the street. Then he walks up the driveway, around the back and down the far side, clockwise, all the way around, before stepping indoors. On the way he notes every door, every window a person could reach from the ground, a porch floor, a porch roof, a fence or a garage roof, where the phone and cable lines come in, and where the yard is hidden by hedges or the neighbor’s garage. He stops at the garage and checks how its doors close, how far it is from the house, and whether anything inside it would make a motion detector worthwhile.

Only then does he go in, down to the cellar first to see the joists, the old alarm cable and the line where the phone service enters, then up through each floor to check what he counted from outside. The result is a list of openings by elevation, front, driveway side, back and far side, plus the garage. In these houses the driveway side and the back almost always carry more of the list than the front, and that list, not a package, is what the quote is built from.

Where the Fiske Terrace Facts Come From

Most of what this page says about Fiske Terrace comes from one long document, the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 2008 designation report, supported by the rules, news items and reference articles below, each read in October 2026. Our own contribution is limited to two things: the prices, from the AESS Brooklyn list, and the descriptions of survey and installation methods, which are how we work in general and not reports about a particular house.

  • NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District Designation Report (March 18, 2008) — about 250 freestanding houses; boundaries and exclusions; Fiske Terrace south of Glenwood Road and Midwood Park north of it; George P. Fiske; T.B. Ackerson Company and “woods into city in eighteen months”; building in series; John R. Corbin precut houses and factory capacity; foursquare, temple-house and bungalow forms; styles; malls, lawns, concrete driveways and freestanding garages from about 1910; Midwood Park entrance posts; Fiske Terrace Association (1907); Avenue H and Foster Avenue store disputes; the 1936 apartment house at 800 East 17th Street; the 1950s concern about break-ins after the dog patrol ended; the Manhattan Beach Railroad tracks
  • Landmarks Preservation Commission, Permit Guidebook — when a permit is required, including exterior work in yards and interior work that affects the exterior or needs a Department of Buildings permit; staff-level permits
  • 63 RCNY § 2-20, Health, Safety and Utility Equipment (text read from the copy at nacmiaslaw.com) — installation criteria for small equipment, including placement off primary facades, attachment through plain material, about ten inches of visible conduit, minimal visibility, and the limit on security cameras per twenty-five feet of street frontage
  • Wikipedia, “Flatbush, Brooklyn” (Fiske Terrace section) — Fiske Terrace as a planned community on Flatbush’s southern edge; its boundaries; 2008 designation of 250 homes; 70th Precinct at 154 Lawrence Avenue
  • Wikipedia, “Avenue H station” — opened around April 26, 1897 as Fiske Terrace; station house built 1906 as a sales office, converted 1907; landmark designation June 29, 2004; Q service
  • NYPD, 70th Precinct — 154 Lawrence Avenue, 11230; covers Midwood, Fiske Terrace, Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South; community council meetings at 175 Lawrence Avenue
  • The Wall Street Journal, “Fiske Terrace, an Enclave in Flatbush, Offers a Taste of Suburbia” (December 26, 2014) — detached houses with driveways, garages, gardens and backyards; streets that dead-end at the subway tracks (opening paragraphs only; the rest is behind a paywall)
  • Brooklyn Paper, print edition PDF (no year confirmed) — Verizon’s 20-foot poles on East 18th Street between Foster Avenue and Glenwood Road; Fred Baer of the Fiske Terrace Association: “We have phone lines in the backyards of our homes”
  • Bklyner, “Burglary Alert Issued By 70th Precinct” (May 4, 2016) — entries through unlocked fire-escape windows and hallway door checks in apartment buildings
  • News 12 Brooklyn, September 14, 2026 — 19 commercial burglaries from August 3 to September 10, 2026, entry by throwing an object through a window, including in Midwood, Flatbush and Ditmas Park
  • Administrative Code of the City of New York, § 24-238 — the rule behind our siren programming: a building’s audible alarm has to be capable of stopping itself within a quarter of an hour
  • New York Department of State, alarm installer licensing page — the Article 6-D license an alarm business needs before installing for customers

Where sources describe the place differently, we say so. Wikipedia bounds Fiske Terrace by Foster Avenue, Ocean Avenue, the Bay Ridge Branch and the Brighton line, while the Commission uses the name more narrowly for the part of the historic district generally south of Glenwood Road, calling the northern part Midwood Park, and draws the district’s eastern line at East 19th Street rather than Ocean Avenue. This page uses the everyday, wider meaning and flags the narrower one where it matters.
