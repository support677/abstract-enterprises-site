The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Gowanus is the low stretch of Brooklyn between Park Slope and Carroll Gardens, built around a 1.8-mile canal dug in the 1860s. Since the City Council approved the rezoning in November 2021, which covers 82 blocks and is expected to bring about 8,500 apartments, new buildings of up to 30 stories have been rising along the water and up to 17 along Fourth Avenue. They stand beside a working industrial zone that the city counted at nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 private jobs in 2021, row houses on the side streets, and factory buildings full of artist and maker studios. All of these buildings have cameras. The reasons those cameras stop working are more particular to Gowanus than to most of Brooklyn, and that is what this page is about.

The first reason is water, and it comes from below. The canal collects the overflow of a combined sewer system that carries rainwater and wastewater in the same pipes. The Red Hook Star-Revue, citing a Sarah Lawrence College researcher, reported that as little as 0.2 inches of rain can send raw sewage into the canal, and that the May 20, 2026 storm left about six inches of water on Gowanus streets according to FloodNet data. NYU and CUNY researchers built their FloodNet street sensors in Gowanus to measure exactly this kind of water. On September 29, 2023, about 50 basement studios at 540 President Street, near the canal between Third and Fourth Avenues, took roughly half a foot of water. Photographer Jan Rattia told Hyperallergic, "Thankfully, my hard drives and my negatives are okay," but lost ten years of framed work. Four years earlier, Bklyner reported that a July 2019 storm filled the basement of Colony at 274 Fourth Avenue halfway up its stairs, and owner Kitty Hernandez described the street as "a river." For a camera system, six inches on a cellar floor is enough: that is where the power strip, the adapters and the network switch usually sit.

The city is working on the water, slowly. In July 2023 the Department of Environmental Protection finished a $54 million storm sewer under Third Avenue between Carroll and State Streets, and Commissioner Rohit Aggarwala said the work had "proved their worth most recently during the 4th of July rainstorms." Streetsblog reported that DEP's three-phase, $233 million plan for Fourth Avenue will not be finished until 2033, and that it is designed for storms of up to 1.75 inches of rain an hour, while Hurricane Ida dropped 3.15. Until then, many Gowanus cellars will keep taking water in a hard storm, and the BK Reader found residents still preparing for it in July 2026. Josephine Guevarra told that paper she found two feet of water in her basement during Ida: "Everything was gone in the basement." Her insurer, she said, did not cover it.

The second reason is that Gowanus cameras get asked for footage more often than they used to. On a Sunday morning in March 2026, News 12 reported, five luxury cars were taken from a parking garage on President Street and a Range Rover from another on Sackett Street. A 911 caller saw four men taking the cars, police said they "entered through the side door," and News 12 found that the side door was reached through the adjoining apartment buildings. One resident told the station, "They have a camera everywhere and people are working 24 hours," and the building said it had "taken additional security measures and are fully cooperating with law enforcement." Whether a building's cameras help in a case like that depends on details owners rarely check: whether the side door is actually covered, whether the garage and lobby recorders keep the same time, and whether the drive kept the night in question.

The third reason is building work. Gowanus has been a construction site for years, and construction is hard on camera systems next door. Excavators cut cables that cross a lot line, scaffolds and sidewalk work loosen mounts, and new towers change the light that night cameras depend on. Theft from fit-outs is not new either: in January 2015 Brooklyn Paper reported that a drill, door handles, smoke detectors and strobe lights, along with a $1,000 security camera, were taken from a parole office being built out on Second Avenue near the canal.

Last is who looks after the system. A studio building may have a landlord, a managing agent and forty tenants, none of whom feel the hallway recorder is theirs. A new rental building may still have its cameras on the installer's logins. A row house may have had the same recorder on the same cellar shelf since before the rezoning. Brick Underground has noted that no city law requires rental buildings to have working cameras at all, so a Gowanus camera repair happens when an owner, a board, a manager or a business decides it should. When we finish a repair, we try to leave the system with a person in charge of it, an alert that tells that person when it fails, and its power and network gear off the floor. Owners who call about cameras often ask us to check the front-door intercom on the same visit.

Six Gowanus facts that shape a camera repair

  • The water arrives through the floor before it arrives at the door. Combined sewers back up through floor drains and toilets in heavy rain, so a cellar can take water on a night when the street looks wet but passable. Camera equipment on the cellar floor is the first thing in the building to go.
  • The sewer fixes have dates years away. The Third Avenue storm sewer between Carroll and State Streets was finished in 2023, but DEP's Fourth Avenue plan runs in phases to 2033, and the two sewage tanks being built on the canal are expected to be done in 2029.
  • Gowanus is where FloodNet was built. NYU and CUNY researchers developed their low-cost street flood sensors as a Gowanus project, and the network's readings now show how high water got on a given night. That is useful when you are working out whether the camera failure and the storm happened together.
  • The neighborhood lost power with Park Slope and Boerum Hill in January 2026. Council Member Shahana Hanif's office set up an outage information page for Park Slope, Gowanus and Boerum Hill when power went out on January 31, and wrote to Con Edison on February 1. Recorders that lose power without warning are the ones that come back wrong.
  • Two precincts share the area. The NYPD lists the 76th Precinct, at 191 Union Street, as serving parts of Gowanus, and police appeals for the blocks on the Park Slope side have come from the 78th. When detectives ask for video, they want it from the right night and with the right time on it.
  • Repair prices follow the fault. HomeAdvisor puts the average Brooklyn camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145, but that figure mixes new systems with repairs. A single repair on our Brooklyn list usually costs $150 to $700.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Gowanus's Architecture

Gowanus packs very different buildings onto the same few blocks. Each of the six types below fails in its own way, keeps its recorder in its own kind of place, and has its own answer to the question we ask before any repair: who is allowed to say yes.

Side-street row houses

Brick and brownstone houses on the streets between Third and Fourth Avenues and toward Bond Street, many with a basement unit, a cellar recorder and cameras on the stoop and the yard. Faults gather where the cables enter the cellar.

Avenue buildings with shops below

Three- to six-story buildings on Third and Fourth Avenues with a store on the ground floor, apartments above and a cellar the store uses for stock. Two camera systems often share one cellar and one electrical room.

New canal-side rentals and condos

Buildings from the rezoning and just before it, many built without a cellar because of the flood risk, with network cameras, a garage, a package room and attended or virtual lobbies. Early faults are handover gaps and equipment placed at ground level.

Garages under and beside apartments

Parking floors with their own gate, ramp and side doors, sometimes run by an outside operator and sometimes by the building. The cameras here are the ones asked for most after a theft.

Old factories split into studios

Masonry industrial buildings divided into dozens of artist, craft and small-business studios, with long corridors, freight elevators and basement studios that have flooded. One landlord's recorder serves many tenants.

Shops, sheds and yards in the industrial zone

Low buildings, auto shops, contractors' yards, fabrication shops and warehouses toward Second Avenue and Hamilton Avenue, with roll-up doors, outdoor storage and cameras on long runs across open lots.

Side-street row houses: the cellar wall is the weak point

On a Gowanus row house the cameras were usually put in by one installer, with the recorder on a shelf or table in the cellar near the electric meter, the boiler and the internet modem. The cables come through the cellar wall or a window frame and climb the back or front of the house. Water does not need to cover the cellar floor to cause trouble here: it follows the cable through the wall, sits in a connector at the point of entry, and the camera at the far end starts flickering weeks later. When we trace a dead row-house camera, we start at the place the cable comes into the cellar and at the power strip it plugs into, because those two spots explain more failures than the camera does.

Many of these houses also have a basement apartment, and the BK Reader has reported that many Gowanus basement units were built before FEMA's flood standards. When the recorder or the camera power sits in that apartment, the tenant both controls access and lives with the water. We move the camera power and the recorder to the owner's side where it is practical, and we keep the cellar cable entries sealed and the connectors raised so a wet night is a cleanup job, not a camera repair.

Avenue buildings with shops below: two systems, one cellar

Third and Fourth Avenue buildings usually have two camera systems. The store has its own recorder, often under the counter or in the stock room, covering the register, the door and the aisles. The building has another, covering the residents' door, the stairs and the cellar. Both often depend on the same cellar, and both lose power when that cellar takes water. Bklyner's report from July 2019 named two Fourth Avenue cellars that filled the same night: Colony at 274 Fourth Avenue, halfway up the stairs, and Taheni Mediterranean Grill at 224 Fourth Avenue at Union Street, to about ankle height. Taheni's own basement pump cleared the water after the rain stopped. Any camera power supply or switch on either floor that night would have been underwater.

The repair in these buildings starts by finding out which system is which. We label each cable by system and by camera, put each system's power on its own protected outlet, and make sure neither the landlord nor the tenant can switch off the other's cameras by accident.

New canal-side rentals and condos: newer cameras, lower equipment rooms

The newest Gowanus buildings use network cameras that rarely fail the way old coax systems do. Their weak points are different. Some were built without cellars at all: the two Lightstone buildings at 363 and 365 Bond Street, about 700 apartments with a 193-space garage, were designed that way because the site is flood-prone. That pushes the camera head-end, the network switches and their battery backups into ground-floor rooms, and ground floor in Gowanus is not always above the water. Buildings in the Gowanus Wharf group, such as Union Channel with its 224 apartments, 24-hour attended lobby and 98 parking spaces, and the 260-unit Douglass Port at 251 Douglass Street, add garages, package rooms and amenity floors, which means more cameras on more switches.

The other weak point is the handover. A new building's camera system is often set up by the electrical or low-voltage contractor during construction and handed to management at opening with the installer's passwords, a recorder set to keep only a few days, and a few cameras that were mounted but never added. A repair in a new building often starts with an inventory: what is installed, who holds the logins, which switch port feeds which camera, and how long the drive actually keeps video. Only then do we chase the dark cameras.

Garages under and beside apartments: the views people ask for

The 2026 thefts on President and Sackett Streets showed how a building garage works as a security problem. The cars went out through the garage, but police said the thieves came in through a side door reached from the apartment buildings. For a camera system, that means the important views are not just the gate and the ramp. They include the door between the garage and the building, the stairs and elevators that lead to it, and the corridor on the residential side. A garage system that covers only the entrance and the exit will show cars leaving and very little else.

Garage cameras also fail in their own ways. Exhaust and dust film the lenses, headlights wash out the picture at the ramp, hosed-down floors put water into low junction boxes, and garages run by an outside operator often have a separate recorder with a different clock from the building's. We repair the cameras, clean and reseal what the garage environment has damaged, and check that the garage and building recorders keep the same time, so footage from both tells one story.

Old factories split into studios: one recorder, many tenants

Gowanus studio buildings occupy former factories on and near Second, Third and Fourth Avenues. Hyperallergic reported in 2022, around the 26th Gowanus Open Studios, that studio landlords had begun requiring tighter tenant screening, insurance and alarms, and in 2023 that the basement studios at 540 President Street flooded. Cameras in these buildings cover long corridors, stairwells, freight elevators, loading doors and sometimes the basement studio floor. They usually belong to the landlord, but the people who notice they are down are the tenants, and a tenant cannot usually authorize a repair. We need the landlord or the managing agent to approve the work and hold the recorder login.

The buildings themselves cause faults too. Thick masonry walls block Wi-Fi, so cameras that were added cheaply on wireless drop out between floors. Long runs from a single recorder push older cameras to the edge of what their cable can carry. In the basements, the flooding that ruins art also ruins the power supplies on the floor. A studio-building repair usually ends with a list for the landlord of every camera, its condition and what it would cost to bring each up to standard.

Shops, sheds and yards in the industrial zone: long runs and harsh work

The city's 2021 plan for the Gowanus industrial business zone described a mix of manufacturing, construction, warehousing, arts, retail and offices, and Brooklyn Paper's report on that plan named auto body shops, big-box stores and artist studios among them. Camera systems here face work that homes and offices never see: welding flash and grinding sparks in fabrication shops, paint mist near spray booths, trucks and forklifts brushing walls, and yards where the only light at night is a work lamp on a timer. Runs from the office to the far corner of a yard can be long, and outdoor connections along a fence or a shed roof take the weather full on.

Repairs in these buildings usually involve moving cameras out of the work, not just replacing them: up and away from a welding bay, behind a protective hood where sparks fly, onto a wall a truck cannot reach. We also move recorders out of dusty shop floors into the office, and put the long outdoor runs in conduit where they cross the yard. Tell us what work happens near each camera when you book.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Gowanus Cellar After the Rain

These notes set out how our technicians handle a Gowanus building whose cameras went out on a wet night, when nobody yet knows whether the water, the power or something else is to blame.

Look before touching. From the stairs, the technician looks for the water line on the walls, wet outlets and power strips, and anything still plugged in on the floor. If water is still touching electrical gear, the space is made safe with the building's power to that area off before anyone steps in.

Find the recorder and leave it alone if it is dry and running. A recorder that stayed above the water usually holds the footage of the night the cameras died, including the moment the water reached the switch or power supply. That clip is copied first, with the recorder's clock noted against real time.

Work from the floor up. Power strips, adapters, the central power box, the PoE switch and any splice or junction box below the water line are checked first, because one wet power box or switch can take down every camera in the building. Anything that sat in water is replaced, not dried and trusted.

Follow the cable to where it enters. Where a camera cable comes through a cellar wall, a foundation or a garage slab, the technician checks the entry for water tracking along the cable and the nearest connector for corrosion. A dry room with a wet connector at the wall is common.

Price it, raise it, record it. You hear what failed and get a written price before anything is replaced, with the cost of lifting power supplies and switches off the floor shown on its own line. After the repair, every camera is checked by day and after dark, the recorder is put on battery backup, alerts for lost video are turned on, and the photos of the damage are left with you for any insurance claim.

Fault by Fault

Five Ways Gowanus Camera Systems Break, Starting at the Floor

Almost every Gowanus camera failure we see comes down to one of five causes. Knowing which one you have is what decides whether the visit ends at the service call or turns into a recorder replacement.

Water from the drains, not just the street

Gowanus sits on a combined sewer system that overflows into the canal at small amounts of rain, and when the pipes are full, water can push back up through cellar floor drains and toilets. That means a cellar can flood from the inside on a night when water never comes through the door. The camera gear most at risk is whatever is plugged in at floor level: power strips, plug-in adapters, the central power box, the PoE switch and the recorder if it sits on the floor. Raising those parts by a few feet, onto a shelf or a wall-mounted board, prevents most of the damage we are called for. A camera on the ceiling can survive the flood and still go dark because its power came from a strip on the floor.

Damp that stays after the water leaves

After the water drains, the damp stays. Cellars near the canal can stay humid for weeks, and that humidity corrodes the bare metal of camera connectors, power terminals and the contacts inside switches. The picture may come back after a flood and then fail a month later, which owners reasonably take to be a new problem. It usually is not. When we find green or white crust on connectors, we cut back to clean cable and re-terminate, put connections in sealed boxes, and check the recorder's drive for errors, since drives that ran hot and damp are the next thing to go.

Power that drops without warning

The outage that started on January 31, 2026 across Park Slope, Gowanus and Boerum Hill is the kind of event that sends camera systems into trouble. A recorder that loses power while it is writing to its drive can come back on a setup screen, ask to initialize the drive, or lose its clock and network settings. A surge when the power comes back can kill adapters and switch ports, so some cameras return and others do not. Floods add a second kind of outage: when a cellar takes water, the building's own breakers may trip or be shut off for safety, cutting the camera system without warning. A battery backup sized for the recorder and the switch, mounted off the floor, rides through short cuts and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in long ones.

Construction on the next lot

With towers rising across the rezoned blocks, many Gowanus cameras now sit next to an active construction site. Excavation cuts cables that cross a property line or run under a yard. Pile driving and heavy equipment loosen camera mounts and connectors. Scaffolds and sidewalk sheds block views, and site floodlights wash out night pictures across the street. When a camera dies the same week work starts next door, we trace the run first, photograph what we find, and put it in the written repair record so you have something to send the contractor.

Systems handed over without an owner

The last cause is not hardware. In new buildings, studio buildings and houses that have changed hands, the recorder often works but nobody can see it, because the login belongs to an installer, a former manager, a former tenant or a relative. At the April 2025 City Council hearing on public housing, NYCHA's Lauren Gray said that "some of our systems are standalone," and that her team aims to repair a reported camera within 24 to 72 hours. That is a public housing answer, but it holds everywhere: a camera gets fixed only when somebody is responsible for noticing it is down. We put the system in the owner's name, set alerts that reach that person, and write down who holds the passwords.

Commercial Security Camera Repair

Business Security Camera Repair in Gowanus

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Gowanus: Questions Answered

These are the situations Gowanus homeowners, landlords, boards, garage operators, studio landlords and business owners describe when they call. Each answer is written for the buildings here.

Water, cellars and the canal

Floodwater only came up to our ankles in the basement studios, yet the hallway cameras up near the ceiling went dead the next morning. How can shallow water knock out a camera mounted that high?

The camera was almost certainly fine; what drowned was its power or its network connection. In most basements the cameras are fed from a central power box, a plug-in adapter or a PoE switch, and those usually sit on the floor, on a low shelf or in a closet at floor level. Six inches of water is enough to short them, and every camera they feed goes dark at once. We test the cameras on a known-good supply, replace the wet power box or switch, and remount the new one well above the water line, so the next flood leaves the cameras running.

Since our cellar took water, the metal ends on the camera cables down there have turned green and crusty. Does all that cable have to be replaced?

Usually not. The crust is corrosion on the bare metal of the connectors, helped along by floodwater and the damp that lingers afterward. Copper inside an intact jacket a few inches back is normally clean. We cut each cable back to bright metal, put on new connectors, and close the connection in a sealed box placed above the floor. Cable that lay in water for a long time, or whose jacket is split, gets tested end to end, and only the sections that fail are replaced.

The power to our cellar was shut off for the flood and the electrician now says it can go back on. Should we switch the camera system back on at the same time?

Not until someone has looked at it. Anything in the camera system that sat in water, such as power strips, adapters, a power box, a switch or a recorder on the floor, can short or fail badly the moment it is powered, and it can take the drive's footage with it. Leave the camera equipment unplugged when the cellar power comes back, send us photos of where everything sits and how high the water got, and we bring the system back piece by piece, starting with the recorder if it stayed dry.

Our recorder restarts by itself during heavy rain, right when the cellar sump pump kicks on. Are the two related?

Very likely. A sump pump motor draws a large burst of current when it starts, and if it shares a circuit or a power strip with the recorder, that burst can dip the voltage enough to reset the recorder. It is the worst possible timing, since the recorder restarts during the storm you most want recorded. We check what else is on the recorder's outlet, put the recorder and switch on a battery backup that smooths out those dips, and recommend that an electrician give the pump its own circuit if it does not already have one.

Our cellar camera went dark while the water was rising, but the recorder upstairs stayed dry. Is what it recorded before it died good enough for our flood insurance claim?

Often it is the best record you have. The clip shows the time water first appeared, where it came in, and how fast it rose until the camera or its power failed. We export that footage with its original timestamp, note the recorder's clock against real time, and add our photos of the waterline and the damaged equipment. Whether your insurer accepts it is their decision.

Garages, side doors and police requests

Detectives asked us to show how thieves got from a side door into our garage and out with the cars, but the garage cameras and the lobby cameras are on two recorders whose clocks are minutes apart. Can the footage be lined up into one timeline?

Yes. We export the clips from each recorder with its original timestamps, measure how far each recorder's clock is from real time on the day of the export, and write down those offsets so the clips can be put in the right order. If one recorder has drifted by a known amount, that offset holds back to its last time change, which the recorder's log usually shows. Then we set both recorders to take their time from the same internet time source, so the next set of clips lines up without arithmetic.

Can our cameras be tied to the door between the residential corridor and the garage, so every time that door opens the recorder marks it?

In most systems, yes. Many recorders and network cameras have alarm inputs that accept a simple door contact, and when the door opens they can start recording at full quality, add a marker on the timeline and send an alert after hours. If the door already has a contact for an access control or alarm system, we can often share its signal. The result is that, instead of scrolling hours of garage video, you can jump straight to every opening of that door on a given night.

Shops, workshops and the lot next door

The camera in our metal shop washes out to pure white whenever someone welds, and the dome has started to look pitted. Is the camera failing?

The camera is probably working; the welding is beating it. An arc is far brighter than anything a camera's exposure is set for, so the picture blows out while the arc is lit, and spatter that reaches the dome burns small pits that blur the picture for good. We move the camera so it sees the bay from the side rather than looking into the arc, set its exposure and wide dynamic range for the bright flashes, and replace the pitted dome or swap the camera for a hooded bullet whose glass sits back from the sparks.

Our bar's back room runs a haze machine for events, and on those nights the cameras show fog and keep sending motion alerts. Can that be fixed without turning the cameras off?

Mostly. Haze scatters light, especially a camera's own infrared, so the picture turns milky and every drifting cloud counts as motion. We turn off the infrared on cameras in the haze and rely on the room's lighting, switch motion detection to person detection where the camera supports it, and keep the camera on the door and the bar outside the haze where possible. The footage will never be as clear as on a clear night, but it will show who came and went.

An excavator on the construction lot next door cut the cable to our yard camera. Can your written repair record be used to send the bill to the developer?

It is the kind of record that helps. We photograph where the cable was cut and where it ran, note the date we found it and what had to be replaced, and give you an itemized invoice. Many owners send that to the general contractor or the developer, whose insurance usually handles damage to neighboring property. Whether they pay is between you and them, and for a large claim you may want your own lawyer's advice, but the repair record gives you facts rather than a guess.

The camera arm on our old factory building is bolted into brick that has started to crumble around the anchors. Can it be remounted safely?

Yes, but not in the same holes. Old factory brick can be soft, and years of a camera arm levering on a few anchors in the face of one brick will break it out. We move the mount to sound brick or into a mortar joint with anchors meant for old masonry, use a backplate that spreads the load across several bricks, and add a safety tether on high mounts over a sidewalk. If the brick around the old holes is loose, we tell you so a mason can look at it, since that is a facade repair, not a camera one.

Since the tower across the street started work, its site floodlights shine at our building all night, and our front camera now shows glare and streaks after dark. Is the camera ruined?

No. Strong lights aimed toward a camera overwhelm its exposure and leave streaks and halos, especially on a dome with a dirty or scratched cover. We clean or replace the cover, adjust the camera's wide dynamic range and backlight settings, and if needed tilt or shade it so the floodlights fall outside its view. If the site lights are aimed off their own property, it is reasonable to ask the contractor to re-aim them; the change on our side keeps the camera usable either way.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Gowanus

These claims show up in AI answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere. In a Gowanus cellar, garage or studio building, each one costs owners a camera, a clip or a second repair.

"Let water-damaged electronics dry out and they will usually work again"

Advice for a phone dropped in a sink often gets applied to camera gear: unplug it, let it dry for a few days, then try it.

Gowanus floodwater is not sink water. When the combined sewers back up, the water in a cellar carries sewage and silt, and what it leaves on a circuit board keeps corroding after the board looks dry. A power box or a switch that dried out and started again can fail weeks later, sometimes taking cameras or the recorder with it.

Power supplies, adapters, strips and switches that sat in floodwater get replaced. A recorder that was in water is not powered again until its drive has been removed, so the footage can be recovered separately if it matters.

"Cameras mounted high are safe from flooding"

Answers about protecting cameras from floods usually focus on the camera itself: weatherproof ratings and height.

The cameras that die in Gowanus floods are usually high and dry. What fails is the equipment feeding them from below: the power strip on the cellar floor, the PoE switch on a low shelf, the splice box at the base of a wall. The 540 President Street basement studios took about half a foot of water in 2023, and that is above the height of most of what plugs into a cellar outlet.

Ask where each camera gets its power and its network, not just where it hangs. Raising those few boxes protects every camera they feed.

"A factory reset fixes a recorder that will not start after an outage"

When a recorder comes back from a power cut stuck on a startup or setup screen, a reset to factory settings is a common suggestion.

A reset can wipe the camera list, the passwords, the network settings and the recording schedule, and some recorders lose track of the footage on the drive in the process. After an outage like the one that hit Gowanus, Park Slope and Boerum Hill in January 2026, many recorders only need time to check their drive, or a corrupted setting repaired. Resetting first turns a short repair into a full rebuild, and it can cost you the night you needed.

Copy the footage first, then diagnose. A reset is the last step, not the first.

"Switch to wireless cameras and flooding will not damage your wiring"

Some answers suggest replacing a flood-hit wired system with Wi-Fi cameras so there is no cable left to damage.

Every wireless camera still needs power, and in a Gowanus cellar its outlet or adapter floods just as a wired system's power box does. The router it talks to often sits beside the modem in the same cellar. Thick factory masonry also blocks Wi-Fi between floors.

Repairing the wired system and raising its power and switches is usually cheaper and steadier. Wi-Fi makes sense for one hard-to-reach spot, set up on the owner's network.

"Move the recorder upstairs and the flooding problem is solved"

Moving the recorder out of the cellar is the standard advice for flood-prone buildings, and it is good advice as far as it goes.

The recorder is one box. In many Gowanus buildings the camera power supplies, the PoE switches and the cable splices stay in the cellar because that is where the cables meet. Move the recorder upstairs without them and the footage survives the flood, but every camera still goes dark when the cellar fills.

A flood-aware repair lists every piece of the system below the water line and raises or seals each one, starting with whatever feeds the most cameras.

"A camera at the garage entrance covers the garage"

Advice for garage security tends to center on the entrance and exit, where cars and plates can be recorded.

The March 2026 garage thefts on President and Sackett Streets suggest otherwise. Police said the thieves entered through a side door that News 12 found was reached through the neighboring apartment buildings. A camera on the ramp shows the cars leaving; it does not show how people got in.

Check the doors from the building into the garage, the stairs and elevators leading to them, and whether the garage recorder and the building recorder agree on the time. Those are the clips detectives ask for.

"Any recorded footage will work as evidence"

Many answers say simply that saving the clip is enough to help the police or an insurer.

Footage helps when it can be trusted. A clip stamped with a clock that is twenty minutes off, a phone recording of the recorder's screen, or a low-quality export that blurs faces gives detectives and adjusters less than it should. In buildings with more than one recorder, clips that disagree about the time cause more confusion than help.

Export the original file to USB, note the difference between the recorder's clock and real time, and keep the recorders synced. That is part of every repair we do.

FAQ

Gowanus Security Camera Repair FAQ

If our cellar floods again after you replace the camera power supply, does the warranty cover the new one?
+

No. A flood is an outside event, like a power surge or physical damage, not a defect in the part, so water damage falls outside the parts warranty on what we supply. That is the reason we raise power supplies, switches and splices off the floor as part of a Gowanus repair: it is the protection the warranty cannot give. The full warranty terms are in the service agreement you receive.

Will our insurance pay for camera equipment damaged in a flood?
+

That depends on your policy, and only your insurer can say. Standard property policies often treat flood and sewer backup differently, and one Gowanus resident told BK Reader her insurer did not cover her basement losses after Ida. What we can do is photograph the damage and the water line before anything is replaced, keep the damaged parts if you ask, and give you an itemized invoice to submit.

Can your technician work in a cellar that still has water on the floor?
+

Only once the building's power to that space is off and the water is low enough to work safely. We do not stand in water next to live outlets or equipment. Until then, the technician can often copy footage from a recorder that sits upstairs or out of the water, and plan the cellar work from photos, so the repair starts as soon as the cellar is pumped out.

Does replacing cameras on our building need a permit from the Buildings Department?
+

Usually not. Under NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, low-voltage wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication and alarm systems does not need an electrical permit when it is done by a licensed or qualified person, and camera repairs on existing low-voltage wiring fall under that. New line-voltage outlets or circuits are different and need a licensed electrician. Landmarked facades follow the Landmarks Preservation Commission's own rules.

Can a camera in our cellar warn us when water starts coming in?
+

Partly. In a cellar that is normally empty at night, motion or change detection on a camera can catch water spreading across the floor and send an alert with a picture. It is not a flood sensor, though, and it misses slow seepage in the dark. A small water sensor near the floor drain is the reliable warning; the camera is how you see what is happening once the alert arrives.

After the power came back, our recorder took close to an hour before it showed any cameras. Is that normal?
+

It can be. When a recorder loses power without shutting down, many models check the whole drive on startup, and on a large drive that takes a long time. Waiting is better than forcing a restart. If it happens every time the power flickers, or the check finds errors, the drive is worn or the recorder needs a battery backup so it can shut down properly.

Since our cellar got damp, the recorder's fan has become loud and the case feels hot. Is it about to fail?
+

It is a warning worth acting on. Damp and dust clog fans and vents, the recorder runs hotter, and heat shortens the life of the drive inside. We clean or replace the fan, check the drive's health, and move the recorder somewhere cooler and drier and off the floor. If the drive is already reporting errors, we copy the footage you need and replace it.

Why do your technicians put a sealed box behind each outdoor camera?
+

Because the connection behind the camera is where water gets in. A sealed back box keeps rain, condensation and wall seepage away from the connectors and gives the extra cable a dry place to sit. On Gowanus walls and fences that face weather, it is the difference between a connection that lasts years and one that fails after its first wet season.

Our new building has an emergency generator. Can the camera system be connected to it?
+

Often it can, but that is the building engineer's and electrician's decision, because emergency circuits are reserved and laid out by code. What we can do is tell them exactly what the camera system needs, which outlets feed the recorder and the switches, and add a battery backup so the cameras keep running in the seconds before a generator takes over.

The recorder and switch have to stay in our ground-floor equipment room. Can they go in a waterproof enclosure instead?
+

Sometimes, but height beats enclosure. A sealed cabinet keeps out splashes and short exposure, but electronics need airflow, and a fully sealed box runs hot. In most rooms the better answer is a wall-mounted cabinet with ventilation set well above the highest water the room has seen, with cables entering from the top or sides so water cannot run down them into the box.

How should camera cables be run through a cellar that takes water?
