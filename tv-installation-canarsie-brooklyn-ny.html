The Neighborhood

Why Canarsie Properties Need TV Installation

Canarsie sits in the southeastern corner of Brooklyn, between Ralph Avenue and Linden Boulevard on the land side and Fresh Creek Basin, Paerdegat Basin and Jamaica Bay on the water side, with East Flatbush, Flatlands, Bergen Beach and Brownsville around it. For most of its history it was a fishing and resort shore at the end of a railroad, and Wikipedia's account of the neighborhood says most of today's houses went up between the end of the Second World War and the 1970s, much of it on filled marshland. The Department of City Planning's 2017 Resilient Neighborhoods report counted roughly 12,000 residential buildings and noted that most homes predate the 1970s. That is the single most useful fact for anyone hanging a TV here. Canarsie is not a brownstone neighborhood with century-old plaster everywhere, and it is not a new-condo neighborhood with steel studs. It is a postwar neighborhood, and postwar houses have their own walls, their own outlet layouts and their own surprises.

The houses come in a few families. The city's report describes detached and semi-detached one- and two-family houses on lots twenty to fifty feet wide and about a hundred feet deep, attached houses on lots twenty feet wide or narrower, and many homes with a garage at the front and a room behind it that families use as a den. It notes that the blocks between Rockaway Parkway and Fresh Creek south of Avenue L were built mainly in the 1950s and 1960s, while the stretch between Remsen Avenue and Rockaway Parkway has houses from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. On the western side are older brick groups such as 649–677 East 91st Street, which Brownstoner described in 2018: built in 1932, "all brick" with clinker brick and stucco details, each house holding two apartments with a two-car garage behind it, one space per apartment. Round that out with the large garden-apartment developments, Breukelen Houses with 30 buildings of three or seven stories completed in 1952 and Bay View Houses with 23 eight-story buildings completed in 1956, and a gated condominium development, Seaview Estates, finished in 2003.

For a TV, each family means something specific. In the 1930s brick two-families, the front wall of the living room is masonry, and the inside face of that masonry is often plaster with a gap behind it. In the 1950s and 1960s houses, the inside walls are usually a thin coat of plaster over gypsum lath boards, which look like drywall, drill like plaster and hold a screw differently from both. In the attached houses, the longest wall in the living room is usually shared with a neighbor. In the garden-apartment buildings the walls are block and brick, and a management office has rules about what tenants may fix to them. And in rooms that were finished later, the den behind the garage, a finished basement, a kitchen extension, the wall may be wood paneling, drywall glued to block, or new drywall over old plaster. We price each of these from the same Brooklyn list, and we never assume which one you have until a scan and one hidden pilot hole confirm it.

The people in those houses shape the work too. Canarsie is a neighborhood of owners and of family houses that hold more than one household: a two-family with the owners on one floor and a tenant on the other, a basement or ground-floor unit rented out, grandparents living with grown children. That means the person who wants the TV is not always the person who decides what happens to the wall, and it means many homes have two or three TVs in different rooms on different floors, each with its own wall and its own signal problem. It is also a neighborhood that watches sport from all over the world. Caribbean Life reported a club cricket match at Seaview Park in Canarsie on May 30, part of a Brooklyn league season that runs from May to September, and cited a 2025 Nielsen estimate that 13 percent of Americans, about 40 million people, are cricket fans. During the World Cup, a Trinidadian restaurant on Flatlands Avenue ran a prix-fixe menu for match days, according to NYC Tourism's listing. Matches from the Caribbean, England, India and Australia come in at every hour of the day and night, almost always through a streaming app, which makes a strong connection at the TV as important as a straight bracket.

Finally, there is the water. The same city report found that about two-thirds of Canarsie's residential buildings are in the floodplain and that Sandy pushed water five to seven feet above grade in places, flooding basements across the neighborhood. We do not build a TV page around a storm, but it decides one thing on almost every Canarsie job: where the boxes go. A cable box, a router, a streaming hub or an amplifier that lives on a basement floor is the first thing lost in a flood and the hardest thing to replace in a hurry. Where the internet comes into the basement, we plan the equipment shelf up high and run the signal up to the living room, rather than leaving the electronics where the water would reach them first.

Same-Day Repair in Canarsie

Need Repair in Canarsie? A Screen That Has Dropped on One Side, a Bracket Loosening in Gypsum Lath, or a Dead Box in the Basement

Three kinds of urgent call come out of Canarsie's postwar houses more than any others. The first is a screen that has started to sit lower on one side or tip forward from the wall. In a 1950s or 1960s house, the cause is often a bracket fixed with drywall anchors into gypsum lath. Gypsum lath is a set of narrow gypsum boards, usually about three-eighths of an inch thick, with a thin hard coat of plaster on top. A plastic plug or a small toggle can feel solid in it on the day, but the board is brittle, and a full-motion arm that gets pulled out every evening slowly crushes the gypsum around the plug until the top fixings let go. The second is a bracket on an outside brick wall that was screwed into the mortar joints, which are softer than the brick and crumble over time. The third is not the mount at all: the TV is fine, but the cable box, the router or the streaming hub sat on the basement floor and took water, and nothing upstairs works.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back into place. If two people can safely lift it, take its weight off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old or failing TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, the remount is priced as a new mount on that wall, $179.99 plus the wall add-on, with the take-down added. When the old holes sit right where the new fixings need to go, we move the bracket a little sideways or up rather than reuse damaged holes, and we tell you before drilling. If the problem is the wall itself, a loose brick, a cracked section of lath or a soft patch where water once came in, we say so plainly so you can have it repaired before a TV goes back on it.

For a box that took water, the fix is usually two calls, not one. Equipment that belongs to Optimum or Verizon has to be swapped by the provider, so the first call is to them. Once the new box or router is in, our setup-only visit, $195 for the first hour and then in 30-minute blocks, reconnects the TV, the soundbar and the streaming devices, signs you back into your apps with your passwords and moves the replacement equipment off the floor onto a shelf where the next storm will not reach it.

TV Installation for Canarsie's Architecture

Six kinds of home cover nearly every Canarsie TV call. They differ in what the walls are made of, where the cable and internet come into the building, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled. Those three things decide the plan well before anyone talks about screen brands.

The 1930s brick two-family

Groups like 649–677 East 91st Street, built in 1932: all brick, two apartments per house, a garage for each apartment behind. Masonry outside walls, plaster inside.

The 1950s and 1960s detached house

The houses south of Avenue L between Rockaway Parkway and Fresh Creek, with a driveway, often a garage at the front and gypsum-lath plaster inside.

The semi-detached and attached row

Houses on lots twenty feet wide or narrower, where one long living-room wall is shared with the neighbor and the other faces the driveway or the next row.

The garage den and the basement room

Rooms finished later behind the garage or below grade, with walls of paneling, drywall over block or bare block, and the internet coming in close by.

The garden-apartment building

Breukelen Houses and Bay View Houses, completed in 1952 and 1956: brick and block construction, long corridors and a management office with house rules.

The newer condo

Developments such as Seaview Estates, finished in 2003, with drywall interiors, an association that sets rules and a gate between the street and your door.

The 1930s brick two-family: masonry on the outside, two households on the inside

The older two-families on Canarsie's western blocks were built as investments and as homes at the same time: one floor for the owner, one floor to rent. Brownstoner's description of the East 91st Street group, with its clinker brick, stucco, peaked hoods over each doorway and a garage for each apartment behind, fits a lot of what we see on the streets around it. Inside, the living room usually runs across the front of the house, and the wall facing the couch is either the front wall, which is brick, or a side wall, which may be brick if it is an outside wall or framed plaster if it backs onto the stair. Brick holds a TV extremely well when the anchor goes into the brick itself, and on our list it is a $65 add-on because it needs a hammer drill, masonry bits and anchors sized for the bracket.

The catch in a house of this age is what sits between the brick and the paint. Builders often fixed wood strips, called furring, to the inside face of an outside brick wall and plastered over them, which leaves a gap of an inch or so between the plaster and the brick. A wall like that sounds hollow when you knock on it even though the house is solid brick. The bracket then needs fixings long enough to pass through the plaster, cross the gap and set into the brick, or it needs to find the furring strips, which are rarely strong enough to carry a large screen on their own. We find out which with a single pilot hole drilled where the bracket plate will hide it. White powder followed by a drop into nothing and then red dust tells us plaster, gap and brick; white powder followed by wood tells us furring. You hear the answer and the add-on before any real drilling starts.

The two households matter as much as the walls. The upstairs tenant and the downstairs owner are neighbors, and the owner may hear every second of drilling and may well be the one who decides whether a bracket goes up at all. If you rent the other floor, a short text to the owner beforehand saying which wall, how many holes and whether any wire goes inside the wall saves arguments later. If you own the house, putting one good bracket and an in-wall kit in the rental unit's living room is one way to stop a series of tenants drilling a series of holes over the years.

The 1950s and 1960s detached house: gypsum lath, shallow boxes and a driveway

Most of the houses south of Avenue L between Rockaway Parkway and Fresh Creek went up in the two decades after the war, according to the city's report. By then, many builders had stopped using wood lath and started using gypsum lath: boards of gypsum about sixteen inches tall and four feet long, nailed across the studs and covered with a thin, very hard skim of plaster. From the room it looks like drywall. Under a drill it behaves like plaster, with a hard surface that can chip and a brittle core. A stud finder usually works on it, which is helpful, but the studs in a house of this era are not always on modern spacing, so we confirm each stud with a pilot hole rather than measuring from a corner. Fixed into two studs with lag bolts, a bracket on gypsum lath carries any normal TV; fixed into the boards alone with plastic plugs, it is the most common reason we get called back to a house of this age. We price it as plaster, +$45, because it needs slower drilling, sharper bits and more care at the surface.

The other things a house of this age brings are electrical. Living rooms often have outlets low on the baseboard and few of them, and some still have the older two-slot outlets. The TV does not mind where the outlet is, but you will mind a cord hanging down the wall. Our in-wall power and cable kit ($169) solves that on a framed wall without touching the house wiring: a recessed inlet low on the wall plugs into the existing outlet, the kit's own rated cable carries power up inside the stud bay, and a recessed outlet behind the screen feeds the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI. If the existing outlet is damaged, loose in the wall or not grounded, we do not build on it. A new or replaced outlet in New York City needs a licensed electrician and a permit, and we tell you so at the visit rather than hide the problem behind a TV.

Then there is the driveway. Most of these houses have one, which makes the carry easy: the van parks close, the box comes straight in, and nobody has to navigate a walk-up stair. Where the front yard slopes down toward a garage, we bring the TV in through the front door rather than up the garage steps, because a 75-inch box is longer than most of those stairways allow when it has to turn.

The semi-detached and attached row: one shared wall and a narrow room

In a semi-detached house, the long living-room wall on one side is shared with the other half of the building, and in an attached row both long walls may be shared. The city's report puts many of these homes on lots twenty feet wide or less, which makes for a narrow living room where the TV can only really go on the short wall at the front or back, or on one of the long shared walls. Shared walls in Canarsie's postwar rows are generally masonry or a fire-rated assembly between the two houses, and either way the fixings stay inside your side of the wall. Our technician measures the depth, drills short, and uses anchors that set in the first few inches of the wall, so nothing approaches the neighbor's side.

A narrow room also changes the bracket. With the couch only ten or twelve feet from the screen, a fixed low-profile bracket at seated eye height is usually the best choice on a long wall. On the short wall at the front of the room, where the window is, a slight tilt helps with glare from the street side. Full-motion arms are worth it when the dining table sits behind the couch and both need to see the screen, but on a shared masonry wall an arm needs every fixing point on its wall plate, because the arm levers its whole weight on the top anchors when it is pulled out.

The garage den and the basement room: walls finished later, signal close by

The city's report describes Canarsie homes with a garage at the front and a room behind it that families use as a den, and homes where the basement or cellar has been turned into living space. These rooms are where the second TV goes, and they are where we find the most varied walls in the neighborhood. A den that a relative finished in the 1970s may have wood paneling on furring strips over block. A basement finished in the 2000s may have drywall glued straight to the foundation. A garage room may still have one bare block wall. Each takes a different fixing, and paneling in particular needs care: a TV is never hung on the paneling itself, only on what is behind it, so the bracket either finds the furring strips and the block, or the fixings pass through the paneling into the masonry.

The good news in these rooms is the signal. The internet in many Canarsie houses comes in at the basement or the front of the house near the garage, so a TV in the den or the basement is often a few feet from the router. The bad news is the floor. A router, cable box or streaming hub on a basement or garage floor is the first thing lost when water comes in. On every below-grade TV job we put the boxes on a shelf or a wall bracket up high and run the cables to them, and if the router itself is on the floor, we say so and suggest where it could go.

The garden-apartment building: block walls and a management office

Breukelen Houses, with 30 buildings and 1,595 apartments on nearly 65 acres, and Bay View Houses, with 23 eight-story buildings and 1,609 apartments, are the largest residential complexes in Canarsie. Buildings of that era and type are built of brick and concrete block, and many apartment walls are block with plaster or paint on the surface. Block is the wall that catches out handymen, because a plastic plug in a hollow core holds almost nothing; the fixing has to go into the solid web of the block or use an anchor made for hollow masonry. Our list has no separate block price; we price block walls at the brick and stone rate.

The bigger question in a development is permission. Any building with a management office has rules about what tenants may fix to walls, what happens to holes at move-out and whether wiring can run inside a wall. We do not interpret those rules for you, because they belong to the landlord. We ask you to check with the office before booking, and if the answer is that nothing can be opened, we use a paintable cord cover ($59), or we place the TV on its stand ($119.99) with no drilling at all.

The newer condo: drywall, an association and a gate

Seaview Estates, a gated development of five buildings finished in 2003, is the clearest example of newer construction in Canarsie, and newer infill houses appear across the neighborhood. Interiors of this age are drywall, usually on wood studs in low-rise construction, which is exactly what our $179.99 base price assumes. The work is the simplest in the neighborhood, but the paperwork can be the most involved: an association may want a certificate of insurance, set working hours and want to know whether any wall will be opened. Tell us what your association asks for when you book, and we send what is needed before the visit.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Canarsie Living Room

These notes describe how our technicians work in houses like Canarsie's. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Find the internet first. Before looking at the TV wall, the technician asks where the internet comes into the house and where the router sits. In Canarsie that is often the basement or a front room near the garage, and it decides whether the TV will stream on Wi-Fi, need a wired line or need the router moved before anything else makes sense.

Look at the outlets. A postwar living room may have two outlets on the baseboard, one of them on the wrong wall. The technician checks whether the one nearest the TV is sound and grounded before planning an in-wall kit, because the kit plugs into it.

Knock, scan, then one hole. A knock along the wall at screen height, a scan for studs and metal, and one small pilot hole hidden behind where the bracket plate will sit. The dust tells the story: white then wood for gypsum lath on studs, white then a gap then red for plaster furred off brick, grey grit for block.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen on the wall, and the technician asks you to sit where you actually watch. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket costs a wall.

Boxes off the floor. If any box will live below grade, it goes on a shelf or a bracket up high, and its cables are labeled before the technician leaves.

Wall by Wall

Gypsum Lath, Brick, Block or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Canarsie Wall and What It Costs

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. Some Canarsie rooms are exactly that, especially newer condos and rooms that have been redone. Most are not, so the add-ons below are the part of the price that changes from house to house. Each exists because that wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time.

Plaster on gypsum lath (+$45). The usual inside wall of a 1950s or 1960s Canarsie house: gypsum boards nailed to the studs with a thin hard coat of plaster on top. It is strong enough for any normal TV when the bracket is lagged into the studs, and weak when it is not. We drill slowly with sharp bits to keep the surface from chipping, and we confirm each stud individually.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The wall of the older houses between Remsen Avenue and Rockaway Parkway and of some 1930s homes: wooden strips with thicker coats of plaster. It holds in compression and fails in pull, so fixings go through into the framing or use toggles rated for the load.

Brick or stone (+$65). Outside walls of the 1930s two-families and many postwar rows, shared walls in attached houses and the occasional stone accent wall or fireplace surround. Anchors go into the brick or the stone, never into mortar joints, at a safe distance from the edges, with a hammer drill and bits sized to the anchor. Where plaster is furred off the brick, the fixings are long enough to cross the gap.

Concrete block. Basements, garage dens and garden-apartment buildings. Fixings go into the solid webs, or we use anchors made for hollow masonry. Our list prices block at the brick and stone rate.

Paneling, drywall over block and drywall over old plaster. Rooms finished later. Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

After "mount it," the most common request is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be run through or concealed inside walls, and for good reason: a cord inside a wall can be pinched by a fixing, can overheat where it is bundled and cannot be inspected. A cord that has been fed through a wall is one of the things we take out when we rework someone else's installation.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list with the kit included. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet with a short cord, carries power up through the stud bay in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI and network cable. Nothing is connected to the house wiring, which is why it does not need an electrician. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits gypsum lath, wood lath and drywall walls, and it does not suit brick, block or concrete. On those walls the options are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match the wall, or placing the TV directly above a console so the cables drop behind the furniture.

There is a line we do not cross, and it is worth knowing where it is. The NYC Department of Buildings states that "No installation and modification to any electrical systems can be performed without a permit," and its owner guidance says that work must be done by a licensed master electrician. Adding a new outlet high on the wall, moving a circuit or replacing a damaged outlet behind a TV falls on that side of the line. AESS does not do electrical work. If your wall needs a new outlet rather than an in-wall kit, we tell you at the quote stage, and the TV can go up as soon as the electrician has finished.

Signal and Streaming

Fiber, Cable, 5G or Satellite: How Canarsie's Internet Decides Where the TV Can Go

A wall-mounted TV is only as good as the signal that reaches it, and in Canarsie the signal can arrive four different ways. BestNeighborhood's table for the neighborhood, built from the FCC's Broadband Data Collection and updated October 19, 2026, shows Verizon's fiber service available at 99.86 percent of locations, Optimum's cable network at 99.61 percent and Optimum's fiber at 93.73 percent. It also shows T-Mobile's 5G home internet available at 99.40 percent and Verizon's fixed wireless at 82.85 percent, with DISH and DIRECTV satellite TV available everywhere. Almost every Canarsie house has a choice, and each choice puts the router in a different place.

Fiber. A fiber line usually enters the house at the basement or the front wall, where the provider fixes a terminal and then puts the router nearby. That is often the far end of the house from the living room, and two floors below a bedroom TV. Fiber is fast, but the Wi-Fi from a router in a basement corner has to pass through a floor, a few walls and, in many Canarsie houses, a brick or block wall before it reaches the screen. A streaming TV that buffers in the evening is very often a Wi-Fi problem, not an internet problem.

Cable. Optimum's service arrives on coax, and older houses usually have coax jacks in two or three rooms from years of cable TV. Those jacks are useful: the cable box can sit right under the TV, and the old coax can sometimes carry a network signal too, with the right adapters at each end. The weak point is the age of the cable inside the walls, which in some houses has been spliced, painted over or left disconnected.

5G home internet. A fixed wireless box from T-Mobile or Verizon has to sit where it gets the best cell signal, which usually means a window on an upper floor, facing the right way. That puts the router wherever the signal is strongest, not wherever the TV is, and a TV at the back of the house may be a long way from it.

Our setup-only visit ($195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks) is where this gets solved. The technician checks the signal at the TV, signs the TV or streaming device into your Wi-Fi and apps, and, where Wi-Fi is weak, sets up a wired line to the TV or suggests where the router should move. When we are already mounting the TV, most of that happens in the same visit. We do not change your provider's equipment; if a box or router belongs to Optimum or Verizon, the provider replaces or relocates it.

Where the boxes live

Every TV comes with boxes: a cable box, a streaming device, a game console, a soundbar subwoofer, sometimes a satellite receiver. In a Canarsie house, we plan their home with the same two questions every time. Can the remote reach them? And will they stay dry? Most streaming sticks hide behind the TV itself. A cable box needs to see the remote or use a remote that works by radio rather than infrared. A router should sit high and central rather than on a basement floor, which is better for the Wi-Fi and better when the basement takes water. We label every cable we leave behind the console or on the equipment shelf, so the next person who needs to swap a box knows what goes where.

Placement and Safety

Placing a New TV Sensibly in a Canarsie House

Where a TV goes is mostly about comfort: the couch, the window, the glare at four in the afternoon. In Canarsie a few other things belong on the list. The neighborhood is mostly private houses with driveways, and over the years Brooklyn Paper has reported 69th Precinct warnings about daytime burglaries of private houses, with officers advising residents to lock windows and doors. We are TV installers, not police, and nothing about a bracket prevents a burglary, but a few placement habits are simple and cost nothing.

  • Think about what the front window shows. A large screen on the wall directly facing a street-level front window is visible from the sidewalk day and night. If another wall works as well for the couch, it is worth considering.
  • Do not advertise the box. A 75-inch carton on the curb tells the whole block what just came into the house. Break it down and put it out inside another bag or on collection day.
  • Keep the TV off the floor where children play. A screen on its stand needs an anti-tip strap to the wall or furniture if small children are around, and a wall mount removes the risk entirely.

Postwar walls

Our house south of Avenue L went up in the late 1950s. What is the living-room wall most likely made of, and does it change the job?

In a Canarsie house of that age, the inside walls are most often plaster over gypsum lath: narrow gypsum boards nailed across the studs, covered in a thin, very hard coat of plaster. It looks like drywall and behaves more like plaster. A bracket lagged into two studs holds any normal TV on it, but anchors that rely on the board alone are the most common failure we see in these houses. We price it as plaster, a $45 add-on to the $179.99 base, and confirm it with one small pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate. If the room was redone at some point, the wall may be drywall, in which case the add-on drops away.

Signal, cameras and screens

Our internet is a 5G home box that only gets a good signal at an upstairs front window. Can a TV in the back of the house still stream reliably?

Often yes, but the 5G box has to stay where its cell signal is best, so the work is getting the Wi-Fi from that window to the TV. FCC-based figures put T-Mobile's 5G home internet within reach of 99.40 percent of Canarsie locations, so it is a common setup here. If the TV buffers, the usual fixes are a wired line from the box to the TV, a small mesh Wi-Fi unit halfway between them, or moving the TV's streaming device to a spot with a clearer path. Our setup-only visit, $195 for the first hour, covers testing the signal at the TV and connecting whichever option suits the house; if we are mounting the TV anyway, it is checked in the same visit.

We already have security cameras with a recorder in the basement. Can the cameras show on the new living-room TV?

Usually, in one of two ways. Most modern camera systems have an app, and if the TV or its streaming device can run that app, or if you can cast the app from your phone, the cameras show on the big screen with no new cables. The other way is a direct video cable from the recorder to the TV, which works when the recorder is close or a cable route exists. Tell us the recorder's brand and model when you book. We install and service camera systems as well as TVs, so if the recorder itself needs attention, we can say so on the same visit.

Our house has coax jacks in three rooms from the old cable TV days, but we switched to fiber. Are those jacks any use now?

Sometimes. With fiber, the TV no longer needs coax for its picture, but the old coax in the walls can sometimes carry a home network from the router to a far room, using a small adapter at each end. That only works if the coax inside the walls is continuous and in good shape, which in a house that has had several cable installs over the decades is not a given. We test the run before relying on it. If it fails, the choice is Wi-Fi or a new network line to the TV, and we price the visit before doing it.

Who sees the screen

Police here have warned about burglars who watch for houses that look empty. Should a big new TV be visible through our front window?

Yes, to a point. Many fixed and tilting brackets have a locking bar or a hole for a small padlock at the bottom of the plate, which stops the screen from being lifted straight off the hooks.
