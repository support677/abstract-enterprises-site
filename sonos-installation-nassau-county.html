The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Homes Need Sonos Installation

A Sonos system is a set of speakers sitting on top of two things the box never mentions: a path for wire and a network that reaches every room. In most parts of the country those two things are roughly the same from house to house. In Nassau they are not. Within a ten-minute drive you can go from a post-war ranch poured on a slab, to a 1920s Tudor with plaster on wood lath, to a Gold Coast estate with stone walls and a pool house, to a canal house on the South Shore that was lifted onto new piers after Sandy, to an oceanfront condo in Long Beach built from concrete and steel. The same Arc Ultra, the same pair of ceiling speakers and the same Amp go into each of those in a completely different way, and the difference is where most of the cost, time and risk of the job actually lives.

Start with the house most people picture when they think of Nassau. Between 1947 and 1951 the Levitts built 17,447 houses in Levittown, and the ranch models sat on concrete slabs with hot-water radiant heating pipes running through them, according to an architecture firm’s history of the development and the Hofstra University Library’s Levittown exhibit. There is no basement to drop a wire into and nothing to drill through underfoot unless you want to find a heating pipe the hard way. Everything goes up: into the attic, along the top plates and down the inside of a wall. Thousands of Capes and ranches across Hicksville, Wantagh, Bethpage, Plainview and the rest of central Nassau were built on the same idea, and many have since grown dormers and rear extensions that cut the attic into pieces. Planning a Sonos system in one of those houses begins with a flashlight in the attic, not with a speaker catalogue.

Head north and the problem changes. Garden City was laid out from 1869 as one of America’s earliest planned villages, and its Estates section is known for Tudors, brick Georgians and centre-hall Colonials. Pre-war houses like these, in Garden City and in the older village centres around the county, usually do have basements, which makes the first floor easy to reach, but the walls and ceilings above are often plaster, the trim is original and owners rightly don’t want it touched. On the Gold Coast it is different again: more than 1,200 mansions were built on Long Island’s North Shore between the 1890s and the 1930s, according to WNET’s Treasures of New York, and fewer than a third of the grand estates survive. The ones that do, and the large modern houses built on the subdivided land around them, need more zones, more Amps and more network than a typical house, sometimes more than one Sonos system can hold.

Then there is water. Much of the South Shore lives on the water, with canal-front houses running behind Merrick Road from the Freeport area out to Massapequa. After Superstorm Sandy, FEMA inspectors found 860 homes in Long Beach alone substantially damaged, meaning they had to be raised or demolished, according to the city’s building commissioner as reported by the Long Beach Herald, and raised houses are now a familiar sight along the bays. A raised house moves the living space up a full storey and leaves an enclosure underneath that is meant for parking, storage and access. FEMA’s guidance for protecting building utilities says electrical equipment should sit above the required flood elevation, and that applies to the things a Sonos system depends on: the router, the network switch, the Amps and their power. A theatre that works perfectly until the next flood warning is not a good installation. In Long Beach, meanwhile, the city has about 43 high-rises of six to ten storeys along the boardwalk — the only oceanfront high-rises on Long Island, according to a 2021 report republished by the New York State Senate — and the salt air that corrodes their balconies is the same air an outdoor speaker has to live in.

Add the newer layer. Villages such as Westbury and Mineola are building apartments beside their Long Island Rail Road stations: Westbury approved a $97 million, 187-unit building in its transit-oriented district and, in September 2026, a further $100 million project on a former MTA parking lot, as reported by Long Island Business News. People moving into those buildings bring Sonos speakers that have to work on an apartment network, with no ceilings to cut and no landlord who wants holes in new drywall.

And then there are the neighbours. Lots are tight across much of Nassau, decks and pools sit within earshot of the house next door, and every town and village writes its own noise rules. The Town of North Hempstead’s noise chapter, for example, prohibits playing radios, sound amplifiers and similar devices between 10 p.m. and 8 a.m. in a way that creates a noise disruption across a property line. The Village of Hempstead’s chapter sets measured limits instead. A backyard Sonos zone that respects those rules is a design decision, made when the speakers are chosen and aimed, not a conversation with a police officer at 10:30 on a Saturday.

Levitt Ranches and Slab Capes

One storey on a concrete slab with radiant heating pipes, an attic above and no basement below. Wire goes over the top and down inside interior walls. Ceilings are often reachable from the attic, which makes ceiling speakers in the living room and kitchen more practical than people expect, as long as insulation is put back properly.

Dormered and Expanded Capes

The same post-war houses after a dormer, a full second storey or a rear extension. The original attic is gone or split into knee-wall crawl spaces. Upstairs rooms are wired from those crawl spaces or from a closet; downstairs ceilings are often under finished floors and stay speaker-free.

High Ranches and Split-Levels

Half the living space up, half down, with a slab under the lower level. The family room and its TV usually sit on the lower half, the kitchen and living room above, and the stairway between them is the natural chase for wire and Ethernet.

Pre-War Tudors and Colonials

Full basements, plaster walls, original trim and, on many Tudors, a stucco exterior. The basement makes the first floor easy; the second floor is reached from the attic. Outdoor speakers need a mounting plan that respects the stucco rather than cracking it.

Gold Coast Estates and Large New Houses

Wings, guest rooms, pool houses, terraces and stone. More rooms than one router or one Amp can serve, long distances between the equipment and the farthest speakers, and a product count that has to be watched against Sonos’s limit of 32 products per system.

Raised Canal and Bay Houses

Living floors lifted a storey above grade, a new enclosure underneath for parking, storage and access, decks facing the water. The equipment shelf belongs upstairs with the people; wire to the deck runs along the house, not through the enclosure’s flood openings.

How the wire travels in each kind of Nassau house

Slab ranches: over the top. In a slab house the attic is the only hallway. We go up the hatch, find the top plate of the wall behind the TV or above the speaker locations, drill it from above, and drop the cable down inside the wall to a low-voltage box. Ceiling speakers in the rooms below are fed directly from the attic. Before any hole is drilled we check what else lives up there — bathroom fan ducts, old knob-and-tube remnants in older sections, a whole-house fan — and we push insulation back over the work so the house doesn’t lose heat for the sake of a speaker. What we will not do in a slab house is drill the floor: radiant heating pipes in the concrete are a repair bill nobody wants.

Pre-war plaster: from below and from above. The basement is a gift in a Garden City Tudor. Wire for a living-room theatre or dining-room ceiling speakers comes up through the floor plate inside the wall cavity, not through the finished floor. The second floor is reached from the attic. Ceiling cut-outs in plaster are scored and cut slowly with the template Sonos supplies for its in-ceiling speakers, after an obstruction check with a stud finder and, where the manual suggests it, a small pilot hole probed with a length of stiff wire. Sonos’s in-ceiling speakers use a Roto-Lock clamp rated for ceilings up to 1-1/4 inches thick with the caps on or 1-7/8 inches with them off, which covers most plaster-and-lath ceilings; we measure before we promise.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau households ask us on walkthroughs, grouped by the kind of house or situation they come from. Each answer is written for this county; questions about Sonos in general are answered on our Long Island Sonos installation hub.

Slab ranches and expanded Capes

Our Levittown ranch sits on a slab with radiant heat in the floor — how does speaker wire get anywhere without a basement?

It goes up instead of down. In a slab ranch the attic is the only route, so we go in through the hatch, locate the top plate of the wall where the cable has to come out, drill that plate from above and drop the cable inside the wall to a low-voltage box near the TV or the speaker location. Ceiling speakers are fed straight from the attic, which is why they are often easier in a ranch than people expect. What we never do is drill into the slab: the original Levitt houses were heated by hot-water pipes cast into the concrete, and many still are. If a route would need to cross the floor, for example to a Sub on the far side of a sofa, we use a wireless Sub instead or a flat raceway along the baseboard. Before quoting we check the attic for depth, insulation, ducts and anything left by earlier owners, and we put the insulation back the way it was.

Our Cape was dormered years ago and there’s no attic left over the bedrooms — can the upstairs still get ceiling speakers?

Often yes, through the knee-wall spaces. A dormer or a full second storey usually leaves triangular crawl spaces behind the short walls at the eaves, and many already have a small access door. From there we can reach the ceiling joists of the dormer rooms at their low edge and the wall cavities that run down to the first floor. Where the dormer ceiling is a flat ceiling with a small space above it, we can sometimes reach it from a hatch in a closet. If the upstairs ceiling has no space above it at all — common where a dormer was built with a cathedral or sloped ceiling — ceiling speakers would mean opening the drywall, and we say so before you buy anything. In those rooms a pair of Era 100 or Era 300 speakers on wall mounts screwed into studs, or on furniture, gives you the same Sonos zone with no cutting.

The attic over our 1950s Cape has loose, pebbly insulation — can speaker wire still be run through it?

Not until someone has identified it. Loose, grey-brown, pebble-like fill in an older attic may be vermiculite, and the EPA’s guidance is direct: assume vermiculite insulation contains asbestos and do not disturb it; asbestos becomes a risk when fibres get into the air, which is exactly what crawling through it and pushing cable across it would do. If we open your hatch and see material that could be vermiculite, we stop, take a photo and leave the attic alone. You can have it tested or removed by a trained professional, as the EPA suggests; until then, we plan the system without that attic — a soundbar and Sub, self-powered Era speakers, or wire routed through a closet or along trim instead. Fibreglass batts or blown cellulose are a different story: we work through them, keep cable on top of the joists where it won’t be crushed and put the insulation back. The same caution applies to any old cloth-covered wiring we find up there; we don’t touch it, and we tell you so an electrician can look.

Pre-war Tudors and Colonials

Can outdoor speakers be mounted on the stucco and half-timbering of our Garden City Tudor without cracking it?

Yes, if the bracket lands on something solid and the stucco is drilled the right way. Stucco is a thin, brittle skin over lath and sheathing; it cracks around a hammer-drilled hole or a bracket that pulls on it. We locate framing behind the stucco, drill with a masonry bit on rotary (not hammer) setting, seal each hole and fasten through into the framing so the stucco carries no load. The decorative timbers on many Tudors are applied boards rather than structure, so we don’t hang a speaker from them unless we can confirm there is framing behind. Sonos’s outdoor speakers by Sonance use FastMount brackets and need 16-gauge or heavier speaker wire back to an indoor Amp, so the other half of the job is getting that wire inside: usually through a single small sealed penetration under an eave or into the basement rim, never through the window frame. If the facade has been restored recently, we can also mount on a pergola, a garden wall or a post instead.

Can a Sonos Amp or Port live inside the built-in bookcases beside our fireplace?

It can, if the cabinet breathes. Sonos’s Amp product guide asks for at least three inches of clearance around the unit and an operating temperature between 32°F and 104°F; the Port guide asks for at least two inches all around on a shelf, not to block ventilation and to keep it away from radiators and other heat sources. A closed cabinet next to a working fireplace, or one with a cast-iron radiator in the wall below it, can run warmer than that. We check by measuring the space and, where it’s tight, by adding ventilation the cabinetmaker would approve of: a slotted back panel, a grille in the toe kick or a small quiet fan. We also bring Ethernet and power into the cabinet so the Amp isn’t depending on Wi-Fi from inside a wooden box. If the built-ins are original woodwork you don’t want altered, the Amp goes to the basement directly below and the speaker wire comes up through the floor plate instead.

The TV in our Colonial hangs over the fireplace — where do the Sonos soundbar and Sub go?

The bar goes directly under the TV, and that usually means on the chimney breast just above the mantel or on the mantel itself. Sonos’s Arc Ultra guide asks for at least four inches between the bar and the TV, and the up-firing drivers that give Dolby Atmos height effects need a clear path to the ceiling, so we check that the mantel shelf or the TV doesn’t overhang the bar. Arc Ultra detects when it is wall-mounted and adjusts its EQ for the wall, according to Sonos’s mount page. Power and HDMI for a mantel-height TV can’t always run inside a masonry chimney breast; where they can’t, we use a paintable cord cover or run the cable to the side wall and across behind the mantel. The Sub does not need to be near the bar — it is wireless — so it goes where the bass measures evenly at the sofa, often in a corner or lying flat under the sofa with the clearance Sonos specifies. Mounting the TV above a fireplace is a published add-on of $130 on top of the Nassau base price.

Estates and very large houses

We want Sonos in more than thirty places across the house, pool house and terrace — is there a limit?

Yes. Sonos’s system requirements state a maximum of 32 Sonos products in one system, and every product counts: each Amp, each Sub, each surround and each soundbar. Passive ceiling and outdoor speakers don’t count individually because they hang off an Amp, which is one of the reasons we use them heavily in large houses — Sonos says one Amp reliably powers up to three pairs of its Architectural speakers in parallel. When a plan still gets close to 32, we have three choices: combine rooms that are always used together into one zone, replace self-powered speakers in secondary rooms with ceiling speakers on a shared Amp, or split the property into two Sonos systems, for instance the main house and a guest cottage. Two systems can’t be grouped together in the app, so we only recommend that split where the buildings are really used separately. We draw the count on the plan before anything is bought.

Raised houses, canals and Long Beach

On our raised canal house, how does wire reach the deck speakers without going through the flood vents?

It leaves the house at the living level and stays there. The enclosure under a raised house is designed for parking, limited storage and building access, with flood openings in its walls so water can pass through instead of pushing the house over; FEMA’s recovery guidance after Sandy notes that every part of a building below the base flood elevation is susceptible to flooding. A cable threaded through those openings gets wet, blocks them with clutter and invites someone to put a power strip down there. So we plan from the top: the Amp sits on a shelf on the first living floor, the speaker wire leaves through one small sealed penetration in the wall of that floor, runs along the rim or under the deck boards in outdoor-rated cable, and comes up to the speakers on the deck rail or the house wall. If the speakers are on a lower dock or patio, the cable drops down a post in protected conduit and nothing powered goes with it. FEMA’s utilities guidance also recommends keeping electrical equipment above the required flood elevation, which is why every powered part of the system stays upstairs.

Our Long Beach condo board won’t allow anything fixed to the balcony or the facade — how do we get music outside?

With speakers that don’t need to be fixed to anything. Sonos’s portable speakers are the honest answer here: the Move 2 is rated IP56 with up to 24 hours of battery, and the Roam 2 is rated IP67 with up to ten hours, according to Sonos’s guides. Both join the same Sonos system as the indoor speakers over Wi-Fi, so the balcony becomes a zone you can group with the living room. Two cautions for an oceanfront building. Sonos says the Move 2’s charging base is for indoor use only and its operating range is 32°F to 104°F, so it comes inside at the end of the evening and in winter. And sand and salt are hard on anything left out; the city’s own building commissioner described the boardwalk buildings as being “sandblasted every day.” If the board will allow a wired speaker on the interior side of the balcony wall, we can show them the mounting details first; otherwise portable it is.

Internet, routers and storms

Can Sonos run on the gateway Optimum or Verizon installed, or do we need our own router?

Most of the time the provider’s equipment will run Sonos; whether it runs it well depends on the house. Verizon’s own Long Island page compares its Fios service with Optimum’s cable internet across Nassau and Suffolk, and one or the other supplies the gateway in most houses we visit. Either company’s gateway can work, provided its Wi-Fi offers 2.4 GHz (Sonos says routers that only provide 5 GHz are unsupported), the Sonos products and the phones are on the same main network rather than a guest network, and the security is WPA2 or WPA3 Personal rather than an enterprise login. Where a gateway struggles is coverage: one box in a front-hall closet rarely reaches the back deck of a ranch or the far wing of a large house. Then we either add a mesh system wired back to the gateway or put the gateway in bridge mode and let a better router run the house. We check the gateway’s settings before the speakers go in, because a Sonos fault that is really a router setting is the most common call we get after a provider swap.

Storms knock our power out every few years — how do we protect the Sonos Amps and the network gear?

Plug the equipment into proper surge protection, keep it in one labelled place and let it come back in order. Long Island knows what a long outage looks like: LIPA’s report on Tropical Storm Isaias counted about 645,000 customer outages in August 2020 and eight days to full restoration. When power drops and returns, the moments of switching are when sensitive electronics take a hit, so we put the Amps, the Port, the switch and the router on a surge-protected power strip or a small battery-backed unit on the equipment shelf rather than on whatever outlet is nearest. We label every plug. When power comes back, the router has to be up before the speakers can find it, and owners on the Sonos community forum note that older products take longer to reboot than newer ones, so the right move is patience before resetting anything. For outdoor speakers, nothing needs unplugging: the passive outdoor pair carries no electronics of its own and is powered from the indoor Amp.

“Hiding the wires in the wall costs about $139”

Thumbtack’s page for Massapequa Park lists “TV wall mount with wires hidden inside wall: $139” alongside a national average for TV mounting between $100 and $130. Figures like that get repeated as the going rate for concealing the cables behind a Sonos soundbar.

The number hides the question that matters: what is in the wall? Behind a TV in an expanded Cape there may be an old exterior wall with sheathing and insulation; behind one in a Tudor, plaster and lath; behind one on a chimney breast, masonry that can’t take a cable at all. And a power cord pushed inside a wall is not a code-compliant way to power a TV; a recessed power-and-cable kit is.

“Faster internet will fix Sonos dropouts”

Provider marketing on Long Island leads with speed: Verizon’s Long Island page advertises Fios plans up to multi-gigabit speeds and compares them with Optimum’s. Upgrading the plan is the first thing many households try when the speakers cut out.

Sonos streams audio at a tiny fraction of those speeds. Dropouts almost always happen inside the house, between the router and the speaker: a gateway in a front closet trying to reach a Sub at the back of a ranch through plaster and brick, interference from cordless phones and baby monitors, or a mesh satellite placed where it can’t hear the main unit. Sonos’s own interference guidance lists plaster and concrete as high obstructions and metal as very high.

So we measure the Wi-Fi at each speaker, move or add access points, and wire one or more products where we can. A faster plan from the same gateway in the same closet usually changes nothing in the den.

“Just run the wire up from the basement”

Installation guides for in-ceiling and in-wall speakers commonly assume a basement or crawl space below the room, so the cable can be fished up through the floor plate. For a lot of the country, that is good advice.

In Nassau, thousands of houses were built without one. The 17,447 Levittown houses of 1947 to 1951 and the ranches and Capes built on the same model across central Nassau sit on slabs, and the Levitt ranches had radiant heating pipes in the concrete. Drill there and you can hit a heating line. The lower half of a split-level or a high ranch is usually on a slab too.

In those houses we route from above — attic, top plate, down the wall — or choose wireless products where the attic can’t reach. Deciding that before the speakers are bought is the difference between a clean job and an expensive patch.

“Any outdoor speaker can live by the water”

General outdoor-speaker advice treats weather as one thing: rain. Plenty of speakers sold as “outdoor” are built for a covered patio in a dry climate, and that advice gets repeated without a mention of salt.

Along the South Shore and in Long Beach, salt is the main enemy. Officials describing the Long Beach boardwalk buildings in 2021 talked about “sea air, blowing sand and corrosive salt water,” and an engineer said balcony steel there “looked like Swiss cheese.” Brackets, terminals and cheap grilles fail first. Sonos rates its outdoor pair by Sonance to Mil Spec 810 for humidity, salt spray, temperature and UV, with IP66 protection.

We specify salt-rated speakers for canal and bay houses, use stainless fasteners, seal the wire entry, and keep the Amp indoors. A Bluetooth speaker left on the deck rail all summer is a consumable; a properly specified outdoor pair is not.

“Put the electronics in the garage or under the house, out of sight”

Tidy-install advice often suggests hiding amplifiers and network gear in a garage, a basement corner or any space nobody uses. It looks clean in the photo.

On a raised South Shore house, the space nobody uses is the flood enclosure, and FEMA’s guidance is to keep electrical equipment above the required flood elevation. In a detached or unheated garage, a winter night can drop below the 32°F floor of the Amp’s operating range, and a July afternoon in a closed attached garage can pass its 104°F ceiling.

We put Amps, Ports and network gear on a living floor, in a ventilated cabinet or closet, on surge protection, with the clearance Sonos specifies. It is still out of sight, just not out of reach of the next flood or heat wave.

“Speaker work never involves licences or permits”

DIY content treats Sonos as a purely plug-in product and the wiring as nobody’s business. For the speakers and their low-voltage cable, that is largely true; we haven’t found a Nassau rule requiring a permit to run speaker wire.

Flood Zones and Raised Houses

A Sonos Checklist for Raised South Shore Houses

A raised house is two buildings stacked on top of each other: a living house that should stay dry and an enclosure below that is allowed to flood. FEMA’s Sandy recovery guidance puts it bluntly — every part of a building below the base flood elevation is susceptible to flooding during the base flood. A Sonos system can be designed for that in an afternoon; it is much harder to move afterwards. This is the list we work through on canal and bay houses from Inwood to Massapequa.

  1. Find the living floor’s equipment spot first. A closet, a media cabinet or the space under an interior stair on the first living level, with an outlet on its own and room for the Amps’ three inches of clearance. The router and switch go here too, so the whole system shares one dry, ventilated home.
  2. Bring the internet line up. Many raised houses still have the provider’s line arriving low, where the old house used to be. Moving the provider’s connection point is the provider’s job, but we plan the network so the gateway or our router sits on the living floor, not next to the meter at grade.
  3. Keep the enclosure clear. No Amps, no switches, no power strips, no Port and no Sub in the parking and storage space below. If a cable has to pass through the enclosure on the way to a dock or a lower deck, it runs high, protected and continuous, with nothing powered down there.
  4. Take deck wire out through the living level. Outdoor speaker wire leaves the house through one small, sealed penetration in the wall of the living floor, runs along the framing or under the deck boards in protected cable, and comes up at the speakers. Flood openings are left alone.
  5. Specify for salt. Sonos’s outdoor pair by Sonance is rated for salt spray and IP66. Brackets and fasteners should be stainless, the wire jacket rated for outdoor use, and the connections sealed. Cheap outdoor speakers near the bay corrode from the terminals inward.
  6. Write it down. We label the shelf and leave a one-page map of what is plugged in where. If a storm warning ever means unplugging and moving things, anyone in the house can do it in five minutes.
Outdoor Music and the Neighbours

Designing a Backyard Sonos Zone for a Nassau Lot

The best outdoor Sonos zone in Nassau is the one the neighbours never notice. Much of the county is built on lots where the deck, the pool and the house next door are all within a few dozen feet of each other, and every town and village has a noise chapter that can be enforced. Designing for that is not about turning the music down; it is about putting sound where people sit and nowhere else.

Spread it out, keep it low

Two large speakers on the back wall have to be loud to reach the far end of a pool, and the sound carries over the fence on the way. Four or six smaller speakers spread along the patio, each playing quietly, cover the same space evenly at a fraction of the level. With passive outdoor speakers on a Sonos Amp, Sonos says one Amp can drive up to three pairs of its Architectural speakers in parallel, so more speakers doesn’t necessarily mean more equipment.

Aim toward the house

Speakers mounted on the house wall and pointed out into the yard throw sound straight at the neighbours’ windows. Speakers mounted on posts, pergolas or the fence line and pointed back toward the house send it into your own siding, which reflects some of it back toward the seating. On a canal lot, that means aiming up the yard rather than across the water, where sound travels with very little to stop it.

Set limits in the app

The Sonos app lets an owner set a maximum volume for a room. We set one for the outdoor zone during hand-over, at a level you choose on the day with the music playing, so a teenager with the app can’t push the deck speakers past it. Where you want the outdoor zone to stop at a certain hour, we show you how the app’s sleep timer and alarms work for that zone.

Know your own code

The Town of North Hempstead’s noise chapter bars sound amplifiers and similar devices from creating a noise disruption across a property line from 10 p.m. to 8 a.m.; the Village of Hempstead uses measured limits, including 50 dBA at a residential property line at night for the equipment its chapter covers. Your own village or town may write it differently. We point you to the right code when we design the zone, and we design to stay well inside it.

North Shore Houses

Planning Sonos for an Estate or a Very Large House

In a Gold Coast house the speakers are rarely the hard part; the distances are. The surviving estates in the villages around Brookville, Old Westbury, Lattingtown and Sands Point, and the large houses built on the land around them, put rooms hundreds of feet from the equipment, with stone, plaster and steel in between. A Sonos system for a house like that is a small network project with speakers attached.

Start with a wired backbone. Each wing, the pool house and any detached garage or guest cottage gets Ethernet from the main equipment room, ideally in a pathway that can take another cable later. That cable feeds a wired access point or mesh node for Wi-Fi, a small switch for that area’s Amps and Sub, and, where the main soundbar is in that wing, one wired Sonos product. Sonos’s recommended settings for wired networks are clear: wire products back to a router or switch only, not into the spare port on a wireless mesh satellite unless that satellite is itself wired back, and don’t use Ethernet-over-power.

Count products before choosing them. A large house reaches Sonos’s 32-product limit faster than people expect once every room has a soundbar or a pair of self-powered speakers, plus subs and surrounds. Passive ceiling and in-wall speakers on shared Amps keep the count down: one Amp, up to three pairs of Architectural speakers, one product on the list. Era 100 Pro speakers powered over PoE+ are another way to cover offices and hallways from a network switch.

Condos, Co-ops and New Rentals

Sonos Without Holes in Long Beach Condos and Station-Area Rentals

Nassau has two very different kinds of apartment, and both need a Sonos plan that cuts nothing. The older one is the concrete-and-steel condo or co-op — the Long Beach boardwalk buildings, the co-ops of Great Neck Plaza and the garden-apartment complexes around the county. The newer one is the rental building going up beside an LIRR station in villages like Westbury and Mineola, with amenity floors, roof terraces and lease terms that require the unit to be handed back as it was found.

In the concrete buildings, ceiling speakers are almost never an option, and cutting a channel for cable in a structural slab is not something any board will allow. The system is built from a soundbar and Sub, Era speakers on stands or furniture, and portable speakers for the balcony. Mounting the TV and bar on a concrete wall takes masonry anchors and a published $75 surcharge; cable runs along the wall in a paintable cord cover. Concrete walls also block Wi-Fi heavily — Sonos’s interference article rates concrete a high obstruction — so in a larger unit we may add a mesh node to reach the bedrooms.

In the new rentals, the walls are drywall and the constraint is the lease. We mount only where screws are allowed, often just the TV and soundbar, and keep everything else freestanding. The most important step is the network: Sonos doesn’t support guest networks or sign-in portals, so a building-wide resident Wi-Fi with a login page will not run it reliably. The answer is a router of your own on the unit’s own internet connection, where the building provides one. If the building only offers managed Wi-Fi, we ask management what is possible before we install anything.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Nassau Sonos Walkthroughs

How our technicians read a Nassau house before suggesting a single speaker (general practice, not one customer’s job)

The first stop is outside, not in. From the driveway you can usually tell whether the house is a slab ranch, an expanded Cape, a split-level or a pre-war house with a basement, and whether it has been raised. Those four or five shapes decide most of the plan before we ring the bell.

Inside, we go to the router and then straight up or down: the attic hatch in a ranch, the knee-wall door in a dormered Cape, the basement stairs in a Tudor. In a raised house we check where the gateway and the panel ended up after the rebuild. We are looking for the route, and for anything that blocks it — ducts, old wiring, a heating line, a finished ceiling with no way in.

Only then do we stand in the rooms with the family and talk about where they want music. By that point we already know which rooms can take ceiling speakers on an Amp and which are better served by a speaker on a shelf, and we can say so plainly, with prices, instead of discovering it halfway through the job.

Nassau County Sonos Installation FAQ

Why does Sonos installation cost more on the Nassau side of the city line than in Queens?
+

Most of it doesn't. The Sonos system setup is $125, a wall-mounted soundbar $129 and a full home-theatre hookup $350 on both sides of the line. If you live in Floral Park, Elmont or Valley Stream, the Nassau prices apply.

Our house is about to be lifted — at what stage of the project should the Sonos wiring go in?
+

While the walls are open on the living floors and before insulation and drywall go back, which is usually after the house has been set on its new foundation and framing repairs are done. At that stage speaker wire, Ethernet and a home run to the equipment closet cost a fraction of what they cost later. Tell your contractor early so the low-voltage visit is on the schedule; the speakers and setup can follow once the family has moved back in.

Which parts of a Sonos job can you price over the phone, and which need a walkthrough?
+

Anything on the published list can be priced from photos: soundbar placed or wall-mounted, Sonos setup, TV mounting with its add-ons, in-wall concealment, cord covers, cable runs up to 25 feet and setup-only visits. Ceiling, in-wall and outdoor speakers, Amp zones and new Ethernet runs depend on attic, basement and wall access, so they are quoted in writing after a technician has looked.

Will ceiling speaker cut-outs in a ranch ceiling let heat escape into the attic?
+

Each cut-out is an opening in the ceiling below an insulated attic, so it is handled with care. The speaker frame fills the hole, we seal around it where the speaker's instructions allow, and we put the attic insulation back over and around the work following those instructions rather than leaving a bare patch. In a slab ranch fed from the attic, that is part of every ceiling-speaker job.

How far ahead should we book Sonos for a summer party or a graduation?
+

For a soundbar or speakers that need no wiring, a few days is usually enough, and same-day is possible when a window is open. Outdoor or ceiling speakers need a walkthrough, a written quote and sometimes an electrician first, so get in touch as soon as the date is set and tell us the date on the first call; we work back from it.

Will a Sonos soundbar work with the Optimum or Fios TV box we already have?
+

Yes. The cable box connects to the TV by HDMI as it does now, and the Sonos soundbar takes the sound from the TV through the TV's HDMI eARC or ARC port, or optically for Ray. We set the TV's audio output so everything from the box, the streaming apps and a games console comes through the bar, and we check that the TV remote or the box's remote controls the volume.

What happens if a ceiling speaker location turns out to sit on a joist or a duct?
+

We check before cutting. Sonos's in-ceiling instructions call for an obstruction survey with a stud finder, and a small pilot hole probed with stiff wire where there is doubt. If something is in the way, we move the speaker a few inches along the joist bay or to the next bay and agree the new spot with you before cutting. Nothing is cut until the location is clear.

What does your written quote for a wired Sonos zone actually list?
+

Each zone separately: which rooms it covers, the speakers and Amp it uses, the route the wire takes through the attic, basement or walls, any Ethernet runs, where the equipment will sit and what is not included, such as patching, painting or new outlets. Published lines such as the $125 Sonos setup appear at their listed prices, so you can start with some zones and add the rest later.

Can the Sonos Sub be hidden inside a cabinet or behind the sofa?
+

Behind or under the sofa, yes; inside a closed cabinet, we advise against it. Sonos's placement guide allows the Sub in a corner, against a wall or behind, under or next to furniture, but asks that the front opening stays clear and that there is at least an inch of clearance above it under furniture. A closed cabinet muffles the bass and rattles. Lying the Sub flat under a sofa is the usual way to hide it.

The TV sound from our Sonos is out of step with people's lips — can that be fixed?
+

Usually, yes. Lip-sync drift is most often a TV setting or the way the sources are connected rather than a fault in the soundbar. We check the TV's audio output format and eARC settings, how the cable box or streaming device is connected and whether the TV has its own lip-sync adjustment, then test with dialogue on screen. On an existing system that is a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour in Nassau.
