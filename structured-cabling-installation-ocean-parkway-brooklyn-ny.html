The Neighborhood

Why Ocean Parkway Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Ocean Parkway is a road before it is a neighborhood, and that explains most of what we find when we wire a building on it. The City of Brooklyn acquired the land in 1868, and the parkway was built between 1874 and 1876, running south from the edge of Prospect Park toward Coney Island on a design by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux modeled on the boulevards of Paris. It is 210 feet wide, with a 70-foot central roadway, two tree-lined malls, two service roads and two sidewalks, and in 1894 its western mall carried the country's first bike path. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the section south of Church Avenue a scenic landmark in 1975, and it went on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983. The northernmost stretch was replaced by the Prospect Expressway in the 1950s. What lines the rest of it, block after block through Kensington, Midwood, Homecrest, Gravesend and on toward Brighton Beach, is the reason this page exists.

The buildings came in waves. Homes went up along the perimeter around 1900, large houses during World War I, and apartment complexes along with one- and two-family homes in the 1920s. The biggest wave came after the Second World War: from 1960 to 1965 alone, twenty-six apartment buildings were developed on the parkway, and many of the parkway's apartment buildings were converted to co-ops in the late twentieth century. Public records show the pattern building by building. 1500 Ocean Parkway was built in 1940 and has 72 units on six stories. 1811 Ocean Parkway, toward Homecrest, has 82 units on six stories and dates from 1953. 540 Ocean Parkway was built in 1962, with 114 apartments on six floors. 770 Ocean Parkway, known as Parkway Gardens, is a 100-unit co-op from 1963 with two elevators and laundry on each floor. At the Kensington end the buildings rise much higher: 135 Ocean Parkway lists 288 units, and the parkway there has rental and co-op buildings of up to twenty stories. Toward Gravesend, the co-ops and condos are mostly six and seven stories.

For a cabling crew, that adds up to a particular kind of job. A mid-century elevator building with a cellar where every provider's line arrives, a long vertical distance to the top floor, apartment closets that may or may not line up from floor to floor, hallways that are shared space owned by the corporation, and a board and a managing agent who have to approve anything that opens a wall or a slab. The listing for 1811 Ocean Parkway names controlled access and a management company; buildings like it have a super, a set of alteration rules and a long list of shareholders with their own providers and their own ideas. Adding a network to one apartment is a small job. Adding a clean, documented riser so that any apartment can be connected later, without each provider drilling its own path, is the job that actually changes a building, and it is the one parkway boards most often ask us to plan.

Some of the pressure for better building wiring comes from the buildings' own records. HPD records for 1075 Ocean Parkway show 108 recorded violations, well above the Midwood average of 34.8 cited on the same page, including a 2026 complaint about no lighting in a hallway. None of those are cabling problems on their own. But the remedies that buildings reach for, a new entry panel, lobby cameras, a package room with its own screen, a management office that can see the building from somewhere else, all depend on cable that reaches the right places and a closet that someone can find and open. Fire has also reshaped buildings here. On June 17, 2026, a fire tore through 188 Ocean Parkway and spread to 184, and the Department of Buildings issued full vacate orders for both. In 2023, firefighters found a lithium-ion battery on the fourth floor after a fire in an Ocean Parkway building in Midwood. Every hole a cable passes through in buildings like these is a hole in a floor or wall that is supposed to slow fire and smoke, and it has to be closed properly.

The prewar parkway apartment house: plaster, an intercom riser and a live-in super

The oldest apartment buildings on the parkway are six-story elevator houses from the 1920s and 1930s. CityRealty's listing for 1601 Ocean Parkway, toward Avenue P, describes a 1929 rental of 59 apartments with a live-in super, and its listing for 1171 Ocean Parkway, between Avenues K and L, describes a 1939 co-op of 64 units with an elevator, an intercom and a live-in super. Buildings of that age usually have masonry outer walls, plaster finishes inside and a cellar that has collected wiring from every decade since: phone cable, the original intercom wire, old coax from the cable companies and newer fiber. Their apartment layouts often repeat from floor to floor, which helps, but their closets were built for coats rather than cable, and many have been rebuilt by shareholders at different times so that the closet on the third floor no longer lines up with the one on the fourth.

The super in a building like this is the person who knows where things actually are. Before we draw a route, we ask the super to show us the cellar entry, the path the intercom wire takes to the floors, any spare sleeves or chases that earlier work left behind and which apartments are easiest to enter. The intercom route is particularly useful as a clue, because it shows how an earlier installer got from cellar to roof, even if the new cable cannot share its space. Plaster gets the respect it deserves: openings are drilled rather than chopped, dust is caught at the bit, and any patch is small and planned in advance. Hallway work happens only in the hours the building approves, because in a six-story house with one elevator, a crew in the corridor is something every resident notices.

The mid-century co-op: stacked apartments, laundry rooms and a board with alteration rules

The buildings of the 1950s and early 1960s are the heart of the parkway, and they are the ones we are asked about most. 1811 Ocean Parkway, from 1953, has 82 units on six stories; 540 Ocean Parkway, from 1962, has 114 apartments on six floors; 770 Ocean Parkway, from 1963, has 100 units, two elevators and laundry on each floor. Buildings of this era were usually built with repeating floor plans, so the same apartment line, A through G or however the building letters them, sits directly above itself from the second floor to the sixth. That repetition is what makes a riser possible. If the closets, the kitchen walls or the bathroom chases of one apartment line stack cleanly, a small sleeve through each floor slab inside one of those spaces can carry the cable for the whole floor without anything appearing in a hallway.

The catch is that the space belongs to someone. A closet in apartment 4C is part of a shareholder's home, and the slab under it belongs to the corporation. Most parkway co-ops have alteration rules that require the board's approval, through the managing agent, for any opening in a floor or wall, and some forbid coring through slabs entirely. We plan around those rules rather than against them. If a clean stack exists and the board will approve sleeves through it, we show the exact locations on a floor plan. If not, the riser goes where the corporation controls the space: a utility closet, a laundry room that repeats on every floor, the wall beside the elevator machine room on the roof or the cellar, or a stairwell where the board allows enclosed raceway. Each choice has a cost and an appearance, and the proposal lays them side by side.

The Kensington tower: a long climb and hundreds of apartments

At the north end of the parkway, near Church Avenue and the Prospect Expressway, the buildings get much taller. Wikipedia describes rental and cooperative buildings of up to twenty stories in Kensington, and a neighborhood cleaning-company guide describes Caton Towers at 135 Ocean Parkway and Park Towers at 370 Ocean Parkway as twelve- to twenty-story doorman co-ops dating to the early 1960s; Redfin lists 288 units at 135. In a building of that size, everything about cabling scales up. The riser is long enough that it needs support at intervals rather than simply hanging, the number of apartments on each floor means a floor panel fills quickly, and a single bad decision repeated on every floor becomes a building-wide problem.

Towers are also where wireless crowding is most noticeable, because hundreds of apartments stacked close together each run their own Wi-Fi, and the result in a dense building is many networks competing for the same air. A wired access point in the room that needs signal helps far more there than a stronger router. For the building itself, we design the riser with spare capacity so that later work, a camera on another floor, a new entry panel, a management office screen, does not require a new sleeve. And because a twenty-story building has a doorman, a staff and a schedule, the work is organized floor by floor around the elevators and the residents rather than in one long push.

The detached house and two-family: a full basement and room for every cable

The parkway's houses are a different job entirely. The two-family at 2156 Ocean Parkway, recorded as built in 1925 with a full basement below grade, is typical of the older stock: a house with a cellar under the whole footprint, wooden floor framing over it and interior walls that are often plaster on framing. A full basement is a gift for cabling. It gives a horizontal highway under every room on the first floor, a natural place for a small panel next to the provider's entry and a starting point for vertical routes up through closets and interior walls to the floors above. In a two-family, the basement is also where each household's provider line usually lands, so it becomes the place where two separate networks start and never touch.

These houses tend to be large and full. The family-home pattern a local guide describes, many children, frequent gatherings, extended family under one roof for holidays, means a network that is quiet on a Tuesday and carrying dozens of phones and tablets on a holiday weekend. That changes how we place access points: one in each main living area rather than one per floor, with a cable behind each, and a switch sized for every device that should be wired rather than just the ones in use today. Because parkway lots keep a 30-foot front yard, any cable to a camera, a gate or a front-of-house access point crosses more ground than it would on a side street, and the front of the house is the part everyone sees, so exterior entries go on the side or the rear wherever possible.

The enlarged or combined house: two cellars, several additions and one network

Wikipedia notes that many of the parkway's houses and mansions have been expanded or combined over the years. That history leaves marks a cabling crew finds right away. A house that was two houses may still have two cellars at slightly different levels, two utility entries, two sets of provider lines and a wall between them that used to be an exterior wall. A house that has been enlarged more than once may have a newer wing on a concrete slab with no basement under it, joined to an older section with a full cellar, and the floor levels may not match where the two meet. Cable has to cross those joins somewhere, and the places where old and new construction meet are where routes most often get stuck.

The plan for these houses starts with choosing one starting point. Usually that is the cellar where the provider's newest line arrives and where there is room for a small panel, and the other old entry is retired, its lines removed and its holes sealed. From there, the route has to cross into each section: through the old party wall between the original houses with a cored sleeve, along a basement ceiling into the addition, or up and over through a floor framing space that both sections share. In a newer wing on a slab, cable usually arrives from the ceiling rather than the floor. The survey records every section, every floor level and every join, so the quote describes a house as it actually is, not as one simple box.

The institution: classrooms, corridors and a hall that cannot close

The parkway's institutions are some of its most demanding buildings for cabling. Wikipedia describes the large number of nursing homes, schools and houses of worship built on the parkway in the 1960s and 1970s, and the high density of synagogues and yeshivas around it. A yeshiva may have classrooms on several floors, a library, an office and a lunchroom, all needing connections, often in a building that has been added onto as enrollment grew. A nursing home has resident corridors that need Wi-Fi for tablets and video calls, nursing stations that need wired computers and a set of specialized systems, from nurse call to resident monitoring, that belong to their own installers. A synagogue has an office, a social hall and a sanctuary whose ceilings, finishes and furnishings are not places to improvise.

In each of them, the building is in use almost all the time, and the people who use it are children, elderly residents or a congregation. Work is planned around school sessions, around the facility's visiting and care routines and around services and events, room by room or wing by wing, with every opening closed and every work area cleaned before the people come back. Equipment goes in a locked room or cabinet that the institution's staff controls. And because institutions change slowly but last a long time, we document everything: a printed port map, the test results for every link and a drawing showing where each cable runs, so the next facilities manager is not starting from zero.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Parkway Co-op Before Drawing a Riser

These notes describe how our technicians survey buildings like those on Ocean Parkway. They describe our practice, not an account of any particular address.

Start where the providers come in. The technician goes first to the cellar room or wall where the building's internet, phone and TV lines arrive. What is mounted there tells the history of the building: which providers have served it, how each one reached the floors, which cables are live and which were abandoned when a company left. It also shows whether there is room for a proper panel and whether the space is dry, locked and reachable by the super.

Look for the repeat. Next comes one typical floor, chosen with the super, and the same position on the floor above. The technician compares apartment closets, the walls beside kitchens and bathrooms, utility closets in the hallway and the laundry room if the building has one on each floor, looking for anything that sits directly above itself from bottom to top. A riser is only as good as the stack it climbs.

Lift a hallway tile. If the corridors have hung ceilings, the technician looks above one: how much space there is, what pipes and ducts run there, whether older cable is resting on the grid and where the space ends at each apartment door. That space often decides whether each apartment's cable enters from the hallway or from inside the stack.

Go to the top. The top floor and the roof bulkhead matter because they show whether a riser can end cleanly and whether any equipment already lives up there. The technician notes what is on the roof without assuming the board will allow anything new.

Ask about the rules. Before leaving, the technician asks the super or the managing agent for the building's alteration rules and approved working hours, whether coring is allowed, and whether the board wants the riser proposal in a particular form for a meeting.

Leave a sketch. The visit ends with a rough riser drawing marked with each floor's panel spot and every proposed opening, so the written proposal describes a plan the building has already seen.

From the Cellar to the Top Floor: How a Riser Climbs Through a Parkway Building

Every structured cabling system has the same parts: an entrance where service arrives, a main equipment spot, a backbone from there to each floor, horizontal cable from each floor's panel to each jack, and the jacks and access points themselves. In a parkway apartment building, the entrance and the main equipment usually share the cellar, the backbone is the riser, and the horizontal cable is the part that has to get from a floor panel into somebody's apartment. Here is how each part is handled.

The cellar entrance. The providers' lines usually arrive through the foundation wall at one point, sometimes at several. We gather them at one wall-mounted panel or small cabinet near that point, labeled by provider, with a clear path from there to the bottom of the riser. If several providers have each brought their own line in through a different hole over the years, part of the job can be consolidating them through one sleeved, sealed entry so the next provider has an obvious place to connect. The cellar panel is the building's handoff point, and it is where the super should be able to see at a glance which cable goes where.

The riser itself. The best riser in a mid-century co-op is a small sleeve through each floor slab inside a space that repeats on every floor: a utility closet, a laundry room, a chase beside a plumbing stack or, with each shareholder's agreement and the board's approval, a line of apartment closets. Where the slab has to be cored, the core is small, located away from beams and existing pipes, checked from both sides before drilling and sleeved afterward. Where the board will not allow coring, the riser follows whatever vertical space the building already provides, sometimes an existing sleeve from an older system, sometimes enclosed raceway in a stairwell or along a utility wall where the board allows it. Every floor gets the same treatment so the riser can be found by anyone who opens the first floor's closet.

Each floor's panel. On each floor, the riser feeds a small panel or a wall box in a corporation-controlled space, sized for the apartments on that floor plus spare ports. From there, horizontal cable reaches each apartment that wants a connection. In buildings with hung hallway ceilings, the cable travels above the tiles on its own supports and enters each apartment over or beside its door through a small, sealed penetration. In buildings with plaster hallway ceilings, the cable may travel in a low-profile raceway along the top of the corridor wall, painted to match, or enter apartments from the stack inside the unit. The choice is the board's to make, and we show what each looks like.

Inside the apartment. Once a cable is inside a unit, it ends at a panel or jack near the entry and continues to the rooms that need it, through closets, inside the soffits that already hide pipes and ducts in many mid-century kitchens and baths, along the top of door frames and behind baseboards. Concrete ceilings generally cannot hide a cable, so where a stretch has to show, it goes in a slim raceway painted to match. An access point in the middle of the apartment's ceiling, fed by one cable, usually does more for Wi-Fi than any number of extenders.

Every opening closed again

Floors and walls in a mid-century apartment building separate apartments, hallways and shafts, and many of them are rated to hold back fire and smoke for a period of time. Every sleeve through a slab and every penetration through a corridor or shaft wall is closed around the cable with listed firestop material as the work goes, not at the end, and each one is marked on the drawing with its floor and location. That record matters to the building: after the fires at 188 and 184 Ocean Parkway in June 2026 and the 2023 fire in a Midwood building on the parkway, boards and managing agents have good reason to ask how a contractor seals what it opens, and the honest answer should be on paper. Sleeves also get spare capacity and a pull string, so the next cable goes through the same opening rather than a new one.

Keeping data apart from everything else in the stack

The vertical spaces in an apartment building are crowded. Plumbing stacks, electrical feeders, intercom wire, old phone risers and sometimes the building's fire alarm wiring all compete for the same chases. Data cable is kept apart from power and crossed at right angles where the two must meet. It never shares a raceway with the fire alarm system, which is separate wiring with its own licensing and permits. It stays off water, steam and gas lines and is never strapped to them. Where an existing chase is already full, we look for another rather than forcing cable into a space where the next plumber or electrician will tear it out. That discipline is invisible on the day the job finishes and is what keeps a building's riser working through decades of renovations around it.

Service Entry

Where Service Arrives: The Curb, the Front Yard and the Cellar Handoff

Cabling inside a building only helps if it starts in the right place. On Ocean Parkway, the starting point is almost always the cellar, but the path from the street to that cellar has features that other Brooklyn blocks do not.

A long way from the curb. The Special Ocean Parkway District, adopted by the City Planning Commission and the Board of Estimate in 1977, requires a 30-foot front yard on lots facing the parkway, and the zoning rules for that yard limit what can be built in it. The building wall is therefore well back from the sidewalk, which in turn sits beyond a service road, a mall and the main roadway. However a provider's line reaches a parkway building, underground from a utility in the service road or by another path the provider has arranged, it has farther to travel to the wall than it would on most side streets, and once it reaches the building it usually enters through the cellar. Getting the line from the street to the building is the provider's work under its own arrangements with the city and the owner. Everything from the point where it enters the building to each apartment's jack is building wiring, and that is where we come in.

The landmark and the street. The 1975 scenic landmark designation covers the parkway from the south side of Church Avenue to the north side of Seabreeze Avenue, bounded by the outer curb lines of the western and eastern service roads. The malls, the benches and the bike path are part of the landmark; the building facades on either side are not, and we found no historic district covering them. For an ordinary cabling job, that means nothing we do inside a building or on its private front yard is landmark work. Street and mall work that a provider might need is a separate matter for the provider and the city. The parkway is also, as Gothamist reported, technically run by the state, which matters for road projects rather than building wiring, but it is one more reason we leave the street to the people who are permitted to work in it.

One building, many providers. In a co-op of eighty or a hundred apartments, several providers may serve different shareholders, each with its own line into the cellar and its own history of how it reached the floors. HPD publishes guidance on residential broadband installation that covers new fiber in buildings with existing wiring, options for reaching individual units and design guidelines, and its contact for owners is GetConnected@hpd.nyc.gov. The practical lesson for an owner is the same one we work to: plan the inside of the building so that any provider can connect at a clear handoff in the cellar and reach every apartment over the building's own riser, instead of each provider designing its own path through the walls.

Where the building's own equipment lives

Shareholders' routers belong in their apartments. What the building owns, the cellar panel, the riser, each floor's panel and any switch that serves the lobby, the entry panel, cameras or the management office, belongs in spaces the corporation controls and the super can lock. In the cellar that means a wall cabinet off the floor, away from the boiler, water heaters and any spot that takes water, with a clear label and a key held by management. On each floor it means a small enclosure in a utility closet or laundry room rather than an apartment. In an institution it means a locked equipment room or cabinet, and in a house it usually means a small panel in the basement next to the provider's entry with the router on a shelf where the family can reach it.

Separate networks for the building and the residents

A building's own systems, the entry panel, lobby cameras, a package room screen or the management office connection, should never ride on any shareholder's network, and no shareholder's traffic should pass through the building's equipment. We give the building its own cable, its own switch and its own provider connection where possible, so a resident changing providers or restarting a router never touches the front door, and a problem in the lobby never reaches anyone's apartment. In an institution, the same separation applies between staff, students or residents and guests.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, the Landmark and the Board: What Governs an Ocean Parkway Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before work begins.

The low-voltage permit rule. Under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and in effect from December 21, 2025, an electrical permit is not required for the installation, alteration, maintenance or repair of wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits, provided the work is performed by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. For most data cabling in a parkway building or house, that means no electrical permit filing. The same code reserves life-safety wiring for licensed electricians, and fire alarm wiring falls outside the exemption and carries its own licensing and permits. Where a job is large, unusual or tied to other filed work, we confirm with the Department of Buildings rather than rely on any website, including this one.

Other permitted work nearby. If a slab core, a new opening in a rated shaft or a change to the building's structure is part of a larger filed alteration, that filing belongs to the building's architect or engineer, and our work follows it. If a building is being rebuilt under a vacate order, as 188 and 184 Ocean Parkway were after the June 2026 fire, the reconstruction's filings and inspections govern the schedule, and cabling goes in when the general contractor and the building's professionals say it can.

Licensed security work. Cameras, door access and burglar alarms are licensed work in New York. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, so when a riser job includes lobby cameras or a reader at a building door, the same licensed company handles the cable and the device.

The scenic landmark. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 1975 designation covers the parkway itself, bounded by the outer curb lines of the service roads, from Church Avenue to Seabreeze Avenue. Building interiors and facades on either side are outside that boundary, and we found no historic district covering them. Cabling inside a building is therefore not landmark work, and nothing we install goes on the malls or sidewalks.

Zoning. The Special Ocean Parkway District's 30-foot front yard is a zoning rule about what may stand in that yard. Low-voltage cable inside a building does not change it, and we do not place equipment in a parkway front yard. Any outdoor device on a house, such as a camera or an access point, goes on the building itself, preferably on the side or rear.

Boards, agents and shareholders. In a co-op, the proprietary lease and the board's alteration rules govern openings in shared walls, floors and ceilings, and many boards require an alteration agreement, specific working hours and approval of the route before work starts. Shareholders whose closets or apartments are on the route have a say in when we enter. We send a certificate of insurance and the written scope to the managing agent before the crew is booked, and we schedule entry to each apartment with the agent and the shareholder together.

Old finishes. Buildings and houses built before 1978 may have lead paint on trim, doors and walls, and mid-century buildings can have older materials in pipe insulation, floor tile or ceiling finishes. We drill small holes through protective sheeting, collect dust at the bit and clean with a vacuum. If a route would disturb a larger painted area or any suspect material, we stop and tell the building before going further, so testing or other arrangements can happen first.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Ocean Parkway: Questions Answered

These are the situations Ocean Parkway boards, shareholders, homeowners, institutions and businesses bring up when they call: stacked closets and reluctant neighbors, laundry rooms and hallway ceilings, crowded towers, combined apartments and combined houses, buildings coming back from fire, classrooms, corridors and sanctuaries, and kitchens full of heat. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

The building riser

Our six-story co-op is from the early 1960s, and the apartment closets in one line seem to sit directly above each other. Can that stack carry the riser, and what happens if one shareholder will not open their closet?

A line of stacked closets is often the cleanest riser a mid-century parkway building can offer, because a small sleeve through the slab inside each closet leaves nothing visible in any hallway or room. It depends on two approvals: the board's, for coring the slabs, which belong to the corporation, and each shareholder's, for entering their apartment and giving up a few inches at the back of a closet. If one shareholder in the middle of the stack declines, the stack breaks at that floor, and the riser has to step sideways to another space for that one level, usually a utility closet or the hallway ceiling, and come back. That is possible but it adds cost and an extra opening, so before proposing a closet stack we ask the managing agent to confirm that every apartment in that line has agreed. Where the line is unreliable, we propose a riser entirely in space the corporation controls, so no single apartment can hold it up.

Our building has a laundry room on every floor. Could that room hold each floor's small network panel?

Often it is one of the best places in the building, with a few conditions. A laundry room that repeats on each floor, as at 770 Ocean Parkway, which lists laundry on every floor, is corporation space, usually sits in the same position from floor to floor, and is reachable by staff without entering an apartment. Those are exactly the things a floor panel needs. The conditions are about water, heat and lint: the panel goes high on a dry wall, away from the machines, any sink and the dryer vents, inside a closed enclosure that keeps lint out, and the riser sleeve through the slab sits away from drains and the floor area that gets wet if a machine leaks. The enclosure is locked, because residents use the room all day. If the laundry room shares a wall with the hallway ceiling space, horizontal cable to each apartment can leave the panel and travel above the corridor from there.

The hallways in our building have hung ceilings that hide pipes. Can cable reach each apartment door through that space?

Usually yes, and it is the most common way horizontal cable reaches apartments in a parkway co-op. Above the tiles there is typically enough room for a separate set of supports that carry each floor's cable without resting on the ceiling grid, on pipes or on other wiring. From a support near each apartment door, a cable drops to a small sealed penetration through the corridor wall just above the door frame, and inside the apartment it reaches a jack or panel near the entry. What decides the details is what the technician finds above the tiles during the survey: how crowded the space is, whether older cable is lying loose on the grid, where sprinkler piping runs and whether the corridor walls are rated. Each penetration into an apartment is firestopped. If the corridor ceiling is plaster rather than tiles, we look at alternatives, including entering the apartments from the riser stack instead.

In a tower the size of 135 Ocean Parkway, with close to three hundred apartments, every unit runs its own Wi-Fi. Does all that crowding hurt our signal, and does a cable fix it?

Crowding can hurt, and a cable helps more than anything else you can buy. In a tall building with hundreds of apartments stacked close together, your router is competing for the same radio channels with many neighbors above, below and beside you, and walls and floors weaken your own signal while the neighbors' signals keep arriving. The effect tends to show up as slow or unstable connections in rooms far from the router, not as a total loss. A wired access point placed in the middle of the apartment, at the ceiling of the room you use most, puts a strong signal where you need it at low power, so it dominates your own space without adding much to the noise around it. Devices that do not move, a desk computer, a TV, a game console, are better on a jack altogether. In a co-op tower, that in-unit cable usually stays inside your apartment, so the board's approval is limited to any small openings in your own walls.

Our building's intercom already has its own riser from the lobby to every floor. Can the new data cable share its sleeves?

Sometimes it can share a pathway, but rarely the same sleeves, and we look at it case by case. An old intercom riser shows where an earlier installer found a vertical route, and that route may have room beside it for a new sleeve or a separate raceway. Putting new data cable inside the same small sleeve as old intercom wire is usually a poor idea: it may be full, the old wire may be fragile, and the next intercom repair could disturb the network. If the intercom is being replaced, which happens often in buildings dealing with entry and package problems, the new entry system may itself run over network cable, and then the two can be planned together, with the entry system on the building's own network. If the intercom stays, we leave its wire alone, label our own route clearly beside it and make sure the intercom technician can still reach everything.

Inside the apartments

We combined two apartments into one, and a concrete wall still separates the two halves. How does a cable get from the router side to the bedrooms on the other side?

Through the wall in one carefully chosen spot, or around it through space that already connects the two halves. When two units are joined, the old demising wall between them often stays, apart from the new doorway cut through it. That doorway is the first thing we look at: a cable can often travel above the door frame inside the new framing or trim, with no new opening at all. If not, a small core through the wall in a closet or behind furniture can carry a sleeve for several cables, but because the wall belongs to the building's structure and fire separation, the board has to approve it, and the core is located away from any reinforcing and firestopped afterward. Sometimes the old wall has a soffit or a kitchen chase that runs through it. The best answer usually depends on where the combination's architect left room, so if you still have the alteration drawings, they save time.

After a fire, and rebuilding

A fire on another floor of our building filled the halls with smoke, and walls near the damaged apartment are being opened. Is network cable that was near the heat and smoke still safe to keep?

Some of it may be, but none of it should be kept on looks alone. Heat can soften a cable's jacket and deform the pairs inside without obvious marks, smoke and soot can coat connectors and get into jacks and panels, and water used to fight the fire can sit in raceways and boxes. Cable in the damaged apartment and anywhere it was exposed to direct heat should be replaced. Elsewhere, we inspect what is visible, open and clean or replace every jack and panel in smoke-affected areas, and run a certification test on each link: a run that passes cleanly can stay, and one that fails or shows marginal results comes out. Insurers and adjusters sometimes want that record, so we give you test results for each affected run and a list of what was replaced and why. Openings made during the fire repairs are the right moment to put in proper sleeves and firestop.

Two parkway buildings were vacated after the June 2026 fire at 188 and 184 Ocean Parkway. When a building comes back from a vacate order, what should the cabling plan include so the riser is better than before?

A rebuild is the one time a building gets to choose its riser rather than inherit it. The plan should include a defined entrance for every provider in the cellar with one consolidated, sealed entry; a riser in corporation-controlled space on every floor with sleeves sized for spare capacity and pull strings left in each; a small panel on each floor and a cable to every apartment, even those whose residents have not asked, because adding them later means opening finished walls; a separate building network for the entry system, lobby cameras and the management office; and firestop at every penetration recorded on a drawing. The timing comes from the reconstruction itself: the cabling has to go in after framing and rough-in and before walls and ceilings close, coordinated with the general contractor and the building's architect, and inside whatever inspections the vacate order's lifting requires. We would ask for the drawings early so the riser is part of the plan, not squeezed into it.

Houses on and off the parkway

Our house was two houses joined into one, and there are still two cellars and two utility entrances. Which one should the network start from?

Usually the one where the newest provider line already arrives and where there is a dry wall with room for a small panel, but the survey decides. We look at both cellars for three things: where each provider's line enters, which cellar sits more centrally under the rooms that need the most connections, and which offers an easy path to the floors above. Once one is chosen, the other entry is retired: its provider lines are moved or removed with the provider's cooperation, abandoned cable is taken out and old holes are sealed. Cable then crosses from the chosen cellar into the other half through the old party wall, with a cored and sealed sleeve in a spot that does not weaken it, or along a shared space if the joining left one. Because the two halves may have floors at slightly different levels, the route upstairs on each side is planned separately, and both sides end at the same labeled panel.

For big family holidays, forty or fifty phones and tablets can be in our house at once. How do you size the access points and the switch for that?

By planning for the busiest day rather than the average one. On an ordinary weekday a large parkway house may have a dozen active devices; on a holiday it can have several times that, crowded into the dining room, the living room and the kitchen. A single router cannot serve that many devices well from one spot, so we place a wired access point in each room where people gather, so that no single unit carries the whole family, and we choose access points rated for many simultaneous connections. The switch gets enough ports for every access point, every wired device and a few spares, and if the access points draw power over the cable, enough power budget to run them all. Separating a guest network from the family's own devices keeps visitors' phones away from your computers and cameras. Most of the result comes from placement: an access point in the right ceiling does more than a bigger one in the wrong room.

Schools, care homes and synagogues

Our yeshiva wants an interactive board or a large screen in every classroom. Where do the cables need to land at the teaching wall?

At the board, at the teacher's station and at the ceiling, each placed before anyone drills. A classroom display usually needs at least one network cable at the board itself, at the height where its connection panel will sit, plus a cable to the teacher's desk or wherever the teacher's computer lives, and often an access point in the middle of the ceiling for students' devices. If the board connects to a computer by a video cable, that run should travel inside the wall or a covered raceway from desk to board rather than across the floor. We mark the positions with the school's staff and the display installer, because the bracket, the power outlet and our jack have to line up. In a building where classrooms repeat from floor to floor, the same layout in each room makes the cabling faster and the rooms easier to maintain. All cable in the room stays out of students' reach and is labeled by room number at the closet.

Our nursing home's resident rooms line long corridors. Where do access points go so every room gets coverage without running cable into each room?

Usually in the corridor ceiling, spaced so their coverage overlaps into the rooms on both sides, with extra units where walls or doors are heavier than the survey expects. That keeps all cabling and all equipment in staff-controlled corridor space, avoids entering residents' rooms for installation or later maintenance, and keeps the access points away from bedsides. Spacing depends on the construction of the room walls and doors and on how many devices residents use, so we measure signal during the survey and after installation rather than guess, and add an access point inside a room only where the corridor cannot reach it. Nursing stations, offices and any wired clinical workstation get their own cable to the equipment room. Specialized systems the facility already runs, such as nurse call, belong to their own manufacturers and installers; our cable stays separate from them, and we coordinate with the facility's vendors before working near their equipment.

Our synagogue's sanctuary has a high ceiling and a gallery above the main floor. Where can access points and cables go without marring the room?

Usually somewhere other than the sanctuary ceiling. A high ceiling makes an access point both hard to reach and less effective, because it sits far from the people it serves, and a decorated ceiling is the last thing a congregation wants opened. Better positions are the underside of the gallery, the walls at the back or sides of the room at a modest height, or the spaces just outside the sanctuary, if their signal carries in well enough. The gallery itself often has a floor or a front edge with space inside it where a cable can travel. We walk the room with whoever is responsible for the building, measure the signal from the candidate spots and show exactly where each device and any visible cable would be before anything is installed. The office, the social hall and classrooms are usually easier and are cabled first, with the sanctuary planned last and most carefully.

Shops and the street

Our restaurant on Avenue U keeps losing its kitchen printer on Wi-Fi near the ovens and the fryer. Will a cable fix it, and what kind of cable survives a kitchen?

A cable usually fixes it completely, because the kitchen is a hard place for Wi-Fi: steel equipment, hoods and walk-ins reflect and absorb the signal, and the printer often sits in exactly the spot the router reaches worst. A network cable to the printer, the kitchen display and any ordering screen removes Wi-Fi from the equation for the devices that cannot afford to drop orders. The cable route matters as much as the cable: it stays well away from the cooking line's heat, above or behind equipment rather than under it, out of reach of mops and grease, and it reaches each device through a covered raceway or a protected drop rather than hanging loose. Where it passes through a ceiling space that carries air, it has to be rated for that space. Payment terminals, the kitchen and guest Wi-Fi each go on their own network, so a guest's phone never shares a path with your card readers.

A new provider wants to bring fiber to our building, and the parkway's malls and service roads are a scenic landmark. Who handles getting the line from the street to our wall?

The provider does, under its own arrangements with the city and with your building. The landmark designation covers the parkway between the outer curb lines of the service roads, so any work in the street, the service road or under the malls is the provider's to plan, permit and carry out; we do not dig in the public right-of-way. Your building's part is to agree with the provider on where the line will enter, which is usually through the cellar wall, and on what route it may take across your private front yard, which the parkway zoning keeps open and landscaped. Our part begins at the entry: a defined handoff point in the cellar, a riser that reaches every floor and a cable to each apartment that wants service, so the new provider connects at one place instead of designing its own path through the building. If you tell us the provider's planned entry point before it arrives, we can have the cellar handoff ready for it.

HPD publishes guidance on bringing broadband into existing buildings. What should an owner take from it before calling a cabling contractor?

The main idea is to decide how your building will be wired before a provider decides for you. HPD's residential broadband installation guidance covers new fiber in buildings that already have wiring, the options for reaching individual units and design guidelines, and it gives owners a contact at GetConnected@hpd.nyc.gov. For a parkway owner, the practical steps that follow from it are the ones we plan with you: know what wiring the building already has and whether it is usable, choose a cellar handoff and a riser route that any provider can use, decide how cable will reach each unit, through the stack or the hallway ceiling, and keep a record of all of it. Owners who arrive with that thinking done get faster, cleaner provider installs and fewer improvised holes. If you would like, we can review HPD's guidance against your building during the survey.

Ocean Parkway Structured Cabling FAQ

Our co-op board meets once a month. How far ahead should your proposal reach the managing agent to make the agenda?
+

Ask the managing agent when the agenda closes and work back from there, allowing time for our survey and the written proposal. If the board wants alternatives, such as a closet stack and a corridor route priced side by side, tell us at the start so both are ready for the same meeting.

The managing agent still has the building's original drawings from the early 1960s. Are those useful to you?
+

Very. Original floor plans and sections show where closets, chases and shafts were meant to stack, where beams run under the slabs and how the cellar was laid out, which helps us propose sleeve locations that avoid structure. Buildings change over sixty years, so we still confirm everything on site, but drawings shorten the survey and make the proposal more precise. Photos or scans of the typical floor plan and the cellar plan are enough to start.

Can the quote separate the building's riser from the work inside individual apartments, so each shareholder pays only for their own jacks?
+

Yes, and in co-ops we recommend it. The proposal lists the shared work, meaning the cellar handoff, the riser, each floor's panel and the cable to each apartment's entry, as the building's cost, and then prices in-unit jacks and access points per apartment so shareholders can opt in. Apartments that do not join now still get a cable to their door if the board chooses, which makes later connection simple.

Many older residents in our building are home all day. How loud is the drilling and coring, and how long does it last in one spot?
+

Coring through a slab is the loudest part, and it usually takes minutes per opening rather than hours, with a short burst at each floor. Drilling small holes in walls is quieter and quicker still, and pulling and terminating cable is close to silent. We tell the managing agent in advance which floors will have coring on which days, so residents can be notified, and we keep the noisy work inside the hours the building approves.

Will any part of the work require shutting off water or gas to apartments?
+

Normally no. Data cabling does not touch the plumbing or gas systems, and sleeve locations are chosen to stay clear of pipes. Before every core, the technician checks both sides of the slab for pipes and other services, and if a planned location turns out to be too close to one, we move the opening rather than ask for a shutoff.

Our hallways have fire-rated doors that close on their own. Will your crew prop them open while working?
+

No. Self-closing doors in corridors and stairwells stay closed and latched, and the crew opens them only to pass through, even when carrying ladders or cable. If cable has to cross a doorway during a pull, it goes over the frame through the ceiling space, never under a door that has been wedged open. Any temporary arrangement the building wants for a delivery is the super's decision, not ours.

Our yeshiva closes for part of the summer. Is that the best window for the classroom cabling?
+

It is usually the easiest, because classrooms can be opened, worked and cleaned without disrupting students, and several rooms can be done at once. If summer is already booked for other renovations, we can coordinate with those trades or work in after-school hours room by room. Tell us the dates the building is empty as early as possible, since the survey and any equipment orders come first.

Do your technicians follow a nursing home's own visitor and infection-control rules while they work?
+

Yes. We ask the facility for its contractor rules before the visit and follow them, including sign-in, any screening, protective coverings for work areas, dust control at every opening and the areas and hours staff allow us to use. Work in corridors is set up so residents and carts can still pass, and every area is cleaned before staff take it back.

Another contractor is renovating our lobby at the same time. Can both crews work without getting in each other's way?
+

Usually, with a little coordination. A lobby renovation is often a good time to place sleeves and a cable for an entry panel or a lobby screen, so it is worth talking early.

Our grandchildren are in and out of the apartment all the time. Is a network jack or a powered cable any danger if a child touches it?

Network cable carries low-voltage signals, and even when it powers an access point or a camera, the power is low voltage that is not a shock hazard the way a household outlet is. The jacks sit flush in faceplates, and any equipment that has to be reachable goes in a closed cabinet.
