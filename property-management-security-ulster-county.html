The County

What Makes Ulster County Rentals Different to Secure

Ulster is a county where most people own their homes and a large minority rent, and the renters are concentrated in a few places. The Census Bureau's American Community Survey for 2019 through 2023 counts 86,178 housing units in the county and 73,105 occupied homes, of which 21,942 are rented, about three households in ten. Out in the towns, the rental market is a scattering of apartments in houses, rooms near the college, cottages and cabins. In the City of Kingston it is a real urban rental market: the same survey counts 5,126 renter households out of 9,951 occupied homes, just over half the city. A management firm working this county moves between those two worlds every week.

Age is the second thing that sets Ulster's buildings apart. The survey puts 21,829 of the county's housing units, about a quarter, in buildings put up in 1939 or earlier. In Kingston the figure is 5,979 of 11,049 units, more than half the city's housing, and in the Village of Saugerties it is 1,118 of 2,026. For security work, that age shows up in the walls. Plaster over lath, stone foundations, brick party walls and stair halls that were never designed to carry cable decide where a wire can go, and they explain why an entry system in an old building costs what it does. They also explain why so many of the buzzer and intercom calls we get from Ulster come from buildings whose original wiring has been patched by three different handymen.

The third thing is seasonal and short-term housing. The Census survey counts 6,545 homes in the county held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use. Most of them are in the mountain and valley towns: 886 in Shandaken, 813 in Woodstock, 702 in Wawarsing, 676 in Rochester and 506 in Marbletown. On top of that, the Ulster County Comptroller's 2025 short-term rental report counted 2,827 housing units operating as short-term rentals in May 2025, 3.28 percent of all housing in the county, and found that 98 percent of them were entire homes rather than rooms. In the Town of Shandaken the share reached 267 of 2,675 units, close to one home in ten, and in the Town of Woodstock 378 of 4,289. A management company in those towns is often running a hospitality business through a door it never sees, and the security plan has to work from a distance.

Each of those three markets has its own rules, which is why this page spends time on them. Kingston's older apartment buildings of six or more units are rent-stabilized under the state's Emergency Tenant Protection Act, a status that has survived a trip to New York's highest court. The city, the Village of New Paltz and the Village of Saugerties each require rental properties to be registered, and owners who live far away must name someone local who answers for the building. The City of Kingston and the Towns of Woodstock and Saugerties each regulate short-term rentals with their own permits, caps or response rules. We are a security contractor, not a law firm, and nothing here is legal advice. We do plan every job with the applicable rule in view and say where each rule comes from, so the owner's attorney can check it.

What breaks most often at Ulster rentals

When an Ulster rental calls us, the cause usually falls into one of five groups. The oldest is the entry wiring in a pre-war Kingston or Saugerties building: a cracked pair in the riser, a corroded call button at the street door, an apartment handset a tenant pulled off the wall, a transformer in the basement that finally cooked. Second comes power and weather. A nor'easter or a summer storm takes a mountain road down for a day or more, and when the lights return, the doorbell transformer, the recorder or the network switch in the garage is dead. Third is connectivity: a cabin in the mountain towns west of Kingston may have weak cell coverage and an internet line that drops whenever it rains, and everything cloud-based stops with it. Fourth is credentials at high-turnover rentals, where a student house or a nightly rental hands out codes and fobs far faster than anyone tracks them. Fifth is the system nobody can log into, because the previous owner, a former tenant or a long-gone handyman set it up on a personal account. Every one of those starts with the $250 service call, and most are closed on the first visit once the technician knows what is in the building.

Who signs off, who meets us and who uses the system

The owner or the managing office is our client, full stop. A management company with an office in Kingston and an owner with a single Saugerties house are treated the same way: they approve the quote, choose the appointment and receive the invoice. If a tenant or a guest phones us directly, we take the details and pass them to the owner or the office rather than booking anything ourselves.

The managing agent of record matters more in Ulster than many out-of-town owners expect. Kingston's rental property law and the Village of Saugerties' rental law both require an owner who does not live in Ulster County to designate a managing agent who does, and the Village of New Paltz requires a property manager living within 15 miles of the village. That person is often the one who meets our technician, so we set them up with their own administrator or user account rather than sharing the owner's login.

The local host is the short-term rental version of a superintendent. Woodstock's code requires a non-owner-occupied short-term rental to have a designated local host, and the Town of Saugerties expects the owner or the designated host to address complaints within two hours. Hosts get codes and app access matched to the job: they can let a locked-out guest in and check the cameras, but they cannot change the owner's settings.

Tenants and guests see the system for a few seconds at a time: opening the street door, letting a visitor in, punching in a code. When something fails, their complaint reaches us through the owner or the office, which keeps every visit authorized and every invoice tied to the right property.

Kingston Rent Stabilization

Kingston's Rent-Stabilized Buildings and the Entry Systems Inside Them

Kingston is the only place in Ulster County where rent stabilization reaches the building's door. New York's Homes and Community Renewal agency, which administers the program, records that on July 28, 2022, the city voted to adopt the Emergency Tenant Protection Act after a study of residential properties with six or more units built before January 1, 1974 found a vacancy rate below the 5 percent threshold. The city's later survey puts that 2022 rate at 1.57 percent. In November 2022 the newly created Kingston Rent Guidelines Board ordered a 15 percent reduction in rents for leases beginning between August 1, 2022 and September 30, 2023, and landlords challenged the emergency and the guidelines in Ulster County Supreme Court.

The litigation went the full distance. On June 18, 2025, the New York State Court of Appeals, in Hudson Valley Property Owners Association v. City of Kingston, upheld the city's vacancy study and its adoption of the ETPA, and let the board's fair-market-rent guideline and 15 percent reduction stand, according to the state agency's summary. A new city vacancy survey in 2025 then found a net vacancy rate of 7.04 percent for sampled ETPA properties, above the emergency threshold, except in buildings of more than 22 units, where the rate stayed below 5 percent. The same survey notes that administrative decisions have left the share of the city's rental units covered by the ETPA under 20 percent. The mayor vetoed the Common Council's resolution to keep the emergency in place in December 2025; Housing Justice for All, a tenant advocacy group, reports that the Council overrode the veto on January 6, 2026, and that a group of landlords filed a new suit in Ulster County Supreme Court on March 18, 2026. Owners of covered buildings should follow the case with their own counsel.

Why does any of this matter to a security contractor? Because rent stabilization changes what an entry system is, legally speaking. The state's Fact Sheet 3 says owners must provide and maintain the services and equipment furnished or required under rent regulation, a list that includes repairs, maintenance, janitorial service, elevator service and ancillary services, and that for ETPA apartments outside New York City the base date is May 29, 1974 or the day before the local effective date, whichever is later. A buzzer, an intercom, a locked front door and a working elevator that a building provided are exactly the kind of building-wide service a tenant expects to keep. When they fail, tenants can file a complaint of decreased services with the agency, and the agency's Fact Sheet 14 explains how rent reductions for decreased services work. Whether a particular device counts as a required service is a question for the agency and the owner's attorney, not for us, but our habit in any Kingston ETPA building is simple: treat the entry system as a service the building owes its tenants, fix it promptly, and document the repair.

What we do differently in a stabilized Kingston building

Repair records that stand on their own. Every visit to an ETPA building produces an invoice that names the building, the failed component, the date the fault was reported to us, the date it was fixed and what was replaced. If a tenant files a decreased-services complaint months later, that is the record the owner's office will need, and we would rather write it on the day than reconstruct it later.

Replacement planned around tenant consent. Fact Sheet 3 says that when an owner replaces equipment in an occupied apartment with new equipment, the owner may be eligible for an Individual Apartment Improvement increase, but only with the tenant's voluntary written consent; in a vacant apartment, the new tenant's consent is not needed. That affects how an owner sequences an intercom upgrade. Building-wide parts such as the street-door panel, the riser and the power supply are one decision; the handsets inside occupied apartments are another. We scope the two separately so the owner and counsel can decide how each is handled, and we never present an apartment upgrade as a rent increase on the owner's behalf.

No loss of service during the switch. In a stabilized building, a front door that stops locking for a weekend while a new system goes in is not just an inconvenience. We mount and test new equipment while the old system keeps working, cut over in one short window, and leave the old door release in place until the new one is proven.

Chapter 332: the city rental registry and the out-of-county owner

Separately from rent stabilization, Chapter 332 of the City of Kingston's code requires every rental property, residential or mixed-use, to be registered with the city's Building Safety Department, and says the city will inspect each rental property at least once every 24 months for compliance with the city code and state law. An owner who does not live in Ulster County must designate a managing agent, a natural person at least 18 years old who actually lives in the county, as the person responsible for the building and the one who can be served on the owner's behalf, and must update that designation within 15 days of any change. Violations can bring fines of up to $500, with each week counted as a separate offense. For our work, the practical effect is that the managing agent is usually the person on site for the inspection and for our visits, and needs real access to the building's entry and camera systems, not a borrowed password.

Historic districts: what goes on a Kingston facade

Much of Kingston's older rental stock sits in its historic districts. The city has been a Certified Local Government since 1986, and its Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission says that projects involving the exterior of historic properties, individually listed or within a district, and visible from the public right-of-way are reviewed by the commission before a building permit is issued and work begins. The Rondout-West Strand district dates to 1979, and the Stockade district holds some of the oldest buildings in the city. A new call panel beside a Stockade doorway, a camera arm on a Rondout cornice or a keypad on a storefront door with apartments above can be the kind of visible exterior change the commission looks at. We do not decide that for the owner. What we do is propose the quietest hardware that does the job, mount it inside the vestibule where that works, and give the owner a photo and a description of anything exterior so the question can be put to the commission before we drill.

Buzzers, intercoms and the street door

A typical Ulster entry call comes from a three-story brick building in Kingston or a mixed-use building on a village main street: the release from one apartment no longer opens the street door, two tenants hear each other's visitors, or the panel at the door hums and does nothing. In buildings of that age the fault is usually small and specific. A basement transformer has failed, the strike has drifted out of line with the latch, a call button has corroded, or one pair in the riser has cracked where it passes a radiator. We trace the fault before recommending anything, and if the old wiring tests clean, the repair stays at the door and in the basement instead of in every apartment.

For fixing what is already on the wall, the prices sit on our door buzzer repair page for Ulster County: an apartment station is $250–$525, a release relay or button $250–$475, a 24-volt transformer $325–$650, a strike set to the latch $450–$950, lobby panel or faceplate work $650–$1,800, and riser pairs repaired or re-terminated $850–$2,400. Replacing the system is a different page, our intercom installation page for Ulster County, which lists $500–$2,500 for a single house, $1,200–$3,500 for video in a four- to eight-unit building, $1,200–$5,000 for multi-unit video in general and $2,500–$8,000 and up for an app-based system in a bigger building. For small buildings that only need a simpler button system, see the door buzzer installation page for Ulster County.

One rule we keep in older rental buildings: a building is never moved to phone-only entry without a way for tenants who do not use a smartphone, or who sit in a dead spot, to answer the door. In a Kingston building with long-term tenants, some of them older, that usually means keeping a handset or a video station in each apartment. In a building of graduate students near the New Paltz campus, an app may be all anyone wants. The owner hears both options before deciding.

Strikes, closers and keeping the way out open

In buildings this old, the door is often the weak part of the system. Frames settle, stone thresholds heave a little every winter, and closers that slam the door knock the latch out of line until the strike no longer catches. We set electrified hardware true to the latch, adjust or flag the closer, and tell the owner when the frame itself needs a carpenter; mechanical locksets on apartment doors stay with the locksmith. Any locked door on an exit route must open from the inside without a key, code or card, and a magnetic lock on an egress door needs a release that works when the power fails and when the fire alarm sounds. If an existing door does not meet that, the owner gets it in writing before we touch anything else.

Keypads, fobs and access control

At a rental, access control is mostly about ending the cost of rekeying and the keys that leave with every former tenant. On one door, a keypad lock or a small reader with an electric strike gives each tenant, the cleaner and the plumber their own code, and the code disappears the day the lease ends. In a small building, one controller in the basement can run the street door, the back door to the lot, the laundry and the storage cage from the same list. Across a garden complex, a networked system lets the office manage every entrance from a desk in Kingston. Our access control installation page for Ulster County publishes $2,100–$6,500 for one door or gate with a keypad, fob reader, smart lock or gate controller, $5,500–$20,000 for multi-door properties and $20,000–$55,000 or more for a full property, and our smart lock installation page for Ulster County covers code locks for side doors, room doors and short-term rentals. Any recurring platform fee is shown on its own line, so an owner can compare a system with no monthly charge against one with a per-door fee over five years.

Elevator buildings are rare outside Kingston, but where a rental building has one, a reader in the car can limit floors to the people who live on them. Our elevator access control page for Ulster County lists Level 1 work from $2,295 per car and Level 2 biometric readers from $3,825 per car, with the elevator contractor's own visit priced by that contractor.

Short-term rental doors and booking codes

A whole-house short-term rental in Woodstock, Saugerties or Shandaken has no front desk and, usually, no one on site. The door hardware therefore does three jobs at once: it lets the guest in without a key handoff, it locks the owner's storage closet against everyone, and it tells the host who opened what and when. We fit an outdoor-rated keypad deadbolt or a reader and strike to the guest entrance, set codes to start at check-in and expire at checkout, give the cleaner and the local host their own codes, and keep a separate lock on the owner's closet that guests never get. The log that results is useful to a host in towns that limit how often a whole house may be rented; Woodstock's code, for example, caps a non-owner-occupied short-term rental at 180 days a year with no more than 26 weekends. A log is a record, not a legal filing, and the permit stays the owner's responsibility.

Alarms, vacant units and the Sheriff's permit

A monitored intrusion alarm earns its cost at an Ulster rental during the weeks nobody lives there: between tenants, while a unit is renovated, and through the winter at a cabin that rents mostly in summer. Central-station monitoring through our provider is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, and basic residential alarm installation starts at $600, both as published on our alarm installation page for Ulster County.

Ulster also has a county alarm law that many owners have never heard of. The Sheriff's Office administers Local Law 13 of 2015, which requires a permit for every structure with an alarm system; the owner, or the tenant who controls the system, applies, and the Sheriff's page lists a one-time $10 registration fee per structure. The first two false alarms in a calendar year carry no penalty, the third costs $50 and the fourth and later ones $200 each, and an unpermitted system gets a warning and 14 days to register before penalties of $100 and $200 apply; unpaid amounts can be added to the property tax bill. The law does not apply everywhere: the Sheriff lists the City of Kingston, the Towns of Esopus, Marlborough, New Paltz, Saugerties, Shandaken, Ulster, Wawarsing and Woodstock, and the Villages of Ellenville, New Paltz and Saugerties as exempt, and some of those, such as the Town of Saugerties, have alarm permit rules of their own. We tell the owner which rule applies to each address and program entry delays, sensor placement and the cancel procedure to keep false dispatches down.

Fire alarm systems for rental buildings

We handle fire alarm systems in bigger rental buildings as well. In Ulster, as everywhere outside the five boroughs, the approving authority is the local code enforcement office of the city, town or village, working under the state Uniform Code, and the sequence is the same each time: an engineer or architect draws the system, the office approves the plans, we install, and the office witnesses the acceptance test. The steps are laid out on our fire alarm installation page for Ulster County. There is no published fire alarm price; each one is priced from its approved drawings. Any electric lock in the building must drop when the fire alarm goes off, so locks and fire alarm are designed as one job.

Cabling, links between buildings and outbuildings

Ulster properties sprawl. A garden complex can have six buildings and a laundry house across a lot, a farmhouse rental may have an apartment over the barn, and a cabin's driveway gate can be hundreds of feet from the porch. Everything above depends on getting a signal between those points. Where conduit exists, we pull Cat6 through it. Where it does not, a point-to-point wireless link is often faster and kinder to the ground than a trench through ledge and tree roots, and fiber makes sense where a run is long or exposed to lightning. Inside, every device gets its own cable back to a PoE switch with battery backup in a locked spot, and the security equipment never shares a router with the tenants. Our structured cabling page for Ulster County publishes typical pricing of $150 to $300 per connection point. Any new outlet or circuit is work for a licensed electrician: Ulster County licenses master electricians through its own Electrical Licensing Board, and we do not do electrical work.

Power, storms and surge protection

Central Hudson's own storm reports show what an Ulster storm does. After the slow-moving nor'easter of late September 2026, the utility said it was working to restore power to more than 19,000 homes and businesses, and named Woodstock, Shawangunk, Plattekill and Hurley in Ulster County among the hardest-hit areas. At a rental, an outage that lasts a day or two decides which doors stay controlled and which cameras keep recording. We put battery backup on the access controller, the network switch and the recorder, add surge protection where outdoor cable runs are long, choose fail-secure strikes that keep the street door locked without power while still opening from inside, and leave a short note at the equipment telling the managing agent or the host what to restart, and in what order, when the power returns.

Internet and cell signal in the mountain towns

A camera, a keypad or a video doorbell is only as useful as its link to the owner's phone. In Kingston and the river towns that link is ordinary broadband. In parts of Shandaken, Denning, Hardenburgh, Olive and Rochester it may be a slow line, a satellite dish or a phone signal that comes and goes. We test the connection at the spot where each device will mount before choosing equipment. Where the internet is unreliable, we favor a recorder or cameras with a memory card that keep recording locally, keypad locks that work without a network, and a cellular backup for alarm reporting, so that a dropped connection costs the owner remote viewing for a while rather than the footage itself.

Who holds the logins

Plenty of Ulster service calls are not hardware problems at all. Somebody set the doorbell up under a personal email years ago, a tenant who moved out still has the camera app on their phone, or the installer code for the alarm belongs to a company that installed it decades ago. Student rentals near SUNY New Paltz

New Paltz is a college town, and its rental rules show it. U.S. News reports that SUNY New Paltz enrolled 6,113 undergraduates in fall 2025, and the college runs 14 residence halls; the campus newspaper, The Oracle, has reported on-campus housing capacity of roughly 3,250 students. Many of the rest rent in the village and the town, often a house shared by four or five students who each sign for a bedroom. The Village of New Paltz's rental chapter requires every rental property to be registered and inspected annually, requires an owner who does not live within 15 miles of the village to appoint a property manager who does, gives a new owner 15 days to notify the code enforcement official after a transfer, and requires the owner to keep the name and contact information of every tenant and subtenant. The village keeps a public index of registered rentals that includes a history of violations, fires and other incidents at each property.

For security work, student rentals mean frequent turnover and a lot of credentials. A house rented by the bedroom usually needs a keypad or reader on the front door and a keyed or keypad lock on each bedroom door, so a departing tenant's code can be deleted without rekeying the whole house. Codes for the summer gap, when the house may sit empty or host a short summer lease, should be set before the spring move-out, not after. Cameras watch the porch, the driveway and the bike storage; they do not watch shared kitchens or living rooms. And because the village inspects every year, we make sure nothing we install blocks a window, an exit or a detector the inspector will look at.

Short-term rentals in Woodstock, Saugerties and Kingston

Each of the county's busiest short-term rental markets regulates them differently, and the security plan has to fit the permit. In the Town of Woodstock, the zoning code requires a short-term rental registration from the town, valid for one calendar year, with the registration number posted on every online listing; an individual or entity may hold only one, a whole house rented with no owner present must have a designated local host, and it may be rented no more than 180 days a year, with no more than 26 weekends. In the Town of Saugerties, a permit is limited to single-family homes and permitted accessory apartments, requires a yearly fire and safety inspection and a copy of the owner's Ulster County registration, and makes the owner or designated host responsible for addressing complaints within two hours of notification. The rental must also post a directory with emergency contacts, a safety and egress plan and quiet hours beginning at 10 p.m. In the City of Kingston, the full short-term rental permit is capped at 1 percent of the city's housing units, set at 106 permits as of August 2023 and limited to certain zones, while resident-occupied and limited permits are uncapped; only one short-term rental is allowed per property, none in an accessory dwelling unit, and the license number must appear in every listing.

Security equipment cannot satisfy any of those rules on its own, and we do not claim it does. What it can do is support the person responsible. A doorbell and an entrance camera let a host in Woodstock see who is at the door from wherever they are. A keypad log shows when guests arrived and left. A noise or occupancy question is answered by a person, but a camera on the driveway shows how many cars are parked there. And a door contact on the owner's locked closet tells the host if anyone opened it. In the Town of Saugerties, where the host has two hours to address a complaint, being able to see the property before driving to it is the difference between a quick call and a wasted trip.

Registration with the county sits under all of this. The Ulster County Department of Finance requires operators of hotels, motels and short-term rentals to register and collect the county's occupancy tax, and says that when a booking service such as Airbnb or VRBO collects and remits the tax, the operator must still file an occupancy tax return. The Comptroller's report adds that sixteen municipalities have adopted some form of short-term rental regulation, that at least ten platforms list Ulster rentals, up from four in 2020, and that enforcement remains a challenge. None of that is our job, but owners ask us about it often enough that we note it here.

Seasonal cabins in the Catskill towns

Shandaken's 886 seasonal homes and the hundreds more in Woodstock, Olive, Rochester and Denning are a different kind of rental: cabins and camps that may sit empty for months, on roads that are plowed late and in valleys where a phone signal is a matter of luck. The risks are break-ins nobody notices for weeks, frozen pipes, and equipment that dies silently in the first cold snap. The plan we use is built for distance and cold: a monitored alarm with door contacts and motion detection that reports over cellular as well as internet, a temperature sensor where the alarm panel supports one, cameras that record locally and alert on people rather than every deer, batteries rated for the cold and changed before winter, and a written checklist the caretaker can follow when the cabin opens in spring.

Garden apartment complexes

Garden complexes in the Towns of Ulster, Lloyd, Plattekill, Esopus and Saugerties are where Ulster rental work looks most like a suburban job: two- or three-story buildings with several entrances each, a laundry room, mailboxes in a vestibule or a kiosk, and a lot. Where buildings are separated by a lot or a lawn, a wireless link between them usually beats trenching.

Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in Ulster County: Questions Answered

Kingston buildings and rent stabilization

Our Kingston building is stabilized under the ETPA. If we replace the old audio intercom with video, can that cost be passed on to tenants?

Not automatically, and that is a question for your attorney rather than for us. The state's Fact Sheet 3 says new equipment installed in an occupied apartment can support an Individual Apartment Improvement increase only with the tenant's voluntary written consent, while a vacant apartment's new tenant does not have to consent. Work on the building-wide parts, such as the street-door panel, the riser and the power supply, is a separate matter. We price those two pieces separately so you and your counsel can decide how each one is handled.

A tenant in our stabilized building filed a decrease-in-services complaint about the front door. What should our repair paperwork show?

The facts, in order. Our invoice for an ETPA building names the building and the door, the date we were told about the fault, the date and time of our visit, what we found, what we replaced and the test that showed the door locking and releasing again. If parts had to be ordered, it says when they were ordered and when they were installed. That record is what your office or attorney will want when answering the complaint, and we write it on the day of the repair.

Our walk-up is in the Stockade district. Does a new call panel or a camera on the front need the landmarks commission's review?

It may. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews projects involving the exterior of historic properties that are visible from the public right-of-way before a building permit is issued. We will propose hardware that can be mounted inside the vestibule wherever the entry allows, keep anything exterior as small and quiet as possible, and give you photos and a description to take to the commission before we drill. The decision belongs to the commission and the building department.

We own rentals in Kingston but live in another county. Who answers for the building, and does that person need access to the entry system?

Kingston's rental property law requires an owner who does not live in Ulster County to designate a managing agent who does, a person at least 18 years old who is responsible for the building and can be served on your behalf. That person is usually the one who meets inspectors and contractors, so we give them their own login to the entry and camera systems with the rights they actually need, and you keep the owner's administrator account.

New Paltz student rentals

Our New Paltz tenants sign one joint lease for the whole house. Does it still make sense to give each of them a separate door code?

Yes, even on a joint lease. Separate codes cost nothing extra on most keypad locks and readers, and they let you see who opened the door when something goes wrong, remove one housemate who leaves mid-year without changing everyone else's code, and give a parent or a visiting friend a code that expires. You remain the administrator; the tenants only get user codes.

Students leave in May and the next group arrives in August. What should happen to the codes and cameras over the empty summer?

Delete every departing tenant's code and fob on move-out day, not when the new leases start. Then set the house to a vacant-period arrangement: an alarm armed if you have one, cameras alerting on people at the doors, and a single code for the cleaner and the summer maintenance crew that expires on a set date. In August, the new tenants get fresh codes the day their leases begin. We write that sequence down for whoever manages the house.

The village inspects our New Paltz rental every year. Can the security work we do cause us to fail the inspection?

It should not, and we plan so that it will not. The village's rental chapter lists conditions such as missing windows in sleeping spaces or missing smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that can stop a property from being rented, and the owner gets a copy of the blank inspection checklist when registering. Our equipment never covers a window, blocks an exit, sits in front of a detector or locks a bedroom so that it cannot be opened from inside. If you send us the checklist, we check our plan against it before we install.

Short-term rentals and seasonal cabins

The Town of Saugerties expects the owner or host to deal with complaints within two hours. How can the equipment help the host do that?

By letting the host see the property before getting in the car. A video doorbell and a camera on the driveway show who is there and how many vehicles are parked; a keypad log shows when guests arrived; a noise complaint about the yard can be checked against the outdoor camera; and remote unlocking lets the host let a locked-out guest in from a phone. The two-hour response is still a person's job. The equipment just makes that person faster and better informed.

Our Woodstock house is a non-owner-occupied rental limited to 180 nights a year. Can the keypad log help us keep track of the nights it was used?

It can help, as a record of every code used at the door with the time it was used. Booking-length codes that start at check-in and end at checkout make the log line up with your reservations. It is not a substitute for your booking records or your registration with the town, which remain the documents that count, but it is a useful cross-check if a question ever comes up about when the house was occupied.

Our Shandaken cabin sits at the end of a long private drive. Where should the first camera go?

Usually at the house end, aimed back down the drive at the spot where every car has to slow and turn, because that is where faces and vehicles are close enough to be recognized and where power and a network connection already exist. A camera at the road end of the drive sees more but identifies less, and it needs its own power, often solar, and a cellular or wireless link. If the owner wants to know when anyone turns in, a driveway sensor or a camera at the bottom can send the alert while the house camera records the detail.

Can a guest's door code at a short-term rental expire on its own at checkout?

Yes, on most modern keypad locks and access systems. We set each guest code to start at check-in time and stop at checkout time, give the cleaner a code that works only on turnover days, and keep the owner's code and the local host's code permanent. Some locks connect to booking platforms to generate codes automatically; where the owner wants that, we set up the lock and the owner connects their own booking account.

Garden complexes, alarms and mixed-use buildings

Our garden complex off Route 9W has six apartment buildings spread across one lot. Where should the recorder and the network equipment go?

In a locked, ventilated room in the building closest to the middle of the site, with power backed up and a clear path to the others. From there, each building is reached by conduit if it exists or by a point-to-point wireless link if it does not. Keeping the recorder away from the leasing office makes it harder for anyone who walks into the office to take the footage with them.

Our tenant arms and disarms the alarm in a house we rent out in Hurley. Who applies for the alarm permit?

Under the county's alarm law, administered by the Sheriff's Office, the owner or the tenant who controls the system applies for the permit, and the Sheriff lists a one-time $10 registration fee per structure. Penalties for repeated false alarms fall on the property owner. Decide with your tenant who will hold the permit, put that in the lease, and give us both names so the monitoring account and the permit match. If your rental is in one of the exempt municipalities, its own alarm rules apply instead.

Our Highland building has a street entrance and a rear entrance from the lot. Should both doors have a call panel?

Usually only the door visitors use needs a panel. The rear door from the lot can take a reader or keypad for tenants, a door contact that reports if it is left open, and a camera, without a call panel at all. Putting two panels on a small building doubles the hardware and splits deliveries between doors. If couriers keep going to the back, a sign and a camera there solve most of it.

What moves an Ulster quote up or down

Five things explain most of the gap between two quotes for rentals that look alike. The first is the age of the walls. Plaster, stone and brick add time to every cable run, and a building whose old riser tests clean can keep its wiring while one with cracked pairs needs new cable to every floor. The second is distance, whether between buildings on a garden site or between a cabin and its gate, because a link costs money whether it is a pair of radios or a trench. The third is the number of controlled doors, each with its own lock or reader, power and wiring. The fourth is recurring fees, which run from nothing after installation to a monthly charge per door, per unit or per camera. The fifth is signal. At a mountain rental, a cellular communicator or a camera that records locally can be the difference between a system that works and one that does not, and it belongs in the price from the start rather than as a surprise later.

Ulster County Property Management Security FAQ

Do you work in the western mountain towns such as Shandaken, Denning and Hardenburgh, or only near Kingston?
+

We work across the whole county, including the mountain towns. Visits to the far west are planned as part of a day in that direction, in the same early-day, two-hour arrival windows as everywhere else, and we ask for directions and any gate or road details up front so the technician finds the property on the first try.

Can you register our short-term rental with the town or the county for us?
+

No. Registration, permits and occupancy tax filings belong to the owner or the owner's representative, and the rules differ between the county, the City of Kingston and towns such as Woodstock and Saugerties. What we can provide is a plain description of the security equipment at the property, if a form asks for one.

Will a battery keypad lock keep working through a winter at an unheated cabin?
+

Only if it is chosen and set up for it. We use locks rated for the cold the door will see, put in fresh batteries before winter, set low-battery alerts to go to the owner or the caretaker, and make sure there is a backup way in, such as a key in a lockbox or a key override, if the batteries do die.

Do guests at our short-term rental need to download an app to get in?
+

No. With a keypad lock or a keypad reader, the guest only needs the code you send with the booking. An app can be offered as an extra, for example to unlock from a phone, but we set the door up so a code alone always works, which matters for guests whose phones are dead or who have no signal on arrival.

Is financing available for an owner with one or two Ulster rentals?
+

Yes. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval. Ask for it when your quote arrives, for a single rental or a larger project.

Will a guest's door code still work if the internet at the rental goes down?
+

On most keypad locks and readers, yes. The codes are stored in the lock or the controller, so a code that was already loaded keeps working during an outage. What stops is remote control: a new code created from the host's phone will not reach the lock until the connection returns, and the entry log will not update remotely. That is why we load each guest's code well before check-in, not on the day.

Is there a monthly fee after the system is installed?
+

It depends on what the owner chooses. A recorder with local storage and a wired entry panel can run with no monthly charge at all. Central-station alarm monitoring is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the app, and some app-based intercoms and cloud access platforms charge per door or per unit. Any recurring fee is shown on its own line in the quote.

Do you serve rentals in the southern towns, such as Shawangunk, Plattekill and Marlborough?
+

Yes. Those towns are on the same Ulster line and the same vendor file as Kingston and the mountain towns, and visits there are often grouped with work in the river towns nearby. The repair visit starts with the same $250 service call.
