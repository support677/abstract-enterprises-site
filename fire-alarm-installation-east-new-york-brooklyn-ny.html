The Neighborhood

Why East New York Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

East New York is a neighborhood of small buildings that hold a lot of people. HPD's housing plan for Brooklyn Community District 5 counts roughly 60,000 homes and says small buildings of one to five units are the main residential building type, with about 60 percent of all units in buildings of fewer than six apartments. That matters more for fire detection than any statistic about towers. In a small building there is usually no super on site, no fire alarm panel in a lobby and no one whose job is to walk the halls. Whether a fire is noticed in time depends on alarms inside each home, on whether those alarms can be heard through closed doors on other floors, and on whether anyone outside the building will ever learn that one is sounding.

The houses are also older than people tend to assume. The same HPD plan reports that about half of the district's housing was built before 1947, another third before 1974, and only about 10 percent after 2000. Some of that older stock is semi-detached two- to four-family houses with cellars that hold the boiler, the water heater, the gas meter and, in many homes, a room someone sleeps in. Some of it is walk-up apartment buildings along the avenues. The newer stock is very different: the houses East Brooklyn Congregations built under the Nehemiah plan from 1983, the steel-module townhouses of Nehemiah Spring Creek, and the mid- and high-rise lottery buildings that followed the 2016 rezoning. Each kind of building brings its own fire alarm questions, and we plan each one separately on this page.

The local fire record, covered in detail further down, shows what is at stake: a 2023 fire in a Sheffield Avenue building with open HPD smoke detector violations, a 2026 fire in a Van Siclen Avenue two-family the city said had become five units, and a 2011 death in an illegal cellar apartment on Drew Street. Each points to a decision that is easy to get wrong here: missing devices in rented apartments, more households than a house was built for, and people sleeping below grade.

Then there is the calendar. HPD's rules require natural gas detectors in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes that the owner does not live in, wherever there is gas piping, starting January 1, 2027. East New York is full of gas: the Nehemiah houses were built with natural gas furnaces, and gas ranges and water heaters are standard in the two-families and walk-ups. For many owners here, the gas detector deadline is the reason to look at the whole system now, while the technician is already in every apartment, rather than paying for two rounds of visits.

Fire Alarm Installation for East New York's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every fire alarm job we see in East New York. They differ in who owns them, who sleeps in them, what the walls and floors are made of, and which city rules reach them. Those differences set the plan well before any model or brand comes up.

Houses built by East Brooklyn Congregations

Nehemiah houses put up from 1983 onward, about 1,000 to 1,200 square feet, with full basements, gas furnaces and parking pads, mostly lived in by the families who own them.

Modular townhouses at Nehemiah Spring Creek

Homes made of factory-built steel-frame modules with concrete floors and fiber cement siding, stacked two to four high on former landfill, finished in phases in 2009 and 2011.

Older two- to four-family houses

Semi-detached and attached houses, many from before 1947, with an owner or tenants on each floor, a shared stair, and a cellar that holds the boiler, the meters and sometimes an apartment.

Walk-up apartment buildings

Two- to six-story buildings on the avenues and side streets, from small buildings like the two-story one on Sheffield Avenue to four-story buildings with two dozen apartments.

Lottery and condo buildings since 2016

New mid- and high-rise buildings with hundreds of apartments, engineered fire alarm systems that were filed and inspected, and a managing agent who keeps the records.

NYCHA and converted public housing

Developments such as the Louis Heaton Pink Houses and Cypress Hills Houses, plus campuses moved to private management under PACT, where the building system belongs to the landlord.

Houses built by East Brooklyn Congregations: a gas furnace below, bedrooms above, one family

The Gotham Center's history of the Nehemiah plan describes what East Brooklyn Congregations built: about 4,500 houses over roughly three decades, starting in 1983, two-bedroom homes of about 1,000 square feet and three-bedroom homes of about 1,200, with full basements, double-glazed windows, natural gas furnaces and parking pads, set on staggered building lines. For fire detection, the important parts of that description are the basement and the gas. In most of these houses the furnace, the water heater and often a gas dryer share the basement, the kitchen with its gas range is on the main floor, and the bedrooms are upstairs. A fire or a carbon monoxide leak that starts below has a short, direct route up the stair to the people who are asleep.

So the plan for a Nehemiah house starts in the basement. We put carbon monoxide detection near the sleeping area on the bedroom floor and on the main floor, and we look closely at where it belongs in the basement itself, close enough to the furnace and water heater to be useful but not so close that normal start-up fumes set it off. Smoke alarms go on every level, inside and outside every bedroom, and at the top of the basement stair, where smoke from below arrives first. Because the houses are compact, a single family usually hears an alarm in the hall, but a closed bedroom door with an air conditioner running can change that, so we test it with the doors shut. Linking every alarm, so that the basement alarm sets off the bedroom alarms, is the single change that helps most in these houses.

Modular townhouses at Nehemiah Spring Creek: steel frames, concrete floors, no hollow plaster to fish through

Architectural Record's account of Nehemiah Spring Creek explains that Alexander Gorlin Architects designed the homes as 20-by-40-foot modules with steel frames, fiber cement siding and concrete floors, built in a factory, stacked two, three or four high on about 30 acres of former landfill, with raised front doors and stoops to the sidewalk. Phase 1 finished in June 2009 and Phase 2 in June 2011, with 248 homes complete and 330 more planned at the time. That construction is very different from the wood and plaster of older Brooklyn houses, and it changes how detection is installed.

A concrete floor between levels means cable cannot simply be dropped through a ceiling to the floor above, and we do not cut into a module's steel frame to make a path. In these homes we start by testing wireless interconnected alarms in place, sounding one at the far end of the house while someone listens in each bedroom, because steel and concrete can weaken a radio signal. Where wireless linking is reliable, it is usually the neatest answer. Where it is not, we use existing chases and closets between floors or a neat surface raceway, and we show you the route before drilling anything. The homes are narrow and tall, so the stair matters: an alarm at the top of each flight is where smoke from below will reach first.

Older two- to four-family houses: separate households sharing one stair

The semi-detached and attached houses of two to four families are the backbone of the neighborhood's rental housing. Many date from before the Second World War. Each floor is a separate household, the owner may live on one of them or nowhere in the building, and everyone uses one front door and one stair to get out. The cellar usually holds the boiler, the gas meters, the water heaters and storage, and sometimes an apartment. When the building has three or more units, it is a multiple dwelling under HPD's rules, with posted notices, records and the full set of owner duties. When it has one or two units and the owner lives elsewhere, HPD's CO and gas detector rules still reach it.

In these houses we plan two layers. Inside each apartment, the detectors that HPD requires: a smoke detector within fifteen feet of the entrance to each room used for sleeping, carbon monoxide where any fuel-burning appliance serves the building, and from January 1, 2027, natural gas detection in each room with a gas appliance. Then the shared spaces: the stair, the entry hall and the cellar, where a fire can grow while every household is asleep behind a closed apartment door. For the shared spaces, linked alarms that sound on every floor are usually the right answer in a building this size, and we write down which devices are the owner's to maintain and which the tenants are expected to test.

Basement apartments: legal, converted under the pilot, or neither

East New York and Cypress Hills were the only district where the city ran its Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program, which HPD describes as open to owner-occupied homes of one to three units, offering loans of up to $120,000 with technical help from the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation, and requiring each converted unit to receive a Certificate of Occupancy from the Department of Buildings. The program is now closed to applications. A unit that went through it has a legal path and paperwork. Many basement and cellar rooms in the neighborhood do not.

We do not decide whether a unit is legal, and an alarm does not make an illegal unit safe. A room below grade with one way out and no window big enough to climb through is a dangerous place to sleep, and the 2011 death on Drew Street happened in exactly that kind of space. What we do is protect whoever is sleeping there tonight: smoke and CO detection in the room, linked to the floors above so the household upstairs hears a fire below and the person below hears a fire upstairs. If the unit is not legal, we tell the owner plainly that the alarm is not the fix. The fix is either making it legal or not using it for sleeping.

Walk-up apartment buildings: in-unit detectors, halls, and a long HPD history

Walk-up buildings range from two stories, like the building at 683 Sheffield Avenue, to four or more, like 255 Pennsylvania Avenue, which News 12 described in August 2026 as a four-story complex of about two dozen apartments whose tenants rallied over leaking ceilings and broken electrical outlets, with nearly 90 HPD violations in a decade. In a building like that, we start the survey with the HPD record, because missing or faulty detector violations, self-closing door violations and electrical complaints all bear on the fire alarm plan, and we want to know what an inspector has already found.

The work then splits in two. In every apartment, the HPD detectors and their records. In the halls, stairs and cellar, whatever the building already has: sometimes a panel with hall detectors and bells, sometimes nothing at all. A like-for-like repair of an existing system is ordinary service work. Adding a building system where there was none, or moving a panel, is a design change that belongs to a registered design professional and has to go through FDNY review before installation. We tell the owner which one their building needs, in writing, before pricing anything.

Lottery and condo buildings since 2016: an engineered system with a records trail

The East New York Neighborhood Plan, approved by the City Council on April 20, 2016, brought a wave of new buildings. Brownstoner reported that The Highland at 268 Barbey Street, attached to the landmarked Empire State Dairy, has 14 stories and 320 apartments; that 2683 Atlantic Avenue has 13 stories and 229 apartments; and that the first building of the Innovative Urban Village at 30 Inspiration Lane has 15 stories and 386 apartments, with 1,975 apartments planned across eleven buildings. 6sqft reported that the Herkimer-Williams project at Broadway Junction, approved in December 2025, will bring four buildings with up to 1,000 affordable homes. None of these reports describe the buildings' fire alarm equipment, so we make no claims about it.

What we can say is how work in buildings of this kind goes. The base system was designed by an engineer, filed with the FDNY and accepted. Inside an apartment, residents and owners deal with the in-unit smoke and CO devices, and anything that touches the building system goes through the managing agent and the building's fire alarm company. For retail and community spaces on the ground floor, a tenant's devices tie into the landlord's system to approved drawings. Height also matters for these buildings: under the code summaries we rely on, buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor, need one-way voice communication, and a 14- or 15-story building is the kind where a designer checks that line.

NYCHA and converted public housing: what belongs to the landlord

East New York has several large public housing developments. The Louis Heaton Pink Houses, finished in 1959, have 22 eight-story buildings and about 1,500 apartments; Cypress Hills Houses was completed in 1955; and Boulevard Houses, Belmont-Sutter, Fiorentino Plaza, Linden Houses and Pennsylvania Avenue-Wortman Avenue have moved to private management under PACT. In all of them, the building fire alarm is the landlord's system. NYCHA's fact sheet on fire alarm replacements describes the scope as new control panels, communicators, strobes, magnetic door holders, manual pull stations and carbon monoxide, heat and smoke detectors, with construction phases of 24 to 30 months. A private installer does not work on those systems unless hired by the landlord, and residents should report problems through their development's management.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Gas Meter, End With the Bedroom Door Shut

The gas meter and every appliance it feeds. In an East New York house the first stop is the basement or cellar, at the meter. From there the technician follows the gas to every appliance it serves: furnace or boiler, water heater, dryer, range, and anything added later. Each one goes on the survey sheet by room, because from January 1, 2027 every room with a gas appliance in a rented home needs a natural gas detector, and because carbon monoxide planning starts at the same appliances.

Who sleeps below the first floor. The next question is whether anyone sleeps in the basement or cellar, tonight or occasionally: a grown child, a relative, a tenant, a guest on a sofa bed. The answer changes the plan more than anything else in the house. If someone does, that room gets smoke and CO detection linked to the floors above, and we look at how that person would get out.

Count the households, not the doorbells. In a two- to four-family house we count how many separate households actually live there, and compare it with what the owner expects. The Van Siclen Avenue house that burned in February 2026 was a two-family the Department of Buildings said had become five. If the count does not match, we say so, because detection planned for two households will miss the other three.

Close the door and listen. Last, with every bedroom door shut and any window air conditioner running, a second person sounds an alarm at the far end of the house. If it cannot be heard clearly in the farthest bedroom, the plan needs linking or another alarm, whatever the code minimum says.

January 1, 2027

Natural Gas Detectors Arrive in East New York Rentals on January 1, 2027: What Owners Have to Do Before Then

HPD's page on smoke, carbon monoxide and natural gas detectors states that natural gas detectors are required in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping, starting January 1, 2027. As of this page's review, that deadline is about seven weeks away. HPD describes where the devices belong: in rooms that contain a fuel-gas burning appliance, and in the area where the gas device is located. In an East New York two-family, that usually means the kitchen of each apartment and the cellar or basement where the boiler, water heater and dryer sit.

Who it reaches here. The requirement covers the buildings that make up most of East New York's rentals: walk-up multiple dwellings, two- to four-family houses with tenants, and one- or two-family houses rented out by owners who live somewhere else. It does not, on HPD's own wording, cover an owner-occupied one- or two-family home, although nothing stops an owner from installing gas detection anyway. If a building has no gas piping at all, HPD describes an exemption, and says more information on the exemption process will follow.

What to buy. In a Brownstoner forum thread about Local Law 157, posters who had been through it advised looking for the UL 1484 listing on the box, and a contractor in the thread added NFPA 715, mounting the detector high because natural gas rises, and keeping it away from the stove so cooking does not set it off. The same thread made a point owners often miss: smoke and CO alarms do not count toward the gas requirement. A gas detector is its own device, or a function in a listed combination device. HPD allows combined devices and sets the owner's maximum charge at $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device.

What to keep. HPD asks owners to give a notice to at least one adult occupant of each unit when devices are installed, to post a notice in the building entrance area of a multiple dwelling, and to keep the date the notice was posted, the date each device was installed and the expiration date of each device's useful life. A forum poster suggested keeping a receipt and a dated photo of each installation as well. When we install detectors, the device list we hand over has the install date and the expiration date for each one, by apartment and room, so that record exists from day one.

Why one visit is better than two. Getting into every apartment in a rented building is the slowest part of detector work. If a smoke detector is near the end of its life or a CO alarm is missing, it makes sense to fix that in the same visit as the gas detector, with one notice to the tenant and one updated record. We price smoke, CO and gas work together for that reason, apartment by apartment, using our per-unit landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit.

What the Fires Here Show

Sheffield Avenue, Miller Avenue, Van Siclen Avenue, Drew Street: Reading East New York's Fire Record

The fires below were reported in East New York by News 12 Brooklyn, Brooklyn Paper and The Real Deal. We describe them only as those outlets did. We do not know, and do not claim, how any of them was detected, whether any alarm worked, or what caused them unless the report said so. We include them because each one points to a specific choice in a fire alarm plan.

683 Sheffield Avenue, October 2023: violations on file before the fire. News 12 reported that a fire in a two-story apartment building near Linden Boulevard started on the first floor, that twelve people were injured, two of them seriously, and that neighbors said some tenants jumped from windows. The building had open HPD violations from June of that year for missing or faulty smoke detectors and a missing self-closing door in a first-floor apartment, and the outlet said it was unclear whether they played a role. The lesson for an owner is simple: a detector violation is not a paperwork item to clear eventually. It is a missing warning in a home where people sleep, and it should be fixed the week it is written.

Miller Avenue, January 6, 2025: three houses, three alarms, 2:15 a.m. News 12 reported a three-alarm fire at 187 Miller Avenue that neighbors said likely started at 185 Miller Avenue and spread to nearby homes, including 181. A firefighter and a 24-year-old man were seriously injured, and the cause was not immediately known. A fire at two in the morning in attached or closely spaced houses is the case linked alarms are made for: the people asleep upstairs need to hear the first alarm, wherever it is in the house, while there is still time to use the stair.

331 Van Siclen Avenue, February 2026: five households in a two-family. Brooklyn Paper reported that a fire in a two-story, two-family home displaced eight people and caused one minor injury, and that the Department of Buildings found the house had been illegally converted into a five-family dwelling. Detection planned for the house on paper would have assumed two households. Detection that protects people has to be planned for the households actually living there.

568 Drew Street, February 2011: trapped in a cellar. The Real Deal reported that a tenant died after being trapped in a cellar dwelling during what it called a small fire, and that the Department of Buildings said the building had been illegally converted to add a basement apartment. A detector would not have added a second way out of that room. It is the clearest local example of why we will install detection for whoever sleeps below grade, and also tell the owner, in writing, when the room itself is the problem.

Business fire alarm work in East New York happens in two very different settings: storefronts on the commercial avenues, usually with apartments above them, and the shops, yards and warehouses of the industrial zone between Atlantic and Sutter Avenues. The first is about protecting families upstairs from a fire in a closed store. The second is about large open spaces, machinery and stock.

A 120-foot-wide primary thoroughfare that forms the edge of the industrial zone and, since the rezoning, carries new towers such as 2683 Atlantic Avenue with space on their ground floors.

Pitkin Avenue

A commercial corridor named in the city's district needs assessment, with storefronts such as the beauty supply shop at 1603 Pitkin Avenue in mixed-use buildings.

Liberty Avenue

The other corridor named in that assessment, running east toward the Queens line past the former 75th Precinct station house at 484 Liberty Avenue.

Pennsylvania Avenue and Linden Boulevard

Two of the neighborhood's primary thoroughfares, with apartment buildings such as 255 Pennsylvania Avenue and the walk-ups near Sheffield Avenue along and between them.

Gateway Center

Compliance notes for East New York businesses and buildings

  • Plans come first. FDNY Fire Code Chapter 9 requires fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. The FDNY's own tips guide says a New York State registered professional engineer or architect determines the system type and prepares the plans, which are filed online with a TM-1 application and a Certificate of Occupancy, an approved Schedule A or a Letter of No Objection.
  • Know the fee and the clock. NYC's business portal lists the FDNY fire alarm application fee as $585. The FDNY guide says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks and the whole process from submission to final approval about twenty to twenty-four weeks, and that resubmitting within six months of an objection avoids a new filing fee. A store opening date should be planned around that, not the other way round.
  • Repair is not redesign. Replacing detectors, pull stations or a control panel with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, according to ASM Integrators' summary of the code. Moving or replacing a control panel with something different needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Small buildings have an exception. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published by UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer if each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. Whether a particular building qualifies depends on how it is actually built, and the two-family houses and small walk-ups here are where that question comes up most.
  • Carbon monoxide in businesses with a fire alarm. The FDNY's guidance says certain occupancies protected by a fire alarm system need CO detectors if they contain fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed parking garage or a loading dock. The groups named include restaurants and bars, places of worship, clinics and banks, and stores, markets and gas stations, and those detectors connect to the fire alarm system rather than standing alone.
  • Who does the work is regulated. Since December 1, 2020, FDNY Rule 3 RCNY §115-01 requires fire alarm work to be serviced by an FDNY-recognized installation, inspection, testing and servicing company, and the FDNY says required detectors are installed by DOB-licensed electrical contractors and programmed by an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness holder. We are a New York State licensed installer, license #12000287431. On work that needs a filed design, we work under the approved drawings alongside the design professional and the building's fire alarm company of record.
  • Inspection needs monitoring in place. The FDNY guide says the central station monitoring connection must be active before inspection is scheduled, the installation must be complete, and the panel must be free of alarm, supervisory and trouble conditions. Acceptance testing follows NFPA 72 as modified by Appendix Q of the NYC Building Code.
  • Records and changes. The city's guidance says owners must keep records of all checks and maintenance for at least three years, available to FDNY personnel, and must report all new alarm connections and changes to existing systems to the FDNY. It also says a monitored system that sends two or more unwarranted alarms in any three-month period is subject to enforcement.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in East New York: Questions Answered

These are the situations East New York homeowners, landlords, church boards and business owners bring to us. Each is answered fully here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Owner-occupied houses and the 2027 rule

Our family owns and lives in a Nehemiah house with a gas furnace, gas water heater and gas range. Does the January 2027 natural gas detector rule apply to us?

On HPD's own wording, no. HPD lists the natural gas detector requirement for multiple dwellings and for one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, where there is gas piping. An owner-occupied one-family house is not on that list, so January 1, 2027 is not a deadline for you. We still suggest gas detection in a Nehemiah house, for the same reason the rule exists elsewhere: the furnace, the water heater and often a dryer share the basement, the range is in the kitchen, and a leak in either place can build up unnoticed. If you install it, the Brownstoner thread on Local Law 157 gives the practical tips: a detector listed to UL 1484, mounted high, kept away from the stove, and one in each room with a gas appliance. The situation changes the day you rent the house, or rent part of it. At that point it is no longer owner-occupied in the rule's sense for that unit, and you pick up the owner's duties for smoke, carbon monoxide and gas detection, including the notice and the records HPD asks owners to keep.

Rented houses and the owner's paperwork

We own a two-family on a side street and live in Queens. Which of HPD's detector duties belong to us as owners who are not on site?

Most of them. On HPD's page, a one- or two-family home the owner does not live in needs carbon monoxide detection wherever there is a fossil-fuel burning device, and natural gas detection wherever there is gas piping from January 1, 2027. The owner provides and installs the devices, gives a notice to at least one adult occupant of each unit when they go in, keeps a record of the install date and the useful-life expiration date of each device, replaces devices at the end of their useful life, and replaces any that are missing or inoperable before a new tenant moves in if the last tenant did not. HPD's page frames the one- and two-family rules around CO and gas, but a working smoke alarm is still the device most likely to wake a household, so owners who are installing CO and gas detection usually have the smoke alarms checked and replaced in the same visit. Because you are not there to notice a dead alarm, we leave each tenant a short sheet on how to test the devices monthly, and we give you the device list by apartment and room so you can check it at each lease renewal.

A tenant's child pulled the smoke alarm off the bedroom ceiling and it is broken. Can we charge the tenant for the replacement?

HPD's rules allow it in a Class A building or a rented private home, within limits. HPD says tenants are responsible for replacing a detector that is stolen, removed, missing or inoperable during their occupancy, and that the owner's charge for an installation caused by the tenant's failure to maintain, loss or damage follows the same schedule as a new installation: $25 for a single smoke, CO or gas detector, $50 for a combined two-function device and $75 for a three-function device in a Class A building, with the tenant given one year from installation to pay. Tenants in Class B buildings do not reimburse owners. Two cautions matter. First, the owner stays responsible for the building having working detectors, so replace the alarm now and settle the charge afterward, rather than leaving a bedroom unprotected while you discuss it. Second, JustFix notes that unpaid detector charges cannot be grounds for eviction. We write the replacement on the device list with its date, which gives you a record either way.

Our three-family has two apartments upstairs and one on the parlor floor. Is it a multiple dwelling for HPD's detector rules, or a private house?

With three apartments, it is a multiple dwelling. HPD's detector page treats multiple dwellings, the Class A and Class B buildings, separately from one- and two-family homes, and the difference shows up in your duties. In a multiple dwelling, every dwelling unit needs at least one approved, working smoke detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room used for sleeping, CO detection in every unit if the building has a fossil-fuel burning device, natural gas detection from January 1, 2027 where there is gas piping, a notice posted in the building's entrance area as well as one given to an adult in each unit, and the dates of posting, installation and expiration kept on file. JustFix adds that in buildings of three or more units, failing to keep self-closing apartment and common-area doors working is a Class C violation, the most serious class. For a three-family with a shared stair, those doors and the detectors work together, and we check both on the survey.

Churches, stores and the industrial zone

Our congregation worships in a converted storefront on Pennsylvania Avenue with a gas boiler in the cellar. Does the FDNY's carbon monoxide rule for places of worship reach us?

It depends first on whether the building has a fire alarm system. The FDNY's guidance on smoke and carbon monoxide alarms says the CO requirement it describes applies to certain occupancies protected by a fire alarm system, places of worship among them, when the space contains fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed parking garage or a loading dock, with a deadline of July 1, 2021. If your storefront has a building fire alarm, the gas boiler puts you in that group, and the CO detectors have to connect to the fire alarm system, programmed by an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness holder, rather than being plug-in alarms on a wall. If the storefront has no fire alarm system, that rule as written does not apply, but the boiler still produces carbon monoxide, and a congregation that gathers for long services and evening programs should have CO detection near the boiler room and in the worship space. Whether a storefront church needs a fire alarm system at all depends on its size, occupant load and construction, which is a design professional's call. We can tell you which question you are facing after one visit.

Our discount store's back room has a loading dock but no gas appliances at all. Can a loading dock alone put us under the FDNY's CO requirement?

Yes, if the store is protected by a fire alarm system. The FDNY lists three conditions that bring an occupancy with a fire alarm under its CO detector requirement: fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed parking garage, or a loading dock. A loading dock is on the list because delivery trucks idle at it, and their exhaust can drift into the store when the dock door is open. Stores and markets are among the mercantile occupancies named. So an all-electric store with a dock can still need CO detectors tied into its fire alarm, placed where the design calls for them near the dock and the stockroom. In East New York this comes up most along Atlantic Avenue and at the edges of the industrial zone, where stores take deliveries from large trucks. If your store has no fire alarm system, the rule as written does not reach it, though detection by the dock is still sensible.

FDNY filing and inspection

The FDNY sent objections on our engineer's fire alarm plans for a Liberty Avenue building. Do we pay the filing fee again when the plans go back?

Not if the corrected plans go back within six months. The FDNY's tips guide for fire alarm approval says a filing fee is not required when plans are resubmitted within six months of the objection date, and it tells applicants to comply with all objections before resubmitting, since corrected plans go through another review. The guide describes the process: the FDNY emails when plans are disapproved, with instructions for viewing the objections, and the corrected plans are uploaded for review again. Because the initial review alone can take up to eight weeks, an objection that sits on a desk can push a project months past its target date. Your engineer answers the objections. What we can do in the meantime is survey the building, confirm cable routes and device locations against the drawings, and be ready to start the moment approved plans come back, so the objection costs you time on paper only.

Our store's monitoring contract is still being negotiated. Can the FDNY inspection of the new system be scheduled anyway?

No. The FDNY guide says that before you schedule an inspection, the central station monitoring connection with the approved monitoring center must be active, and it asks for a copy of the monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60 application. It also requires that the installation is 100 percent complete, that the panel is permanently powered and free of alarm, supervisory or trouble conditions, and that any sprinkler system is working. The inspection itself is requested with form B-45, and the guide lists what to bring to the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn. So the monitoring contract belongs early on the schedule, not at the end. For businesses that use monitoring through our partners, the rate is $30 a month with a keypad or $45 a month with the app, and we can have the account set up while the installation is being finished.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in East New York

Generic answers from AI tools and national guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for an average American house. These shortcuts mislead East New York owners most.

"East New York is mostly public housing, so fire alarms are NYCHA's job"

NYCHA is a real presence here, with the Pink Houses, Cypress Hills Houses and others, but HPD's housing plan puts NYCHA at about 10 percent of the district's housing. More than half is unregulated, and about 60 percent of all units are in buildings of fewer than six apartments.

Those small buildings are owned by thousands of private owners, and HPD's detector duties fall on each of them, not on any agency. An owner of a rented two-family carries the CO and gas detector rules personally.

So the typical East New York fire alarm job is not a housing authority contract. It is one owner, one house, two or three apartments and a cellar, and a list of devices that has to be right.

"Every building with tenants needs a full fire alarm panel"

Many do not. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published on UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard.

What every rented home does need are the HPD detectors: smoke within fifteen feet of each bedroom entrance in a multiple dwelling, CO where fuel burns, gas detection from 2027. Those are household devices, not a panel.

Selling a panel to a building that does not need one wastes money. Missing the cases that do, such as a store or church added to a building, is worse. We sort that out before pricing anything.

"There is plenty of time before the natural gas detector rule"

There is not. HPD's requirement starts January 1, 2027, about seven weeks after this page was reviewed, and a forum poster in the Local Law 157 thread said they expected no further extension.

For a landlord with several apartments, the slow part is getting into every unit, giving each tenant notice and recording each device. That takes longer than buying the detectors.

Owners who start now can combine the gas detectors with any smoke and CO replacements in one visit per apartment, which is cheaper and easier on tenants than two rounds.

"Your combination smoke and CO alarm already covers gas leaks"

It does not. Carbon monoxide is a product of burning fuel. Natural gas is the fuel itself, leaking before it burns. A CO sensor is not built to detect it.

Posters in the Brownstoner Local Law 157 thread were clear that smoke and CO alarms do not count toward the gas requirement, and that the detector should carry a UL 1484 listing.

HPD does allow combined devices, and caps the owner's charge for a three-function smoke, CO and gas device at $75. A combination unit works only if gas detection is actually one of its listed functions.

"Any electrician can put in a building fire alarm"

Not in New York City. The FDNY says a registered professional engineer or architect designs a building system, plans are filed and approved before installation, and since December 1, 2020 fire alarm work must be serviced by an FDNY-recognized company.

The FDNY also says required detectors are installed by DOB-licensed electrical contractors and programmed by an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness holder, and an S97 or S98 holder must be present at the inspection.

Household alarms in a two-family are a different, simpler job. Knowing which of the two a building needs is the first thing a good installer should tell you.

"FDNY approval takes a couple of weeks"

The FDNY's guide says initial plan review alone can take up to eight weeks, and estimates twenty to twenty-four weeks from submission to final approval.

The monitoring connection must be live before the inspection can be scheduled, and objections send plans back for another review, though a resubmission within six months avoids a new filing fee.

Anyone opening a store, a clinic or a church space that needs a filed system should start the design months before the planned opening, not weeks.

"Landlords can charge tenants whatever detectors cost"

HPD sets the limits: $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device, for Class A tenants, who get one year to pay.

Class B tenants do not reimburse owners at all, and HPD lists a $25 charge per CO or gas detector for tenants of private dwellings.

JustFix adds that unpaid detector charges cannot lead to eviction, and the owner stays responsible for installing the devices either way.

FAQ

East New York Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Can I get free smoke alarms from the FDNY before paying anyone to install them?
+

Yes. The FDNY's Get Alarmed NYC program, run with the FDNY Foundation and the American Red Cross, provides and installs free smoke alarms for residents of all five boroughs who request an appointment online. The FDNY cites the NFPA estimate that working smoke alarms cut the risk of dying in a home fire by about 60 percent. It is a good start for a household. Owners of rented buildings still carry HPD's duties for CO, gas detection and records.

We saw $585 twice, once as an FDNY fee and once as your service visit. Are they the same charge?
+

No, they only share a number. The city's business portal lists $585 as the FDNY fire alarm application fee, paid to the city when a filed system's plans are submitted. Our $585 is a service visit outside warranty: $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum. A household alarm job pays neither, and a filed system may involve the FDNY fee, the design professional's fee and our installation price, each quoted separately.

What should the box say when we buy natural gas detectors for our tenants' apartments?
+

Look for the UL 1484 listing, which posters in a Brownstoner thread on Local Law 157 named as the thing to check, and a contractor there also mentioned NFPA 715. The same thread said plug-in, sealed ten-year battery and hardwired models can all qualify. Mount it high, since natural gas rises, keep it away from the stove, and put one in each room with a gas appliance.

Our tenant has not paid the $25 detector charge after ten months. Can we remove the detector or take it to housing court?
+

Do not remove it. HPD gives Class A tenants one year from installation to pay the charge, so ten months is still inside the window, and the owner stays responsible for the building having working detectors. JustFix notes that unpaid detector charges cannot be grounds for eviction. Keep the device in place, keep the install date on record, and collect the charge as an ordinary debt if it goes unpaid.

Our last tenant moved out and took the carbon monoxide alarm. Who replaces it, and by when?
+

The owner, before the new tenant moves in. HPD says owners must replace devices that are stolen, removed, missing or inoperable before a new tenant takes occupancy, if the previous tenant did not replace them. Treat every turnover as a detector check: smoke, CO and, from January 1, 2027, natural gas, with new install dates written into your records and a notice given to the incoming tenant.

Our monitored store alarm sent the fire department out twice this fall with no fire. Are we in trouble with the FDNY?
+

Possibly. The FDNY's guidance says a monitored system that transmits two or more unwarranted alarms in any three-month period is unlawful and subject to enforcement. The fix is finding the cause, which is usually a detector in the wrong place for the room, dust, steam, a failing device or a wiring fault. We read the panel's history, test the suspect devices and correct the cause, then record what was done.

Can HPD's Home Improvement Program loan help pay for new alarms in our East New York house?
+

Ask HPD directly. Its East New York housing plan describes the Home Improvement Program as offering loans of up to $30,000, at rates as low as 2.5 percent, to low-income owner-occupants of one- to four-family homes for repairs, but the plan does not list which repairs qualify. If alarms are part of a larger repair the loan covers, we can provide a written, itemized price for the detection work to include in the application.

Does HPD let an owner charge more for one combined smoke, CO and gas device than for three separate detectors?
+

No. HPD's schedule for Class A tenants is $25 per single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device, so one combined unit costs the tenant the same $75 as three separate devices. The combined unit means fewer things on the ceiling. It only counts for gas if gas detection is one of its listed functions, and it has to be placed where every function it covers is required.

We are adding one detector to our store's existing fire alarm panel. Does the FDNY need to hear about it?
+

Yes. The city's fire alarm guidance says owners must report all new alarm connections and changes to existing fire alarm systems to the FDNY. Swapping a failed device for an identical one is a repair, but adding a device changes the system, and depending on the building it may need a design professional and an amended filing. We tell you which applies before connecting anything.

NYCHA is replacing the fire alarm in our development. What should residents expect while the work goes on?
+

NYCHA's fact sheet describes new control panels, communicators, strobes, magnetic door holders, pull stations and smoke, heat and CO detectors, with planning, design and procurement phases of 6 to 12 months each and construction of 24 to 30 months. Residents get advance notice of power or system outages, plastic barriers around work areas, drilling and test noise, and training on the new system. That work is done by NYCHA's contractors.

Do we have to tell our tenant when a new detector goes into their apartment in our two-family?
+

Yes. HPD asks owners to give a notice to at least one adult occupant of each unit when devices are installed, and to keep the date of each installation and the end of each device's useful life. Posting a notice in the building entrance applies to multiple dwellings, not to a two-family. We leave a short written notice and a testing sheet in each apartment we work in.
