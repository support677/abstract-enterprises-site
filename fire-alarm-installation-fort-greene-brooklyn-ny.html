Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Fort Greene's Architecture

Seven kinds of building cover nearly every Fort Greene fire alarm call. They differ in height, in how many households share a way out, and above all in how much hidden space a fire can use before anyone notices it. Those things set the plan; the brand of detector is the last decision, not the first.

The one-family brownstone

Three or four floors over a cellar, an open stair rising through the middle of the house, and a kitchen that has often moved to the garden floor.

The rowhouse split into flats

Two to four apartments sharing one stair, each altered at a different time, with plumbing stacked in new chases through old floors.

The small brownstone co-op

Shareholders who own the building together, like the South Portland Avenue co-op that took a Landmarks Conservancy loan for building-wide work in 2019.

The walk-up over a business

Prewar buildings on Myrtle, Fulton, DeKalb and Greene with a restaurant, bar or shop below, such as the buildings in the Greene Avenue and DeKalb Avenue fires.

The tower

From the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place to newer buildings on Fulton Street and Ashland Place, each with an engineered system from the start.

New infill inside the districts

New rowhouses approved at 352–360 Clermont Avenue, the 24-story building permitted at 130 St. Felix Street, and restorations such as the corner rowhouse at 54 Greene Avenue.

The one-family brownstone: a stair that works like a flue

In a single-family Fort Greene rowhouse, the stair is the spine of the house. It rises from the parlor floor or the garden floor to the top, often under a skylight or a hatch, and every bedroom opens onto it. That makes it the escape route and also the shortest path for smoke from a fire on a lower floor to reach the bedrooms. Kitchens in these houses frequently sit on the garden floor at the back, and many have been opened into a dining room, so cooking smoke and heat from a real kitchen fire take the same route upward. The plan therefore puts smoke detection on every level of the stair and outside every sleeping area, heat detection where a smoke detector would false-alarm, such as close to a garden-floor range, and carbon monoxide detection near the boiler and outside the bedrooms. Every device is linked so that one sounding starts the rest.

The rowhouse split into flats: several renovations stacked in one house

Most Fort Greene rowhouses are no longer one home. A garden apartment below, a duplex on the parlor and second floors, and a top-floor flat is a common arrangement, and three or four separate units is common too. Each apartment was usually created or renovated at a different time by a different contractor, so the walls between them hide a record of every job: new plumbing chases, abandoned flues, gaps around old gas lines, and dropped ceilings with open space above. Those are exactly the kinds of void spaces and pipe chases the FDNY described in the Greene Avenue fire. Inside each apartment, the plan is familiar: smoke detection outside sleeping areas and on each level of a duplex, carbon monoxide detection where fuel-burning equipment or a shared boiler calls for it. The difference in a divided house is the shared parts.

The stair, the halls and the cellar belong to the building, and a fire in any apartment threatens all of them. We place detection on every landing and in the cellar, and where household alarms are the right path, we link the shared-area devices to each apartment so a fire in the cellar or on the parlor landing reaches the top-floor tenants. HPD treats apartment and public-area doors that do not close on their own as a violation condition, and in a house with three or four flats those doors matter as much as the detectors, because a closed door holds back smoke while a family leaves. We also tell owners plainly where the house stands with the code. The 2022 Building Code's exception from a manual fire alarm system covers buildings of two stories or fewer under stated conditions, and a three- or four-story rowhouse is outside it; what the building actually needs then depends on its legal occupancy and unit count, which we read before pricing anything.

The small brownstone co-op: one building, several owners, one decision

Fort Greene has a number of small co-ops in converted rowhouses, where a handful of shareholders own the building through a corporation and decide together on building-wide work. Habitat Magazine's 2019 report on the South Portland Avenue co-op, which qualified for a Landmarks Conservancy Historic Properties Fund loan because it sits in a historic district, is a reminder that these boards do take on major work. Fire detection is a good fit for board-level planning, because the shared stair and cellar have to be done as one system and the apartments work far better when they are linked to it. For a co-op we write one plan for the whole building, show the board which devices sit in common areas and which sit in each apartment, and price the two parts separately so the board can decide how its own proprietary lease or house rules split the cost.

The walk-up over a business: the ground floor runs on different hours

Prewar walk-ups with a business at street level line Myrtle Avenue, Fulton Street, DeKalb Avenue and parts of Greene and Lafayette Avenues. The Greene Avenue fire of 2019 is the local case that shapes how we plan them: a fire believed to have started in a first-floor business, burning for a while and moving through void spaces and pipe chases to the third floor. At 219 DeKalb Avenue, smoke appeared through small gaps in the walls of a four-story building with a restaurant on the ground floor, and the cause appeared to be between the first and second floors. In both cases the space between the business and the first apartment is the critical zone: a ceiling cavity, often crowded with ductwork, pipes and old wiring, that nobody on either side can see into.

So in a mixed-use walk-up we cover three layers at once. The business gets detection suited to what it does, which in a kitchen usually means heat detection near cooking and smoke detection away from it. The residents' stair and halls get smoke detection on every landing. Each apartment gets its own detection near sleeping areas. Then we look hard at the space in between: where ducts and pipes rise from the business into the building, where an exhaust duct runs up the back, and where the ceiling cavity over the business can be reached. The ownership is usually split, with a commercial tenant holding the store's lease and keys and the owner holding the rest, so before a device goes into the business we put in writing who maintains it, who is called when it signals and who can open the space for a technician. Where the building's use requires a filed system, the same layers are met through the panel and its devices on approved drawings.

The tower: an engineered system, and a clear line around our role

The western and southern edges of Fort Greene hold its tall buildings. The Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place, 41 stories and 512 feet, was finished in 1929 and converted to condominiums between 2005 and 2007. Newer towers on Fulton Street and Ashland Place, including developments such as 300 Ashland Place and 810 Fulton Street, rise far higher than any rowhouse block. Buildings of this kind are designed with fire alarm systems from the first drawing, and many are tall enough for further requirements: according to a summary of a DOB buildings bulletin from SPARC, one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. The FDNY's Chapter 9 says design and installation documents for fire alarm systems go to the department for review and approval before installation.

New buildings and restorations inside the historic districts

The historic districts are not frozen. CityLand reported that LPC approved five new four-story rowhouses at 352–360 Clermont Avenue in 2019, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2020 that LPC permitted a 24-story building at 130 St. Felix Street on a street of mostly three- and four-story rowhouses, which led a community group to sue. Restorations continue too: the LPC heard a proposal on June 23, 2026 to restore a long-vacant corner rowhouse at 54 Greene Avenue and create three apartments in it, and in April 2026 the commission found that 158 South Oxford Street did not qualify for individual designation, with applications filed to replace it with a five-story residential building. For a new rowhouse or a gut restoration, the best moment to plan detection is while the walls are open, so devices can be hardwired and linked cleanly and every new penetration is sealed. For a house that will hold three apartments, that plan also has to anticipate the shared stair and the paperwork that comes with more households.

Public housing beside the rowhouses

The Walt Whitman Houses, completed in February 1944, have fifteen buildings of six and thirteen stories with 1,659 apartments, and the Ingersoll Houses next to them have buildings of six and eleven stories. Both are run by NYCHA, which maintains their systems and has carried out its own modernization work, including a 2009 renovation program and, in summer 2025, an exterior brick and façade upgrade at both developments. We mention them here because they are a large part of Fort Greene's housing and because one of the fires we cite, at 102 Cumberland Walk, was in the Whitman Houses. Fire alarm work in NYCHA buildings goes through NYCHA, and this page does not present us as working there.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Fort Greene House for the Spaces Between the Rooms

Look for the stacks. In a rowhouse that has been divided, the first thing a technician does after the walk-through is find the bathrooms and kitchens on each floor and see whether they line up. Where they stack, there is almost always a pipe chase running from cellar to roof, and that is a likely path for a fire to climb.

Open what can be opened. Access panels behind tubs, the door of a utility closet, the ceiling tile over a shop's back room, the scuttle to the cockloft. A flashlight and a few minutes at each one tell us more than the floor plan does.

Smell and listen. Old fires leave traces. Smoke staining around a ceiling fixture, a patched hole in a cellar ceiling, scorched joists. We note them and ask about them, because they point to where a building has burned before.

Count the sleepers, not the rooms. A room called an office on the plan may hold a bed. A cellar called storage may have a cot. Detection goes where people sleep.

Then the paper. Only after the walk do we compare what we saw with the Certificate of Occupancy, any HPD notices and the board's or owner's records. When they agree, the plan is simple. When they disagree, the owner hears that before a price is written.

Compliance notes for Fort Greene businesses and mixed-use buildings

  • Plans come before installation. The FDNY's Chapter 9 says design and installation documents for fire alarm systems must be submitted to the department for review and approval before installation.
  • A like-for-like swap is a repair; a panel change is not. Trade summaries from ASM Integrators explain that replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, while moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Height brings voice communication. SPARC's summary of a DOB bulletin says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. That reaches the towers, not the walk-ups.
  • Low buildings have a conditional exception. The 2022 Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system for dwelling units in buildings of two stories or fewer when units are separated by one-hour fire partitions and each has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. Most Fort Greene mixed-use buildings are taller than that.
  • The housing code covers the apartments upstairs. HPD lists missing smoke detectors, missing carbon monoxide detectors and doors that do not self-close among the conditions its inspectors cite whenever they find them.
  • Landmark districts govern the outside. In the Fort Greene and BAM Historic Districts, LPC requires a Certificate of Appropriateness for major alterations such as significant façade changes. We confirm any exterior device or conduit with the owner and the requirements before work starts.

We are a licensed low-voltage installer, not a law firm or a code consultant. These notes are how we read the public rules when sorting a building into the right kind of job, not legal advice.

Hidden Routes

Cocklofts, Pipe Chases and Gaps in the Wall: Planning Detection Around How Fort Greene Buildings Burn

A smoke detector on a ceiling responds to smoke that reaches it. That sounds obvious, but it explains why the fires described in Fort Greene's news reports are a planning problem. When a fire starts inside a wall, under a floor or in a ceiling cavity, the smoke goes wherever the hidden space lets it, and it may reach a living area somewhere far from where the fire is burning, or reach nowhere anyone is standing until the fire breaks out. This section takes the routes those reports name and explains what we do about each one. It is our practice, drawn from the public accounts; it is not a finding about any particular building.

The cockloft. A cockloft is the concealed space between the ceiling of the top floor and the roof. In many rowhouses and walk-ups it is low, unfinished, sometimes continuous along several buildings, and visited by nobody. The 464 Clinton Avenue fire ran from the third floor up through the sixth and into the cockloft. Once a fire is in that space it can travel along the roof line, and the top-floor ceiling below it hides it. We check whether a cockloft can be reached, usually through a scuttle or a hatch, and where it can, we place heat detection in it or immediately below the access point. Heat detectors respond to temperature and tolerate the dust, heat and cold of an unconditioned space far better than smoke detectors. The cockloft device is tied to the alarms near the top-floor bedrooms, or to the building panel, so a fire overhead sounds where people are.

The pipe chase. When bathrooms and kitchens are stacked floor over floor, the pipes run up a chase, an enclosed vertical channel, and the openings around those pipes are rarely sealed well in older buildings. The FDNY chief at the Greene Avenue fire described a fire traveling through void spaces and pipe chases to the third floor. A detector cannot see into a chase. What it can do is sit where the chase meets the living space: on the ceiling of a utility closet the chase passes through, at the top-floor landing above it, or in the cellar where it starts. We map the stacks during the survey and place devices at the openings, and when we find unsealed holes around pipes in a floor or ceiling, we tell the owner, because closing them is construction work for a contractor and it slows a fire in a way no detector can.

The gap in the wall. At 219 DeKalb Avenue, the restaurant's manager said smoke came out of small gaps in the walls and the cause appeared to be between the first and second floors. That is the classic picture of a fire in a floor-ceiling cavity: the smoke finds the weakest seams, around outlets, baseboards and pipe sleeves, and seeps into rooms in small amounts. A detector in the room may or may not catch smoke arriving that way, depending on how much and where. Our response is to treat the space between a business and the first apartment as a zone in its own right. Where the cavity can be reached through an access panel or a removable ceiling in the business, we consider detection there, matched to the conditions in it. We also place detection on the first residential floor closest to where the business's ducts and pipes rise, and we look for and report the gaps.

The top story. The 354 Myrtle Avenue fire began on the top story of a six-story building. Top floors collect heat and smoke from below, sit directly under the cockloft, and are farthest from the street door. In a walk-up we make the top-floor hall a priority, with smoke detection at the landing and near the roof door, and we check that the top-floor apartments' alarms can be heard with bedroom doors shut. The roof door itself matters: if it is chained or blocked, tell the owner, because the roof can be a way out.

The ground floor. Two of the fires above, on Greene Avenue and at 219 DeKalb Avenue, began in or near a business on the first floor. A fire there can burn after closing with nobody inside, and it can climb into the building through the routes already described before it is seen from the street. Detection in the business, linked to the building where the occupancy allows or reporting to the building's panel where one is required, is the most important single step in a mixed-use walk-up. Monitoring through our partners adds a call to a responder when nobody is in the space at all.

Batteries and Fuel

Three 2026 Fires, One Common Thread: Batteries, Propane and the Spaces Behind Fort Greene Buildings

Three of the fires reported in and around Fort Greene in 2026 involved lithium-ion batteries, and one of them also involved a propane tank. They happened in three very different kinds of place: an apartment in a 13-story residential building, a shipping container behind a storage facility, and the rear stepback of a commercial building. Read together, they say something about where detection planning has to reach on these blocks.

Inside a tall building. The May 29 fire at 68 Cumberland Walk was on the eighth floor of a 13-story building; 21 FDNY units and 79 fire and EMS personnel responded and a hazmat unit was requested because a lithium-ion battery was involved. A battery fire can start fast and produce heavy smoke, and in an apartment building the people most exposed are those in the unit and the hall outside it. Fire Alarm Installation in Fort Greene: Questions Answered

These are the questions Fort Greene owners, co-op boards, tenants and business operators bring to us: fire moving through hidden space, the code lines that old rowhouses cross, batteries and fuel, the firehouse down the street, and rooms that fill with an audience. Fire that moves where nobody can see it

The 464 Clinton Avenue fire climbed from the third floor through the sixth and into the cockloft. In a building like ours, which spaces between floors should detection reach?

The places where hidden vertical routes open into the building, plus the top of the building where they end. In a walk-up or a divided rowhouse those routes are usually the stair itself, any pipe chase running through stacked kitchens and bathrooms, utility closets that line up floor over floor, and the cockloft above the top-floor ceiling. amNY reported that the February fire at 464 Clinton Avenue ran vertically from the third floor through the sixth and into the cockloft, affecting 24 apartments; the report does not say how the alarms performed, and we make no claim about them. For a plan, we put smoke detection on every landing, detection in or at the doors of closets a chase passes through, and heat detection in the cockloft or just below its access hatch, tied to the top-floor alarms or the panel. The cellar, where most chases begin, gets detection too. On the survey we draw the stacks on a simple floor-by-floor sketch, so the owner can see why each device is where it is.

An FDNY chief said the Greene Avenue fire had probably burned for a while inside void spaces. Can any detector actually sense a fire inside a wall?

Not reliably, and anyone who promises otherwise is overselling. Standard smoke and heat detectors respond to smoke or heat that reaches the device, and a fire inside a closed wall or floor cavity may not send enough of either into a room until it has grown. ABC7's report on the 2019 Greene Avenue fire quotes the FDNY chief saying it had probably been burning for a while and traveled through void spaces and pipe chases to the third floor. What detection can do is catch that fire at the earliest point it shows itself: at the top of a chase, in a closet it passes through, in an accessible cavity over a business, or in the cockloft where hidden fires often end up. Placing devices at those points is the practical answer. The other answer is construction rather than alarms: sealing the gaps around pipes and wiring where they pass through floors, which slows a hidden fire and keeps smoke out of rooms. We point out the openings we find so a contractor can close them, and we seal our own penetrations as we go.

At 219 DeKalb Avenue smoke came out of small gaps in the walls. If we ever smell smoke at home but every detector stays silent, what should we do?

Call 911 first and get out; do not wait for an alarm to confirm what your nose is telling you. Patch reported that at 219 DeKalb Avenue, a four-story building with a restaurant on the ground floor, the manager said smoke came from small gaps in the walls and the cause appeared to be between the first and second floors. Smoke arriving through seams can be too thin, or too far from a detector, to set it off at first, and a fire in a cavity can be well established before it shows. Once everyone is safe, tell us when and where you smelled it, because that tells a technician a lot: which wall, which floor, what sits on the other side or below. We will check whether the detectors in that area are working and well placed, look for a cavity or chase nearby that should be covered, and press-test every linked device. A silent detector during a real smell of smoke is also worth reporting to the owner in writing, so the building's record shows it.

Rowhouses, co-ops and the lines old buildings cross in the code

Our rowhouse has four floors and three apartments, and people keep mentioning a two-story exception. Does it apply to us?

No, and it is worth being clear about what that does and does not mean. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system for dwelling units in buildings of two stories or fewer, provided each unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, an exit court or a yard. A four-story house is outside that exception by its height alone, before the other conditions come into it. Falling outside the exception does not automatically mean your house needs a building panel with pull stations; what it needs depends on its legal occupancy and number of units, and that is read from the Certificate of Occupancy or the building's records and, where a filed system is in question, by a registered design professional. Whatever the answer on paper, a three-apartment house needs linked detection in every unit and on every landing, carbon monoxide detection where fuel is burned, and hall and apartment doors that close on their own, which HPD cites when they do not. We tell you which path the house is on before we price it.

We are a four-unit co-op in a brownstone, similar to the one on South Portland Avenue. How should the board split the cost between the stair and the apartments?

The split is the board's to decide under its own proprietary lease and house rules, but we make it easy by pricing the two parts separately. Common areas, meaning the stair, the landings, the halls, the cellar and any cockloft or roof access, form one building-wide system that protects every shareholder equally, and most boards treat it as a building expense. The devices inside each apartment sit in one shareholder's space and serve that household first, although linking them to the common-area system protects everyone. Our written quote lists every device by location and labels it as common or apartment, using our Brooklyn list: per-unit alarm setup for landlords and boards runs $400 to $800, and a custom hardwired system for the whole building runs $2,000 to $6,000 depending on what is there and how it is wired. Habitat Magazine's report on the South Portland Avenue co-op shows small Fort Greene boards do take on building-wide work; with fire detection, doing the whole building in one coordinated job is cheaper and safer than each shareholder buying alarms separately.

An HPD inspector came about a heat complaint and noticed an apartment had no smoke detector. Will the inspector look at the rest of the building too?

HPD says that on every inspection, its inspector issues violations for a short list of conditions whenever they are found, and missing smoke detectors and missing carbon monoxide detectors are both on that list, along with apartment or public-area doors that do not close on their own. HPD also says its inspections come mainly in response to complaints received through 311 and Housing Court. So a visit about one issue can produce a violation about another if the inspector sees it, in the apartment or the public areas they pass through. The practical response is to fix the cited condition and check the rest of the building for the same thing, because a second complaint from another apartment brings another visit. We can survey every unit and common area, replace or add detectors where they are missing, and give the owner a written, room-by-room record of what is installed and where, which helps when correcting a violation. How to certify the correction with HPD is a paperwork step for the owner; we provide what the work shows.

Two of our tenants pay rent through Section 8. Do the smoke alarms in their apartments have to be a particular type?

Yes. HPD's inspections page says that under the NSPIRE standard, effective December 29, 2024, Section 8 inspections require smoke detectors to be hardwired or to have sealed ten-year batteries. A detector that runs on a replaceable nine-volt or AA battery may not satisfy that inspection even if it works. For a Fort Greene rowhouse with a mix of subsidized and market-rate tenants, the simplest approach is usually to bring every apartment up to the same standard, because it makes maintenance uniform and avoids a failed inspection when a tenancy changes. Where walls are open or the wiring path is easy, hardwired alarms with battery backup, linked across the apartment, are the stronger choice; where opening plaster is not practical, sealed ten-year units are the alternative the standard allows. We note each unit's detector type and installation date on the handover map, so the owner can show an inspector exactly what is in each apartment.

Batteries, fuel and the back of the building

We rent in one of the new towers on Fulton Street, and the building has its own fire alarm system. What is left for us to take care of inside our apartment?

Usually less than in a rowhouse, but not nothing. A tower's base system, its panel, hall detectors, notification devices and, in buildings over 125 feet, voice communication, is designed by an engineer, filed with the FDNY and maintained by the building's contractor. Inside your apartment, the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are generally the owner's responsibility to provide, and your part is to keep them working: test them, report a chirp or a missing device to management in writing, and never cover or remove them. The May 29 fire at 68 Cumberland Walk, on the eighth floor of a 13-story building, involved a lithium-ion battery, which is a reminder that the most common fire risks in a modern apartment are the ones residents bring in. Charge e-bikes, scooters and large batteries away from the door you would leave by, and do not charge them overnight next to where you sleep. Because the in-unit devices and the building system are under management's control, any change to them goes through the building, and we do not alter a tower's system at a tenant's request.

The 245 Willoughby Street fire started in a rear stepback where batteries and a propane tank were stored. We keep a propane heater and supplies on our restaurant's back terrace. Does detection belong out there?

Not in the open air, but the building next to it needs attention. Smoke detectors are made for indoor conditions and will either fail or false-alarm in rain, wind and cold, so they do not go on an open terrace or stepback. Brooklyn Paper reported that the September 25 fire at 245 Willoughby Street started in the rear stepback and that hazmat crews removed batteries and a 40-pound propane tank. A fire on a terrace attacks the building through its doors and windows, so we protect the room behind the terrace door, the windows opening onto it and the floor above it, with detection suited to each, and in a restaurant we tie those devices to the business's system and, if you want it, to partner monitoring. A covered, enclosed storage room off the terrace is a different case and can usually be detected. How much propane may be stored, and where, is a fire code and insurance question rather than an alarm question, and we suggest you ask your insurer and the FDNY about it.

Firefighters had to cut the roll-down gates to reach the Carlton Avenue battery fire. Our shop's gate is down all night. Should the alarm plan deal with that?

Yes, in two ways. The first is notification: a fire behind a closed gate at 3 a.m. is a fire nobody inside will hear about, so detection in the shop should reach someone outside it. Central station monitoring through our partners, at $19.99 a month, sends signals to a monitoring center and on to your call list. The second is access, which is about people rather than devices. Before the system goes live, we put in writing who is on the call list, in what order, how quickly each person can get to the shop with keys, and what the monitoring center should know about the building, such as whether apartments sit above and which door the residents use. News 12 Brooklyn and Brooklyn Paper reported that at 1 Carlton Avenue the fire was in a shipping container behind the storage facility and that firefighters cut through the roll-down gates to get to it. Firefighters will always do what they need to in an emergency; a current call list and a keyholder who answers mean the owner learns about it in minutes, not when the morning shift arrives.

Neighbors, firehouses and audiences

Engine 210 is a few blocks away on Carlton Avenue. With a firehouse that close, can we count on a quick response instead of a better alarm?

A nearby firehouse shortens the time between a call and an engine at your door. It does nothing about the time between a fire starting and somebody making that call, and in Fort Greene's older buildings that first stretch can be long. Engine 210 is housed at 160 Carlton Avenue, and Engine 207, Ladder 110 and Battalion 31 work out of 172 Tillary Street, so the neighborhood is well served. Detection is what ends that hidden period: it wakes the household, starts the evacuation down a shared stair, and prompts the call. In a building with partner monitoring, it also gets a call to a responder when nobody is home. The fastest engine company in Brooklyn still depends on somebody noticing the fire, and the purpose of a well-placed alarm is to make that happen sooner.

We run a small gallery in a storefront near BAM and host evening openings that fill the room. Does a crowd change the fire alarm we need?

It can. A room's legal occupancy, meaning the use and the number of people it is approved for, is what the fire code works from, and a space approved as a shop may be classified differently once it is regularly used for gatherings. That is a question for the Certificate of Occupancy and, where a change is involved, a registered design professional; we do not decide classifications. What we can do is look at the space as it is really used: where people stand during an opening, how many exits there are and whether they are marked and clear, what sits in the back room, and whether the apartments above would be warned of a fire during an event. For many small galleries the right answer is detection suited to the room, linked or reported to the building, with clear signals that people unfamiliar with the space will notice. FAQ

Fort Greene Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

What is the difference between a cockloft and an attic, and why does it matter for detection?
+

An attic is usually tall enough to stand in or store things in, while a cockloft is a shallow concealed space between the top-floor ceiling and the roof that nobody normally enters. In many Fort Greene rowhouses and walk-ups the space above the top floor is a cockloft. It matters because a fire can travel through it unseen, as amNY reported happened at 464 Clinton Avenue in February 2026, so where a cockloft can be reached we place heat detection in it or right below its access point and link it to the alarms below.

Our walk-up's panel shows a trouble light but nothing is sounding. Is the building still protected?
+

Possibly only in part. A trouble signal means the panel has found a fault, such as a broken circuit, a missing device, a failed battery or lost power, and some devices may not be able to report a fire until it is fixed. Do not silence it and forget it. Note what the display says, take a photo, and call us; until it is repaired, treat the affected area as unprotected and make sure residents have working alarms in their apartments.

Can a building panel tell the super which floor or apartment a signal came from?
+

Yes, if it is set up that way. An addressable panel identifies each device individually, and a conventional panel reports by zone, which can be a floor or a group of rooms. Either way the display is only useful if the labels match the building, so we program and label every device or zone with its floor and location and leave a printed map at the panel that matches it.

Our parlor floor has very high ceilings. Does that change the detector or how it is tested?
+

It changes placement and maintenance more than the device. Smoke from a small fire can spread out before reaching a very high ceiling, so we follow the manufacturer's placement limits and sometimes add a device rather than rely on one in the center of a large room. We also think about who will test it: if a ladder is needed every time, hardwired alarms with battery backup or sealed ten-year units save the household from risky climbs.

Our linked alarms are from a brand that no longer makes that model. Do we have to replace all of them?
+

Often, yes, because interconnected alarms generally only link reliably with compatible models from the same maker. Mixing in a different brand can leave one alarm sounding while the others stay silent. On the survey we check the labels and dates; if replacements that link with your set are still available and the units are within their service life, we can replace selectively, and if not, we replace the set so every alarm talks to every other one.

What happens to the old detectors you take down?
+

We remove them, cap and label any wiring left behind, and dispose of the old units unless you ask to keep them. Some older smoke detectors carry disposal instructions on their labels, and we follow those. We also note on the handover map which locations were changed, so nobody later wonders why there is a patched spot on a ceiling.

Why does your technician ask for access to the roof hatch or bulkhead on a survey?
+

Because the top of a Fort Greene building is where hidden fires tend to end up. The hatch or bulkhead often gives the only view into the cockloft, and the roof door is also part of the way out. Please have the key or padlock code ready, and let us know if the hatch has been sealed or painted shut so we can plan for it.

How much notice do tenants need before you work inside their apartments?
+

The notice owners must give is set by the lease and housing rules, which the owner should follow. For our part, we give the owner or board a written schedule showing which apartments we need on which day and roughly how long each takes, plus a short note they can pass to residents explaining what will be installed and when test sounds will go off. If a tenant cannot give access that day, we finish everything else and return for that unit.

How do we stop tenants or contractors from taking detectors down?
+

Labels and the right devices help most. We label each device with its location and a short line saying it is a fire safety device that must not be removed, and in apartments where nuisance alarms led to removals before, we choose devices and placements that cut false alarms, such as heat detection near a range. Owners should also tell renovation contractors in writing that detectors must stay in service or be replaced the same day.

After an alarm goes off in our building, what should we write down?
+

The date and time, which device or zone sounded, what anyone saw or smelled, whether the fire department came, and what the panel display said if there is one. A photo of the display is ideal. That record tells a technician whether it was a real event, a nuisance alarm from cooking or steam, or a device fault, and patterns across several entries usually point straight to the cause.

Our stair rises to a skylight at the top of the house. Where should the top detector go?
+

Near the top of the stair, but not right in the skylight well. Hot air can collect in a skylight shaft and keep smoke away from a device mounted inside it, and direct sun can bother some detectors. We usually place the top-landing device on the ceiling just outside the well, where smoke rising up the stair will reach it, and check the result with test smoke before we leave.

Why does your written quote list every device by its location?
+

So the owner, board or manager can see exactly what they are paying for and why each device is where it is. In a divided rowhouse or co-op it also makes it easy to split common-area costs from apartment costs, and after installation the same list becomes the handover map, matching the label on each device.
