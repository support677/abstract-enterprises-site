The Neighborhood

Why Mount Hope Properties Need Security Cameras That Catch the Small Thefts

Mount Hope sits on high ground in the west-central Bronx, roughly between Jerome Avenue and Webster Avenue, with Burnside Avenue to the north and the Cross Bronx Expressway to the south. Bronx Community Board 5 notes that the name was never formally mapped and may simply have been a local name for the elevation. It is a neighborhood of apartment houses. By our count of the city's PLUTO tax-lot records across the ten census tracts that make up the Mount Hope area, there are about 865 residential lots holding roughly 20,612 homes, and 727 of those lots carry buildings from before 1946. Most of those are five- and six-story brick apartment houses with a street door, a vestibule, a bank of mailboxes, one or two stairs and a cellar. That shape decides where a camera does the most good.

When people call us from Mount Hope, the problem they describe is rarely dramatic. It is a tenant whose checks keep disappearing from the mail panel, a super who finds the cellar door propped open in the morning, a store owner who suspects a worker or a regular customer, a shop on Jerome Avenue that lost a set of tools from a car left at the curb. The NYPD's own numbers line up with that. The CompStat report for the 46th Precinct for the week of November 2 to 8, 2026 shows 1,029 petit larceny complaints year to date against 845 at the same point in 2025, a 21.8 percent rise. Robbery was up 8.5 percent, to 396, and burglary up 5.7 percent, to 185. Grand larceny of autos went the other way, down 32.9 percent, from 216 to 145. The precinct covers four neighborhoods, so these numbers describe a wider area than Mount Hope alone, but they describe the same kind of streets.

Small thefts are exactly where cameras earn their keep, and also where they most often fail. A mail thief works for a few seconds, close to the panel, with his back to a camera mounted high in the corner of the lobby. A package taken from a vestibule shows up as a cap brim and a shoulder if the camera is on the ceiling looking straight down. A shoplifter at a crowded counter on East Tremont Avenue is a blur if the camera behind the register is set for a wide view of the whole store. In September 2024, tenants on Shakespeare Avenue in neighboring Mount Eden told News 12 that their eight-box mail panel could be opened by anyone, and the station reported that "the lack of security cameras in the building has made it difficult to file a police report and catch the culprit." That sentence describes a lot of buildings on our side of Jerome Avenue too. The fix is not more cameras. It is the right camera at the right height, looking at hands and faces instead of floors.

Mount Hope is also changing quickly, and that changes camera work. The 2018 Jerome Avenue rezoning opened the corridor to housing, and new buildings have been permitted along Jerome and Walton avenues, Morris Avenue and Mount Hope Place, some of them twelve, thirteen and fifteen stories tall. A Bronx Times lottery notice for 66 East 177th Street, a twelve-story building with 55 homes, lists security cameras among the features residents get. New buildings like that arrive with systems someone else designed, and owners need to know what they bought. Older buildings, meanwhile, carry decades of tenant history: in 1998 City Limits reported tenants at 2244 Morris Avenue, 2342 Ryer Avenue and 2400 Valentine Avenue organizing over repairs, including unlocked street doors that left buildings open to anyone. Cameras don't fix a door that won't lock. They work best when the door, the light and the mail panel are fixed first and the camera records what happens after.

What the record shows about Mount Hope and cameras

  • Small theft is rising fastest. In the 46th Precinct, petit larceny complaints rose from 845 to 1,029 year to date through November 8, 2026, up 21.8 percent, and grand larceny rose 2.6 percent, to 582.
  • Car theft is falling. The same report shows grand larceny of autos down 32.9 percent, from 216 to 145, which changes how much of a budget a curb camera deserves.
  • The long view is far better than it was. Measured over 33 years, the report shows burglary down 90.7 percent and robbery down 78.7 percent in the precinct, so cameras here are a record-keeping tool, not a siege measure.
  • Old buildings dominate. About 727 of the area's roughly 865 residential lots hold buildings from before 1946, by our count of the city's PLUTO data, and only about 65 lots have buildings completed since 2015, holding roughly 4,590 homes.
  • A landmarked row. The Morris Avenue Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on July 15, 1986, covers two tenements and 35 two-family row houses built between 1906 and 1910, where camera work on the front needs LPC review.
  • An auto corridor that is shrinking but still busy. City Limits reported in 2021 that around 150 auto and auto-related businesses still operated along the two-mile, 90-block Jerome Avenue corridor from East 167th to 184th streets, after 35 to 40 were displaced following the 2018 rezoning.

Mail, Packages and the Vestibule

The Mail Panel Problem: Cameras for the Thefts That Fill a Mount Hope Precinct Report

If one spot in a Mount Hope apartment house deserves the best camera in the building, it is usually the mail panel. Checks, benefit cards, replacement bank cards and parcels all pass through it, and a thief at a panel needs only a few seconds and a bent tool. The trouble is that most older systems were never aimed at the panel at all. They were aimed at the lobby, from a corner near the ceiling, because that is where the cable was easiest to run. The result is video that shows someone was in the lobby at 2:14 in the afternoon, but not what they did with their hands, and not their face. Here is how we plan the vestibule and mail area so the video answers the questions a tenant, a super or a police officer will actually ask.

Two cameras, two jobs

One camera cannot show both a face and a pair of hands well. We usually give the mail area two. The first sits at roughly head height facing the path people take to the panel, so anyone walking up is seen face-on, under the light, before they turn their back. The second looks down and across the face of the panel from the side, close enough to show which box is being opened and whether a key or a tool is used. In a narrow vestibule on Creston, Walton or Valentine Avenue, where the panel sits on a side wall a step inside the inner door, the face camera often goes above the inner door frame looking out toward the street door, and the hands camera goes on the opposite wall. Neither one looks out through the glass at the sidewalk, where daylight would turn every face into a silhouette.

Light before lenses

Many prewar vestibules are lit by a single fixture in the middle of the ceiling, which throws a shadow down every face and leaves the panel itself in shade. Before we choose a camera, we look at the light at the times mail is delivered and the times it tends to vanish, usually late afternoon and evening. Often a brighter, even fixture over the panel does more for the picture than a more expensive camera. We don't do electrical work, so if a fixture needs replacing we tell the owner exactly what to ask an electrician for, and we set the cameras after the light is fixed.

Recording that is easy to search

A missing check is usually discovered days later. By then the person reviewing the recorder may have to look through a week of video to find the few minutes that matter. We set the mail-area cameras to record continuously, with motion markers on a zone drawn tight around the panel itself, so the recorder's timeline shows a mark each time someone stands at the boxes. Combined with the time the carrier usually arrives, that turns a week of video into a short list of moments. We also set the recorder clock to correct itself automatically, because a clip with the wrong time is harder to use in a police report.

Packages in the vestibule

Few of Mount Hope's older buildings have a package room, so parcels end up on the vestibule floor or on top of the radiator. The face camera that covers the mail panel usually covers that spot as well, if it is placed with the package pile in mind. When a building has room, a simple locked package shelf or a locker reduces the problem more than any camera, and the camera then records who opens it. Brick Underground's advice to tenants in a December 2023 column made the same point from the other side: a tenants' association can push for a better lock, intercom or package locker, not only for video.

Who looks at the video

Brick Underground was blunt in that column: "Unfortunately, your landlord isn't obligated to share surveillance footage with you." A landlord must turn it over when police request it or a court requires it. That is why we ask owners to decide in advance, and write down, how a tenant's report of a missing package or check will be handled: who reviews the recorder, how quickly, and whether the clip goes to the tenant, to the police or to both. Buildings that settle this before the first theft avoid a lot of arguments afterward, and tenants are more willing to report problems when they know someone will actually look.

Cameras that survive being noticed

In 2015, CBS New York reported that a group of men walked into several apartment buildings on Davidson Avenue in Morris Heights, just west of Mount Hope and in the same precinct, and damaged numerous surveillance cameras inside and outside. A camera that can be reached from the floor will eventually be knocked, twisted or covered. In a vestibule we use low-profile, vandal-resistant housings mounted high enough to be out of easy reach while still seeing faces, run the cable inside the wall or in steel where it cannot be yanked, and put the recorder somewhere other than the lobby, so damaging one camera never takes the video of the others with it.

Licensed Shops

Cannabis Dispensary Cameras in Mount Hope: Building to the State's Surveillance Rule

Storefronts along East Tremont Avenue, East Burnside Avenue and Jerome Avenue change hands often, and some of them are becoming cannabis shops. In November 2023, ABC7 reported from a small store on East Tremont Avenue in Mount Hope that neighbors said had recently changed from a deli to a cannabis dispensary, where surveillance video recorded a masked robbery suspect walking in and being shot seconds later. We don't know from that report whether the store held a state license. What we do know is that a licensed adult-use dispensary in New York has to meet a detailed video surveillance rule, 9 NYCRR §125.3, and that a generic four-camera kit from a deli will not meet it. This section is for owners applying for or holding a license, and for landlords leasing to them.

What the rule asks cameras to cover

The rule requires video coverage of entrances and exits and any parking lot, of safes, vaults and sales areas, of anywhere cannabis is handled or stored, and of cash-handling areas. It says cameras must be positioned at entry and exit points and at each point-of-sale area. It also says cameras must suit the normal lighting conditions of the area they watch, and it asks for clear identification of people and activity rather than setting a fixed resolution. In a narrow Mount Hope storefront that usually means a face camera at the customer entrance, one camera per register, a view of the sales floor, cameras on the vault or safe room and the stock area, a camera at the rear or delivery door, and a camera on the cash-counting spot, each placed for its own job.

How long and how the video is kept

Under the rule, recordings must be kept for at least 60 days, with continuous recording during hours of operation and whenever cannabis is handled, and motion-activated recording at other times. If an investigation or proceeding is pending, an unaltered copy has to be kept until it is closed. Every recording needs a date and time stamp synchronized to the national time standard that does not significantly obscure the picture. The recording equipment has to be secured against tampering or theft, the surveillance room has to stay locked with access limited, and the licensee has to be able to give the state's Office of Cannabis Management an unaltered copy, and remote viewing access, on request.

Power and testing

Two parts of the rule catch owners by surprise. First, alarm and video equipment must keep working for at least eight hours during a power outage, which means a battery system sized for the recorder, the switch that powers the cameras and the cameras themselves, not a small unit that only protects the recorder for ten minutes. Second, equipment has to be tested and inspected at intervals of no more than 30 calendar days. We build the system so the monthly check is quick: a written list of every camera, its view and its recording schedule, and a simple procedure to confirm each one is recording, the clock is correct and the retention target is being met.

Planning before the license, building after the lease

Because storage for 60 days of continuous recording on a dozen cameras is a real cost, and because the camera layout depends on where the registers, vault and stock room will be, the camera plan should be drawn at the same time as the store layout. We can walk an empty or half-built storefront, mark where each camera and cable run goes, size the recorder and battery system, and write a description of the surveillance system the owner can use in planning. Nothing is ordered until the layout is settled and the owner approves the quote.

What the landlord upstairs should know

Most Mount Hope storefronts sit under apartments. A dispensary's outdoor cameras at the entrance will show the residential door next to it, and the building's own cameras may show the store's door. Both owners should agree in writing on what each side's cameras cover, with privacy masks where a view would otherwise include the other's space. The dispensary's recorder and surveillance room stay inside the store's leased space and under its lock, separate from the building's system.

The same thinking helps any late counter

Bodegas, delis, cellphone shops, money-transfer counters and takeout spots don't fall under the cannabis rule, but its logic is sound for any shop that handles cash late at night: a face at the door, a camera on each register, a view of the cash drawer and safe, a camera on the back door, a clock that is right, and video kept long enough to be found. Most small shops don't need 60 days. Two to four weeks is usually enough to notice a problem and export the clip, and the drives are sized to match.

Landmarks

Cameras on the Morris Avenue Row Houses: Working Inside the Historic District Rules

The Morris Avenue Historic District is a short run of Morris Avenue between East Tremont Avenue and East 179th Street. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it on July 15, 1986. Its designation report describes two tenements and 35 two-family row houses built between 1906 and 1910 by the developer August Jacob to designs by the architect John Hauser, with stoops, wrought-iron railings and paired wood-and-glass front doors under transoms. These are some of the most photographed fronts in Mount Hope, and they are also houses where families want to see who is at the door. Both things can be true at once if the cameras are planned with the rules in hand.

What LPC staff can approve

LPC's Permit Guidebook, Chapter 11, explains when staff can issue a permit for security cameras under LPC Rules section 2-20. Cameras must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered look. On small residential or commercial buildings, no more than two cameras may be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage; on large buildings, no more than three. The general criteria say equipment should be as small as possible, painted to blend with the surrounding material if possible, and attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, not through cast iron or on decorative surfaces. Equipment cannot go on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, and then only in a discreet spot. Conduit has to be concealed, with only about ten inches of visible conduit allowed, painted to match. Manufacturer cut sheets are required with the application.

How that plays out on a row house front

On a Morris Avenue row house the street front is the primary facade and is full of the features the district was designated for. So we start by asking whether a camera needs to be on the front at all. Often the better answer is a camera inside the vestibule or just inside the front door, looking at the door, which records everyone who enters and is not exterior work. If an outside camera is truly needed, for the stoop or the sidewalk in front, we look for a discreet spot on plain masonry, a small dome finished to match, cable carried inside through the nearest possible point, and we prepare the description, photos and cut sheets the application needs. We don't drill into the original doors, the transoms or carved trim.

The rear and the side

The backs of the row houses face yards and the backs of other buildings. A rear camera is usually simpler from a landmarks point of view than one on the front, but it brings its own rule: New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a neighbor a civil claim if adjoining video surveillance records their back yard without consent. A rear camera on Morris Avenue is aimed at your own yard and back door, with privacy masks over neighboring yards and windows.

Outside the district

Most of Mount Hope is not landmarked. Ordinary walk-ups and elevator houses on Walton, Creston, Ryer, Valentine and Anthony Avenues, and on the Grand Concourse through Mount Hope, don't need LPC review for cameras unless the building itself is designated. Because the district boundary runs house by house, we check the exact address on LPC's map before planning anything on a front in the blocks near Morris Avenue and East Tremont Avenue.

Five-story walk-ups: the panel, the stair and the cellar

The five-story walk-up is the most common camera job in Mount Hope. A typical system is five to eight cameras: the face and hands views at the mail panel, a camera inside the inner door looking at the street door, cameras on the stair at alternate landings or at every landing where budget allows, one in the cellar facing the cellar door and one at the top of the stair facing the roof door. The recorder goes in a locked metal box in the cellar, off the floor and away from the boiler and any water line, with a battery unit beside it. Cable rises through an existing chase where one exists, or in a stair corner in painted conduit, and every hole through a floor is fire-stopped. Because these stairs are long and many walk-ups have dim landings, we check each landing's light before choosing the camera for it.

Six-story elevator houses: two stairs and a lift

Six-story elevator buildings add an elevator, often a second stair, a larger lobby and sometimes a courtyard. The ground-floor elevator door is the busiest place in the building after the street door, so it gets its own camera. Each stair is covered at the ground floor door and at the roof, at a minimum, because a stair that only one camera watches becomes the way in for anyone who wants to avoid it. When an elevator is out of service, as one Mount Hope building's was for months in 2024 according to News 12, every resident and every visitor uses the stairs, and a system designed around the elevator loses most of its value. We plan stair coverage that still makes sense when the elevator is down. Cable to upper floors runs in existing risers or a stair chase, never inside the elevator shaft, which belongs to the elevator contractor.

Morris Avenue row houses: inside first

The row houses in the historic district are two-family homes with a shared front door, a stoop and a cellar. The simplest strong plan is one camera inside the front vestibule facing the door, one at the cellar or rear door and, if needed, one for the rear yard aimed at the owner's own yard. An outside stoop camera is possible but goes through LPC review, so we treat it as a second step. The recorder sits in the cellar. In a two-family, the owner sees every camera, the second household sees the shared front door, and every adult has a personal login.

Frame houses and two-families: basement as the hub

East of the Grand Concourse, toward Valentine, Anthony, Ryer and Park Avenues, Mount Hope has a scattering of small houses, some of them wood-frame. A house gets a plan built around its front door, its side passage, its yard and the curb where the car sits. Cable leaves the basement through sleeved holes, mounts on siding sit on blocks that spread the load and keep water out, and the recorder lives in the basement or a utility closet. Wi-Fi cameras can work in a frame house more easily than in a brick walk-up, but we still wire the cameras that matter most.

Apartments over shops: two systems, two owners

Where a store sits under apartments, as along East Burnside Avenue, East Tremont Avenue and Jerome Avenue, we keep the store and the building on separate recorders with separate logins. The store shouldn't watch the residents' entrance and mail panel, and the landlord shouldn't watch the register. Each system follows its own cable path, the store's through the store ceiling and the building's through the cellar and stair, and the two owners agree in writing about any camera on the shared front.

New towers and affordable buildings: decide on paper

Mount Hope's new buildings are taller than anything around them: New York YIMBY has reported permits for a twelve-story building with 99 homes at 1917 Morris Avenue, a fifteen-story building at 29 Mount Hope Place and a nine-story building at 1712 Jerome Avenue, and 121 Mount Hope Place is a seven-story building with 27 homes. In a building like that the camera plan belongs on the drawings, with a box and a run back to a head-end room for every entrance, the lobby, the mail and package area, every stair door, the elevator landings, the cellar, trash and bike rooms, the roof door and the building corners. The 46th Precinct is close, and detectives do ask

Export the original, not a phone video of the screen

The most common mistake we see is a phone recording of the recorder's screen. It looks fine to the person who made it and loses most of the detail that matters: the face, the plate, the time stamp. Every system we install is set so the main recording stream is kept at full quality, and we show the owner, the super or the store manager how to export an original clip to a fresh USB drive the same day something happens. A short laminated card next to the recorder lists the steps.

Keep it long enough to be found

A missing check might be noticed a week later. A detective might ask two weeks after an incident. A recorder that keeps only five days because its drive is undersized loses exactly the video people need. We size drives around the cameras that matter most, so the mail panel, street door and register keep video longer, and set less important cameras to use less space. The clock has to be right

A recorder that drifts a few minutes a month will be off by an hour within a couple of years, and then some time changes later nobody trusts its times. We set every recorder to sync its clock automatically from an internet time source, check it at handover and show the owner where to look. For dispensaries, the rule calls for time stamps synchronized to the national standard.

Before a clip goes online

After something happens on a block there is pressure to post video. A clip from a Mount Hope lobby can show children, neighbors, apartment numbers and people who had nothing to do with anything. Give the original to police first. If a copy has to go anywhere public, blur bystanders in the copy and keep the original untouched.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Mount Hope: Questions Answered

Mail, packages and the building's own setup

Our carrier fills the mail panel around midday, and checks go missing before evening. How should the mail camera be set up so those few hours are quick to search?

Record continuously, and draw a tight motion zone around the face of the panel only, not the whole lobby. Each time someone stands at the boxes, the recorder places a marker on its timeline, so reviewing an afternoon means jumping between a few dozen marks instead of watching hours. Pair that with a face camera on the approach path and the recorder clock syncing automatically, and a tenant's report of a missing check can usually be narrowed to a handful of people within minutes.

Our recorder sits on a shelf in the super's first-floor apartment. Is that a safe place for it?

It is better than the lobby, but it ties the building's video to one person's home. Getting video means knocking on a private door, the recorder leaves with the super if the super moves, and anyone who wants to destroy evidence knows where it is. We usually move the recorder to a locked metal box in the cellar or a utility closet, with a battery unit, and give the super a personal login on a phone, so he can still see and export video without the equipment living in his apartment.

Our new cameras and the intercom both need cable up the same stair. Can they share one riser path?

They can share a pathway, such as a chase or a conduit run up the stair corner, as long as each system has its own cable and the path has room. Camera cable and intercom wire are both low-voltage, but we keep them as separate cables, label both, and never splice into the intercom wiring. If the path is already full, we add a second sleeve or conduit rather than forcing more cable in, and every floor penetration is fire-stopped again after the pull.

A package was taken from our vestibule and the camera recorded it. Is it worth filing a police report over a single package?

Yes. A report creates a record that ties the video to a date and a complaint number, and it lets a detective connect your clip to other thefts in the area. Petit larceny complaints in the 46th Precinct were up 21.8 percent year to date as of November 8, 2026, which tells you the precinct is seeing many cases like yours. Export the original clip, note the time, keep a copy, and give the complaint number to your landlord or managing agent so the video is kept.

Stores, counters and licensed shops

We have applied for an adult-use dispensary license for a storefront on East Tremont Avenue. Can the camera plan be worked out before the license is granted?

Yes, and it should be, because the cameras depend on where the entrance, registers, vault, stock room and cash-counting spot will be. A licensed dispensary has to keep video for at least 60 days. How much recording space does a small shop need for that?

It depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, how much movement each one sees and how many hours the store is open, since the rule calls for continuous recording during operating hours and motion recording otherwise. A shop with a dozen cameras recording continuously for 60 days usually needs several large drives, not the single small one that comes with a basic kit. We calculate storage from the actual camera list and settings, then add a margin, and we check after the first weeks that the recorder is on track to keep the full period.

Our counter on East Burnside Avenue has a thick clear partition between staff and customers. Will a camera see faces through it?

It can, if it is placed carefully. A camera behind the partition looking out sees reflections of the store's lights and its own infrared glow on the glass, which can wash out a face. We usually put the face camera on the customer side, high and angled down toward the counter, and a second camera on the staff side looking at the cash drawer. Turning off a camera's infrared and lighting the counter evenly also helps. We test the view with the real lights on before fixing the mount.

Our Jerome Avenue repair shop leaves customers' cars parked at the curb overnight waiting for parts. Can our camera watch cars that are on the street rather than on our property?

A camera on your building can record the public street in front of it, and many shops do. Under the elevated tracks the curb is dark at night, so a low-light camera with a little added light works better than relying on infrared. The Law in Plain Words

New York Rules Behind a Mount Hope Camera Job

Walk-ups, row houses, new buildings and stores in Mount Hope all run into the same short list of New York rules. This is a plain summary of how we work within them, not legal advice. For a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Cameras are the owner's choice, not a city mandate

Security sources that cover New York building rules say there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. What many buildings are required to have at the entrance is a working intercom and self-closing, self-locking doors, which is a separate job. Cameras in a Mount Hope apartment house are a decision the owner makes for the building's own problems.

Private places stay off camera

Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people without consent where they reasonably expect privacy, such as inside a home, a bathroom or a changing area. A hallway camera may watch the corridor; it may not look into an apartment when a door opens. Privacy masks black out those parts of the frame in live view and in recordings.

Sound is treated differently from pictures

Recording a conversation without the consent of at least one participant can be eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. A two-way talk feature on a door camera is a conversation you are part of. A lobby camera quietly recording tenants is not, so continuous audio stays off on common-area and outdoor cameras.

Neighbors' yards

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. Rear cameras on Mount Hope houses and walk-ups are aimed at the owner's own yard and door and masked over the rest.

Who decides on common areas

In a rental building the landlord controls the halls, lobby and facade, and tenants need written permission for anything there. In May 2024, News 12 reported that management at a Mott Haven building gave tenants a deadline to remove cameras from their apartment doors, and one tenant took her doorbell camera down for fear of a violation or a fine. Asking first avoids that. In a co-op the board decides; in a nonprofit-owned building the owner or its managing agent does. A store tenant controls the inside of the store within its lease, and the building owner controls the front.

Who may install

Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, a New York State license is required to install video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement, with an exemption for an owner who installs a system personally, or through an employee, on their own property. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance. Ask any installer for a license number before work starts.

Landmarks and licensed cannabis shops

Houses and tenements in the Morris Avenue Historic District need LPC review for exterior camera work under Rules section 2-20. A licensed adult-use dispensary must meet the surveillance requirements of 9 NYCRR §125.3, including coverage of entrances and points of sale, at least 60 days of storage, eight hours of backup power and testing at least every 30 days.

  • Cable paths. An existing chase from cellar to roof is quick; a walk-up with no chase needs conduit up a stair corner, fire-stopped at every floor.
  • Height of the building. A five-story walk-up, a six-story elevator house and a fifteen-story tower need very different run lengths and risers.
  • Wall material. Old brick, block, plaster, siding and new drywall take different anchors and time.
  • Light. A dim vestibule or a dark curb under the elevated tracks may need a lighting fix by an electrician before any camera can do its job.
  • Days of storage. More cameras, higher quality and longer retention mean a bigger recorder and more drives; 60 days for a dispensary is a large step up.
  • Backup power. Minutes of battery for a walk-up recorder cost far less than the eight hours a licensed cannabis shop must have.
  • Approvals. An LPC application for a Morris Avenue front, or a board's or nonprofit owner's paperwork, adds planning time, not just hardware.
  • Logins and policies. Separate views for an owner, a super, a managing agent, store staff and tenants take setup time.

FAQ

Mount Hope Security Camera FAQ

When we replace our building's old recorder, what happens to the video stored on its drive?
+

Before anything is removed, we ask whether any of it has to be kept, for a police case, an insurance claim or a dispute, and export those clips first. The old drive then stays with the owner. It holds months of tenants' comings and goings, so it should be wiped or physically destroyed, not thrown out or left in the cellar. We can wipe it on site or hand it to you to keep for a set period if a case is still open.

Do you need to get into every apartment to wire cameras for the halls and stairs?
+

Usually not. Common-area cameras are wired through the cellar, the stair, existing chases and the hallways themselves, so we rarely need to enter apartments. The exception is an old building where the only path between floors runs through a closet or a wall inside an apartment. If that comes up on the walk-through, we tell the owner which apartment and why, and the owner arranges access with proper notice.

Can a camera watch over our construction site while the new building on our lot goes up?
+

Yes. A temporary system on a site can watch the gate, the material storage area and the equipment overnight, recording to a recorder in a locked site office or a weatherproof cabinet, with phone access for the owner and the superintendent. We plan it so the cameras and cable can move as the building rises, and so the permanent building system can be designed separately and installed once walls are ready.

Can tenants be given a written list of what each building camera covers?
+

Yes, and we recommend it. At handover we give the owner a plain list of every camera, where it is, what it is meant to see and that audio is off. Many owners post it near the mailboxes or send it to tenants. It answers the questions tenants worry about, such as whether a camera can see into their door, and it makes clear the cameras are there for the doors, the mail and the stairs.

A customer says she was shortchanged at our counter and wants to see the video. Do we have to show her?
+

Our sources don't point to any rule that makes a store show its recordings to a customer on request, and the video shows other customers too. Many owners review the clip themselves and tell the customer what it shows, and keep a copy in case the complaint goes further. If police or a court ask for it, it must be provided. For a specific dispute, ask a lawyer. A counter camera aimed at the cash drawer is what makes these disputes quick to settle.

We are selling our building. What should the buyer receive for the camera system, and what happens to recordings already on it?
+

The buyer should get the master account and password, the list of cameras, the recorder and switch locations, the cable labels and any warranty papers, and the old owner's and old staff's logins should be removed on closing day. Decide before closing whether existing recordings stay on the recorder or are exported and erased, and keep anything tied to an open case. We can do the account handover so nobody from the old ownership keeps access.

Will the cameras keep recording if the building's internet goes down?
+

A wired system with a recorder on site keeps recording without internet, because the cameras send video to the recorder over their own cables. What you lose during the outage is the ability to watch from your phone and any alerts. When the internet comes back, remote viewing returns and nothing recorded in the meantime is lost. Cloud-only cameras are different, since many of them save nothing while the connection is down.

Can the recorder warn us if one of the cameras stops sending a picture?
+

Most recorders we install can send a phone notification when a camera loses its connection or a drive reports an error. We turn those alerts on for the owner or the super, so a camera knocked out of position or a failing drive is noticed in days, not discovered months later when someone needs the video.

Can a register camera be set so it never records customers entering their card PIN?
+

Yes. We aim register cameras at the cash drawer, the counter and the customer's face rather than straight down at the card reader, and where the reader still falls in view we draw a privacy mask over it so that part of the picture is blacked out in both live view and recordings. Customers' PIN entries should never be on a store's video.

Can the cameras tell us when the cellar door has been left propped open?
+

Partly. A camera can alert when someone crosses a line drawn at the doorway, or when the scene stays changed, but it can't reliably tell an open door from a closed one in every light. The dependable way is a simple door contact that reports when the door stays open, which can be tied to an alarm system. The camera then shows who propped it and when.
