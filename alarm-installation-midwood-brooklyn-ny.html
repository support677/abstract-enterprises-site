The Neighborhood

Why Midwood Properties Need Alarm Installation

Midwood is one of the parts of Brooklyn where a house still means a house. When the Department of City Planning rezoned roughly 80 blocks here in 2006, between Avenue H and Avenue P and from Coney Island Avenue to Ocean Avenue, it described the area as mostly two- and three-story residences, many of them detached or semi-detached, with apartment houses of four to eight stories concentrated on Ocean Avenue, parts of Kings Highway and Avenue K. A real estate agent put it more simply to Brick Underground: “Midwood is a mix of semi-detached homes and co-ops located on quiet, tree-lined side streets.” Those quiet side streets are exactly what shapes an alarm here. A house with a side driveway, a backyard and a fence has a lot of windows that nobody on the sidewalk can see.

The burglaries that have made the news in Midwood follow that geography closely. In June 2015 CBS New York reported a burglar on Avenue J who pried open a window around one in the morning, removed the screen, climbed in, and then put the screen back on behind him; he took a laptop, credit cards and cash from upstairs, close to where family members were asleep. “There were 11 people here at the time, and you know, people were awake, and nobody detected him,” Yonah Gugenheim told the station. In the fall of 2017 the same station followed a man who had targeted at least ten homes on East 31st and East 32nd streets and nearby Flatbush, entering through back windows reachable from the yards, in one case stacking four chairs to reach a window until a window lock stopped him. A neighbor summed up the method: “Very neatly took out screens and got in through windows in the back.”

Earlier still, at a 2012 meeting of the 70th Precinct Community Council, officers described four homes burglarized in one month on East 17th, East 19th, East 22nd and East 23rd streets in South Midwood, every one of them through an unlocked first-floor window. During Sukkos in 2017, Yeshiva World News reported a run of break-ins across Flatbush and the East 30s streets, many through unlocked windows, some while residents slept and at least one during Shabbos, with more discovered when families came home. The police advice printed with that report was blunt: “Make sure you have working cameras, alarm systems and your driveways and backyards are well lit up.”

None of this calls for panic, and we will not sell it as a crisis. The 70th Precinct, the command DNAinfo’s crime report files Midwood under, recorded 80 burglaries in 2026 through the week ending November 8, against 117 a year earlier, a drop of 31.6 percent, and its burglary count is down 70.8 percent over sixteen years and 96.8 percent over thirty-three. What the reports do show is a consistent pattern: entries at the back and sides of houses, through windows rather than doors, often at night, often while people are home. That is a pattern an alarm can be built around. A contact on a rear window does not care whether the screen was put back on. A night setting that keeps the ground floor armed while the family is upstairs turns the scenario in those reports, someone walking through a downstairs room unnoticed, into a siren and a phone call within seconds.

The second reason Midwood owners call us is the life of the house itself. Many families here go away for several days at a time around the Jewish holidays and in summer, leaving a house that looks occupied but is not. Many houses are two- and three-family buildings with an owner on one floor and a tenant or relatives on another. And a lot of the alarms we find are old hardwired panels installed decades ago, still on a phone line that was disconnected years before, sounding a siren that no one outside the house will act on. Updating those systems, or replacing them, is a large part of our work here.

Need Repair in Midwood?

An Old Panel Still Dialing a Dead Phone Line, a Window Zone That Will Not Clear, or a Keypad Code Nobody Remembers

Many Midwood repair calls start with a house that changed hands or a parent who moved in with the children. The alarm on the wall was installed twenty or thirty years ago by a company that no longer exists, the master code went with the previous owner, and the panel still tries to report over a landline that was cancelled when the family switched to cable or fiber. The keypad beeps with a trouble light, the siren works, and nobody outside the house is told anything when it goes off. Other calls are simpler: a contact on a back window that reads open even though the window is shut, because the house settled and the magnet moved; a motion detector in the kitchen that trips when the heat comes on; a backup battery that died and took the panel down with the power during a summer outage.

Most of these are fixed in one visit, often in one to two hours. A panel that still has sound wiring can usually be reset to a new master code, fitted with a cellular communicator so it reports again without a phone line, and connected to the app. Where the panel is too old for parts, we quote a replacement that reuses the existing wires to the doors and windows. Service on systems from other companies is billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), and we tell you before we start whether the visit is likely to fix it or whether replacement is the better spend. Call (347) 934-8335, read us the name printed on the keypad and what its screen shows, and we will tell you which it is likely to be.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Midwood’s Architecture

An alarm plan in Midwood depends mostly on two things: how many sides of the building a stranger can reach without being seen, and how many households live behind the same street door. A detached house on a side street can have twenty or more reachable windows; a seventh-floor co-op on Ocean Avenue may have none. A semi-detached two-family has one wall nobody can touch and two families who need to come and go on different schedules. These are the building types we see most, and how the plan changes for each one.

The detached frame or brick house

Two or two and a half stories, a front porch or stoop, a side driveway to a garage at the back, a backyard behind a fence, and windows on every side. The most openings, and the most hidden ones.

The semi-detached house

A pair of houses sharing one party wall, each with a driveway or alley on its open side. Fewer windows than a detached house, but the side alley is a blind spot from the street.

The 1980s three- and four-family

Semi-detached buildings with ground-floor garages, a lobby or shared stair, and apartments stacked above. The garage and the entrance are shared; each apartment is its own home.

The prewar apartment house

Five- and six-story walk-up and elevator buildings on Kings Highway, Avenue I and the blocks near the avenues, with fire escapes on the rear and courtyard sides.

The postwar and new co-op or condo

Elevator buildings on Ocean Avenue and Avenue K, from 1960s co-ops with a live-in super to recent condos with a virtual doorman, where your alarm lives entirely inside your own apartment.

The avenue building with a store below

Two- and three-story buildings on Avenue J, Avenue M and Coney Island Avenue with a shop on the ground floor and one or two apartments above, sharing a cellar and sometimes a back door.

The detached house: protecting the side nobody sees from the sidewalk

A freestanding Midwood house gives a burglar choices. The front faces the street and the neighbors, but the driveway side, the back and the far side face a fence, a garage, a hedge or the back of another yard. Almost every window on those sides can be reached from the ground, and many more from a porch roof, the flat roof of a one-story kitchen extension, a garbage enclosure or a pile of garden furniture. The 2017 burglar on East 31st Street stacked four chairs to reach a window; that is the kind of reach we assume when we walk a property. So the survey of a detached house starts at the back corner by the garage and works around, window by window, asking one question each time: can someone stand here, out of sight, and open this?

Every window that gets a yes gets a contact, a small magnet and sensor pair that reports when the sash moves. Large rear windows, sliders to a deck and the glass in a back door also get glass-break coverage, so that a pane broken without the frame moving is still caught. Inside, one or two motion detectors cover the ground-floor rooms a burglar would have to cross from the back to reach the stairs, usually the kitchen and the hallway. Upstairs bedrooms rarely need motion detection; what they need is the siren, loud enough on the sleeping floor to wake everyone, and a keypad or fob within reach of the bed so whoever wakes first can see which zone tripped.

The semi-detached house: one shared wall, three exposed sides

Semi-detached houses are common across Midwood, and the shared party wall simplifies the plan: no windows on that side, nothing to protect there. The open side usually has a narrow alley or driveway running from the sidewalk to the yard, often behind a gate, and that alley is where we spend the most time. The windows along it are at head height or lower, they sit between two walls nobody can see into, and in many houses one of them is the window over the kitchen sink that is left open an inch on summer nights. Those windows get contacts, and if the family wants to keep one cracked for air, we fit a second magnet so the window can be left open a hand’s width with the alarm still armed and any further movement still reported.

The three- and four-family semi-detached: shared garage, separate homes

The groups of semi-detached three- and four-family houses with ground-floor garages that went up in the 1980s, and newer buildings like them, have a different shape. The ground floor is mostly garage and entry, the apartments start one floor up, and the windows a burglar can reach from the ground are fewer. What these buildings do have is a shared garage, a shared lobby or stair and a cellar or meter room that everyone uses. The building owner may want an alarm on those shared spaces, and each apartment may want its own system inside its own door.

We handle that with areas on one panel or with separate small panels, depending on who pays and who decides. The garage doors and the lobby door become the owner’s area, armed overnight on a schedule or by the last person in; each apartment is its own area with its own keypad and codes. A family member who owns the building and lives on the top floor can see the whole thing in the app; a tenant sees only their own apartment. No one’s comings and goings are reported to anyone who should not see them.

Prewar apartment houses: the fire escape and the courtyard

The five- and six-story prewar apartment houses on Kings Highway, Avenue I and the blocks around them were built with fire escapes on the rear and courtyard sides. For a tenant in one of these buildings, the alarm protects the apartment door and any window on the fire escape, plus any window on the first floor or over a low roof or courtyard wall. We fit wireless contacts on those windows, never anything that blocks the window from opening, and where a window has a landlord-installed gate we put the contact on the window itself so the gate can still be opened in a fire without fuss. The siren stays inside the apartment, and the system reports over cellular, so nothing depends on the building’s wiring or the landlord’s permission.

Ocean Avenue co-ops and condos: everything inside your own door

The elevator buildings on Ocean Avenue range from postwar co-ops such as 1801 Ocean Avenue, a seven-story building from 1962 with keyless entry, an intercom and a live-in superintendent but no doorman, to new condos such as 2025 Ocean Avenue, an eight-story building of 16 apartments finished in 2020 with a virtual doorman. In buildings like these the lobby is the board’s or the managing agent’s business. Your alarm covers what is yours: the apartment door, any terrace or balcony door, and windows on a low floor or over a setback roof. A wireless system with adhesive-mounted contacts, one motion detector and a keypad by the door can go in without drilling into anything the building owns, and it can come with you when you move.

The avenue building with a store below: two alarms, one cellar

On Avenue J, Avenue M and Coney Island Avenue many buildings have a shop on the ground floor and an apartment or two upstairs, sometimes lived in by the shop owner’s family. The cellar is usually shared, and the back door of the store often opens onto the same yard or alley as the apartment stairs. We keep the two systems separate in their codes and their call lists, even when they share a panel: the store’s area is armed at closing and reports to the owner and the monitoring station, the apartment’s area belongs to whoever lives there, and the routes between them, the cellar door and the stair door, are covered so that neither one can be used to reach the other unnoticed.

Windows and Night Arming

Downstairs Armed, Upstairs Asleep: Planning a Midwood Alarm Around the Back Window

Most alarm packages are designed around the moment you leave the house: you set the alarm, you lock the door, the house is empty, and anything that moves inside is a problem. In Midwood the reports point to a different moment. The 2015 Avenue J burglary happened at one in the morning with eleven people in the house. The 2017 East 31st Street break-in came early on a Saturday while the family slept. Yitzi Gruen, describing the burglar who worked those blocks, noticed the same thing: “He only went to the ground floor.” The people upstairs were never the target; the laptops, wallets and bicycles downstairs were. An alarm that is only switched on when the house is empty does nothing in that situation, and a lot of alarms in Midwood are only ever switched on when the house is empty.

So the center of a Midwood plan is the night setting. On the panels we install it is often called Stay or Night mode, and what it does is simple. Every door and every reachable window is armed, so any of them opening sounds the alarm at once, with no entry delay on the back windows. Motion detectors in the bedrooms and on the stairs are left off, so the family can move around the upper floors freely. Depending on the household, the kitchen motion detector can be on or off: in a house where nobody goes downstairs at night, it stays on and covers the room a burglar would cross; in a house where someone gets up for a glass of water or a baby bottle, it stays off and the window contacts do the work. Either way, the setting is one button on the keypad or the fob by the bed, so it actually gets used.

The second part of the plan is the window itself. A burglar who removes the screen and puts it back, as the Avenue J burglar did, leaves a window that looks untouched from inside, but the contact on the sash reports the moment the window rises. A burglar who breaks the glass of a locked window, or cuts it, is heard by a glass-break detector that listens for the specific sound of breaking glass. A window with an air conditioner in it is a weak point the 70th Precinct singled out in 2012, telling residents to secure units so they cannot be pushed in or pulled out. We put a contact on the lower sash, a bracket or stop that keeps the sash from rising past the unit, and glass-break coverage in the room, so the air conditioner cannot be used as a door.

The third part is the alert. In those reports, families found out in the morning. A siren on the sleeping floor changes that, but so does what happens next: the panel sends the alarm over cellular to the monitoring station, or to your phones if you self-monitor, and the app shows which window tripped. Knowing it was the kitchen window and not the front door tells you whether to go down, whether to wait for the police, and what to tell them when you call. For houses where an elderly parent lives on the ground floor, the plan can include a panic button and a medical pendant on the same panel, and we arrange the zones so a parent who opens a door at night does not set off the whole house.

Finally, we look at the habit that every one of those reports turns on: the unlocked window. The 2012 South Midwood burglaries all came through unlocked first-floor windows; the 2017 burglar was stopped by a window lock. The panels we install can show, before you arm, which windows are open, and they will not arm with a back window open unless you choose to bypass it. That one feature, a keypad that says “kitchen window open” at eleven at night, goes straight at the gap every one of those reports describes.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in Midwood

Midwood’s businesses sit on a handful of strips the city has mapped for commerce: Coney Island Avenue, Avenue J between Coney Island Avenue and East 16th Street, Avenue M between Coney Island and Ocean avenues, and Kings Highway, with smaller clusters near Brooklyn College and along Ocean Avenue. Most are small and owner-run: groceries, bakeries, pizzerias, kosher and halal food stores, clothing and jewelry shops, pharmacies, offices for doctors, accountants and lawyers, and the shuls, schools and community centers that are as much a part of the street as the stores. What they have in common is a ground-floor space with a gate or a glass front, a back door onto a yard or alley, a cellar, and an owner who is usually not there at night.

Avenue J

The strip from Coney Island Avenue to East 16th Street, home to Di Fara Pizza and a row of food stores, bakeries and services, many with apartments above the storefronts.

Avenue M

Groceries, restaurants, pharmacies and offices from Coney Island Avenue to Ocean Avenue, with a Q stop at East 16th Street and a commercial overlay that the city trimmed to a narrower depth in 2006.

Kings Highway

Shops, banks and medical offices around the B and Q express station at East 16th Street, with larger co-op and apartment buildings in between the storefronts.

Coney Island Avenue

Auto shops, restaurants, grocery and clothing stores, offices and houses of worship, including the Makki Masjid community center at 1089 Coney Island Avenue.

Around Brooklyn College

Cafés, copy shops, food counters and small offices near the campus that opened in 1930, busy on weekdays during the semester and quiet at night and in the breaks.

Shuls, yeshivas and community buildings

Synagogues such as the Young Israel of Flatbush, founded in 1921, and the East Midwood Jewish Center, from 1924, plus schools and community centers that stand empty for long stretches each week.

What actually happens at a Midwood storefront

The thefts reported at Midwood businesses are not all break-ins. The Brooklyn Eagle reported a broad-daylight theft of about $94,000 in jewelry from a showcase at Aqua Jewels by Monica on East Fourth Street, around one in the afternoon on a Sunday. News 12 reported a June 2026 theft by a man who took a 69-year-old man’s phone and wallet from a changing room at the Makki Masjid on Coney Island Avenue just before 11:30 at night. Neither of those is a case for a siren. They are cases for a panic button behind the counter that reports silently, a camera that records the showcase and the door, and a door contact that logs who went where and when.

Overnight break-ins still happen on Brooklyn commercial strips, and they tend to follow one method. In March 2025 News 12 reported a burglar who shattered the front door of a bagel store on Avenue Z early on a Saturday, damaged three cash registers and fled, the same way the store had been hit six months earlier. “Same M.O., same thing. Break the window, come in, rummage around,” the owner, Ben Devito, said. For a store behind a glass front, that means the alarm has to react to breaking glass and to a person inside, not only to a door opening, and it has to report to someone in seconds, before the rummaging is finished.

Gates, glass and the back door: how we lay out a store alarm

For a typical Midwood storefront the plan has four layers. First, the gate: a heavy-duty contact on the roll-down gate tells the panel the moment it is lifted, which is the earliest warning you can get on a closed store. Second, the glass: acoustic glass-break detectors placed to hear the whole front window, so that a smashed pane behind an open-mesh gate is caught even if the gate itself is never lifted. Third, the interior: a motion detector aimed across the sales floor toward the register and the back, so that someone who gets in by any route is detected crossing the room. Fourth, everything else: the back door, the cellar door or hatch, the roof hatch and any rear window, each on its own zone so the report tells you where the entry was.

Employees get their own codes, so the opening and closing history shows who armed and disarmed and when. If you have a manager who opens at seven and a cashier who closes at nine, the app shows both, and a store that was never armed on a Tuesday night shows up as an alert the next morning rather than as a surprise after a loss.

Food stores, bakeries and pizzerias

The food businesses on Avenue J, Avenue M and Kings Highway have ovens, walk-in refrigerators and long hours, and a few specific problems. Heat from ovens and the blast of a walk-in door can confuse an ordinary motion detector, so we use detectors rated for those conditions and aim them away from the heat sources. Many of these stores also close for Shabbos and holidays and open again late Saturday night or Sunday, which means a full day or more when no one is inside. The panel can carry temperature sensors for the walk-in and the freezer and a water sensor under the dishwasher or by the floor drain, and those alerts go to the owner’s phone even when the burglar side of the alarm has nothing to report. A freezer that failed on Friday afternoon is worth knowing about before Sunday.

Jewelry, electronics and stores with high-value stock

Stores that sell jewelry, watches, phones or electronics need more than a perimeter. Display cases and the safe or stock room become their own areas with their own codes, given only to the people who need them. A shock sensor on the safe and a contact on the stock room door report even when the rest of the store is open for business. Panic buttons go under the counter and, where the owner wants one, on a pendant, and they report silently so that nobody in the store is put at greater risk. For these stores we strongly suggest central station monitoring rather than alerts to a phone, because the station can call the police while you are still dealing with what is in front of you.

Shuls, yeshivas and community buildings

Houses of worship and schools in Midwood are full for a few hours and empty for many more. In 2022 UJA-Federation of New York set up a $250,000 fund through its Community Security Initiative for small synagogues in Brooklyn, naming Midwood among the neighborhoods, to pay for new doors, locks and video cameras. An alarm is the piece that tells someone when those doors are opened at the wrong time. We divide these buildings into areas, the sanctuary, the office, the storage room, the classroom wing, so that each can be armed when it is empty while others are in use, and we set the call list to people who live within a few minutes’ walk. For congregations that keep Shabbos, we plan arming around timers and schedules set before Shabbos and discuss the details with the rabbi or the board, because we install alarms, we do not rule on how they are used.

Compliance notes for Midwood businesses and buildings

  • The siren must shut off on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes of going off. Every siren we install is set to do that, and we can shorten it for a store with apartments above.
  • The installer must hold a state license. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ask any company for its license number; ours is #12000287431.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, but a building that is required to have a fire alarm system needs one designed and filed for that purpose. We tell you which applies before quoting.
  • Landmarked blocks have exterior rules. North of Avenue H, in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District designated in 2008, the Landmarks Preservation Commission asks that equipment stay off the primary facade where possible, attach to mortar joints rather than decorative surfaces, and be “as small as possible and painted to blend with surrounding building material.” Interior alarm work is not affected.
  • Insurers may ask for paperwork. Brick Underground reported that most insurers credit a centrally monitored burglar, fire or low-temperature alarm and that the installer should provide a certificate of installation. We provide one on request.
  • Repeated false alarms have costs. A monitored smoke detector that trips again and again brings the fire department again and again, and homeowners on the Brownstoner forum have described summonses after repeated incidents. We place smoke detectors away from kitchens and bathrooms and use heat detectors where steam or cooking would cause trouble.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Midwood: Questions Answered

These are the questions Midwood homeowners, renters, store owners and building committees bring to us, grouped by the situation they come from. The answers reflect how we install; where a rule or a source is involved, we say which one.

Houses at night

The Avenue J burglar in 2015 put the window screen back after he climbed in. Would a window sensor still have caught him?

Yes. A contact sits on the window sash and its frame, not on the screen, so it reports the moment the sash is raised whether or not anything outside looks disturbed. With the house in its night setting, that report sounds the siren at once, with no entry delay, before the person climbing in is fully through the window. A screen that has been taken off and replaced fools a person looking at the window from inside; it does not fool the magnet.

Our parents sleep in a ground-floor bedroom off the kitchen. How do we arm the downstairs at night without boxing them in?

We leave the motion detectors near their room off in the night setting and rely on contacts on every outside door and window, so they can walk to the kitchen or the bathroom without tripping anything. Their own bedroom window gets a contact like the rest. A keypad or a fob near their bed lets them disarm if they want to step out to the porch, and a panic button on the same panel lets them call for help if something is wrong, whether it is a stranger at the window or a fall.

On a tight budget, what comes first: the back windows, the side alley windows or a motion detector in the kitchen?

For a Midwood house the back and side windows reachable from the ground come first, because that is where the reported entries happened and because contacts catch an intruder before he is inside rather than after. A single motion detector in the room a burglar must cross from the back to the stairs is the next step, and it also covers any window you could not afford to put a contact on. Upper-floor windows over a porch roof or a rear extension come third. A good starter system can be extended later without replacing anything.

Several generations under one roof

My married daughter’s family lives on our second floor and uses the same front door. Do we need one alarm or two?

Usually one panel divided into areas. The shared front door and hallway become a common area that arms when both families are in for the night or both are out, and each floor is its own area with its own codes. Your daughter can arm her floor when she leaves for work while you are still home downstairs, and neither family sees the other’s comings and goings in the app unless you both want that. One panel and one monitoring account cost less than two separate systems and keep the siren and the call list consistent.

Grandparents come to stay for Yom Tov and do not know any codes. What do we give them instead?

A key fob with their own name on the panel, set up before they arrive. A fob arms and disarms with one press, so nobody has to remember a number, and the history shows the fob was used rather than a family code. When they go home you can switch it off from the app. If they will be in the house while everyone else is out, we can also put a simple panic button by their bed.

When our whole family and the neighbors are away at the same time, who should the monitoring station call?

Someone who will actually be in the neighborhood and can answer the phone, which on some holiday weeks is not the usual list. Before a long trip, update the call list in the app with a relative or a friend who is staying in Midwood or nearby, and tell the station how long you will be away. If nobody on the list can go to the house, the station still dispatches police on a confirmed alarm; the call list is about getting someone with a key there afterward to check the door, the windows and the water.

Apartments and co-ops

Our Ocean Avenue co-op already has keyless entry at the lobby. Does an apartment alarm add anything?

The lobby system controls who gets into the building; it says nothing about who gets into your apartment, and in buildings with dozens of residents, deliveries and contractors, the risk is more often an apartment door than the front door. An apartment alarm reports your own door opening when you are out, covers a terrace door or a low window, and can carry smoke, carbon monoxide and water sensors that alert your phone. It does not touch the building’s system or common areas, so it usually needs nothing from the board beyond the usual contractor notice.

We rent on the fourth floor of a Kings Highway walk-up with a fire escape outside the bedroom. What would a small system cover?

The apartment door, every window on the fire escape, and one motion detector in the room you would have to cross from the fire escape to the rest of the apartment. That is often four or five devices and a keypad, mounted with adhesive so nothing is drilled into the landlord’s walls, and it reports over cellular so it works even if the building has no phone line. When you move, the system comes with you.

Stores and offices on the avenues

Our Avenue M bakery is closed from Friday afternoon until Saturday night. Can the alarm watch the ovens and the walk-in too?

Yes. Temperature sensors in the walk-in and the freezer and a water sensor by the floor drain or the dishwasher go on the same panel as the burglar zones, and they send alerts to your phone whether the alarm is armed or not. If the walk-in warms up on Friday evening you know about it then, not when you open on Sunday. We can also add a heat detector in the oven area, which is less likely than a smoke detector to report steam or flour dust as a fire.

We sell jewelry on Kings Highway. Should the panic button sound the siren or stay silent?

For a robbery in progress, silent, and that is how we program it unless you ask otherwise. A silent panic signal goes straight to the monitoring station, which calls the police without anyone in the store being seen to raise the alarm. The siren is for break-ins when the store is closed. Many jewelry and electronics stores have both: silent buttons under the counter and on a pendant, and a loud siren for the overnight zones.

Older alarms already on the wall

Our house came with a hardwired alarm from the 1990s. Is any of it worth keeping?

Often the wires are. The contacts on the doors and windows and the cable that runs to them inside the walls usually last decades, and reusing them saves the cost of new sensors on every opening. The panel, the keypad and the dialer are a different story: most systems that age report over a landline, have no app, and use parts that are hard to find. A panel replacement on existing wiring is a line on our Brooklyn list, and we test every old zone before we promise to reuse it.

The alarm company named on our keypad went out of business years ago. Can someone else take the system over?

Usually, yes. If the panel is in good order, we reset it to a new master code, which on many panels means opening the cabinet and working with it directly, then fit a cellular communicator and register it with a monitoring station or with your phones. If the panel is locked to a dealer we cannot get into, or too old to support a communicator, we tell you so at the visit and quote a replacement instead of billing hours trying.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Midwood

Ask a search engine’s AI summary or a chatbot about home alarms in this part of Brooklyn and you get answers written for a generic American suburb, or for a Manhattan apartment, and Midwood is neither. These are the claims we hear repeated most often from customers who read them first, and what the local record and our own work here show instead.

“Most burglars come through the front door, so a door sensor and a doorbell camera cover you.”

Front and back doors are common entry points in national surveys, and a contact on every outside door is part of every system we install. But the Midwood burglaries that reached the news went a different way. The South Midwood break-ins described at the 70th Precinct Community Council in 2012 came through unlocked first-floor windows. The Avenue J burglar in 2015 came through a pried window. The 2017 pattern on East 31st and East 32nd streets came through back windows reachable from the yards.

A doorbell camera on the front porch saw none of those. It watches the side of the house that faces neighbors and traffic, the side a careful burglar avoids.

So a Midwood quote that covers only the doors is incomplete. Ours start with the back and sides, count every window that can be reached from the ground, a porch roof or an extension, and price contacts or glass-break coverage for each one.

“An alarm is something you turn on when you leave the house.”

That is how most people use one, and it is why so many alarms did nothing on the nights described in those reports. The Avenue J family had eleven people in the house. The East 31st Street family was asleep. A system that is only armed when the house is empty protects the house during the hours it is least likely to be entered on these blocks.

Every panel we install has a night setting that arms the doors and windows while leaving upstairs motion detectors off. The difficulty is not the technology but the habit, and we put real effort into making it easy: a keypad or a fob by the bed, a reminder on the app at the time you usually go to sleep, and a keypad that tells you which window is still open before you arm.

“Midwood is one of the safer neighborhoods, so an alarm is unnecessary.”

The numbers are genuinely good. The 70th Precinct recorded 80 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, down from 117 the year before, and burglary there is down 96.8 percent over thirty-three years. We would not tell anyone in Midwood that their block is dangerous.

But low averages do not help the household that is hit, and the same report shows 361 grand larcenies so far this year, theft without forced entry, which an alarm’s door history and user codes help to trace. In February 2026, News 12 reported a group forcing their way into a home near East 13th Street and Avenue P at about 1:35 in the morning; a 31-year-old man was struck in the head.

An alarm is not a response to fear. It is a way to know, within seconds, that something has happened, and to have the police and the right family member called while it is still happening.

“Wireless alarms stop working when the Wi-Fi goes down.”

Some self-install kits do depend on the home’s internet connection, and if the router is unplugged, the power is out or the service is down, they cannot send an alert. Vincent Ingrisano of Alarms R Us put it plainly to Brick Underground: “The point is, you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.”

The panels we install report over a built-in cellular communicator, with a backup battery that keeps the panel and the communicator running through a power cut. The wireless part of a wireless alarm is the link between the sensors and the panel inside the house, which does not use your Wi-Fi at all.

“Professional alarm installation costs thousands of dollars.”

Some does. Brick Underground reported basic professional installations in New York at $600 to $5,000 depending on the number of sensors, and custom hardwired installations at $6,000 to $30,000.

Most Midwood homes do not need that. A wireless system on our Brooklyn list starts at $600, most houses fall between $600 and $1,200, and complete protection for a large house with smoke and carbon monoxide detection starts at $2,500. A panel replacement that reuses the wiring of an old system runs $800 to $1,500.

What raises a Midwood price is the number of reachable windows, not the size of the house, which is why we count them from your photos before quoting.

“Any alarm work on a landmarked house needs Landmarks Commission approval.”

Most of Midwood is not landmarked. The Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, designated in 2008, sits north of Avenue H, and even there the Commission regulates the exterior, not what goes on inside the house.

An alarm is almost entirely interior: the panel in a closet, contacts on the inside of window frames, motion detectors on walls and ceilings, sirens inside. The parts that can touch the exterior are cameras, outdoor sirens and strobes and floodlights, and the Commission’s permit guidebook asks that equipment stay off the primary facade where possible and be small and painted to blend in.

For a house in the district, we keep sirens indoors, put any outdoor device at the back or side, and tell you before the visit which parts, if any, need a Landmarks application.

“Monitored alarms always bring the police and fire department, so a false alarm is just an inconvenience.”

A false alarm costs more than inconvenience. Homeowners on the Brownstoner forum have described a monitoring company that “calls the fire department every time,” and a summons after repeated incidents. In New York City, Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop on its own within fifteen minutes.

Most false alarms come from a few causes we can design around: a smoke detector next to the kitchen or a steamy bathroom, a motion detector aimed at a radiator, a pet, a door that does not quite latch, or a family member who never learned the code.

We place detectors to avoid those, give every household member a code or a fob, and review the alarm history with you after two weeks to fix anything that trips without reason.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Stand in the Backyard Before You Stand at the Keypad

The first habit our technicians bring to a Midwood survey is to start outside, at the back. Before looking at where the panel will go, they walk the driveway or the side alley to the yard and look at the house the way someone who is not supposed to be there would: which windows are hidden by the garage or the fence, which ones sit over a porch roof or the flat top of a kitchen extension, where a garbage enclosure, a grill or a stack of chairs would give someone a step up. Then they go inside and open each of those windows, because a window that has been painted shut for years needs no contact, and one that slides open with a finger needs one more than any other.

The second habit is to ask, before writing anything, how the household lives at night. Who sleeps on which floor, who gets up early, whether a parent sleeps downstairs, whether there is a dog in the kitchen, whether a tenant comes through the cellar after midnight. Those answers decide where motion detectors go and which ones stay off in the night setting. A system that matches the household gets armed every night; a system that keeps waking people up gets switched off within a month, and then it protects nobody.
