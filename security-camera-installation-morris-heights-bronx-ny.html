The Neighborhood

Why Morris Heights Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Morris Heights is a neighborhood of apartment buildings, and in an apartment building the camera's job is not to film the street. It is to record what happens in the shared spaces nobody owns personally: the vestibule, the lobby, the long hallways, the stairs and the cellar. Wikipedia places the neighborhood between West Burnside Avenue on the north, Jerome Avenue on the east, the Cross Bronx Expressway on the south and the Harlem River on the west, with University Avenue, also signed Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard, as its main street. It sits in Bronx Community Board 5, uses the ZIP codes 10452 and 10453, and is covered by the 46th Precinct from 2120 Ryer Avenue, with NYCHA property handled by Police Service Area 7. Wikipedia describes the housing as mostly five- and six-story buildings with a number of public housing developments. City lot data we pulled for the twenty tax blocks south of Burnside Avenue counts 513 private buildings with three or more homes, holding 17,861 apartments, before any NYCHA building is added. About 208 of those private buildings are prewar five- and six-story walk-ups with roughly 9,272 apartments between them. Eight are private high-rises built between 1963 and 1974. Ninety-eight went up after 2000. And the NYU Furman Center puts homeownership in Community District 5 at 7.5 percent, against roughly a third citywide. Put plainly, almost everyone here rents, and almost every camera decision is made by an owner, a managing agent, a nonprofit sponsor or a co-op board on behalf of a building full of people.

The local reporting shows where those cameras matter and what goes wrong with them. CBS New York reported several men walking into buildings along Davidson Avenue, at building numbers between 1985 and 2024, around one o'clock on a Friday afternoon, and damaging numerous surveillance cameras inside and outside before running off. ABC7 and News 12 covered the September 2026 fire at 1484 Montgomery Avenue, where a resident's doorbell camera captured a man carrying something with an open flame through a hallway and using it to light what appeared to be a cardboard box before heading into an apartment; one person died and others, including firefighters, were hurt. HPD records showed 151 open violations at the building, and the Buildings Department said inspectors were checking whether the fire doors worked. CBS New York also reported a burglary at 1759 Montgomery Avenue on a Friday, March 22, after which police released surveillance photos of a man and a woman walking through the lobby and out the front door, the man carrying a television and the woman a large bag. PIX11 obtained surveillance video of the moment part of 1915 Billingsley Terrace collapsed in December 2023. And News 12 reported that Virginia Alvarez was attacked on her way to work on October 15, 2025, in an assault captured on surveillance video. None of this ranks one block against another, and none of it describes most buildings here. What it does show is that in Morris Heights cameras get attacked, hallway footage ends up as evidence in the worst kind of case, and the clips that matter are often the ones nobody planned for.

History explains why so many buildings here carry wiring from different eras. The neighborhood grew after the city annexed this part of the Bronx in 1874, and by the 1920s Irish and Jewish families were moving into new five- and six-story buildings along University Avenue. From 1896 until after the Second World War, the Gas Engine & Power Company and the Charles L. Seabury Company built boats on the waterfront. The Cross Bronx Expressway cut through just to the south, and by the 1970s Morris Heights was at the center of the South Bronx crisis, with arson leaving many buildings damaged or gone. From the late 1970s on, buildings were rehabilitated by the city and nonprofits, subsidized townhouses went up on empty lots, and many Dominican families moved over from Washington Heights. More recently, the 2018 Jerome Avenue rezoning and a run of new projects, from 1884 University Avenue to Starhill on Featherbed Lane and a senior building planned at 105 West Tremont Avenue, have added new buildings with their own developer-installed camera systems. A single block can hold a 1920s walk-up with cable left from three owners, a rehab with a 1990s intercom and a new building with a network camera system, and each one needs a different plan.

Four sets of rules shape where cameras go. Permission comes first: renters need the owner's agreement before putting anything in a hallway or lobby, Brick Underground and JustAnswer readers ask about this constantly, and in public housing the lease decides, as a Parkside Houses tenant learned when she was served an eviction notice that said her cameras breached it. Licensing comes second: the NYS Department of State licenses companies that install security systems, with a waiver for licensed master electricians working in their own municipality. Landmarks come third: the Messiah Home for Children at 1777 Andrews Avenue is a designated city landmark, and exterior work there would likely need Landmarks Preservation Commission review. Fire and privacy come last and matter most: nothing we mount touches a self-closing door, its closer or its frame; every opening through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop; no camera goes inside an apartment; and hallway views are framed so they record the corridor, not the inside of someone's home.

  • Cameras have been the target, in daylight: the Davidson Avenue damage happened around 1 p.m., inside and outside several buildings, so housings, mounting heights and overlapping views matter as much as resolution.
  • Hallway footage has become evidence in a fatal fire: the clip from 1484 Montgomery Avenue came from one tenant's doorbell, not a building system, which is luck rather than a plan.
  • A mostly private, mostly rented stock: 513 private buildings with three or more homes and 17,861 apartments on the blocks south of Burnside, with homeownership in the district at 7.5 percent.
  • Public cameras cover public housing only: Sedgwick Houses, seven buildings of 14 to 15 stories between Undercliff and University Avenues, received new CCTV and entrance work under NYCHA's $24 million security program completed in 2023. Those cameras watch NYCHA property, not the private walk-up across the street.
  • Some lobbies already have an audience: 1520 Sedgwick Avenue, where hip-hop is traced to a party on August 11, 1973, draws visitors, and a CUNY blog notes it carries an intercom and heavy camera coverage for that reason.
  • Old and new wiring side by side: prewar walk-ups, city rehabs from the late 1970s on, 1960s and 1970s high-rises such as River Park Towers at Richman Plaza, and post-2018 buildings off Jerome Avenue all sit within a few blocks.

What Morris Heights owners often add next. A building that starts with lobby and hallway cameras usually comes back to ask whether the entry door can go on fobs, so that a lost key no longer means a stranger with access, or whether the lobby view can show on the intercom panel. Planning for a building where cameras have been attacked

The Davidson Avenue report tells us something specific about how cameras fail here: not slowly, from weather or age, but suddenly, from people who walked in and went for them. That changes the shopping list. A plastic consumer camera on a short bracket at seven feet is easy to twist, pull or break with a single swing. A metal-bodied dome in an impact-rated housing, mounted at nine or ten feet on a ceiling rather than a wall, takes real effort and time, and time is what gives the next camera along a chance to record the face of the person doing it. It also changes the budget. An owner who has had cameras smashed should plan for one or two spare housings in the cellar, so a damaged position can be back up the same day rather than after an order arrives. And it changes the paperwork: we name each camera by floor and position on the recorder, so when the log shows four cameras going dark in a two-minute window, anyone reading it later can see the path someone walked through the building.

Planning for a building that has had a fire

A building that has had a fire, or sits on a block that has, usually asks two things of a camera system: show what happened in the hallways, and do not make the building less safe. The second comes first. Camera cable that passes from floor to floor goes through sealed openings, and every hole we make through a rated wall or floor is closed with rated firestop, so a camera job never leaves a path for smoke. Nothing is mounted on a stair door, a fire door, its closer or its frame, and no camera or cable is placed where it could stop a self-closing door from latching. The first question, showing what happened, depends on where cameras are and where the footage lives. A corridor camera at each end of a long hallway shows who walks the hall; a camera at each stair landing shows who goes up and down. And because a fire on one floor can reach a closet or a cellar room, the recorder belongs somewhere separated from the hallways, with copies of flagged clips sent off the property where the internet line allows. If the building's fire alarm company agrees, the camera recorder can also be told when the alarm sounds, so it marks and keeps the minutes around it.

Planning for footage someone else will judge

Footage that matters in Morris Heights is often reviewed by someone who has never been in the building: a fire marshal, a detective, an insurance adjuster, a lawyer or a judge. They need to know which camera recorded a clip, where that camera points, whether the clock was right and whether anything was changed. So the plan includes the boring parts. Each camera is named by floor, door and direction. The recorder syncs its clock to an internet time server and keeps it there. Entrance and hallway cameras record continuously, not just when they sense motion, so there are no gaps in the minutes that matter. The drive is sized for the number of days the owner actually needs. And the handover includes a short lesson in exporting a clip in the recorder's own format with its verification file, which is the version investigators prefer over a phone video of the monitor.

Prewar walk-ups: long hallways, turning stairs, nowhere to hide cable

In the 1920s walk-ups that line University and Davidson Avenues, the camera plan is decided by the stair hall. These buildings rarely have a clean vertical chase from cellar to roof, and the plaster walls are not something to open floor by floor. Where a pipe chase or stacked closets exist, cable climbs inside them. Where they do not, we use slim steel conduit painted to match the hall, fixed into the brick behind the plaster, because in a building where cameras have been attacked, a plastic raceway is just an invitation to pull. The recorder normally goes into the cellar, in a locked metal cabinet in a dry spot away from the boiler and away from any door to the street or yard. Hallway cameras on each floor are mounted at the ends of the corridor, high, looking along it. Long hallways in these buildings often bend around the stair or an air shaft, and each leg of the bend usually needs its own view. We trace the old wiring first, because these cellars carry intercom pairs and abandoned camera coax from several owners, and the tenants' buzzers have to keep working while we are there.

City-rehabbed buildings: dropped ceilings and the rehab's leftovers

Many Morris Heights buildings were gutted and rebuilt from the late 1970s on, and the rehab usually left block partitions, dropped ceilings in the corridors and an intercom and sometimes a camera system from that era. The ceilings make cable easier to route, but they also make it easier to reach: someone can lift a tile and pull a camera's cable from above. So we fix runs to the structure with supports rather than laying them on the grid, keep the last stretch to each camera inside a short length of conduit, and set the recorder to raise an alert the instant any camera loses its connection. Where the rehab left old coax that still reaches a landing, it can sometimes carry an HD-over-coax camera instead of being torn out, which keeps the plaster and ceilings intact. Where it does not, we pull new network cable beside it and tag the old run as abandoned, so the next contractor does not waste a day on it.

Towers: riser closets, stairs nobody uses and elevator landings

The private high-rises along Sedgwick Avenue and at Richman Plaza are a different scale of job. A camera on the thirtieth floor cannot run straight back to a lobby recorder on one cable, so the system is built in sections: a managed switch in a riser closet every several floors, each powering the cameras near it, all feeding one recorder in a locked equipment room. Stair cameras in towers matter more than people expect, because stairs on upper floors can go hours without anyone passing, which makes them both a place where trouble happens and a place where a camera can be attacked without witnesses. We mount those cameras high in hard housings and set a tamper alert on each. Elevator landings in the lobby and on a few key floors usually come before every floor gets a view, and work inside the elevator cab is done with the building's elevator contractor, never around them.

New mid-rises, three-family houses and stores below apartments

Buildings that opened after the 2018 Jerome Avenue rezoning usually arrive with a developer's camera system on the lobby, entrances and package room. The common gaps are side and service doors, cellar storage and bike rooms, and the stair at the far end of the building, and we add those to the same recorder rather than building a second system. Three-family houses need far fewer cameras: the stoop, the gate, the side passage and the yard, with cable through the basement and the views stopping at the neighbors' windows. In buildings with a store downstairs, the store and the building keep separate recorders unless the owner and the tenant agree in writing to share. Both systems usually pass through the same cellar, so every cable gets a label with its owner as well as its position, and the next store tenant does not cut the building's stair camera by mistake.

Delis, pharmacies and corner stores: protect the clerk and the recorder

The corner store on University Avenue is usually a long, narrow room with one clerk, a counter near the door and a stock room or cellar at the back. Its cameras do four jobs. A face camera just inside the door, at head height, records each person as they walk in. A counter camera above and slightly in front of the register shows hands, cash and the customer. A rear camera high on the back wall looks toward the front, so nobody can work the back aisles unseen. A stock room or cellar-hatch camera shows who goes back there. A monitor at the register lets the clerk see the back of the store without leaving the counter. The recorder is the part stores most often get wrong: on a shelf behind the counter, it is the first thing a robber takes. We put it in a locked steel box above the ceiling or in the back room, on a battery backup, with clips from closing hours copied off site, so a robbery that includes the recorder does not erase itself.

Auto and body shops under the 4 train

The shops along Jerome Avenue are open boxes by day and steel boxes by night. When the roll-up doors are up, the bays are part of the street, and cameras have to handle the change from deep shade under the elevated tracks to bright daylight in the doorway. When the doors come down, the danger moves to the lot, the side gate and the roof. We cover each bay from inside the back wall, looking out toward the door, so a car's arrival and the people around it are recorded against the light rather than into it. The office and the key cabinet get their own view. The parts room, where removed parts and new stock are kept, gets a door camera. Outside, a camera high on the building watches the lot and the gate after hours, with alerts set only for closed hours. Cable runs in conduit inside the shop, away from lifts, compressors and anything that gets hot.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Morris Heights

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, exam rooms and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we check every view so a mirror or glass partition does not show one.
  • The landlord decides outside the store. Facades, sign bands, gate housings and shared halls belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement before mounting anything there.
  • City structures are off limits. Nothing private goes on lampposts, street signs, bus shelters or the elevated structure over Jerome Avenue.

Field Notes: Walking a Morris Heights Building the Way Someone Would Attack It

On a walk-through in a building like the ones on Davidson or Montgomery Avenue, the first thing I do is walk it twice. The first time I walk it as a tenant: street door, vestibule, mailboxes, elevator or stair, my hallway, my door. The second time I walk it as the person who wants the cameras gone. Where would I stand to reach the lobby camera? Which stair landing is quiet enough that nobody would see me pull a camera down? Where is the cable exposed? Where is the recorder, and could I carry it out? That second walk decides more of the plan than the camera brand ever will. Usually it shows that the lobby camera is too low, that the stair cameras have no backup view, and that the recorder is in a closet off the lobby with a lock any screwdriver would open. Then I check what happens if there is a fire: which closets are near the stairs, which doors must stay free, where cable crosses floors. Only after that do I start drawing camera positions, and I draw them in pairs.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Morris Heights: Questions Answered

When the cameras themselves are the target

Men walked into several Davidson Avenue buildings and wrecked the cameras inside and out. If someone breaks our lobby camera, what records them doing it?

Another camera that they cannot reach first. The answer is to place the important cameras in pairs, so that each one sits inside the view of another. A dome over the mailboxes, for example, is watched by a second dome high in the opposite corner of the lobby, and the second one is watched by the vestibule camera. Anyone who reaches up to smash the first is recorded, face up and close, by the second. The second camera should be the harder one to get to: higher, on the ceiling rather than the wall, in a stronger housing. The recorder logs the exact moment each camera stops sending a picture, so the order in which cameras went dark shows the route through the building. One lobby camera on its own, however good, records an attack only until the moment it is hit.

Vandals cannot reach the camera on our stair landing, but the cable runs down the wall where anyone can reach it. How should that run be protected?

Put it in steel conduit, or better, get it off the wall entirely. Cutting a cable is quieter and quicker than breaking a camera, and it blinds the camera just the same. Where a pipe chase, a closet or a ceiling cavity can carry the run, that comes first, with only the last few inches to the camera exposed, and that last stretch runs into the back of the camera's mounting box rather than out of its side. Where nothing is hidden, as in many prewar stair halls, we fix steel conduit to the brick behind the plaster, painted to match. Plastic raceway can be pried off with fingers. The recorder also raises an alert when a camera loses its connection, so a cut cable gets noticed in minutes, not weeks.

The installer keeps saying "vandal-resistant." What does that rating actually mean, and is it worth paying for here?

It usually refers to an impact rating, written as IK followed by a number, measured by dropping a set weight onto the housing from a set height. IK10 is the top common rating and means the housing survived a strong blow without breaking open. It does not mean a camera cannot be damaged; a determined person with a hammer and time can get through anything. What it buys is time and difficulty, and in a lobby with a second camera watching, time is what produces a clear face. In buildings that have had cameras attacked, we use metal-bodied, impact-rated domes in the lobby, vestibule and stairs, and save lighter cameras for places nobody can reach, such as high exterior corners. It is worth it on the positions people walk past; it is wasted on a camera twenty feet up.

When the footage becomes the evidence

The only video of the man lighting the hallway fire at 1484 Montgomery Avenue came from one tenant's doorbell. Where should a building's recorder sit so a fire does not destroy its own footage?

Away from the hallways and stairs, in a room with a solid door, and with a copy of the important minutes somewhere else. Fires often start in hallways and stairs, and the closet next to the stair is a common place for a recorder, which is exactly the wrong place. A locked room in the cellar, separated from the stairs, is usually better, provided it is dry and away from the boiler. The second safeguard is off-site copies: with a reasonable internet line, the recorder can send clips flagged by alerts, or a short window around a fire alarm, to cloud storage or to the owner's office as they happen. Smoke darkens a hallway quickly, so the most useful pictures are often the minutes before. Continuous recording on hallway cameras makes sure those minutes exist.

Should a hallway camera show which apartment someone walks into, or is that too much?

It should show the corridor and who walks it, framed so that a person reaching a door can be seen going toward it, without staring into any apartment. That balance matters in both directions. In the Montgomery Avenue case, the report says the man headed into an apartment after lighting the box, and a view of the corridor that shows that is the kind of footage investigators use. But a camera aimed straight at one tenant's door feels like surveillance of that household, and tenants rightly object. So corridor cameras go at the ends of the hall, looking along it, where every door is a small part of a long view. Privacy masking blocks out any part of the picture that would look through an open apartment door. And who may watch that footage is limited to named people, written down.

Police released photos of the 1759 Montgomery Avenue burglars walking out the front door with a TV. Should our lobby camera be aimed at people leaving as well as arriving?

Yes, and in most lobbies that takes two positions, not one. A camera inside the vestibule facing the inner door records faces as people come in. People going out face the other way, toward the street, and are usually carrying things, looking down, or holding the door with one arm. A second camera, placed on the street-door side looking back into the lobby, records those faces and what is in their hands. In the Montgomery Avenue burglary, the useful pictures showed the man with a television and the woman with a large bag, which is departure, not arrival. The two cameras also cover each other, which helps if one is attacked. Where the lobby is too small for two, one camera to the side of the path, looking across it, catches part of both directions.

If our hallway clip ends up in a court case or an insurance dispute, how do we show it was never edited?

Export it from the recorder in the recorder's own format, with the verification file or watermark most professional recorders can produce, and keep the original on the recorder, locked against being written over. The native export carries the camera name, the start and end time and a checksum or digital signature that shows whether the file has been altered, and most manufacturers provide a free player that checks it. A phone video of the monitor, a clip re-saved through an editing app or a file sent through a messaging app loses some of that. Write down who exported it, when and onto what, and hand over a copy, not your only one. We show the super and the managing agent how to do this during the handover, using one of the building's own cameras.

Who else is looking

People come from all over to photograph our building because of its history. How do we keep our cameras focused on security without filming every visitor's tour?

Frame the cameras for the doors, not the sidewalk crowd, and be clear with residents about what is recorded and who sees it. 1520 Sedgwick Avenue is the obvious case: tourists come because of the August 1973 party in its community room, and a local blog points out that the residents never signed up to have their lobby photographed. Exterior cameras on a building like that should cover the entrance and the few yards in front of it, not the whole street where visitors stand. Inside, cameras watch the vestibule and lobby for people who actually come in. Footage stays with a short list of named accounts. And if visitors try the door or slip in behind a resident, the vestibule camera records it, which gives management something concrete to act on.

We read that the NYPD can watch NYCHA's cameras live. If we connect our private building's cameras to the internet, can police see them too?

Not on their own. New York Focus reported in 2025 that the NYPD was gaining live access to NYCHA's CCTV system, which is the housing authority's own network of more than 20,000 cameras. A private building's recorder belongs to its owner. Connecting it to the internet for remote viewing does not hand anyone outside the owner's chosen accounts a way in, provided the system is set up properly: unique passwords, two-step sign-in, current firmware and no open ports. Police can ask an owner for footage, and can obtain it through legal process, but that is a request to the owner, not a live feed. If an owner ever wants to share cameras voluntarily with a public program, that is a separate decision, made in writing, with the building's lawyer if needed.

Shops on Jerome Avenue

Our body shop on Jerome Avenue keeps its roll-up bay doors open all day. Where do cameras go so the bays are covered both open and shut?

Inside, on the back wall of each bay, looking out toward the door. With the door up, that camera sees the car, the people around it and the street behind them; with the door down, it sees the inside of the bay and anyone who gets in through a side door or the roof. A camera mounted at the doorway looking in faces the bright street all day and turns everyone into a dark shape, so we avoid that position for the main view, or use a wide dynamic range camera where it cannot be avoided. The elevated tracks over Jerome Avenue throw hard shadows across the doorway, which is another reason to test each view at different times of day. One outside camera, high on the building, watches the lot, the gate and the front of the closed doors overnight.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Morris Heights

Quick answers from search tools and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to treat every neighborhood as a suburban house with a driveway. These are claims we hear repeated, and what the local sources and the work on the ground say instead.

"Visible cameras stop vandalism."

Visible cameras deter some people some of the time, and that is worth something. It is the most common reason owners give for wanting them.

But the Davidson Avenue report describes the opposite: several men walking into buildings in the early afternoon and going straight for the cameras, inside and out. For them, a visible camera was the target, not the deterrent.

So in Morris Heights, a camera plan assumes cameras will be tested. Impact-rated housings, mounts out of easy reach, cable in conduit and a second camera covering the first turn an attack on the system into a recording of the attacker.

"A video doorbell covers your hallway."

A doorbell camera on an apartment door does see part of the hallway, and in the 1484 Montgomery Avenue fire one did record the man carrying an open flame and lighting a box. That clip mattered.

It was also luck: one tenant, one door, one angle. A doorbell sees a short stretch of corridor in front of one apartment, records on its owner's account, and may or may not be pointed at the right place when something happens. It does not cover the stairs, the other end of the hall or the lobby.

Building hallways need building cameras, at the ends of each corridor and on the stair landings, recording continuously to a recorder the owner controls, with footage kept where a fire is unlikely to reach it.

"Public housing cameras already cover the neighborhood."

NYCHA does run a large camera system: more than 20,000 cameras citywide, according to New York Focus, and Sedgwick Houses received new CCTV, lighting and entrance work under the $24 million program completed in 2023.

Those cameras watch NYCHA's own grounds, lobbies and entrances. They do not cover the private walk-up across University Avenue, its lobby, its halls or its cellar, and their footage is not something a private owner can pull up when a tenant asks.

With 513 private buildings holding 17,861 apartments on the blocks south of Burnside, most Morris Heights homes are outside any public camera's view. Their cameras have to come from their owners.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for an older building."

Wireless cameras skip the cable run, and for a renter's single indoor camera, that is often fine. Cost sites often recommend them for older buildings to avoid opening walls.

In a 1920s walk-up with brick and plaster walls, Wi-Fi signals weaken quickly from floor to floor, and a camera on the fifth-floor landing may lose connection exactly when it matters. Battery cameras also need recharging, and a camera nobody can reach to charge is a camera that will be dead in a month.

For building systems we wire cameras to one recorder over network cable that carries both power and picture. Where walls cannot be opened, cable climbs a chase, a closet or steel conduit on the wall, and the wireless option is saved for places cable truly cannot reach.

"Cloud storage means your footage is always safe."

Cloud storage does protect footage from a stolen or burned recorder, which is a real concern in buildings that have had fires or burglaries.

But uploading continuous video from twenty or thirty building cameras needs far more upload speed than a typical building internet line provides, and per-camera cloud plans add a monthly cost that grows with every camera. When the internet line drops, cloud-only cameras record nothing.

The approach that works here is local recording on a locked recorder for everything, plus cloud or off-site copies of the clips that matter most: alerts, after-hours events and the window around a fire alarm. That way the building keeps full coverage and the most important minutes survive.

"Anyone can install security cameras in New York."

Many online answers say camera installation is simple handyman work. Others say only a licensed electrician may do it. Both are incomplete.

NYC Business describes a New York State Department of State license for companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and licensed master electricians can register for a waiver that covers work in the municipality where they are licensed. New electrical work, such as a new outlet or circuit, belongs to a licensed electrician. Low-voltage camera cable powered over the network is a different kind of work.

AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance. Morris Heights managing agents, boards and insurers usually ask to see both before anyone starts, and they are right to ask.

"Most installs are a few cameras on a house, priced per camera."

Cost sites build their numbers around single-family homes with a few exterior cameras, and present a per-camera range as if it applied everywhere.

In Morris Heights, where homeownership in the district is 7.5 percent and most homes are apartments, the typical job is a building: lobby, halls on every floor, stairs, cellar and roof door. The cost is driven by floors, distance, brick and block walls, conduit where cable is exposed, recorder protection and the number of accounts, not by a camera count alone.

FAQ

Morris Heights Security Camera Installation FAQ

Someone keeps pulling the fire alarm pull station in our hallway as a prank. Can a camera show who is doing it?
+

Yes, if it is placed well. A corridor camera at the end of the hall, aimed so the pull station sits clearly in its view at a moderate distance, records whoever reaches for it, and continuous recording means the moment is there even if motion detection misses it. The camera never goes on the pull station itself, on its cover or anywhere that blocks it, and its cable stays clear of fire alarm wiring. Matching the time of the alarm to the recording is easy once the recorder's clock is synced, so the super can find the clip in a minute.

Can our cameras start saving clips automatically the moment the building's fire alarm goes off?
+

Often, yes, with the fire alarm company's agreement. Many recorders accept an alarm input, and a relay from the fire alarm panel can tell the recorder that the alarm has sounded, so it marks and locks the minutes before and after on every camera and can send those clips off site. The connection has to be made by or approved by the company that services the fire alarm, so the alarm system is never affected, and tying the systems together can raise permit questions, which we check before any work. Continuous recording stays on regardless.

There is a crack in the outside wall of our building. Can a camera keep watch on it?
+

A camera can record what the wall looks like day to day, but it is not structural monitoring, and nobody should treat it that way. PIX11 obtained surveillance video of the moment part of 1915 Billingsley Terrace collapsed in December 2023, which shows cameras can capture a failure, not prevent or predict it. A crack that is growing needs a licensed engineer and, where required, the Buildings Department. What a camera can do is show when work crews were on site and give the engineer dated pictures. Call the engineer first.

The building next door probably filmed what happened on our sidewalk. How do we ask for that clip before it gets erased?
+

Ask quickly, in writing, and be specific. Many recorders keep only a week or two before writing over the oldest footage, so contact the owner, managing agent or super the same day if you can, give the exact date, time window and the camera you think saw it, and ask them to save that section. They are not obliged to hand it to you, but most will preserve it if police or an insurer may need it. If police are involved, tell the detective which buildings have cameras facing the spot, since investigators can request footage directly.

Can the super look back at last night's footage without the recorder stopping recording?
+

Yes. A properly set up network recorder keeps recording every camera while someone plays back, searches or exports footage, either at the recorder's monitor or from a phone or computer. What does interrupt recording is turning the recorder off, unplugging it, resetting it or pulling its drive to look at it elsewhere, none of which is necessary. We set the super's account to allow playback and export on the cameras he needs, and show him how to search by camera and time so he is not scrolling through a whole night.

The building across the street already has a camera pointed at our entrance. Do we still need our own?
+

Usually, yes. A camera across the street sees your entrance from a distance, at an angle, through traffic and parked cars, and it records to someone else's recorder on someone else's schedule. It rarely gives a clear face, never sees inside your vestibule, lobby or halls, and you cannot get the footage when you need it without asking. Your own camera inside the door, at head height, records faces up close, keeps the footage under your control, and covers the spaces you are responsible for. The neighbor's view is a useful bonus, not a substitute.

Our hallways bend around the stair in an L shape. Does each leg need its own camera?
+

In most cases, yes. A camera at one end of an L-shaped corridor sees only to the corner, and anything past it is invisible. One option is a camera at the far end of each leg, both looking toward the corner, which covers every door. Another is a dual-lens camera mounted at the corner itself, with one lens down each leg, which saves a cable run. The second option suits corners high enough to keep the camera out of reach; in a low prewar ceiling, two separate cameras at the ends are harder for anyone to get at.

After our cameras were ripped down, can the new ones go into the old mounting holes so we do not drill more brick?
+

Sometimes, but only if the old holes are sound and the old position was a good one. Holes torn out in an attack are often cracked or enlarged, and a new camera anchored in damaged brick or plaster is easier to pull off a second time. We check each hole, reuse those that will hold a proper anchor, and fill and seal the rest. Often the old position was the reason the camera was easy to reach, and moving it higher or onto the ceiling is worth a few new holes. We show you each change before drilling.

Our old recorder will not start up, and the footage of an incident is on it. Can that footage still be recovered?
+

Often, yes, if the drive itself is not damaged. Many recorder failures are the power supply or the board, not the hard drive, and the drive can be moved into a working recorder of the same make or read with the manufacturer's tools. Do not reset the recorder, format the drive or keep switching it on and off. Label it, note the date and time of the incident, and keep it somewhere dry. If the drive has failed, data recovery specialists may still help. Either way, the replacement recorder should go somewhere safer than the one that died.

When a tenant opens their apartment door, our hallway camera can see into the apartment. Can that part of the picture be blocked?
+

Yes. Most professional cameras and recorders support privacy masking, which blacks out chosen areas of the picture permanently, so the doorway of each apartment can be covered while the rest of the corridor records normally. The better fix is placement: a camera at the end of the hall, looking along it, sees each doorway edge-on, so even an open door shows very little of the inside. We combine both, then check every view with doors open during the handover, so tenants can see for themselves what the camera does and does not show.
