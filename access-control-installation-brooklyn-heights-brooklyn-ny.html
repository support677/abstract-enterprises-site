Access control installation in Brooklyn Heights is mostly a question of where things are allowed to go. Nearly every house and apartment building here sits inside New York City’s first historic district, so a reader, keypad or door station has to fit beside a doorway the Landmarks Preservation Commission cares about, and the cable has to stay off the front of the building. Behind those doors are very different users: a family in a single brownstone, shareholders in a 78-unit post-war co-op whose doorman works part of the day, students in a converted hotel, law offices near Court Street, and shops on Montague Street and Atlantic Avenue with glass front doors. We plan each entrance around who uses it, what the Landmarks rules permit at that opening, and what the door must do when the power fails.

The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Access Control

Brooklyn Heights is small and dense. Wikipedia puts it at about a third of a square mile, bounded by Old Fulton Street, Cadman Plaza West, Atlantic Avenue and the river edge along the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway, with 25,092 residents at the 2020 census, all in ZIP code 11201 and Community District 2. The lots were laid out at 25 by 100 feet after 1823, the neighborhood was almost fully built by 1890, and after the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883 and the subway arrived in 1908 many of the big single-family houses were divided into apartments and rooming houses. That history explains the door problem we are called about most often: a front door built for one household that now serves three, four or six, plus a ground-floor door under the stoop that someone else uses, and a cellar that every household reaches.

The second thing that shapes every job is the historic district. The Historic Districts Council records the Brooklyn Heights Historic District’s designation on November 23, 1965, the first historic district in New York City, and The Cultural Landscape Foundation describes it as roughly 140 acres. Inside it, door equipment is a Landmarks matter. The Commission’s Permit Guidebook says that light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells “must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening,” that a maximum of one intercom panel is allowed per door opening, and that equipment “cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists.” The guidebook does not mention card readers or keypads by name, so in practice we treat a reader at the door the way staff treat an intercom panel: one compact device, in the door opening or right beside it, sized and finished to suit the house, with its cable hidden.

The third thing is the mix of buildings. Between the rowhouses stand mid-century co-ops of real size. CityRealty lists 100 Remsen Street as a 1950 co-op of eight floors and 78 apartments with a part-time doorman and a live-in super, and Nest Seekers lists 60 Remsen Street as a 1956 co-op of ten floors and 78 apartments with a full-time doorman. Buildings like these have a lobby, an elevator, a laundry, storage and a bike room, and a staffing pattern that leaves some hours of the day with nobody at the door. Access control decides what the front door does in those hours, who can reach the cellar, and how a fob gets cancelled when a shareholder sells.

Then there are the institutions. The former Hotel St. George, which Wikipedia describes as once the largest hotel in the city with 2,632 rooms, has been partly co-ops since 1984 and partly student housing since the late 1990s. The St. George Towers residence at 100 Henry Street lists a 24-hour security desk at the main entry and key-card entry to the building and rooms. Brooklyn Law School says a school ID must be shown every time someone enters its main building at 250 Joralemon Street and Feil Hall at 205 State Street. Hundreds of residents and staff moving through card-controlled doors every day is a different job again from a rowhouse stoop, and it lives in the same few blocks.

The burglary reporting we found is older, but it points to doors, not to dramatic crimes. DNAinfo reported seven burglaries in the neighborhood in the first week of January 2017, including one where a key had been left in a mailbox at Hicks and State Streets. Brownstoner reported a 2011 Brooklyn Heights Association email about five daytime break-ins in four days, all through unlocked doors. On the commercial side, the Brooklyn Eagle reported in July 2022 that thieves broke the glass front door of a phone store at 160 Montague Street before 4 a.m., and News 12 reported in March 2024 that two people broke through the glass of a convenience store on Atlantic Avenue while two others kept watch. We found no 2025 or 2026 burglary reporting specific to Brooklyn Heights, so we do not claim a trend. What the stories share is a door that was left unlocked, a key that was not where it should be, or a glass door with nothing behind it.

Mail and packages are the everyday version of the same problem. In April 2018 the Brooklyn Heights Association warned, as Brownstoner reported, that “in recent months, there has been a raft of mail being illegally removed from street corner mailboxes in Brooklyn Heights.” Street mailboxes are the Postal Service’s business, not ours, but the incident made many buildings look again at their own vestibules, where parcels and letters sit between the street door and the inner door. A street door that locks every time, and a package room or mail area that only residents and couriers can open, is the practical answer we can give.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Brooklyn Heights’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware, and in Brooklyn Heights the building also decides who has to approve it. These are the six kinds of property we see most here.

Single-family rowhouses

Brownstone and brick houses in the Greek Revival, Italianate, Gothic Revival and later styles that fill streets such as Willow, Pierrepont, Remsen and Joralemon. Usually a controlled parlor door at the top of the stoop, a garden door beneath it, and a contact on the cellar or rear door, with the controller in the cellar.

Rowhouses divided into apartments

Houses split into flats after the bridge and the subway arrived, many still with one street door for every household. A station with a call button per apartment, individual fobs, and an inner vestibule door that keeps parcels and mail out of the stair hall.

Late Federal houses in the north

The oldest houses, in the northern blocks of the neighborhood, with wood doorways and narrow surrounds. Little wall to mount on, so the lock usually goes into the door itself and the station sits in the opening rather than on the brick.

Mid-century and prewar co-ops

Elevator buildings like the two on Remsen Street, with lobbies, laundries, bike rooms and a doorman for some or all of the day. Networked readers on several doors, schedules tied to staffing, and board-approved privacy rules.

Converted hotels and institutions

The Hotel St. George complex, student residences and school and law school buildings, where cards are already a way of life. Readers that tie into an existing card format, a staffed desk, and doors that release with the fire alarm.

Carriage houses and newer buildings

Converted stables on lanes such as Grace Court Alley, and newer multi-family buildings including the planned conversion at 25–30 Columbia Heights. Surface-mounted stations on the lanes, and full lobby, amenity and package systems in the larger buildings.

What the materials mean for the install

Brownstone is softer than it looks and does not forgive a mistake. The Landmarks guidebook says equipment must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and that is how we would mount it anyway: in a joint or on the wood of the door frame, so that removing it later leaves a joint to repoint rather than a chipped carving. Brick rowhouses and post-war buildings take fixings in the mortar the same way.

Old wood doors are the delicate case. A parlor door that has had three or four generations of locks bored into its edge may not have solid wood left for a strike or mortise lock, so we fill or splice that section before anything electric goes in, or move the lock to the frame. Iron areaway gates and railings are often original or replicated to match, and the guidebook rules out attaching through cast iron, so a gate lock goes on a plain bar or the gate post, or the gate stays mechanical and the controlled door is the one behind it. Lobby doors in the mid-century co-ops are usually aluminum or steel with glass, where the narrow stiles leave little room for a lock and the electric hardware goes into an exit device or the frame header.

One Panel, One Opening: Fob Entry on a Landmarked Heights Doorway

The Permit Guidebook sentence that matters most on a rowhouse is short: “A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening.” On a brownstone that already has a working buzzer panel beside the parlor door, that leaves no room for a separate reader box. So the plan starts with what is on the doorway now and what can come off it. In most houses the answer is to remove the old buzzer panel and put one combined device in its place: a station that is the intercom, the camera and the fob or code reader in a single housing, set in the same spot so the doorway does not gain a second object.

Where on the doorway it goes is the next decision. The guidebook asks for equipment within or adjacent to the door opening, compatible in design, size and finish with the building, and fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork. On a typical Heights stoop that means one of three places: the flat face of the door frame inside the opening, the plain masonry return beside the door, or, for the garden door, the wall under the stoop, which the guidebook names among its examples of discreet locations along with beneath a band course, behind an areaway wall or fence, and behind plantings. We bring finish options to the site visit, usually a dark bronze or black faceplate on a brownstone and brushed steel only where the existing hardware is already steel.

Then the cable. The guidebook says conduit must be concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding, that staff may approve a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit, and that surface conduit must be painted to match. On a rowhouse we almost never need even that much. The cable enters through the frame or a mortar joint directly behind the station and runs down inside the wall or the stoop structure to the cellar, where it meets the controller. For a lock in the door itself, power crosses from frame to door through a concealed hinge or a small transfer device on the hinge edge, which is invisible with the door shut.

Who applies, and when the work happens

Exterior work on a landmarked building needs a Landmarks permit, and the guidebook says staff can issue permits for health, safety and utility equipment when the general criteria are met, with anything outside them going to the Commissioners at a public hearing. The application is the owner’s, because the permit attaches to the building, but we supply what it needs: photographs of the doorway, a marked-up photo showing exactly where the station goes, the product cut sheet with dimensions and finish, and a note on the cable route. Interior work, such as a reader on the cellar door or the inner vestibule door, is generally not visible from the street, but if your building is in the district we still ask you to check with the Commission before we start. We do not mount anything on the front of a landmarked house until the owner has the approval in hand.

What stays on the door

The original mechanical key lock stays and keeps working, so nobody is locked out by a dead battery or a forgotten code. Original knockers, mail slots and bells stay where they are; where a bell pull is decorative and no longer works, we leave it alone rather than wire into it. If the house has a second, inner door in the vestibule, that door often becomes the controlled one, because a reader there sits indoors, out of the weather and off the facade altogether, while the outer door stays on its key during the day.

The Hours Nobody Is at the Desk: Co-op Lobbies on Remsen Street and Beyond

Two co-ops a block apart on Remsen Street show the range. One, by its CityRealty listing, has a part-time doorman and a live-in super for 78 apartments; the other, by Nest Seekers, has a full-time doorman for the same number. Many buildings here sit between those two: a doorman in the evening, a porter during the day, a super who lives in but is not at the door. The question for the board is not whether to have a fob system, but what the street door should do during each part of the day, and who answers it when nobody is in the lobby.

We usually build the lobby around two states. While the doorman is on duty, the outer door can stay on its normal closer, with staff buzzing visitors in from the desk and residents using fobs only if they choose. When the shift ends, the system switches to resident mode on a schedule: the street door relocks every time it closes, every resident uses a fob or a phone key, and visitors reach the apartment they are calling through a video station that rings the resident’s phone or apartment monitor. The state Multiple Dwelling Law expects the entrance of a newer building to be self-closing and self-locking and kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, which fits that two-state pattern exactly.

Behind the lobby are the rooms that cause most of the complaints: the bike room, storage cages, the laundry and the package area. Habitat’s 2013 account of one Manhattan co-op’s system describes the approach we use here too: access targeted by door, so only residents who bike can open the bike room, and outside workers such as dog walkers and cleaners can open only the doors their jobs need. That co-op’s super put the benefit plainly: “As soon as I’ve used a key fob, [the usage is] going to come up on this computer.” In a Heights co-op the same log tells the board which fob opened the bike room on the night a bicycle disappeared, which is usually the question that started the project.

What a board has to decide before we install

The decisions that hold a project up are rarely technical. A board needs to agree who administers the system (the managing agent, the super, or a board member), who may look at the entry records and for what, how many fobs each apartment gets and what a replacement costs, and what happens to a sublet tenant’s fobs when the sublet ends. In a building with rental apartments the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law adds written requirements on top: a privacy and retention policy given to tenants, consent, a limit on what data is collected, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. We give boards a one-page decision sheet before the proposal so these answers are in the minutes before the hardware is ordered.

Card Entry for Hundreds: Student Residences, Schools and the Former Hotels

A surprising share of the people who walk through Brooklyn Heights doors every day carry a card, not a key. St. George Towers at 100 Henry Street lists key-card entry to the building and to rooms, alongside a 24-hour security desk at the main entry, while the same listing also mentions hard-key entry to some rooms. Brooklyn Law School requires its ID at 250 Joralemon Street and at Feil Hall on State Street, and says both buildings have round-the-clock security. These are institutional systems with their own security staff and their own vendors, and we do not claim to work on them.

Where they affect our work is next door and downstairs. A co-op that shares a block with a large residence, a landlord who rents apartments to students for the academic year, or a small school or program that leases a floor in a converted building all end up wanting the same things the institutions have: credentials that start and stop on fixed dates, a card format that people already carry, and a desk or a phone that can see who is at the door. For a small landlord renting to students, that means fobs or phone keys with an end date that matches the lease, so a graduating tenant’s credential stops on the last day without anyone remembering to cancel it. For a program leasing space, it means its own readers on its own suite doors, on its own schedule, separate from the building’s residential doors.

The converted hotels add one more requirement: many separate entrances and a staffed desk. In a building with several street doors, the doors that are not watched by staff are the ones to control most tightly, with readers, door contacts that report a door held open, and alarms that reach the desk. The watched door can be friendlier. Every one of those doors still has to open freely from inside in a fire.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Brooklyn Heights

The Brooklyn Eagle’s coverage of the Montague Street BID’s 2024 annual meeting quoted the description of Montague Street as the former City of Brooklyn’s main commercial corridor that “remains the commercial heart of Brooklyn Heights,” and an earlier Eagle report described the BID as one of the city’s smallest, with more than 100 businesses. Around it are the other places where Heights businesses open doors in the morning.

Montague Street

Shops, restaurants, banks and services between the Promenade end and Court Street, many in the ground floors of houses and apartment buildings. Glass storefront doors in front, and back or cellar doors that need a staff schedule and an alert when they open after closing.

Atlantic Avenue

The neighborhood’s southern edge, with storefronts and restaurants facing Cobble Hill and Boerum Hill across the street. Shop doors with roll-down gates, and residential street doors beside them that must stay locked while the shop runs its own hours.

Court Street and the Borough Hall side

The eastern edge toward Downtown Brooklyn, where law, accounting and professional offices fill upper floors near the courts. Suite readers, file-room doors and visitor entry that does not depend on a receptionist being in the room.

Henry Street and Clark Street

Smaller shops, cafes and services near the Clark Street station and the former Hotel St. George. Single storefronts sharing a building entrance with apartments, which need two schedules on one wall.

Remsen and Joralemon Streets

Townhouses converted to professional offices, religious and community buildings, and schools. Doors that are open to the public at set hours and locked to everyone else outside them.

Churches and institutions

Congregations and cultural institutions in landmarked buildings, with office doors, side entrances and community rooms used by outside groups. Codes that work only during each group’s booked hours.

Glass doors and 4 a.m. phone calls

The two shop break-ins we found in local reporting began the same way. At the phone store on Montague Street in 2022, the Eagle reported, the glass front door was broken and the store’s alarm company woke the manager at 4 a.m.; at the convenience store on Atlantic Avenue in 2024, News 12 reported, two people broke through the glass while two watched the street. An access system cannot make glass unbreakable, and we will not pretend it can. What it changes is everything around the glass: a roll-down gate and a glass door can be paired with a door contact that reports a forced opening the moment it happens, the stockroom and office behind the counter can have their own reader so a smashed front door does not open the rest of the shop, and the morning log shows which credential opened the shop and when, which settles a lot of questions after a bad night.

Most Heights shop owners call us about something more ordinary: keys held by former staff, a back door to a cellar or rear yard that is sometimes found unlocked at opening, or a shared building door that the shop wants open during business hours while the residents upstairs want it locked. Each of those has a straightforward fix with individual credentials and schedules.

Rules that apply to Brooklyn Heights business doors

  • Landmarks. A storefront in the historic district is subject to the same Landmarks rules on visible equipment as the house above it. A reader is easiest to approve when it sits inside the glass line, on the interior side of the storefront frame, rather than on the facade.
  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit must release when power is lost and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Shop and office exit doors always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above shops. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. A residential street door beside a shop cannot ride on the shop’s daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where the building has apartments and uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to the residential doors. The shop’s own staff door is a separate matter.
  • Permits. As Lori Greene of I Dig Hardware wrote in 2013, building-code permit exemptions generally “do not include access control systems or the installation of electrified hardware,” and some jurisdictions require special permits for magnetic locks. We confirm what each job needs before we start, and any 120-volt circuit work is done by a licensed electrician.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.

Rowhouses inside the historic district

Our parlor door opens into a small vestibule with an original glass-paneled inner door. Would the reader be better on that inner door than out on the stoop?

Often, yes. A reader and lock on the inner vestibule door sit indoors, out of the rain and off the facade, so nothing new appears on the front of the house and the Landmarks question about the doorway largely disappears. The outer door stays on its key and can be left on the latch during the day, as many Heights households already do, while the inner door relocks every time it closes and opens for each person’s own code, fob or phone.

The trade-off is the vestibule itself. Parcels and visitors wait in it, so a visitor still needs a way to call you from the stoop, which usually means keeping or replacing the station beside the outer door, and the inner door’s glass panels need checking: if a panel sits within reach of the inside handle, we fit hardware that does not open from a simple reach-through, while still opening freely from inside in a fire.

Our door still has a brass bell pull and a mail slot. Do they have to come off before a reader can be approved?

No. Original features like these stay, and we plan around them. The Landmarks guidebook limits the number of new devices at a door opening (no more than one intercom panel and two light fixtures) and asks that new equipment suit the building; it does not ask owners to strip original hardware to make room. A working bell pull can stay working. A decorative one that no longer rings is left alone rather than wired into, because drilling an old pull to hide a cable is exactly the kind of damage the rules are meant to prevent.

The mail slot matters for one practical reason: if it sits close to the lock, someone could try to reach the inside lever with a tool through the slot. We check the distance on the site visit and, where it is close, choose a lock whose inside operation cannot be worked from the slot.

Our late Federal house has a narrow wooden doorway with no plain masonry beside it. Where can a station go?

In the opening itself. On the oldest houses in the northern part of the neighborhood the door surround is often wood all the way to the brick, with carved or reeded trim, and there is no flat masonry return to mount on. The guidebook allows equipment on wood and within the door opening, so the usual place is the flat inner face of the frame, set back from the trim, where a slim station is visible only to someone standing at the door. Cable passes straight through the frame behind it into the wall.

If even that face is too narrow, two other choices work: a keypad or reader built into the lock on the door itself, with power through a concealed hinge, or a station inside the vestibule with only a small call button outside. We bring sample sizes to the visit so you can see what fits before anything is ordered.

Our converted carriage house still has its big stable doors, fixed shut, with a small person-sized door cut into one leaf. Can that small door take an electric lock?

Usually, but it needs checking first. A door cut into a larger leaf is lighter and often less square than an ordinary door, and the frame around it is part of the big door rather than the building, so the lock has to be one that tolerates movement. Our usual approach is an electrified lock in the small door itself, with power carried across its hinge, rather than a strike in a frame that flexes every time the larger leaf is pushed.

Citylore notes that the carriage houses on Grace Court Alley almost all keep their hay cranes, a sign of how much original fabric survives on these lanes. That is a reason to mount the station on the plainest surface available, typically the inside of the door reveal, and to bring the cable in from inside rather than across the old front.

Co-ops and apartment buildings

A shareholder told our board that a broken entry panel is a habitability problem the co-op must fix. Does that apply to a panel that also reads fobs?

The intercom part almost certainly does. Habitat’s February 2024 legal Q&A explains that under the Multiple Dwelling Law, buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom from the front door to each apartment, and quotes attorney Nancy Kourland: “As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom.” The same article notes that courts have treated intercoms as an essential service. A combined station that is both the intercom and the fob reader carries that obligation with it.

We are not lawyers, and your proprietary lease and counsel decide the details. Practically, it means a board should treat a dead entry panel as urgent, keep a spare power supply and a service contact on file, and choose a station whose intercom keeps working even if the fob side or the cloud account has a problem.

The doorman’s desk wants to see who comes in through the side door. Can the desk screen show entries live without the building keeping a permanent record?

Yes. A live event screen at the desk and a stored history are two separate settings on most systems. The desk can see each entry as it happens, with the name attached to the fob, while the stored log is kept only as long as the board decides and then deleted automatically. For a building with rental apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, so the retention setting is not optional there.

We set the retention period with the board at handover, write it into the building’s privacy policy, and limit who can search the history, usually the managing agent and one board officer, so the desk sees today’s traffic without browsing a resident’s past.

Shareholders can open the storage cage room only by finding the super. Can each resident open it with their own fob without getting into the boiler room too?

Yes, and it is one of the most popular changes in buildings with storage in the cellar. The cage room door gets its own reader and lock, and each shareholder who rents a cage gets that door added to their fob, with the hours you choose. The boiler room, the meter room and the super’s workshop stay on separate doors that only staff can open. The log shows which fob opened the cage room, which ends most arguments about missing items.

The cage room door must still open freely from inside without a fob, so nobody can be shut in, and if it lies on an exit route from the cellar we use hardware that releases with the fire alarm.

Students, offices and shops

Most of our tenants are students who already carry a university ID card. Can the reader on our building’s door accept that same card?

Sometimes. A campus ID works on another building’s reader only if the reader understands the card’s technology and format, and many institutions use secured formats that are deliberately not readable outside their own systems. We test a sample card at the site visit with the reader we propose. If it reads reliably and the school permits it, the card can be enrolled like a fob; if it does not, tenants get a fob or a phone key, which is cheaper than fighting the format.

Even when a campus card works, your building should control its own list. The card is just a number to your system; you decide who is enrolled and when they stop working, and the school has no access to your door.

Our small law office near Court Street keeps client files in a back room. Should that room have its own reader, separate from the suite door?

For most firms it is worth it. A reader on the file room gives that door its own list of people and its own hours, so a cleaner who has the suite door for the evening does not also open the files, and the log shows who opened the room and when. Many firms also add a door contact so the system reports if the file room is left open at night. Whether your professional obligations require such a log is a question for your own counsel; what we can do is make the record available and simple to export.

Our Montague Street shop already has an alarm that calls the manager in the middle of the night. What would access control add?

The alarm tells you something happened; access control decides who can open the doors and records who did. In the 2022 break-in at the phone store on Montague Street, the Brooklyn Eagle reported that the alarm company woke the manager at 4 a.m. That is the alarm doing its job. What it cannot tell you the next morning is which staff member opened the back door at 6, whether the cellar door was left unlatched the night before, or whether a former employee’s key still turns the lock.

The two work best together. The access system can arm the alarm when the last credential leaves and disarm it when the first authorized one arrives, a door contact on the stockroom can report a forced opening separately from the front glass, and each person’s credential ends when they leave the job.

Our office in a converted townhouse has a receptionist only three days a week. How do clients get in on the other two?

Through a video call to whoever is in. The street-door station rings a group of phones or desk screens inside the office, so the first person free can see the visitor, speak to them and release the door from wherever they are working. For regular visitors with appointments, a code that works only during the appointment window is another option, sent the day before and expiring that evening.

We spend part of each year away from our Heights house. Can a house-sitter get a code we approve from abroad, and can we see when they come and go?

Yes. With a cloud-managed controller, the house-sitter’s code or phone key can be created, scheduled and cancelled from your phone wherever you are, and each entry appears in your app with the person’s name and the time. The code can be limited to the days the sitter is booked and to the doors they need, for example the parlor door but not the garden apartment or the cellar.

The door itself does not depend on the internet. If the house loses its connection while you are away, the controller keeps every code working locally and sends the stored entries once the connection comes back.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Brooklyn Heights

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Brooklyn Heights door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“You can put a keypad on any front door.”

On most of the country’s front doors, that is true. In Brooklyn Heights it usually is not, because the house sits in a historic district where the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews equipment on the facade.

The Commission’s guidebook allows intercom panels and doorbells within or beside the door opening, one intercom panel per opening, designed and finished to suit the building, and nothing on the primary facade unless there is no other option. A keypad stuck on the brownstone beside a station that is already there does not fit those rules.

The answer is not to give up on keyless entry. It is to replace what is on the doorway with one combined device, or to move control to the inner vestibule door, and to get the permit first.

“Wireless means no wiring and no permission needed.”

Battery locks avoid running cable, and on an interior door that can be all you need. But the permission question in a historic district is about what is visible, not whether it has wires.

A wireless keypad on the outside of a landmarked door is still a new device on the facade. Meanwhile a wired station whose cable passes through the frame into the wall, as the guidebook asks for concealed conduit, can be the less visible choice.

We use wireless where cable genuinely cannot go, and wire the doors that matter, so nobody depends on a battery change on the coldest night of January.

“A fob system means you can retire keys.”

Not for apartments. amNY reported in 2019 that under the Department of Buildings’ protocols, fobs, cards and metal keys all count as keys, and that such systems may not replace a key-based option; the same article quoted the NYCLU’s Lee Rowland: “It’s fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data.”

In a Heights co-op or rental building, a fob system works alongside physical credentials rather than forcing everyone onto one technology.

Every system we install keeps a mechanical override and offers residents a fob or card, not only a phone app.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a lock off the shelf and call it a door. That is one part.

A controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. A co-op with a lobby, a vestibule, a cellar and a bike room on one cloud-managed system is $4,000 to $15,000.

Brooklyn Heights pays the Brooklyn base list, with no extra charge for the historic district. The district changes where the hardware goes and what paperwork is needed, not the price list.

“A doorman building doesn’t need access control.”

Some of the neighborhood’s co-ops have full-time doormen, but the listings we read show others with part-time door staff. Even a fully staffed building has doors the desk cannot see: the cellar, the bike room, the service entrance and the package room.

A doorman also goes on break, calls in sick and retires. The system covers the hours and the doors that staffing does not.

We plan the desk and the readers to work together, so staff see entries at the doors they cannot watch, and the street door switches to resident mode when the shift ends.

“A landlord’s access logs are just a building convenience.”

In New York City, smart access in a multiple dwelling comes with legal limits. HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law page describes requirements for consent, a written privacy and retention policy, limits on what is collected, no sale of data, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized.

Those rules apply in Brooklyn Heights rental buildings, and in co-ops and condos with rented apartments, the same as anywhere in the city.

We set short retention by default, write the policy with the owner, and keep search access to the logs limited to the people who need it.

“Cameras and access control are the same thing.”

They answer different questions. A camera records what happened at the door; access control decides whether the door opens and records which credential opened it.

In the shop break-ins on Montague Street in 2022 and Atlantic Avenue in 2024, the reports describe glass doors broken from outside. No credential prevents broken glass, and a camera alone does not stop the next person walking through an unlocked back door.

The useful pairing is a controlled door with a contact that reports a forced opening, and a camera that shows the door when it opens. We install both, and we are honest about which problem each one solves.

Codes, fobs, cards and phone keys set up per person with schedules, then every door tested on battery and with all power removed, to prove it always opens from inside.

8. Handover and follow-up

Administrator accounts and a one-page guide for the owner, super or manager, and a follow-up after the first weeks to adjust closers, schedules and alerts.

Twice a year: a five-minute door check

When the clocks change, open each controlled door and let it close by itself: does it latch? Old Heights doors swell in summer and shrink in winter, and a door that latched in April may not in October. Check the battery level of any keypad in the app before the first cold week. Read the user list and remove anyone who no longer needs access: last season’s contractor, a sublet tenant who left, a shareholder who sold, a former employee. If anything sticks or drags, a call now is cheaper than a lockout later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Doorway Before You Choose the Device

This describes how we approach a historic-district rowhouse in general, not a particular customer. Before we talk about hardware, we stand on the sidewalk and photograph the doorway the way a Landmarks reviewer will see it: the surround, the existing bell or buzzer, any lights, the railings and the areaway. Then we count what is already there, because the guidebook’s limit of one intercom panel and two light fixtures per opening means anything new usually has to replace something old.

Next we go down to the cellar, which decides the cable route and most of the price. We look for the panel, a dry wall for the controller, and a straight path to the spot under the door. We check the door last: does it close and latch by itself, and is there sound wood where the lock or strike will go? Only when the doorway, the cellar and the door all agree do we choose the station. A clever device on a door that does not latch protects nothing, and a beautiful one in the wrong place will not get approved.

FAQ

Brooklyn Heights Access Control FAQ

Does being inside the Brooklyn Heights Historic District make access control cost more?
+

Not on our price list. Brooklyn Heights pays the same Brooklyn base prices as the rest of the borough, so one controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500. What the district changes is where the device goes, which device is chosen and the paperwork for the owner’s Landmarks application. A doorway that needs a lock built into an old wood door, rather than a simple strike, can sit higher in the range because of the extra work on the door, not because of the address.

What finish do you usually choose for a door station on a brownstone doorway?
+

Usually a dark bronze or black faceplate, because it sits quietly against brownstone and painted wood and matches the old hardware on many Heights doors. Brushed steel is chosen only where the door already carries steel hardware or the building is a later one. The Landmarks guidebook asks for a design, size and finish compatible with the building, so we bring finish samples to the site visit and photograph them against the doorway for the application.

Will the camera in a stoop station record the sidewalk and the houses across the street?
+

It sees whoever stands at the door, and a little of the stoop around them. Door station cameras are short-range and aimed at the visitor’s face, and most let us mask parts of the picture so a neighbor’s windows or the opposite stoop are blacked out. We aim and mask each camera at handover, and in a building with tenants we set it to record only when someone calls or a door opens, rather than all the time.

Our house already has an alarm from a national company. Can a new door system work alongside it?
+

In most cases, yes. The access system and the alarm stay separate products, but they can be connected so that the alarm disarms when an authorized code opens the door and arms again when the last person leaves, or simply so that the door contact is shared. Whether that link is possible depends on the alarm panel model, so we ask for it before the visit. If you prefer to keep the two completely separate, both still work normally side by side.

What happens to our door system if the company that made the reader stops supporting it?
+

That is why we prefer readers and controllers that use open wiring standards and credentials not tied to a single cloud service. If a product line is discontinued, a reader of that kind can be replaced with another make on the same cable and controller, and in many cases the existing fobs keep working. Before you buy, we tell you which parts of the proposed system are proprietary, and we hand over the administrator account and the user list so the building is never locked into one company.

Do your technicians need to get into every apartment to put a reader on a co-op lobby door?
+

Not for the reader itself. A lobby reader, its lock and its controller need access to the lobby, the vestibule and the room where the controller goes, usually the super’s office or a basement room. Apartments are involved only if the project also replaces the intercom handsets or adds indoor monitors, and many buildings avoid that by having the station call residents’ phones instead. If any apartment visits are needed, the managing agent schedules them in advance.

Who signs the contract for a lobby project in a Brooklyn Heights co-op, the board or the managing agent?
+

Whoever the co-op’s own rules authorize, which is usually the managing agent acting for the board after a board vote, or a board officer. We send the written proposal to the person the board names, show the vote or approval in the job file, and invoice the corporation, not individual shareholders. If the project needs a Landmarks permit, the application is in the corporation’s name as owner of the building.

Will your insurance certificate satisfy a Heights managing agent before work starts?
+

We send a certificate of insurance naming the building, the corporation and the managing agent as the agent requires, before the first visit. Send us the agent’s insurance requirements and we will check them against our policy and confirm before anyone is scheduled. Work is done under New York State license #12000287431, and the license number goes on the proposal as well.

Can the cellar door stay open for the porter during the day and lock itself at night?
+

Yes. The cellar door can run on a schedule, unlocked during the porter’s working hours and locked by itself when the shift ends, with residents and staff using fobs after hours. A door contact can report if the door is left standing open in the evening. If the cellar door is also a way out of the building, it opens freely from the inside at all times, and the schedule only controls who can open it from outside.

Can an electric strike work with the old mortise lock in our original front door?
+

Often it can, if the old lock’s latch is in good condition and lines up with the frame. The strike replaces the plate in the frame and releases the latch electrically, while the mortise lock and its key stay as they are. If the latch is worn, the door has dropped, or the frame has been cut for several earlier locks, we repair the frame or recommend an electrified mortise lock that fits the existing pocket in the door, so the original look of the door is kept.

Can the station at our stoop ring everyone in the household at once?
+

Yes. A station can call a group of phones at the same time, or one after another, along with any indoor screens in the house. Each adult can answer, see the visitor and open the door from wherever they are, and you can change the group, for example adding a nanny on weekdays, from the app. In a divided rowhouse each apartment has its own group, so a visitor for the top floor never rings the garden apartment.
