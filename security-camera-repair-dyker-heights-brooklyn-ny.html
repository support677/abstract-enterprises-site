Victorian-era originals: mounting into wood and finding the old cable

About half of the roughly 150 houses built in the first wave of the development still stand, according to the history of the neighborhood, and the ones we see have the features of their time: deep wooden porches, turned posts, shingle or clapboard walls, and cellars under the whole house. Cameras on these houses tend to be mounted where the first installer found something solid, which is often a porch beam or a corner board. Wood moves with the seasons, so a camera on wood slowly tilts, and the screws that hold it loosen. The cables usually enter through a drilled hole in trim and then travel through framing that nobody has opened in decades.

Our approach is to re-mount a tilting camera on a proper junction box or mounting plate that spreads the load, seal the entry so water stops following the cable, and trace each cable from the camera to the cellar before cutting anything. If a cable runs inside a wall we cannot open, we look for a path along the porch ceiling, a downspout line or a corner where a short exposed run in a painted raceway disappears against the trim. The aim is a repair that does not leave new holes in original woodwork.

Detached houses with a garage out back: the long run is the weak point

On a deep Dyker Heights lot the garage camera can sit well over a hundred feet from the recorder, and the way its cable got there decides how it fails. Cable stapled along a fence picks up water at every staple and gets cut by landscapers. Cable buried in the yard without conduit gets nicked by a shovel or crushed when a driveway is repaved. Cable strung overhead between the house and the garage sags and chafes. A Wi-Fi camera on the garage depends on a signal that has to pass through a brick or stucco back wall and then across the yard, and it fades every time someone parks a car between the house and the garage.

For a coax camera, distance shows up as a soft or grey picture; for a network camera it shows up as dropouts, because the camera is starved of power at the end of the run. Reolink's support guide makes the same point: video loss is often caused when the "Cable is too long, or one cable is supplying too many cameras at once." We measure at the far end, not only at the recorder, and then decide whether the run needs a new connector, a new section, a new path or a different link altogether.

Semi-detached pairs: two owners, one wall, one driveway

The semi-detached houses common in the postwar parts of Dyker Heights put two owners side by side. A camera on the shared side of the house, or over a driveway the two houses use together, records both properties. When that camera fails or needs to be moved, the repair is partly a camera question and partly a neighbor question. We can narrow the view, set privacy masks so part of the picture is blacked out on the recording, or move the camera to see only one household's door, but we suggest both owners agree before we change what the camera records.

Rebuilt houses: hidden cables and many cameras on one recorder

The large houses built on older lots over the last few decades often came with ten, sixteen or more cameras set into soffits and eaves, with network cable run inside the walls before the drywall went up and a recorder in a closet, a laundry room or a mechanical room. These systems fail differently from older ones. The cameras are usually good, but the recorder is overloaded, the PoE switch is too small for every camera's night-vision power, the drive is full of continuous recording, or the installer's login is the only one anyone ever had.

Because the cables are inside finished walls, we start by testing each one from the recorder end and labeling it, then work out which camera each one feeds. A soffit camera can usually be replaced from the outside without touching the interior. If a cable inside a wall has failed, we look first for a spare cable or an empty conduit the builder left before suggesting a new path.

Attached rowhouses: short runs, crowded cellars

The rowhouse blocks toward the edges of the neighborhood have systems that are easier to reach and easier to fix. Runs are short, the recorder is usually in the cellar near the boiler or the electric meter, and most problems are power, a drive or a camera body. The common trouble is heat and dust in a cellar corner, or a recorder plugged into the same strip as a dehumidifier or a freezer, so we look at where the recorder lives as part of the repair.

Condos and small apartment buildings: who approves, who logs in

In the condo buildings and walk-ups near the avenues, the cameras that matter are at the lobby door, the mailboxes, the stair and the parking area, and they belong to the building. We need to know who can approve the price, who holds the recorder's login and whether the board has a rule on who may view footage. With that settled, the repair itself is often quick: a failed camera, a dead power box or a recorder that needs a new drive.

Stores with apartments above: separating what should be separate

Mixed buildings on 13th Avenue, 11th Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway sometimes have one recorder covering both the store and the residential entrance, or the store's cameras running on the same internet line as an apartment upstairs. When the store owner and the building owner are different people, that arrangement causes trouble the day one of them changes the router or the lease ends. A repair is a good time to give each party its own recorder or its own login and to label which cameras belong to whom.

Field Notes: Starting a Dyker Heights Camera Repair at the Curb and Ending at the Back Fence

These notes describe how our technicians work through a camera system on a Dyker Heights lot. They are our working method, not a story about any one address.

Start at the curb. Before going inside, the technician stands where a car parks overnight, at the driveway apron and at the side door, and checks on a phone what each camera actually sees from those spots. Check the night, not just the day. A camera that looks fine at noon can fail its real job after dark. Where the complaint is about night pictures or display-season glare, the technician checks the night settings and, when the visit allows, the camera's view in its night mode.

Price first, then leave a record. You hear the cause and get a written price before any part is replaced. When the work is done, each cable carries a label, and you get a short list of what is where: which camera is on which channel, where the recorder and power supply are, and who has a login.

Fault by Fault

Curbside Cars, Lit-Up Lawns, Deep Lots and Weak Power: How Dyker Heights Camera Systems Fail

Five patterns explain most of the camera repairs we see in Dyker Heights. Knowing which one you are dealing with saves a visit, and sometimes saves buying a camera you do not need.

The curb and the driveway: seeing a car is not the same as identifying a person

Most driveway cameras were put up to show that a car is still there. After a theft, the owner needs something different: a face, clothing, a second person, a plate, the direction a car left in. That takes pixels on the target. The European camera standard EN 62676-4 sets out four levels, summarized by the design site CCTV Design Tool as about 25 pixels per meter to detect a person, 62.5 to observe, 125 to recognize and 250 to identify. A wide-angle camera over a garage door may give a person at the curb only a small fraction of the identification level, so the clip shows someone was there and nothing more.

The repair is usually one of three things. The camera is re-aimed and its lens set narrower so the spot where the car sits, or the path to the side door, fills more of the frame. The camera's recording settings are raised, because many recorders save a low-resolution stream to stretch storage. Or a second camera is added with one clear job, such as watching the curb or the front door, so the wide camera can keep the overview. We show you the before and after on your phone from the curb, so you can see the difference rather than take our word for it.

The display weeks: when the lights confuse the camera

Decorative LEDs, projectors and animated displays are hard on cameras in three ways. The first is flicker. Swann's support guide explains that "Horizontal bands happen when your camera's frame rate or shutter speed doesn't match the frequency of the light source," and the fix is to set the camera to the local power frequency, which is 60 hertz in North America. Some cameras leave the factory set wrong, and some displays flicker at their own rates, so the setting and the shutter both need attention. The second is mode-switching. A camera decides between day and night mode by how much light it sees, and colored light near that threshold can make it flip back and forth, moving the filter inside the camera each time. Swann notes that frequent switching can "gradually wear down the internal mechanisms of the filter," which is why a camera that clicked all December sometimes comes out of January stuck in one mode.

The third is glare. A camera pointed across a bright lawn toward a dark sidewalk will expose for the lights and lose the people. Re-aiming, a backlight or wide dynamic range setting, a fixed night schedule for the weeks the display runs, and sometimes moving the camera a few feet so the brightest pieces are out of frame usually solve it. We prefer to fix these in early November, before the display goes up, but we can make the changes with the lights on so you can judge the result.

The back of the lot: distance, weather and Wi-Fi

The camera on a detached garage or at the far fence is at the end of the longest run on the property, and everything along that run works against it: voltage drop on a long power cable, water getting into an outdoor splice, a cable chewed or cut in the yard, or a Wi-Fi signal that has to cross brick, stucco and open yard. The symptoms are recognizable. The camera drops out in rain and returns when things dry, freezes at night when its infrared lights draw more power, or simply shows "offline" a few times a day.

We test from both ends. If the cable is sound, the fix may be a better connector, a sealed junction box or a power supply at the far end. If the cable is damaged, we replace the bad section or run a new one along a protected path, such as conduit along the garage wall or under the yard where it will not be dug up. If wired is not practical, a dedicated point-to-point wireless link between the house and the garage is far steadier than a camera trying to reach the household router on its own.

Brownouts on an overhead grid: damage without a blackout

Camera systems in Dyker Heights have lived through both kinds of power trouble: full outages when overhead lines came down, and deliberate voltage reductions, like the 8 percent cut Con Edison made across the area in 2020 and the reduced power News 12 reported in Dyker Heights on June 25, 2025. A full outage is simple for a camera system: it goes off and comes back. Long low-voltage stretches are harder. Power supplies run hot trying to hold their output, recorders restart repeatedly, and drives can be corrupted mid-write. Reolink's guide puts it plainly: "The insufficient power supply is, very often, the culprit of the video loss on one or all security cameras."

The repair starts with measuring what each camera receives, then replacing a tired power supply or PoE switch, giving the recorder and switch a battery backup sized for the real load, and adding surge protection where cables come in from outside. We also check that the recorder restarts cleanly on its own and that every camera returns after power is cut and restored, so you are not finding out the next time the block goes dark.

Settings that drift: the camera works, the recording does not

A surprising share of repairs involve no failed part at all. A recorder was set to record only on motion and the motion zone was moved, so nothing triggers. A drive filled up and the overwrite setting was off, so recording stopped weeks ago. A firmware update reset the schedule. Swann's guide says that "Recording settings, schedules, or storage problems are usually the cause" when footage is missing, and that these problems "can stop recordings even when the camera appears online." That is the trap: the live view looks perfect, so nobody notices until the night they need the footage. We check every channel's recording, not just its live picture, and we set up an alert that tells you when a camera or a drive stops.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Dyker Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Dyker Heights homeowners, two-family owners, condo boards and store owners bring to us, grouped by where on the property they happen.

Cars, driveways and the curb

Our car was opened and driven off the driveway without a single broken window. What should our cameras have caught, and why didn't they?

If nothing was forced, a relay theft is likely. In that method one person stands near the house to pick up the key fob's signal while a second person opens the car, and Help Net Security describes the whole thing taking under 30 seconds. The most useful camera was the one covering your front door and the wall nearest where the keys are kept, because that is where the first person stood. If your cameras only watch the driveway, we re-aim or add one to cover the door and that side of the house, and we suggest keeping the fob in a signal-blocking pouch away from outside walls.

We park at the curb, across the sidewalk from the house. Can a camera on the porch identify someone at the car, or only show that somebody was there?

That depends on how many pixels the camera puts on a person at that distance. The EN 62676-4 levels call for about 250 pixels per meter to identify someone and 125 to recognize them, and a wide porch camera often gives a person at the curb far less. A narrower lens, a camera mounted lower and closer to the curb, or a dedicated curb camera can reach recognition or identification. We test it from the curb with you before and after the change.

The cable to our garage camera ran under the driveway, the driveway was repaved last summer, and that camera has been dead since. Does the driveway have to come up?

Rarely. We first test the cable from both ends to confirm it was cut or crushed rather than disconnected at a joint. If it is damaged under the paving, we usually run a new cable along a different path, such as up the house wall and across in an overhead span, along the fence line in conduit, or through the garage side, or we link the garage with a point-to-point wireless bridge. Breaking up a new driveway is the last option, not the first.

The lights weeks

Every December the picture from our front camera pulses or shows dark bands once the display is switched on. Is the camera failing?

Almost certainly not. Bands appear when a camera's shutter or frame rate does not match the way the lights flicker. Setting the camera to 60 hertz, which is the North American power frequency, and adjusting its shutter usually clears it. Some displays use controllers that flicker at their own rate, and for those we tune the exposure or move the camera so the worst pieces fall outside the frame.

When the display is lit, our porch camera keeps clicking between color and black-and-white all evening. What is it reacting to?

The light level around it is sitting right at the point where the camera decides between day and night mode, and colored display light makes that worse. Each switch moves a small filter inside the camera, so weeks of switching wear it out. We fix the camera in one mode on a schedule for the season or adjust the switching threshold, and if the filter is already stuck we replace the camera.

The decorators plugged the display into the same outlet as our camera power box and ran their cords along our camera cable. Now the camera drops out when the display timer comes on. Why?

The display is pulling the outlet's voltage down, tripping a GFCI, or putting electrical noise onto the line the camera's power supply depends on. Cords bundled tightly with a camera cable can also add interference to older coax cameras. We move the camera's power to its own outlet or a battery backup, separate the cables, and check whether the power supply survived. You may need an electrician if the circuit itself is overloaded; we do not do electrical work.

We moved a camera indoors, behind the front window, to keep it away from the crowds. At night it shows nothing but a white glow. Can that be fixed?

The glow is the camera's own infrared light bouncing off the glass. Turning the camera's infrared off and relying on the street and display light, pressing the lens flat against the glass with a hood, or using a camera made for viewing through windows solves it. If the view outside is too dark without infrared, the better answer is an outdoor camera mounted high enough to be out of reach.

Long side yards and the hardware on them

Our side-yard camera shows the whole driveway, but anyone at the side door is too small to recognize. Can the camera we have be changed, or does it need a different lens?

If it is a varifocal camera, we can zoom and refocus it so the side door fills more of the picture, and that costs only the service call. If it has a fixed wide lens, the choice is a camera with a narrower or adjustable lens in the same spot, or a second camera dedicated to the side door. We show you both views on your phone before deciding.

The pan-tilt camera on the corner of our house stopped turning and now stares at one spot. Repair or replace?

First we check the power, because pan-tilt cameras need more of it when the motors move and a weak supply shows up there first. Then we check its settings and firmware, since a lost preset or patrol schedule can look like a dead motor. If the motor or gears have failed after years outdoors, replacement is usually cheaper than repair, and for many lots two fixed cameras covering the views you actually used work better than one camera that turns.

The siren and floodlight on our deterrence camera go off at night when nobody is there. Is the camera faulty?

Usually it is the trigger, not the camera. Moving branches, headlights on the street, display decorations blowing in the wind, cats and even rain can trip a motion rule set too wide. We narrow the detection area to the parts of the lot people actually walk, switch the siren to fire only on person detection, schedule it for the hours you choose, and test it in place. If the sensor itself is failing, we replace the camera.

Stores and offices on the avenues

Burglars have been prying store doors before dawn on our avenue. Can our existing cameras be changed to show someone working on the door, not just the doorway after it opens?

Yes, in most stores. The outside camera is re-aimed to show the full door and the person standing at it, mounted out of reach, and the inside camera is turned to face the door. We set both to record continuously overnight at full quality rather than in short motion clips, and add an alert to your phone for activity at the back door after closing.

Our clothing store's camera near the fitting rooms has stopped working. When it is repaired, where is it allowed to point?

At the aisle and the fitting-room entrance, never into the rooms. New York General Business Law section 395-b bars owners from knowingly allowing a device that secretly records the inside of a fitting room or restroom. We repair or replace the camera so it watches the corridor and the door, use a privacy mask if any part of the picture could see inside, and show you the recorded view before we leave.

FAQ

Dyker Heights Security Camera Repair FAQ

Can a camera be mounted on the utility pole in front of our house to get a better view of the street?
+

No. The poles on Dyker Heights streets belong to the utility and the companies that share them, and private equipment cannot be fixed to them. For a better street view we look at a higher spot on the house, the garage, a gate pier or a post on your own property, or a camera with a longer lens in the spot you already have.

Do you repair cameras that save to a memory card inside the camera instead of to a recorder?
+

Yes. When a camera like that stops saving clips, the cause is usually a worn or full memory card, a card that was never formatted for the camera, or a recording setting that changed after an update. We test and replace the card, set the camera to overwrite old clips, and tell you if that camera would be better recording to a recorder in the house where a thief cannot take the card with it.

Our recorder was set up years ago with ports opened on the router so we could watch from outside. Is that something you close during a repair?
+

Yes, if you want it closed. Open ports leave the recorder's login page reachable from the internet, and older recorders often have outdated software. We can close those ports, update the recorder where the manufacturer allows, change the admin password and set up remote viewing through the manufacturer's app instead, then confirm everyone in the family can still see the cameras.

Do you repair solar-powered cameras at the back of the lot?
+

Yes. Solar cameras usually fail because the panel is shaded by a tree, a garage roof or winter sun that sits low behind the houses, because the battery has aged, or because the Wi-Fi link to the house is weak. We check the panel's position, the battery and the signal, and if the spot cannot get enough sun in December we suggest a wired or bridged camera instead.

Is a cellular camera an option for a spot the house Wi-Fi cannot reach?
+

Sometimes. A camera with its own cellular connection can work on a far garage or a gate where Wi-Fi fails, but it needs a data plan and decent signal and it usually records short clips rather than around the clock. On most Dyker Heights lots a cable or a point-to-point wireless bridge from the house is cheaper to run and records more, so we price both before you choose.

Can the repair include a sign saying the property is under video surveillance?
+

Yes. We can put up a small surveillance notice at the gate, the driveway or a store entrance during the visit. For businesses that watch employees, New York law has its own notice rules, and a sign at the door does not replace the notice and acknowledgment an employer may owe staff.

Is it legal for our house cameras to record the sidewalk and the street in front?
+

In general, video of a public sidewalk or street seen from your own property is allowed in New York. The limits are on recording places where people expect privacy, such as the inside of a neighbor's windows, and on recording conversations. We aim cameras at your property and the public way, mask any view into a neighbor's windows, and can turn off camera microphones. This is general information, not legal advice.

After someone reset our recorder, one camera's picture is upside down and another is mirrored. Is that a repair visit?
+

Often it is not. Image flip and mirror are settings on the camera or recorder, and a reset can change them. Send us a photo of the screen, and if you can log in we can usually walk you through it by phone or text. If nobody has the password, or the reset also lost the recording schedule, the fix is part of the $150 service call.

Our zoom camera over the driveway goes out of focus every winter. Is it failing?
+

Not necessarily. Motorized zoom cameras can drift out of focus as the housing contracts in the cold, and many have an auto-focus or one-push focus setting that was never turned on. We refocus it, set it to correct itself at night, and check that moisture is not getting behind the front glass. If the focus motor has failed, replacing the camera is usually the better value.

The camera on a tall pole in our yard sways in the wind and the picture shakes. What can be done?
+

Shaking usually comes from the pole or the mount, not the camera. We tighten or replace the bracket, brace or shorten the pole, or move the camera to the garage or house wall where it is held firmly.
