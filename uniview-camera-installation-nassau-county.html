The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need Uniview Camera Installation

Start with the houses. Census figures for Nassau count 477,860 housing units, and 364,529 of them are single-family detached homes — roughly three in four. The median one was built in 1955, and more than 150,000 units date from before 1950. That is the Cape Cod in Levittown and Wantagh, the brick-and-shingle colonial in Garden City and Rockville Centre, the high ranch in Massapequa and Plainview, the Tudor in Great Neck and Manhasset. About 82 percent of occupied homes are owner-occupied, so the person deciding on cameras usually owns the eaves, the attic and the basement where cable has to travel. That makes a properly wired system possible in a way it often is not in a city rental, and it is why a wired Uniview NVR with PoE cameras makes more sense here than a handful of battery cameras stuck to the siding.

Then the map. Nassau is divided among the towns of Hempstead, North Hempstead and Oyster Bay and the cities of Long Beach and Glen Cove, with 64 incorporated villages and more than a hundred unincorporated hamlets inside them. Several villages run their own police departments; the rest of the county is patrolled by the Nassau County Police. That patchwork matters after a break-in. Garden City’s department, for example, runs a voluntary registry where residents and businesses list their address as a place with cameras, so detectives know whom to ask for video — and states plainly that registering gives the police no access to the system itself. A Uniview recorder set up so its clock is right, its channels are named and a clip can be exported in a few minutes is what turns that request into evidence.

Third, the people are away. Nassau is commuter country: one adult is on the Long Island Rail Road before seven and home after dark, the other may be working in Mineola, Lake Success or Manhattan too. In July 2026 the county police announced the arrest of a Pennsylvania man charged with six counts of burglary for entering homes across Nassau between July 2025 and May 2026 and taking jewelry. Daytime and evening break-ins into empty houses are exactly the pattern a camera system has to answer, and the answer is less about recording than about knowing: the right alert on a phone at 2 p.m., and a face recorded clearly enough at the side door to identify someone afterwards.

Finally, the South Shore water and the grid. On the weekend of September 26, 2026 a nor’easter flooded Island Park badly enough that the LIRR suspended the Long Beach Branch from Saturday morning to Sunday night, with crews pumping millions of gallons as the tides kept returning. PSEG Long Island restored nearly 112,000 customers after the same storm and named Oyster Bay among the hardest-hit areas. A recorder on a basement floor in Freeport or a garage slab in Oceanside, with no battery behind it, fails in exactly the hours you most want it working. Where the recorder lives, what it is plugged into and how it recovers afterwards are design choices we make on day one.

How cable and mounts go in, house by house

  • Vinyl and aluminum siding. Cameras sit on a mounting block or junction box screwed into the sheathing behind the siding, with the cable entering through the box, not a separate hole. The block lets the siding move with temperature without cracking around the camera.
  • Brick and stucco fronts. Where a colonial has a brick or stucco face, we mount on wood trim or a soffit where we can, and drill masonry only into mortar joints so a later owner can patch it invisibly.
  • Attics with blown-in insulation. We pull cable along the top plates and avoid trampling insulation; the run is planned from the eave so nobody wades across the middle of the attic.
  • Finished basements. In a finished basement the recorder goes in the utility area beside the panel and water heater, on a shelf, never on the floor, with cables dropped down an interior wall or the chimney chase where the code allows.
  • Detached garages. Copper network cable reaches about 100 metres, which covers almost every Nassau garage. Underground runs use direct-burial cable in conduit; we price the trench separately so you can compare it with mounting a camera on the house that looks at the garage.
  • Line voltage stays with electricians. PoE cameras need no outlet at the camera. If a recorder location needs a new receptacle, a licensed electrician installs it; in the Town of Hempstead, for example, professional electrical work requires a town master electrician’s licence.

Rules that apply to Nassau camera work

  • State installer licence. Camera and alarm contractors in New York answer to the Department of State, which issues security and fire alarm installer licences under article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours, held by Anwar Timothy, is NYS license #12000287431, and it covers shops and offices as well as houses.
  • The county home-improvement licence has a carve-out. Nassau’s home-improvement law (Administrative Code section 21-11) licenses contractors working on homes of up to three families, but its definition of home improvement expressly leaves out burglar alarm and automatic fire alarm systems. Camera work on a house is low-voltage security work; any carpentry, siding or electrical work around it brings in the other licences.
  • Alarm permits. The county requires a police alarm permit for alarms that call the Nassau County Police, gives a newly installed alarm a 90-day grace period for up to three false alarms once a permit is issued, and expects residential alarm calls to include call-back attempts. Cameras alone don’t need a permit; they help when an alarm does go off.
  • Staff areas. New York Labor Law section 203-c bars employers from video-recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms designated for changing, except by court order.
  • Hidden recording. Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to use an imaging device to view or record people in places such as bedrooms, changing rooms and restrooms without their knowledge.
  • City rules don’t follow you over the line. New York City’s biometric sign law and Tenant Data Privacy Act apply in the five boroughs; a shop in Valley Stream or Elmont is under state law, county law and its own village or town code instead.
Your Questions Answered

Uniview Camera Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau owners raise at a walkthrough, grouped by the situation behind them: the village and its police, the commute, the street out front and the power behind the recorder, the strip-center shop, and the house with more than one household in it.

Villages, Police and Paperwork

Our village has its own police department — how do detectives find out we have cameras?

Mostly the old-fashioned way: after a burglary or a car theft, officers walk the block and knock on doors looking for video. Some departments add a shortcut. Garden City’s police run a voluntary Private Camera Registration Program where a resident or business lists the address and the number of cameras, so detectives have an instant list of places to ask. The department is explicit that registering gives it no access to the cameras; it only tells them where footage may exist, and they may then ask to see video from a limited period. The Nassau County Police, who patrol most of the county, publicize their own information-sharing program for residents and businesses, called SPIN.

Whichever department covers your street, the request lands on you, usually by phone. What makes the difference is how quickly you can answer it. We name every channel in the recorder by what it sees (“driveway”, “side door”, “back yard”), keep the recorder’s clock synchronized, and show you how to pull a clip from the phone app or onto a USB stick at the recorder. Five minutes, not an afternoon of scrolling.

Registering, or not, is your call. Nothing in the Uniview system sends video to any police agency on its own; footage stays on the drive in your house until you choose to share it.

Which license should a Nassau camera installer hold, given how the county’s home-improvement law is written?

The one that covers security systems: a New York State security and fire alarm installer licence from the Department of State under General Business Law article 6-D. Ours is #12000287431. Homeowners often ask for the Nassau County home-improvement licence instead, because that is the licence the county advertises, and it is worth knowing how that law is drafted. Nassau’s Administrative Code section 21-11 requires a county licence for home-improvement businesses working on houses of up to three families, and then its definition of home improvement specifically excludes burglar alarm systems and automatic fire alarm systems.

So a camera company should be able to show you the state licence and proof of insurance. If the same job also includes building a soffit, replacing siding or adding an outlet, that part brings in the home-improvement licence or a licensed electrician; electrical licensing in Nassau is handled by the towns and cities, and the Town of Hempstead, for one, requires a town master electrician’s licence for professional electrical installation.

Ask for the licence number and look it up before anyone drills. A company that hesitates over that step will probably hesitate when it is time to hand over the admin password too.

Our burglar alarm already has a Nassau police permit — what changes if cameras are added to it?

For the permit, nothing. The county’s alarm permit covers alarm systems that call the police; cameras that record to your own recorder aren’t alarms in that sense, so adding them doesn’t require a new permit. If your alarm company links a camera to the central station for video verification, ask them whether they treat it as a change to the alarm system and whether your permit details need updating.

The practical change is in how you answer an alarm. Nassau police say they are asked to respond to more than 125,000 false alarms a year, and the county code charges permit holders for repeated false alarms on a rising scale. With a camera aimed at each alarmed door and a person-detection alert on your phone, the call from the central station stops being a guess: you can look at the back door before deciding whether to cancel or to ask for a response.

We place cameras to match alarm zones for exactly that reason. A motion detector in the family room and a camera in the far corner of the yard don’t help each other; a camera that sees the door the motion detector covers does.

A camera on our side yard can see into the neighbor’s back yard — where is the line in Nassau’s villages?

The firm line is in state law: Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to use an imaging device to watch or record people in places like bedrooms, bathrooms and changing rooms without their knowledge. A neighbor’s fenced back yard is a softer case, and villages have noticed. In 2017 the Village of Flower Hill considered a local law aimed at imaging devices whose only real purpose is to view a person in a part of their own property where they otherwise couldn’t be seen, after a resident complained about two cameras mounted high in a tree pointing over the fence.

On a narrow suburban lot, a camera covering your own side path will catch some of the house next door. We handle it with aim first and software second: cameras angled down your path rather than across the fence line, and Uniview’s privacy masking blacking out a neighbor’s windows or pool area in the recorded image itself.

If a neighbor asks, it is easy to show them the masked view on your phone. That conversation usually ends better than a complaint to the village does.

The Commute and the Empty House

We are on the LIRR from 6 a.m. to 7 p.m. — which phone alerts are worth keeping, and which will we mute within a week?

Mute-worthy: plain motion on any camera that sees a street, a tree or a road. Nassau streets carry landscapers, school buses, delivery vans and dog walkers all day, and generic motion alerts from a street-facing camera will hit your phone dozens of times before lunch. Within a week most people silence the whole app, and then the alert that mattered goes unseen too.

Keep: person detection drawn as a zone on the back door, the side gate and the basement stairs, set to alert only during the hours nobody should be home; a line-crossing alert on the driveway after dark; and a daily health message if the recorder loses a camera. Uniview’s smart detection on its newer recorders and cameras sorts people and vehicles from leaves and shadows, so a zone drawn on the porch steps, rather than the whole frame, is what keeps the phone quiet until something is worth seeing.

On a Cape Cod with a low front stoop, where does the camera go to record a face rather than the top of a cap?

Lower and closer than most people expect. A camera tucked under the gable peak of a Cape looks almost straight down at the stoop, and a person in a cap or hood shows up as a brim and a pair of shoulders. Uniview’s fixed 2.8 mm turrets have a wide view, so we usually place one at door-frame height beside the entry, or on the porch ceiling a few feet out, angled so the face of anyone at the door fills a useful share of the frame.

The high camera still has a job: overview of the walk and the driveway. The pair together answers both questions an investigator asks — what happened and who it was. The county police’s July 2026 burglary arrest involved homes entered across Nassau over ten months; cases like that are built from video that shows a face, a gait or a vehicle clearly, and a recording of the top of a hat is little help.

Side and back doors get the same treatment. On many Capes the side door by the driveway is the one people actually use, so it gets its own face-height camera.

Thieves have taken catalytic converters near the Syosset and Hicksville stations — can a home camera protect the car in our own driveway overnight?

A home system can’t watch the station lot: it isn’t your property, and CBS New York has reported that MTA police work with Nassau County police on patrols of lots where converter thefts happened. What a Uniview system can do is cover the car that stays home overnight, or the second car parked in the driveway all day.

Converter theft happens low and fast, under the car. A camera mounted high on the garage sees the roof of the car and little else; one at about eight feet on the house corner, aimed along the driveway, sees under the bumper line and anyone kneeling beside the car. Add a line-crossing rule at the end of the driveway after midnight and an alert reaches you while it is happening, not the next morning.

Colour night imaging matters here. Uniview’s ColorHunter cameras keep colour in low light, so the recording shows the colour of the vehicle that pulled in and the clothes of the person who got out, which plain infrared images reduce to shades of grey.

The Street, the Grid and the Record

A school bus stop is at our corner — is it a problem if a camera records the children waiting there?

Recording a public sidewalk from your own house is generally lawful in New York, and a driveway camera on a corner lot will inevitably catch the bus stop. The question is less legal than neighborly. Parents notice a lens pointed at their children every morning, and a recording of other people’s kids is something you would rather not have to manage.

So we aim for what you need and mask what you don’t. The driveway camera is angled down the driveway and the front walk, and if the stop still falls inside the frame, a Uniview privacy mask blanks that patch of sidewalk in the recording. If the corner itself is the concern — cars cutting across the lawn, or something that happens at the stop — we can leave it unmasked, but that should be a deliberate decision, not an accident of where the bracket went.

Either way, footage that shows other families stays on your recorder unless police ask for it. Posting clips of neighbors’ children on a community page is how a camera turns into a feud.

Can a camera go on the utility pole or the street tree in front of the house for a better angle?

No, and it isn’t a good idea even where nobody would notice. Utility poles belong to the utility, and attaching anything to them is the pole owner’s business, not ours. The planting strip and street trees along the curb are generally public right-of-way, looked after by the village, the town or the county depending on the road, so a camera there is mounted on someone else’s property, powered by a cable crossing the sidewalk.

The better angle almost always exists on your own property: a camera high on the garage corner looking down the driveway toward the street, or one on the porch post at the front walk. On a deep lot, a short post set inside your own front-yard line, with the cable buried in conduit to the house, gets a camera closer to the street without touching public property.

If a car or a person in the street is what you need to capture, Uniview’s 8MP turrets and ColorHunter bullets give enough detail from the house to identify a vehicle at the curb in most Nassau front yards, especially with corridor mode turning the frame upright along a narrow side yard or driveway.

The recorder’s clock was wrong after the last outage — does a bad timestamp matter if police need the clip?

It matters more than most people think. A clip showing a car in your driveway at “3:12 a.m.” when it was really 4:12 sends a detective looking at the wrong hour of every other camera on the street, and a defense lawyer will ask why your clock was an hour off. Recorders that lose power for long enough can come back with a drifted clock, and a daylight-saving setting left at the factory default can put every recording an hour out for half the year.

We set each Uniview recorder to take its time from an internet time server, set the correct US Eastern time zone with daylight saving, and push the recorder’s time to every camera. After an outage the recorder corrects itself once the internet is back.

If you are exporting a clip for police and you know the clock was wrong, say so and say by how much. A known offset is easy to work with; an unknown one undermines the whole recording.

Strip Centers, Shops and Staff

Our shop is one unit in a strip center — can our cameras cover the sidewalk and parking field the landlord controls?

Usually, with the landlord’s written okay. The lease typically gives you the inside of the unit and, sometimes, the storefront glass; the facade, the canopy and the lot belong to the center. Most landlords will approve a tenant camera above the door on the canopy fascia, aimed at the entrance and a slice of the sidewalk, provided it is mounted neatly and doesn’t cross the neighbors’ frontages.

The practical answer is often a camera inside the glass looking out. Uniview’s turrets can sit on the ceiling just inside the door, see the entrance and the first few parking spaces, and avoid the landlord question entirely, though glare and reflections need careful angling. For the lot itself, ask the landlord what they already record before paying to duplicate it.

We send the landlord a one-page drawing showing camera positions and cable routes when approval is needed. It makes yes a quicker answer.

The center’s owner runs his own cameras — can our Uniview recorder and his system share views?

Technically, sometimes. Uniview recorders can add many third-party IP cameras that speak the ONVIF standard, and the landlord’s recorder may be able to add one of yours the same way. In practice it raises questions that have little to do with technology: whose network the video crosses, who can see the inside of your store, and who answers when a camera drops.

The cleaner arrangement is two separate systems and an agreement to share clips. When something happens in the lot, the landlord exports their view and you export yours. If you really want a lot view on your own phone, the landlord can give you a view-only account on their recorder, and nothing of yours has to be exposed.

Either way we keep your Uniview recorder on your own network, with its own internet connection where the lease allows, so a change on the landlord’s side doesn’t take your cameras down.

How do we watch the register without recording customers’ card PINs?

Aim at hands, cash drawer and counter, not at the keypad, and then mask what still shows. A camera behind and above the cashier records the till, the counter and the customer’s face across it; one directly over the card terminal records exactly what you don’t want on your drive. Uniview recorders let us draw privacy masks that black out a region of the image permanently in the recording, so even a slightly misaimed camera can’t capture keypad entries.

This is good for the business as well as the customer. A recording that shows PINs is a liability if the recorder is ever stolen or accessed by someone who shouldn’t see it, and card processors don’t want terminals filmed.

A second camera on the customer side of the counter, at head height, gives you faces at the moment of purchase, which is the shot a shoplifting or fraud investigation needs anyway.

When a shoplifting crew hits several stores, which camera view do detectives actually use?

Three shots, in order of usefulness: a clear face at the door on the way in, a full-length view of the person in the aisle showing what they took, and a vehicle view in the lot showing what they left in. A ceiling camera over the middle of the store shows the event but rarely identifies anyone, which is why a single fisheye in the center of a shop is a supplement, not a plan.

For a typical Nassau unit we put an 8MP turret inside the door at about seven feet, aimed to catch faces as people come in, turrets down the main aisles with Uniview’s 9:16 corridor mode on the long narrow ones so the aisle fills the frame top to bottom, and a fisheye overhead for the whole floor. If the landlord allows, a camera on the canopy covers the curb.

Then the clip has to get out. We show the owner and the manager how to export from the recorder or the app in a format that plays on an ordinary computer, because a detective arriving the next morning to cover several stores won’t wait while someone hunts for a password.

Can a camera cover the back room where staff change before a shift?

Not if it is used for changing. New York Labor Law section 203-c bars employers from video-recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms and rooms designated for changing clothes, except by court order, and Penal Law section 250.45 makes covert recording in such places a crime. A combined stockroom-and-changing-room is common in small Nassau shops, and it is the one place a camera can turn a theft problem into a legal one.

The fix is in the floor plan. Give staff a changing space with no camera, even a curtained corner, and put the stockroom camera on the door to the back room and the shelves, not on the whole room. Uniview’s privacy masking can black out a curtained corner permanently if the camera’s view has to pass near it.

Tell employees where the cameras are and what they cover. It answers the question before anyone asks it, and it deters inventory loss better than a hidden camera would.

Shared, Seasonal and Rented Houses

How do we keep the accessory apartment’s entrance off the main household’s phones?

By giving each household its own login on the recorder with only its own cameras. Uniview recorders have user levels and per-camera permissions: the owner’s admin account sees everything, the apartment’s account sees the apartment door and the shared driveway, and the main household’s everyday account sees the main entrances and the yard. In the phone app each person logs in with their own account and only the allowed cameras appear.

That arrangement matters in Nassau’s many two-family and mother-daughter houses. A parent or tenant downstairs has a reasonable expectation that their comings and goings aren’t on everyone’s phone, and the owner still has the whole picture as administrator if something happens.

The shared driveway is where most disagreements start, so we settle it at the walkthrough. If both households park there, both get the driveway camera; if only one does, the other household’s account can be left without it. The same goes for a shared back yard. Writing that decision down before the cameras go up is easier than renegotiating it later, especially when the downstairs unit changes tenants.

We set the accounts up during installation and write down which camera belongs to which login, so changing a tenant later is a five-minute job, not a visit.

We rent the Long Beach house out for the summer — how do we switch the indoor cameras off for tenants and back on afterwards?

Indoor cameras and short-term tenants don’t mix. The clean way is to take the indoor channels out of the recording schedule and cover or unplug those cameras for the rental period, while the outdoor cameras at the doors and the driveway keep recording. On a Uniview recorder that is a schedule change, done from the admin account in a few minutes, and reversed in September.

Tell the tenants in writing which outdoor cameras record and that indoor cameras are switched off and covered. Rental platforms and local rental rules often have their own requirements about disclosure, so check what applies to you.

Before the season starts, also change the Wi-Fi password the tenants will use if the recorder shares that network, or better, put the recorder on a separate network from the guest Wi-Fi altogether.

Jammers and Wireless Weak Spots

Can a Wi-Fi jammer knock a wired Uniview system offline the way it can a battery camera?

No. A jammer floods the radio frequencies Wi-Fi uses, so a camera that sends its video over Wi-Fi stops reaching the router and records nothing, or only to a card inside the camera that a thief can take. Police in New Jersey have described a burglary suspect they believe used a jammer to knock out a home’s cameras and the homeowner’s phone signal at the same time. A wired Uniview camera sends its video down a network cable to a recorder inside the house, and no radio is involved.

A jammer can still interfere with the last step: the recorder telling your phone. If the home internet is cable or fiber, that path is wired too; only a household relying on a cellular or wireless internet connection is exposed. Either way the recording itself continues on the drive, and you can see it once the signal returns.

This is the strongest argument for replacing battery cameras on a Nassau house with wired ones, and it is why we keep the recorder somewhere a burglar won’t walk past on the way in.

What AI Gets Wrong About Uniview Camera Installation in Nassau County

Google’s AI Overview couldn’t be read from the environment this page was built in, so instead we took seven claims that keep appearing in the cost guides, dealer pages and forum threads those summaries are built from, and tested each one against Nassau conditions and the original documents.

1. “Installation costs $100 to $300, or about $1,289 on average”

Security.org says installation costs $100 to $300 depending on complexity, and Angi puts the 2026 national average for security camera installation at $1,289, with most homeowners paying $590 to $2,040. Both are national figures that mix one-doorbell jobs with full systems, and neither tells a Nassau homeowner what a wired four-camera system costs on their own house.

Our published Nassau Uniview list puts a complete four-camera system — four 4MP ColorHunter cameras, a four-port PoE recorder and a 4 TB drive, installed — at $2,524, and individual cameras at $449 installed and up. That is above the national averages because it includes the recorder, the drive, cable through the attic and set-up of the app, which a “$100 to $300” figure doesn’t.

Compare like with like. Ask any quote to separate equipment, labor per camera, recorder set-up and anything for hidden wiring, then put the numbers next to ours.

2. “Uniview is banned in the US like Hikvision and Dahua”

Some comparison pages state that Uniview hardware is banned under NDAA Section 889. The statute’s named list is Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua; Uniview isn’t on it. The FCC’s Covered List, checked October 2026, names Hikvision and Dahua video surveillance equipment and doesn’t mention Uniview.

What did happen is narrower: in December 2024 the Commerce Department added Zhejiang Uniview Technologies to the Entity List, which restricts exports of US-regulated items to the company. That rule doesn’t prohibit a homeowner or shop in Nassau from buying or using Uniview cameras.

Federal contractors and some companies have their own procurement rules, and those are worth checking if your business sells to government. For a house in Bellmore or a deli in Mineola, the practical issues are buying from an authorized channel and keeping firmware current.

3. “In Nassau, any contractor with a county home-improvement license can install cameras”

Ranking contractor directories often treat the county home-improvement licence as the licence that matters for any work on a house. Nassau’s own law says otherwise for security systems: its definition of home improvement excludes burglar alarm and automatic fire alarm systems.

Security system installation in New York is licensed by the state Department of State under General Business Law article 6-D. A handyman with a county licence may be a fine carpenter, but that licence doesn’t cover the work this page describes.

Ask for the state licence number. Ours is #12000287431, and it appears on every page and every invoice.

4. “Wireless cameras are just as reliable as wired ones”

Retail pages describe Wi-Fi cameras as equivalent to wired systems, with easier installation. For a renter or a single doorbell that can be true. For a Nassau house, two failure modes argue the other way: radio interference, including the jammers New Jersey police have described in burglaries, and power, which is not something a nor’easter respects.

A wired PoE camera draws its power from the recorder or switch, so one battery backup in the utility room keeps every camera running when PSEG is out, instead of a scatter of batteries and plug-in adapters around the house. During the September 2026 storm PSEG reported nearly 112,000 restorations across its territory; a single backup is far easier to plan around than a dozen outlets.

Wireless still has a place, for a pool house or a camera on a fence where a cable can’t reasonably go. It is the exception on our drawings, not the plan.

5. “If you register your cameras, police can watch them”

Forum answers often assume that a camera registry gives police a live feed. Garden City’s registry says the opposite in writing: it is voluntary and doesn’t provide the department access to privately owned systems. It is a list of addresses where detectives may ask to see video.

Live sharing is a different arrangement that some companies sell, and a Uniview recorder doesn’t do it unless you deliberately give someone an account. Public plate readers on Nassau roads are separate systems run by police agencies, not connected to private cameras.

If you want to help investigations without giving away access, registration plus a recorder that exports clean clips quickly is the right combination.

6. “Cloud recording is always safer than a recorder at home”

The argument is that a burglar can steal a recorder but not a cloud. True as far as it goes, but cloud-only cameras stop uploading the moment the internet is down or the Wi-Fi is jammed, and most require a monthly plan for the history you would want after a burglary.

A Uniview recorder keeps recording through an internet outage, and we place it where it isn’t in plain sight: a basement utility area, a closet or a locked cabinet. Phone access comes through Uniview’s app; Uniview has announced that its UNV-Link app replaces EZView, which it expects to stop service in 2027, so we set new systems up on UNV-Link from the start.

If an off-site copy matters to you, the recorder can push alarm clips to an email or a storage account. That gives you a cloud copy of the moments that matter without paying to upload every hour of an empty driveway.

7. “Plug it in and it’s secure out of the box”

Installation videos and how-to pages repeat Uniview’s long-standing default login, admin and 123456. Uniview’s own help document confirms most of its products shipped with that default, and the company publishes security notices for its recorders and cameras, including a 2024 cross-site-scripting notice for some NVRs.

An installer who leaves the default password, old firmware and open ports on a recorder has built a system that strangers can find. We set a strong admin password, update firmware to the current release before hand-over, disable features you don’t use, and avoid exposing the recorder directly to the internet.

Then we give you the password. Not a sticky note on the recorder, and not kept only by us: a sealed card, and a walkthrough of how to change it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Which Door the Family Actually Uses

On a Nassau house the first question at a walkthrough isn’t about cameras at all. It is which door the family uses. On a surprising number of Capes, colonials and high ranches the front door is ceremonial: guests and packages arrive there, but the household comes and goes through the side door by the driveway, the garage, or a back door off the kitchen. Burglars tend to prefer the doors people don’t watch. If the system puts its best camera on the front step and a distant overview on the side door, it has things backwards for that house.

So we ask, and we look for the evidence: the worn path across the lawn, the doormat that actually gets used, the recycling bins by the side gate. The answer decides where the face-height camera goes, which alert zone is armed during the commute, and which camera the owner opens first when the phone buzzes at work. It takes two minutes, and it matters more to how the system performs than any spec on the datasheet.

Will screw holes in vinyl or aluminum siding let water in behind it?
+

Not when the camera goes on a proper mounting block. We fix the block through the siding into the sheathing, seal the screw penetrations, and bring the cable through the block so there is no separate hole exposed to rain. A drip loop on the cable inside keeps water from running along it. Cameras screwed straight to vinyl are the ones that loosen and leak over a couple of winters.

Why does the night picture turn white when a camera sits behind a storm door or under a porch ceiling?
+

Because the camera's infrared lights are bouncing straight back into the lens. Glass in a storm door reflects them, and a low porch ceiling or soffit just above the camera catches them and floods the image. The fix is placement: outside the glass, a few inches below the soffit edge, or tilted so the ceiling isn't in the frame. Uniview's dual-light cameras can also switch to their warm white light, which reflects less.

Do you take away the old DVR and cameras, and what happens to the recordings on that old drive?
+

We take the old equipment away if you want us to, and we leave it with you if you would rather keep it. Before anything leaves the house, we ask whether you need to save any recordings; if so, we export them first. If you want the old drive destroyed, we can remove it and hand it to you so you can dispose of it yourself, which is the only way to be certain where it ends up.

Can a camera watch the pool gate and alert us when it opens?
+

It is a camera alert, not a pool alarm: it depends on the phone, the app and the internet, so it does not replace the gate latch, door alarms or any pool barrier the building code requires. Treat it as an extra pair of eyes.
