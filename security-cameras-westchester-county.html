The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Buyers Need a Camera Store That Knows Lobbies, Storefronts and Long Driveways

Westchester is the most urban county in the Hudson Valley, and that changes what a camera store has to stock. South of the Cross County Parkway, the typical buyer is not a homeowner with a barn; it is a co-op board in Yonkers, a superintendent in Mount Vernon, a condo association in a White Plains tower, or a shop owner on Central Avenue or Main Street in New Rochelle. Then, twenty-five minutes north on the Saw Mill or I-684, the buyer changes completely: a stone colonial in Bedford, a horse property in North Salem, a house in Pound Ridge at the end of a quarter-mile drive. We sell security camera equipment to both halves of the county, and the equipment is different for each.

The Census numbers show the split clearly. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020–2024 five-year estimates, table B25024) counts about 394,400 housing units in Westchester County. Only about 43 percent are single-family detached houses. About 99,300 units — one in four — are in buildings with 20 or more apartments, and roughly 68,100 of those are in buildings with 50 or more. Table B25003 shows about 38 percent of occupied homes are rented. For a camera buyer, that means the most common Westchester purchase is not a four-camera house kit. It is a recorder with 16 or 32 channels, dome cameras for corridors and lobbies, and a plan for who in the building gets to watch.

White Plains stands out: nearly half its homes are in buildings that size, many of them downtown towers. Pound Ridge sits at the other extreme, with almost every home a detached house.

What the crime numbers say about where cameras earn their keep

New York's Division of Criminal Justice Services, in its Index Crimes by County and Agency dataset, reports 831 burglaries, 7,679 larcenies and 812 motor vehicle thefts across Westchester County in 2024. The four southern cities account for an outsized share: Yonkers reported 284 burglaries, 1,538 larcenies and 315 vehicle thefts; Mount Vernon 97, 737 and 193; New Rochelle 50, 985 and 56; White Plains 56, 1,006 and 29. By our arithmetic, those four cities account for about 59 percent of the county's burglaries, 56 percent of its larcenies and 73 percent of its vehicle thefts. (These DCJS figures were pulled and checked for our Westchester CCTV research in November 2026.) The pattern is theft of things in busy places — shelves, lobbies, porches, parked cars — more than break-ins at quiet houses.

Package theft, caught on someone's camera

Westchester porch theft cases tend to start with a homeowner's own video. In May 2025, News 12 Westchester reported that the Port Chester Police Department shared Ring camera video of a man in a black hoodie walking up to a porch on S. Regent Street and leaving with three packages. In January 2024, ABC7 New York reported that a Yonkers homeowner watched a man take two packages on his Ring doorbell camera, called 911, and that a Yonkers public works employee knocked the fleeing man to the ground; the man was charged with grand larceny and criminal possession of stolen property. Charges are accusations, not convictions.

Both cases show the job a front door camera really has: record clearly enough, and alert fast enough, that someone can act. In apartment buildings the problem moves indoors. A package shelf in a lobby or a delivery room behind a locked door is where boxes sit for hours, and security cameras for holiday package theft in a Westchester building go on that shelf and on the door to it, not on the street.

Parked cars and catalytic converters

Street parking and garages under buildings make vehicles a camera topic in southern Westchester. In March 2023, Patch reported that Yonkers police, answering a call about a suspicious vehicle in a parking garage behind 80 Alexander Street, found 17 cut catalytic converters and cutting tools in two cars, including a BMW reported stolen from Suffolk County, and arrested three New York City men. Police Commissioner Chris Sapienza called catalytic converter theft "a serious problem in our region" and told residents, "trust your instincts, if you see something out of the ordinary, give us a call." A garage camera aimed along a row of parked cars, low enough to see the side of each vehicle, is what records the minutes those calls are about.

Commuter towns and the corridors between them

Metro-North's Hudson, Harlem and New Haven lines run the length of the county, and the villages built around those stations — Tarrytown, Hastings-on-Hudson, Scarsdale, Bronxville, Larchmont, Rye, Harrison and Chappaqua among them — have a daily rhythm that matters for cameras: houses empty from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., deliveries arriving in the middle of the day, and cars parked all day at the station. Commuters want security cameras with remote monitoring they can actually check from a train seat, which is a question of alerts and app setup more than camera count. The MTA added a commuter garage at the Harrison station in 2021 with 598 spaces, 475 of them for Metro-North riders — one sign of how many cars sit at stations every weekday.

Westchester's retail runs along a few long roads: Central Park Avenue, the "Central Avenue" everyone knows, from Yonkers through Hartsdale toward White Plains, Route 9 along the river towns from Yonkers to Peekskill, Route 119 (Tarrytown Road) across Greenburgh into White Plains, Route 1, the Boston Post Road, through New Rochelle, Larchmont, Mamaroneck, Rye and Port Chester, and the main streets of each downtown — Main Street in New Rochelle, Mamaroneck Avenue in White Plains and Mamaroneck, Gramatan Avenue in Mount Vernon. Car dealerships, delis, pharmacies, nail and hair salons, restaurants and strip plazas line them. Those owners buy business security cameras by the door, the register and the parking spaces, and in Yonkers some of them are buying to a written city standard, covered in the laws section below.

New towers, old walk-ups

Westchester's apartment stock spans a century. Pre-war walk-ups and elevator buildings line the streets of southwest Yonkers, Mount Vernon and parts of New Rochelle, often with coax or nothing at all in the walls. Downtown White Plains and New Rochelle have added glass-and-concrete towers; the Governor's office reported in July 2024 that Highgarden Tower, a 19-story, 219-unit building beside the New Rochelle train station, had been completed. A 1920s walk-up may reuse coax through a DVR; a new tower runs PoE cameras through a riser. We stock both.

Weather and power

Cameras in Westchester see real winters. Meteorologist Don Sutherland's winter 2025–2026 summary noted single-digit lows in White Plains on two consecutive days, February 7 and 8, 2026. Summer storms take down trees and power: Con Edison reported in June 2026 that more than 5,000 Westchester customers lost power as a storm brought trees down across parts of Bronxville, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle, Pelham Village, Scarsdale, White Plains and Yonkers. Outdoor cameras with a cold rating that covers a single-digit night, and a small battery backup on the recorder and the switch, are on every Westchester parts list we write.

Indoor cameras for lobbies, corridors and mailrooms

In Westchester, indoor surveillance cameras outsell outdoor ones in a way they never do in the rest of the Hudson Valley, because so many buyers own or run apartment buildings. A lobby security camera has a specific job: see the face of every person coming through the inner door, not just the tops of heads. That means mounting it inside the vestibule looking in, or inside the lobby looking back at the door, at a height where a person walking toward it fills the frame. A second lobby camera usually covers the mailboxes and the package shelf, and a third covers the elevator landing.

Hallway security cameras for long double-loaded corridors need a narrower lens than people expect; a wide camera at one end of a 120-foot hallway shows the far end as a few pixels. Stairwell security cameras go at each landing door, pointed at the door rather than the stairs. Basement security cameras cover the laundry room, the boiler room door, the storage cages and the door to the yard where the trash goes out. Elevator security cameras, inside the cab, need the elevator company's sign-off on the traveling cable. Dome and turret cameras are the shapes for all of these, because they sit flat on a ceiling and do not invite a hand.

Outdoor cameras for entrances, garages and yards

Outdoor surveillance cameras in the southern cities mostly watch close, busy places: the stoop of a two-family, the sidewalk in front of a store, a building's side door, the ramp into a garage under the building, the alley where the trash cans sit. North of White Plains they watch far: a driveway curving through trees, a gate at the road, a barn or a pool house. Our house-brand outdoor cameras are rated IP66 or IP67 depending on the model, with infrared night vision from 60 to 120 feet across the range. Those figures come from the package spec sheets, and we quote the sheet, not a guess.

Weatherproof security cameras for winters in Westchester need more than a sealed body. The weak point is the cable joint, so outdoor cameras on brick, stone or stucco go on a junction box that hides the connector. Cameras that face a parking lot plowed into a snowbank get mounted high enough that the bank never blocks the view. Outdoor winter security cameras on the north side of a building stay in shade and ice up less; cameras facing south take the low winter sun, and those get a sun hood or a slight downward tilt. 4K, 8MP, 5MP, 2K and 2MP: how sharp is sharp enough

Each Westchester quote goes out as two complete options for the same views, a 2K system and a 4K system, because the right answer depends on distance. In a 12-foot vestibule, a 2K camera already shows a face clearly. Across a 40-car parking lot behind a Central Avenue plaza, or down a 300-foot estate driveway, a 4K camera keeps enough detail to identify a person or a car. Here is how the tiers compare:

The catch is that a fisheye spreads its pixels thin, so it records what happened in the room but rarely a usable face. We pair it with a door camera that does.

PoE, WiFi or coax: why the walls decide

PoE security cameras run on one Cat6 cable each, carrying power and video from a PoE NVR or switch. That is the standard for new work in Westchester buildings, because one cable per camera is reliable, and standard Ethernet, PoE included, runs up to 100 meters (about 328 feet) per segment. In a tall building that limit matters vertically: a camera on the 14th-floor landing is more than 328 cable-feet from a basement recorder once the run climbs the riser and crosses each floor. The answer is a PoE switch in a closet partway up, linked back to the recorder.

WiFi security cameras struggle in the same buildings. Concrete floors, brick party walls, steel stairs and elevator shafts swallow WiFi signal, and a lobby camera competing with fifty residents' routers on the same channels drops frames. WiFi has a place for one view in a single-family house, or where no cable can go. Wired analog cameras — HD coax on RG59 or Siamese cable, recorded by a DVR — are still common in pre-war Westchester buildings that had a CCTV system in the 1990s or 2000s. If the old coax tests clean, an HD DVR and new coax cameras give a big jump in picture without opening walls.

Buy PoE cameras, wired security cameras or wireless security cameras here, and we size the parts to the building: the switch power budget, riser-rated or plenum-rated cable where the fire code calls for it, and which runs need an outdoor jacket.

Night vision under streetlights and glass

Southern Westchester is rarely dark; it is badly lit. Streetlights, storefront signs and headlights create glare and deep shadow in the same frame. Starlight and low light security cameras handle that mix better than plain infrared, and full color night vision cameras keep the color of a jacket or a car, which helps when describing someone to police. Night vision security cameras inside a glass vestibule have their own problem: infrared bounces off the glass and blinds the camera. Turret cameras mounted with the lens close to the glass, or with IR switched off where the lobby lights stay on all night, solve it. North of I-287, real darkness returns, and long-range infrared or a color camera with a soft white light covers the drive.

Smart detection that cuts the noise

A camera facing a busy Mount Vernon sidewalk sees hundreds of people a day. Motion detection cameras alone would send hundreds of alerts. Security cameras with person detection and vehicle detection, plus a zone drawn around just the door or just the driveway, alert only when someone actually enters that area. Several house-brand packages list smart AI human and vehicle detection on their spec sheets; smart search security camera systems then let a superintendent search a night of recordings for "person at the package room door" instead of scrubbing the timeline.

License plate recognition cameras (LPR, also sold as ANPR cameras or license plate capture cameras), facial recognition security cameras and people counting cameras are separate products with their own lenses and setup. We describe a product as having LPR, facial recognition, NDAA compliance, ONVIF, H.265, IP67, IK10 or edge AI only when the manufacturer's spec sheet says so, and we send that sheet before you buy.

Storage math for a 30-day building

Thirty days of footage is a common target in Westchester: some boards ask for it, insurers like it, and Yonkers requires it of the businesses its camera ordinance covers. Here is how we estimate drive size for a 16-camera building. It is an estimate; the real figure depends on each camera's bitrate, compression, how busy each view is and whether recording is continuous or motion-only.

StepCalculation (example only)Result
1. Bitrate per cameraAssume 4 Mbps (a 4MP-class camera at a moderate setting)4 Mbps
2. Per camera, per hour4 Mbps × 3,600 seconds ÷ 8 bits per byte1.8 GB per hour
3. Per camera, per day1.8 GB × 24 hours43.2 GB per day
4. Sixteen cameras, per day43.2 GB × 16691.2 GB per day
5. Thirty days691.2 GB × 30about 20.7 TB

The formula is bitrate times seconds, divided by eight to turn bits into bytes, times hours, days and cameras. Two things bring the number down without losing what matters: lower bitrate on views where nothing happens for hours, such as a roof door, and motion or smart-event recording on those same views while the lobby and package room record continuously. Four things push it up: 4K cameras, busy sidewalk views, high frame rates and long retention. Security camera laws for Westchester homes and businesses

Four rules come up most. The first is local to one city. Yonkers City Code Chapter 84, adopted in 2014, requires certain businesses to keep a video camera system: holders of state liquor licenses, businesses open to the public between midnight and 4 a.m., check cashers, licensed secondhand precious-metal dealers, state-licensed pharmacies and city-licensed pawn shops. The code calls for cameras at every entrance and exit that capture full faces, at least eight frames per second, a digital recorder that can export to portable media with date and time, recording during business hours and two hours after closing, 30 days of retention, locked storage of the recorder, an access log, police access on request and an annual certification to the Yonkers Police Department by January 31. Covered Yonkers businesses should buy to that list; we size the drive for the 30 days.

The second is audio. Under New York Penal Law §250.05, eavesdropping is a class E felony, and §250.00 defines mechanical overhearing as recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party. Audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras are set up deliberately: usually off in lobbies, stores and offices, on only where there is a clear reason and people know. The third is private spaces at work: New York Labor Law §203-c bars an employer from video recording employees in a restroom, locker room or room designated for changing clothes, unless authorized by court order.

The fourth is a rule that stops at the city line. New York City's biometric identifier signage law, which requires certain stores and restaurants to post signs if they collect face data, is part of the New York City Administrative Code and covers the five boroughs; it does not reach a store in Yonkers or White Plains, though Westchester's cities, towns and villages have their own codes. Security camera laws, CCTV regulations and security camera privacy laws for homes come down to common sense: record your own property and shared areas, not the inside of anyone else's home, a bathroom or a changing area, and do not aim cameras into a neighbor's windows. Are security cameras legal on a Westchester house, building or store? Video of your own property and common areas generally is; the edges are audio, private spaces, other people's property and, in Yonkers, the extra duties Chapter 84 puts on covered businesses. This is general information, not legal advice.

Your Questions Answered

Buying Security Cameras in Westchester County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Westchester buyers ask before they spend anything, answered the way we answer them on the phone. Most come back to three things: which doors matter, where the recorder lives, and who needs to watch.

Which camera covers a co-op lobby with a glass vestibule that faces the afternoon sun?

Put the camera inside, looking back toward the door, not outside looking in. A camera facing a sunlit glass wall sees bright glass and dark silhouettes; a camera mounted inside the lobby, aimed at the inner door from slightly to one side, sees faces lit by the lobby lights as people walk in. Choose a turret rather than a dome bubble if the camera must sit near glass, so infrared does not reflect back into the lens at night, and pick a model with wide dynamic range on its spec sheet. A second camera over the mailboxes catches the same people from another angle.

How many cameras does a Yonkers or Mount Vernon walk-up need for its entrance, mailboxes and basement?

For a typical five- or six-story walk-up, eight to twelve cameras covers it: the street door, the vestibule or lobby looking at the inner door, the mailboxes, each stair landing door on the ground and top floors, the roof door, the basement corridor, the laundry or boiler room door and the rear yard or trash area. A 16-channel recorder leaves room for more. If the building already has coax from an old system, a 5MP DVR kit may reuse it; if not, IP cameras on new Cat6 up the stairwell are the cleaner long-term choice.

How do cameras on every floor of a Westchester high-rise get back to one recorder?

Through the building's riser and a small network. Cameras on each floor connect to a PoE switch in a closet on that floor or every few floors, and each switch links down the riser to the recorder, usually by fiber in tall buildings because it ignores distance limits and electrical noise. This keeps every camera run under the 328-foot Ethernet limit and means a problem on one floor does not take down the rest. Cable in the riser needs a riser-rated jacket; we sell the right cable, switches and fiber converters for that layout.

Which recorder size fits a Westchester garden apartment complex with several buildings and parking courts?

Count the cameras first: usually two or three per building (entrance, rear, laundry or basement door) plus two per parking court and one each on the dumpster area and the office. A ten-building complex can reach 40 cameras. The choice is one large 64-channel NVR in the office with links to every building, or a 16-channel recorder in each cluster of buildings, all shown in one app. One recorder is easier to search; several recorders mean a cable cut affects only one cluster. We quote both.

How should a car lot on Central Avenue split cameras between the showroom and the overnight rows?

Spend the resolution outside. Inside the showroom, 2K turrets or domes cover the sales floor, the desks and the key cabinet well because everything is within 40 feet. Outside, the overnight rows need 4K or long-range cameras on the building corners and light poles, aimed down each row so a person between cars is visible, plus one camera on the lot entrance with vehicle detection set to alert after closing. A 32-channel recorder with room to grow is typical, and the outdoor cameras should be rated for the winter on their spec sheets.

What should a Yonkers bodega, bar or pharmacy buy to meet the city's camera ordinance?

Start from Chapter 84's list: a camera at every entrance and exit placed to capture full faces, recording at eight or more frames per second, a digital recorder that exports to a USB drive or similar with date and time stamps, enough storage for 30 days, and a locked place for the recorder. In practice that means turret cameras at head height facing each door, a 2K or better camera over the register, an 8- or 16-channel NVR with a drive sized for 30 days, and a lockable cabinet. Keep the access log and file the January 31 certification. Check the current code text before you buy; this is not legal advice.

Can a home camera help when my car is parked on a Yonkers or Mount Vernon street overnight?

Yes, if the car is parked in view of your house and the camera is chosen for the job. A 4K or varifocal camera at the second floor, aimed along the curb where you usually park, with color night vision or good low-light performance, records a person crouching beside the car. Vehicle and person detection with a zone drawn around your parking spot can send an alert at 3 a.m. without alerting on every passing car. Keep the camera aimed at the street and your own property, not into neighbors' windows.

Which cameras suit a stone Tudor or colonial in Scarsdale, Bronxville or Pelham?

Turret cameras under the deep eaves these houses usually have, in white or black to match the trim, on junction boxes where they meet stone or stucco. Five to eight cameras covers most lots: the front walk and door, the driveway, the detached garage, the side passage, the back door and the yard. Cable runs through the attic and basement so nothing crosses the facade, and the recorder goes in a basement closet or a home office. A 2K system is usually enough close to the house; the driveway view benefits from 4K.

Should a North Salem or Pound Ridge estate keep its recorder in the main house or the barn?

The main house, nearly always. It has the best power, the internet connection, and someone to notice if the recorder goes offline. The barn, garage or pool house gets a PoE switch for its own cameras, linked back to the house by buried cable in conduit, fiber or a wireless bridge with line of sight. Put a small battery backup on the recorder and on the switch at each building so cameras keep recording through a short outage, and keep the recorder away from the entry doors so it is not the first thing a burglar finds.

Which camera shows faces at a Westchester building's intercom door when tenants buzz in visitors?

A turret camera inside the vestibule, aimed at the space in front of the intercom panel at face height, records who was buzzed in and when. The intercom's own video, where it has one, shows only what the panel sees at that moment; a separate camera on the building recorder keeps 30 days of it. Security Camera Prices in Westchester County: What Moves the Number

Security camera prices, CCTV camera prices and security camera system cost questions in Westchester come down to a short list, with one item that matters more here than in most of the Hudson Valley: the building.   • Building type and floors. A house kit needs one recorder; a 12-story building needs switches on several floors, riser cable and often fiber.
  • Camera count and recorder channels. Past four cameras you need an 8-channel recorder; past eight, 16; buildings often need 32 or more.
  • Resolution. 2K, 5MP and 4K cost progressively more, and 4K needs a 4K-capable recorder and more drive space.

  • Camera features. Varifocal lenses, full color night vision, sirens and strobes, two-way talk, PTZ and person and vehicle detection.
  • Accessories. Junction boxes for stone and brick, fire-rated cable, PoE switches, lockable recorder cabinets and battery backups.
  • Installation. For installed systems, the cable route: an open basement and a drop ceiling are quicker than plaster walls and finished lobbies.

FAQ

Westchester County Security Cameras FAQ

Does Yonkers' video camera ordinance apply to stores in White Plains, New Rochelle or Mount Vernon?
+

No. Chapter 84 of the Yonkers City Code applies to covered businesses inside the City of Yonkers: liquor licensees, businesses open to the public between midnight and 4 a.m., check cashers, pharmacies, pawn shops and precious-metal dealers. Other Westchester cities, towns and villages have their own codes, so a business elsewhere should check its local rules. Many owners outside Yonkers still use the Yonkers list as a sensible standard: a camera on every door that captures faces, and 30 days of recording.

Can a Westchester co-op board, super and managing agent each have their own view of the lobby cameras?
+

Yes. The recorders we sell support separate user accounts, each with its own password, its own list of cameras and its own permissions. The super can see live views and play back the last few days, the managing agent can export clips, and board members can be given the access the board's policy allows. When someone leaves, their account is removed without changing anyone else's login.

Can cameras watch the garage under a Westchester condo building and the cars parked in it?
+

Yes. Garage coverage usually means a camera on the entrance ramp facing out so it sees each vehicle's front, a camera on each pedestrian door into the building, and cameras aimed down each row of parking at a height that shows people between cars. Garages are dim and full of concrete columns, so we choose low-light cameras and place them to look along the rows rather than across them.

Which camera watches the package room or delivery shelf in a Westchester apartment building?
+

Two cameras do it best. A turret or dome over the door of the package room, aimed at face height, records who goes in and out. A second camera inside, high in a corner or as a fisheye on the ceiling, shows the shelves so you can see which box was taken. With 30 days on the recorder, a resident who reports a missing package a week later can still be helped.

Can you set up cameras for a Westchester swim club, tennis club or private pool?
+

Yes, for the entrances, parking, gate, pool deck perimeter and equipment areas. Cameras stay out of changing rooms, showers and restrooms entirely, and staff locker rooms are off limits under state labor law. Many clubs use cameras mainly in the off-season, with person detection set to alert when someone enters the closed pool area at night.

Can I buy cameras for a Westchester townhouse complex where the HOA owns the exterior walls?
+

You can buy them, but mounting on walls or roofs the association owns usually needs the board's written approval first. Ask the managing agent for the association's rules on exterior changes. In the meantime, a camera inside a window or a doorbell camera at an approved spot is often allowed. For the association itself, we quote the entrance, clubhouse, pool and mailbox areas as one system.

Can a Westchester doctor's or dental office keep cameras out of treatment rooms and still cover the front desk?
+

Yes, and that is the normal layout. Cameras cover the suite entrance, the waiting room, the front desk and the corridor to the back, and stay out of exam rooms, operatories and restrooms. Audio stays off. The camera over the front desk is aimed at the counter and the people at it, not at screens or paperwork.

Can I order a camera kit as a holiday gift for a parent in Westchester and have you set it up?
+

Yes. You can order and pay for the equipment, have it delivered to your parent's home, and book installation so our crew mounts it and sets up the phone app. We can add you as a second user so you can check the front door view from your own phone if your parent agrees. Gift orders follow the normal equipment and installation terms; there is no special holiday pricing unless we announce one.

Will the cameras keep recording if my Westchester co-op switches internet providers?
+

Yes. The recorders we sell record to drives inside the building, so changing internet providers does not stop recording. What changes is remote viewing: after the switch, the recorder has to be reconnected to the new router so the phone app can reach it again. Tell us the date of the switch and we can talk the super through it or reconnect it on a visit.

Can a private road association or gated community in Westchester buy cameras for its entrance?
+

Yes. The usual setup is a camera at the gate or the road entrance aimed to catch each vehicle and, where a plate camera's spec sheet supports it, the plate, plus a wider camera on the approach. Power and internet at the entrance decide the parts list: a gatehouse with power is simple; a bare post may need a long cable run, a wireless bridge from the nearest house, or a solar-supported camera.

Do the house-brand cameras come in black to match a Westchester building's dark trim?
+

Some do. Most house-brand packages come in white, and the 2MP eyeball package lists both white and black. Where a black camera is important, such as under a dark canopy or on black window trim, we can suggest a model that comes in black, including name-brand options, and confirm the color before you order.
