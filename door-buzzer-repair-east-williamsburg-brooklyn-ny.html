The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

East Williamsburg's buzzers fail in two different landscapes, and the repair that suits one is wrong for the other. The western half of the neighborhood, toward Graham Avenue, Grand Street and Bushwick Avenue, is mostly three- to six-story walk-ups and apartments over storefronts, with a street door, a vestibule, a narrow stair and one entry panel serving every apartment. The eastern half is the East Williamsburg In-Place Industrial Park, one of eight such parks in the city, where low brick factories, concrete and heavy-timber lofts from the 1920s and corrugated-steel warehouses sit along Morgan Avenue, Bogart Street, Ingraham Street, Stagg Street and Johnson Avenue. City Neighborhoods NYC notes that many of those warehouses became live-work lofts in the 1990s, and Untapped Cities describes Morgantown factory owners converting empty buildings into loft housing in the same years, including the McKibbin Street lofts that housed roughly 400 people.

That history is the root of most of the strange buzzer faults we expect to meet east of Bushwick Avenue. A factory was not built with an intercom. When people started living and working in it, somebody added one: sometimes the owner, sometimes a contractor hired by the tenants, sometimes a resident with a roll of bell wire and a free weekend. Later, as buildings went through the Loft Law process, which the Loft Board's own fact sheet describes as Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law, enacted in 1982, requiring owners of interim multiple dwellings to bring the converted spaces up to code, walls moved, units were split or joined and entrances were rebuilt. The buzzer wire often stayed where it was. So a single loft building can have a directory panel from one decade, stations from two others, splices hidden above a dropped ceiling, and a strike in a heavy industrial door that was never meant to be released electrically at all.

West of Bushwick Avenue, the problems are more familiar but no less real. The buildings are old, mostly built for workers' families near Graham and Bushwick Avenues, and many are rent-stabilized. Openigloo's listings put the neighborhood at roughly four hundred buildings, and one tenant review of a 1920 four-unit building on Metropolitan Avenue sums up the issue in five words in its list of cons: "Intercom system didn't work." In these buildings the street door, the panel and the transformer are shared, every apartment has its own station, and the repair usually comes down to one bad station, a corroded junction in the cellar, a strike that has worn loose from a frame that has been painted a dozen times, or a closer that no longer pulls the door shut.

What ties the two halves together is the cost of a failure. When a buzzer dies in a loft building, residents start leaving the street door unlatched for friends, couriers and clients, and a building full of studios, tools and equipment becomes a building anyone can walk into. When it dies in a walk-up, an older tenant walks down four flights for every visitor, or the vestibule door gets a wedge. News 12 reported in December 2022 on a tenant in neighboring Bushwick whose building doors would not latch, under the headline "There is no protection." That is the real cost we see behind a broken buzzer: not the inconvenience, but a lock that someone paid for and that no longer protects anyone.

Most East Williamsburg owners who call us about a dead buzzer end up asking about the door itself too, because a strike can only release a door that closes and latches properly. Many also pair the repair with access control for a studio floor or a side door, which we cover on our other East Williamsburg service pages.

Six East Williamsburg numbers behind a buzzer repair

  • Intercom violations are steady, not rare: HPD's violation records for its East Williamsburg neighborhood area show 10 intercom violations in 2022, 5 in 2023, 7 in 2024, 9 in 2025 and 11 between January 1 and November 11, 2026, already the highest year since 2019.
  • The class mix shifted in 2026: all 9 of 2025's intercom violations were class B, the hazardous class; 2026 has 5 class A and 6 class B.
  • Door violations dwarf buzzer violations: the same records show 302 violations mentioning a self-closing door in 2026, after 321 in 2025 and 341 in 2023. Of the 623 written since January 2025, 111 also mention a vestibule, a building entrance, a front entrance or a street door.
  • Complaints in the wider district are high: 311 logged 153 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints in Brooklyn Community Board 1, which covers Greenpoint, Williamsburg and East Williamsburg, between January 1 and November 11, 2026, after 131 in all of 2025 and a peak of 236 in 2023.
  • Most complaints never get an inspection: of those 153, 52 closed because the inspector could not get in and heard nothing back, and 27 more closed because the inspection could not be completed. Only 21 closed with violations issued.
  • The industrial park is large: Wikipedia gives East Williamsburg 895.74 acres and a 2010 population of 34,158, and most of the land east of Bushwick Avenue sits inside the In-Place Industrial Park managed by EWVIDCO since 1982.

HPD's East Williamsburg area is drawn more widely than many residents would draw the neighborhood: it reaches west to streets such as Union Avenue, Lorimer Street and Manhattan Avenue that many people would call Williamsburg or Greenpoint. Buzzer violations are few; door violations are many. The first thing the table shows is the gap between the second column and the fourth. HPD writes an intercom violation in this part of Brooklyn about once a month. It writes a self-closing door violation most days. Many of those door violations are about apartment entrance doors, which have their own self-closing rule, but 111 of the 623 written since January 2025 also name a vestibule, a building entrance, a front entrance or a street door. For a buzzer technician, that matters more than it looks. A strike can release a door, but it can't lock one that doesn't close. A good share of what tenants report as "the buzzer is broken" turns out, on the day, to be a door that drifts short of the frame, bounces off the latch or is held open by a closer that has lost its oil.

2026 is already the busiest year for intercom violations since 2019. Eleven violations in a little over ten months is more than any full year since the 14 recorded in 2015 and 2016. What the records do show is where they landed: three on Metropolitan Avenue, one each on Kingsland Avenue, Skillman Avenue, Thames Street, Seigel Street, Bushwick Avenue, Stagg Street and Grand Street, and a dismissed one on Metropolitan Avenue in April. That is a spread across the neighborhood, not one problem building.

Most district complaints end without an inspection. Of the 153 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints filed in Community Board 1 so far in 2026, 52 closed with HPD's standard note that an inspector "was not able to gain access," left a card, sent a letter, did not hear back in time, and closed the complaint. Another 27 closed because the inspection could not be completed. Thirty-two closed after an inspection found that the conditions "did not violate the housing laws," which most often means the buzzer was working when the inspector tried it. Only 21 closed with violations issued, 17 were still open on November 11, and 3 closed after someone at the complainant's phone number said the condition had been fixed.

The same apartments come back. In the violation records, one apartment on Lorimer Street was written in September 2024 as class A and again in November 2024 as class B. A Maujer Street building has violations for apartment 1L in June 2022 and again in July 2024, and a different apartment in December 2025. Two Meserole Street apartments were written within a month of each other in mid-2025, and a third in the same building's private hallway that December. On Metropolitan Avenue, one 2026 violation is marked "not complied with," which means HPD came back and the condition was still there. Repeat violations at one address are the clearest sign that earlier repairs fixed a symptom at one apartment while the cause stayed somewhere in the shared part of the system.

Reading the Violation

How HPD Words an East Williamsburg Buzzer Violation, and Where Each Phrase Sends a Technician

Every recent intercom violation in HPD's East Williamsburg area is written under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, the section that makes owners responsible for keeping a building in good repair. Inspectors describe the failure as a path: where it starts, where it ends, which wall and which room the apartment's station is on, and which apartment it serves. Read closely, the wording is a work order. These are phrases from real East Williamsburg violations and what each tells us before we arrive.

Kitchens, foyers and private hallways. East Williamsburg's violations place stations in kitchens on Union Avenue, Beadel Street and Maujer Street, in foyers in most of the walk-ups, and in "private hallways" on Meserole Street, Maspeth Avenue and Union Avenue. In a railroad flat or a converted loft, that tells us how far the station sits from the apartment's door and where its wire is likely to come in.

Class A and class B. HPD's correction periods are generally 90 days for class A and 30 days for class B, according to ButterflyMX's summary of HPD guidance, which also notes that classification depends on what the inspector observes rather than following an automatic rule. Both classes stay on the building's record until the owner certifies the correction and HPD accepts it. In East Williamsburg, the 2026 violations split almost evenly, five class A and six class B.

Status words. In the East Williamsburg records we found "NOV sent out," which means the notice has gone to the owner; "violation will be reinspected"; "not complied with," which means HPD came back and the condition was still there; "CIV14 mailed," which in HPD's process follows an owner's certification of correction and means HPD has written to the tenant to ask whether the repair was really made; "second no access to re-inspect violation," on a Kingsland Avenue building; and "violation dismissed" or "violation closed." An owner who sees "not complied with" or "CIV14 mailed" on a buzzer violation should make sure the repair actually works from the street door before anyone tells HPD it does.

What we leave behind. After a repair we write down which unit we tested, from which entrance, what failed, what we replaced or remade, and how we tested it afterwards: a call from the entrance to the unit, a two-way conversation where the system has voice, and a release that opens the door. We date it and leave it with the owner or managing agent. We don't file certifications with HPD, because that is the owner's statement to the city, but the note is written so that an owner can rely on it when they do.

Loft Buildings

Buzzers in Converted Lofts: Why a Factory's Entry System Fails Differently From a Tenement's

A walk-up tenement was built around a stair. Its buzzer wire follows the stair up, one apartment per side per floor, and when a station dies the fault is usually within a few feet of it. A factory was built around a floor. When it became lofts, the units were laid out along corridors that can run the length of the building, often added in stages, and the buzzer wire went wherever the person installing it could get it: above a dropped ceiling, along a beam, through a sprinkler room, across a roof bulkhead, or down an old freight shaft. The result is a system that may look modern at the street door and be held together behind the walls by splices nobody has seen in years.

Four things that make loft buzzers different

The wire is long and it was rarely planned. New York YIMBY describes the blocks around 101 Morgan Avenue as zoned for low-density industrial and commercial use, with older three-story walk-ups nearby. In the buildings that did become housing, the entry cable often runs a long way from the panel to the far units and through several splices on the way. Long runs aren't a problem on their own. Unplanned splices are. When a loft building's buzzer works for some units and fails for others, the dividing line usually falls at a junction box, not at a floor.

The door was built for trucks or workers, not visitors. Many loft entrances are heavy steel doors, some fire-rated, some originally hung with a simple deadbolt and a closer. An electric strike can be fitted to these doors, but it has to be the right strike for the door's weight, its latch and its frame, and the door has to close square and latch on its own every time. A strike that was fitted to a door that sags, or that was installed in a hollow metal frame without proper support, will release erratically or not at all. In our experience a good part of "the buzzer doesn't open the door" calls in this kind of building are really door and frame problems.

Units were split, merged and renumbered. Legalization under the Loft Law means bringing an interim multiple dwelling into code compliance, and the Loft Board's fact sheet notes that units must have an entrance that doesn't require passing through another unit. In practice, that has meant new corridors and new unit numbers in many buildings. The directory panel at the street door may still list the old numbers, or a button may still be wired to a unit that was split in two. When a resident says "we never get our buzz, the people next door do," the wire is often doing exactly what it was told to do years ago.

Nobody holds the history. Ownership of loft buildings in this neighborhood has changed often, and DNAinfo reported city crackdowns in 2016 and 2017 on illegal hotel use at the McKibbin Street lofts, a reminder that some buildings have also been through periods of very short-term occupancy. A new owner or managing agent may have no drawings, no installer's name and no record of what was done. That's why our first visit to a loft building is as much about mapping the system as about fixing the one failure that prompted the call.

Before You Call

What a Resident, Owner or Studio Tenant Can Tell Us Before the Visit

In a walk-up, the tenant knows what happens upstairs and the owner knows who touched the system last. In a loft building, add the commercial tenants on the ground floor, the residents' association and sometimes a building manager who was hired after everyone else moved in. Brick Underground's repair guide quotes Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech: "A lot of times it's user error." Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith adds, in the same article, "It's all in the wire connections." Both can be true in the same building, and a few minutes of watching and listening separates them. Please don't open any box or touch any wire; watch, listen and write it down.

Residents: keep a short log

Ask a friend or a regular courier to ring your unit from the street door a few times over two or three days, and write down what happened each time: did it ring, could you talk, did the door open. The 311 figures for this district show how many complaints close because the inspector came on a day the buzzer worked or couldn't get in at all. A written log of when it fails is the most useful thing a resident can hand an owner, a manager or a technician.

Owners and managers: know where the power is

Somewhere in the building there is a transformer or power supply that runs the entry system. In a tenement it is often in the cellar near the meters. In a loft it might be in a closet behind the entrance, a utility room, an old office or above a ceiling tile in the corridor. You don't need to open it; just know where it is, and whether the key to that room is with you, the super or a commercial tenant. If nobody knows where it is, say so. That is useful information too, and it changes how we plan the visit.

Door Buzzer Repair for East Williamsburg's Architecture

East Williamsburg has been a village, a factory district, a neighborhood of Italian, Puerto Rican and Latin American families, and an artists' quarter, and its buildings show every one of those layers. Each building type has its own kind of entrance, its own wiring habits and its own person who can approve a repair. These are the ones we expect to meet.

Heavy-Timber and Concrete Factory Lofts

The 1920s and 1930s buildings along Morgan Avenue, Meadow Street, Ingraham Street and Bogart Street, now apartments, studios or both. Long corridors, a heavy steel entrance, a directory or code panel and wire that went in after the building was built.

Loft Law Buildings

Interim multiple dwellings still moving through legalization, or finished with it. A system put in by tenants, a previous owner or a contractor, units renumbered along the way, and an owner who now answers for all of it.

Walk-Up Tenements

Three- to five-story brick buildings on Maujer, Stagg, Meserole, Ten Eyck and Jackson Streets and the blocks off Graham Avenue. A street door, a vestibule door, one panel and a station in each apartment's foyer or kitchen.

Apartments Over Stores

Mixed-use buildings on Graham Avenue, Grand Street and Metropolitan Avenue, with a residents' door beside a shop. A small system, heavy daytime traffic through the same door, and a button plate exposed to the sidewalk.

Small Houses With Tenants

Two-, three- and four-unit houses, some with separate entrances for the store and the apartments, like the 1920 four-unit building on Metropolitan Avenue described in one tenant review. A bell or simple audio system and a transformer in the cellar.

Newer Elevator and Affordable Buildings

Larger new buildings such as Cooper Park Commons at 89 Maspeth Avenue, an 18-story, 311-home affordable building. A networked panel, an administrator login, an installer's warranty and a management company that has to approve every change.

Rules and practical limits at business and mixed-use doors

  • The building's door and the tenant's door. In a mixed-use building the residents' entrance, its panel and everything serving the apartments belong to the owner, who answers to HPD. A store or studio can usually have its own release on its own door, but should not tap into the residents' system without the owner's written OK.
  • Magnetic locks need a way out. Some studio and office doors are held by a magnet instead of a strike. Code conditions for electrically locked egress doors require that people inside can always get out, typically with a request-to-exit sensor or a release that cuts power to the lock. A repair keeps that working.
  • What happens when the power fails. Allegion explains that a fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power and a fail-safe strike unlocks, and that with a strike, people inside can still leave by the lever. Which one suits a workshop, a venue's stage door or a residents' entrance is decided before the replacement is ordered.
  • Reach heights. The 2010 ADA Standards set the maximum high forward reach for a control at 48 inches. A replacement call button or reception release goes within that reach, which matters at offices, studios that run public events and any business that serves the public.
  • Self-closing residents' doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as a class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice. When a store's deliveries go through the residents' door, the closer and latch on that door get checked on every buzzer visit.
  • Line-voltage work. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work at line voltage. Our work stays on the low-voltage side; if a door supply needs a new outlet or circuit, the quote names it as a separate electrician's job.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Starting an East Williamsburg Loft Job at the Door Before the Panel

On a buzzer call to a converted factory, the first ten minutes happen outside. Before opening the panel, the technician lets the street door swing shut on its own three or four times and watches what it does: does it close square, does the latch catch the strike keeper, does the door bounce, does it stop an inch short. Then someone upstairs presses release while the technician leans lightly on the door, then not at all. A strike that releases only when the door is pushed is telling you the door is pressing on it, not that it is broken. Heavy steel doors in these buildings sag on their hinges over the years, and the keeper ends up taking the door's weight.

Only after the door makes sense does the technician ring units. One that works, one that doesn't, and if possible one on the same corridor as the failing one. If the working and failing units are on the same corridor, the fault is probably at the failing unit or just before it. If a whole corridor is dead, it is at the junction where that corridor joins the main line.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in East Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Our loft is an interim multiple dwelling under the Loft Law, and the buzzer was wired years ago by the people living here before legalization. Is it the owner's to fix now?

The Loft Law places the duty of bringing an interim multiple dwelling up to code on the owner, and once residents live in a multiple dwelling, the owner also answers to HPD for its entry system. Who originally ran the wire doesn't change who has to keep it working today. If there is a lease term, a Loft Board order or a private agreement in your building that says otherwise, that is a question for a lawyer, not for us.

On the technical side, a tenant-installed system can usually be repaired rather than ripped out. We map what is there, identify the panel and stations, test the strike and the supply, and tell the owner in writing which parts can stay and which can't. If the original equipment has no parts left, the quote says which compatible replacement we would use and why.

Our HPD record shows "CIV14 mailed." What does that status mean for us?

In HPD's process, that status generally follows an owner's certification that a violation has been corrected. HPD then writes to the tenant at that apartment to ask whether the repair was actually made. If you are the tenant and the buzzer still doesn't work from the street door, that letter is your chance to say so. If you are the owner, it means HPD may hear from the tenant before it accepts your certification.

Two of the East Williamsburg area's open intercom violations carried that status in November 2026, on Metropolitan Avenue and Stagg Street. In both kinds of building, the practical answer is the same: before anyone tells HPD the buzzer is fixed, test it from the actual street door to that apartment, both ringing and releasing, and keep a dated note of the test.

The violation tells the owner to repair the buzzer "with similar material." Does the new station have to be the same brand as the old ones?

The wording appears on an April 2026 violation on Metropolitan Avenue, and it is about the result rather than a brand name: the repaired apartment has to work with the building's system the way the others do. In practice that usually means a station from the same maker or a compatible model, because a station from a different system may not ring from the existing panel, may not carry voice on the same pair, or may not release the same strike.

If the original maker still supplies parts, we use them. If it doesn't, we choose a station built to work with that type of system and test it from the street door before we leave. The quote names the model, and the condition note records what was installed, so the owner can show what was done.

Our company rents space in a warehouse inside the industrial park, and nobody lives in the building. Does the city's intercom rule apply to our door buzzer?

HPD's housing maintenance code is about residential buildings, and the intercom requirement described in the Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide applies to multiple dwellings, meaning buildings where people live. A purely commercial building's buzzer is a business decision about security and convenience, not an HPD obligation. What still applies is everything about safe exit: an electrically locked door has to let people inside get out, and a magnetic lock needs a working release.

That changes the repair conversation. In a commercial building, the question is what you need the door to do: let staff in with a code, let visitors be buzzed in from reception, let a driver at the loading door reach someone over the noise. We repair the existing equipment toward that, and the written quote says what each part costs.

Our small building on Metropolitan Avenue has one door for the store and another for the apartments, and nobody can remember the intercom ever working. Where does a repair start?

With finding out what is there. One tenant review of a 1920 four-unit building on that avenue lists both "separate entrances" as a plus and "Intercom system didn't work" as a minus, which is a common pairing: separate entrances keep the store's traffic away from the residents, but they also mean the residents' door has its own system that nobody uses daily and nobody notices when it dies.

On the visit we identify the panel, find the transformer, check whether the stations are still connected or were removed during a renovation, and test the strike. Sometimes the whole system is there and one failed transformer explains everything. Sometimes the stations are gone and the wire was cut. The quote then tells you whether this is a repair or whether the system needs rebuilding, with the price of each.

Before we bought our loft building, some units were rented out by the night, and we have no idea who still knows the entry code. What does starting fresh on the keypad involve?

It involves getting into the panel's programming, which is the hard part if nobody handed over the installer or master code. Depending on the maker, that might mean the code from the previous owner, a reset procedure from the manufacturer, or, for some older keypads, replacing the controller. Once we are in, we clear the old codes, set up the current units, and give the owner the master code in writing.

DNAinfo's reports on illegal hotel use at the McKibbin Street lofts in 2016 and 2017 show why this matters in this neighborhood. A code panel is only as secure as the list of people who know the code, and after a period of short-term rentals that list is unknowable. We also recommend confirming that every unit's button or code still reaches the right unit, since renumbering often happened in the same period.

Our loft building has about fifty units on one directory panel. If the panel has to be replaced rather than repaired, how long will residents be without the buzzer?

It depends on what is behind the panel. Brick Underground's repair guide quotes estimates of about a day to replace a five-apartment system and about two days for a hundred units, and a fifty-unit loft falls between those. If the new panel works with the existing stations and wire, the entrance can usually be back in service the same day it is installed, with the remaining units tested and programmed afterwards.

Before any replacement, we look for a repair. Many directory panel faults are in one module, the speaker, the display or a button board, and can be fixed without replacing the whole unit. If replacement is the answer, the quote says so, lists the panel, and gives a plan for the day so the owner can tell residents when to expect the door to be on manual keys.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in East Williamsburg

"Intercom repair usually costs $150 to $400."

That range is real, but it is a national planning figure. ButterflyMX's NYC repair article cites "$150 to $400 for minor repairs as a national planning estimate," and separately a New York source listing "$100 to $300 for diagnostics, $500 to $1,500 for component replacement." Cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr average jobs the same way.

In East Williamsburg, the job decides the price. A dead station in a Graham Avenue walk-up and a corridor of silent lofts in a converted factory are both "intercom repairs," and they are nowhere near the same amount of work. Our Brooklyn list starts at a $250 service call with diagnosis included and runs from $250 for a station to $2,400 for riser or corridor wire repair.

The useful number is the one you get after someone has tested the door, the supply and the failing unit. That is why our quote comes after the diagnosis, in writing, before any part is installed.

"Old buzzer systems are obsolete, so it's better to replace them than repair them."

Some old systems really are at the end of their life. But Brick Underground's repair guide makes the opposite point for many of them: older systems such as Lee Dan may be outdated, but their core wiring is typically fine, and most problems are in the connections. "Intercoms are getting outdated," Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith told the site, but outdated is not the same as broken.

In East Williamsburg's tenements, the original wire is often the most reliable part of the system, and what fails is a station, a transformer or a strike. In both cases, a replacement that reuses bad splices or a misaligned door won't work any better than the old system did.

We recommend replacement in writing when parts no longer exist, when the wire itself has failed in places that can't be reached, or when shared parts keep failing. A single dead station or strike is not a reason to replace a system.

"If the buzzer sounds but the door won't open, it's an electrical problem."

ButterflyMX lists the electric strike or maglock, release wiring, relay and power supply as the usual suspects when "the buzzer sounds, but the door stays locked," and that's a fair list. What it leaves out is the door itself. A strike can only release a latch that isn't being pressed against it.

In a converted factory, the street door is often a heavy steel door that has sagged on its hinges. In a tenement, it is often a wood door that has been painted until it binds. In both, the door leans on the strike keeper, and the strike hums without letting go. HPD's records for the East Williamsburg area show hundreds of self-closing door violations a year, and over a hundred since January 2025 mention a building entrance or vestibule.

So our technicians check the door before the electronics: does it close square, does it latch, does it release when someone presses release and nobody pushes. Many "electrical" faults are solved with a hinge, a closer adjustment or a realigned keeper.

"New York's intercom law requires a video intercom or an app."

It doesn't. The Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide describes the requirement for multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments as a two-way voice intercom with a buzzer that opens the door, and ButterflyMX notes that the statute "does not, by itself, require video, mobile access, or remote management." Older buildings must provide self-locking doors and an intercom if a majority of apartments ask for one.

That matters when someone tells an East Williamsburg owner that a dead audio station means the whole building has to move to video. A working audio system that rings, talks and releases meets that requirement.

The practical question for an owner with a violation is simpler: does the cited apartment ring from the entrance, can the two sides talk, and does the release open the door. That's what we test and write down.

"Call 311 and an inspector will come and make the landlord fix the buzzer."

Sometimes. The 2026 figures for Brooklyn Community Board 1 show how often it doesn't work that way. Of 153 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints, 52 closed because the inspector couldn't get in and didn't hear back, 27 closed because the inspection couldn't be completed, and 32 closed with no violation found. Only 21 led to violations.

Brick Underground recounts a renter elsewhere in the city who waited three months for a 311 response about an intercom and a propped door. Its advice, from tenant lawyer Sam Himmelstein, is to notify the landlord in writing first, because written requests get better responses and serve as documentation. A complaint is a way to get a record, not a repair.

For owners, the lesson runs the other way. A closed complaint doesn't mean the buzzer works. Many of the repeat violations we found in East Williamsburg sit at buildings where earlier complaints had already closed.

"Lofts in an industrial zone aren't covered by housing rules, so the buzzer is the tenants' problem."

The Loft Law exists precisely because people were living in industrial buildings. The Loft Board's fact sheet describes it as Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law, enacted in 1982, which created interim multiple dwellings and requires owners to bring the converted spaces into code compliance. Several of the HPD intercom violations in the East Williamsburg area sit on streets inside or right beside the industrial park.

That doesn't mean every space in a factory building is a home in the eyes of the law; many are commercial, and the rules for those are different. It does mean that an owner can't assume a building with residents is outside the housing code because of its zoning.

We don't decide which rules apply to a building. We do ask, before we start, whether the building is residential, commercial or mixed, and whether it is a registered interim multiple dwelling, because it changes who approves the work and what the owner needs from our condition note.

"Most buzzer problems are user error."

There's truth in it, and it comes from a real source. Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech told Brick Underground, "A lot of times it's user error," because some systems need the button held longer or pressed more firmly than people expect.

But a building in HPD's records is not a case of user error. An inspector rang the apartment and it failed. Repeat violations at the same address, like the Lorimer Street apartment written twice in 2024 or the Meserole Street building with three written in 2025, are not people pressing the button wrong.

Our approach is to rule user error out quickly: we hold the release for the length the system needs and show residents how long that is. Then, if it still fails, we find the fault.
