The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Gowanus runs along the canal between Park Slope and Carroll Gardens, roughly from Wyckoff Street down to the Gowanus Expressway and from Bond Street east to Fourth Avenue. The canal was dug in the 1860s, and the housing that grew up around it was built for the people who worked on it. Brownstoner's history of 194 Butler Street, a detached wood-frame house between Bond and Nevins that first appears on the 1880 tax maps, notes that every other house left on that block "were built as wood-framed tenements," and that the house itself "has always had three units." Those buildings, the brick row houses on the numbered streets and the walk-ups of the 1920s and 1930s are where most door buzzer installation work in Gowanus happens.

The other half of the neighborhood is brand new. The city rezoned Gowanus in November 2021 for about 8,500 apartments, roughly 3,000 of them affordable, and Commercial Observer's January 2026 survey lists the results block by block: Union Channel at 240 Third Avenue with 224 apartments, finished early in 2025; Douglass Port at 251 Douglass Street with 260, due in mid-2026; the two Nevins Landing buildings at 310 and 340 Nevins Street with 348 and 306, leasing in 2027; and a 27-story building at 175 Third Street with more than 1,000 rentals, expected in 2029. Bisnow's tour of Union Channel found residents opening the building and its amenities with a smartphone app, with key fobs as a backup.

That contrast is the reason the older buildings call us. A tenant in a four-family on Union Street can walk two blocks and see a lobby where a courier rings a phone and a visitor shows up on video, then come home to a door where, as one review of a 1931 Union Street building on Openigloo put it, "There is no functional intercom in this apartment" and "You can't receive any packages, or let in anyone for maintenance." For the owner, that review is the case for a new system, and it is also the brief: get the visitor to a button, get a voice upstairs, and get the door open and shut again.

The buildings also change shape more often here than in most of Brooklyn. A 2019 6sqft feature on a 1901 row house at 191A 8th Street described an owners' entrance "on the parlor floor, through a bright orange vestibule" and a garden rental "with a private entrance under the stoop," and noted the garden floor could be folded back into a single-family house. Brownstoner's 2024 look at a Gowanus corner renovation followed a building that began as "two rental units above a store" and became one house. Every one of those changes moves the count of households behind the door, and the buzzer has to follow.

And the neighborhood is low. The canal overflowed into streets and basements in Hurricane Sandy, and Brownstoner's readers watched Fourth Avenue and Carroll Street flood again in a 2010 cloudburst, with water running toward the canal and sewer backups reaching "people's homes up the slope." That shapes where a power supply and junctions belong on a Gowanus installation, a decision we make on every walkthrough rather than leaving it to whoever mounted the last transformer on a cellar wall at knee height.

Six Gowanus numbers behind a buzzer installation

  • Old houses dominate the side streets. In our study area, 623 tax lots hold houses of one to four homes, 1,420 homes in all, and 505 of those lots carry a city construction date of 1901 or earlier.
  • The walk-ups are small and old. 122 older apartment buildings of five or more homes hold 1,320 homes, most of them four-story walk-ups the city dates to 1931.
  • The avenues mix shops and homes. 114 store-and-apartment lots hold 343 homes, 59 of those lots on Third Avenue and 26 on Fourth Avenue.
  • The new buildings are now a large share. About 5,000 of the study area's roughly 11,800 homes are in buildings the city dates 2022 through 2025.
  • The city's record is thin. HPD has written 33 intercom violations at 15 Gowanus buildings across the years its data covers, and 311 logged 31 buzzer complaints from 18 addresses between 2020 and October 2026, almost all at the older walk-ups and store buildings.
  • The law draws a line at eight and at 1968. The Attorney General's tenants' guide says buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have "a two-way voice intercom system from each apartment to the front door," while older buildings need one "if requested by a majority of the tenants."

Install or Repair in Gowanus

Need Repair in Gowanus Instead? How We Tell a Fix From a First System

Plenty of installation enquiries from Gowanus start with one dead button. A tenant on 12th Street can no longer buzz the street door, or the speaker on a Fourth Avenue walk-up crackles and then goes quiet. If one part has failed, such as a bell transformer, a strike whose screws have stripped out of an old frame, or a single apartment's handset, that is a repair, and we price it as one.

It stops being a repair in three situations we see often here. The first is a building that never had a talking system, only bell buttons and a release, so there is nothing to repair into talk-back. The second is a building whose apartment count changed: a store turned into a unit, a garden floor split off or folded back in, a cellar finished. The third is an old system where every station is a different make from decades of one-off swaps and the wire between the cellar and the floors no longer tests sound. In each, a new door station, new power and stations on tested or new cable end the cycle. We say which it is after metering every pair, and if a repair is all your building needs, our Brooklyn door buzzer team will quote it that way.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Gowanus's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Gowanus door we are asked to wire. What separates them, for a buzzer, is how many households share the door, what the walls are made of, whether there is one door or two between the sidewalk and the stairs, and how likely it is that the building's count of apartments will change again in the next few years.

Canal-side frame tenements

Wood-frame houses built for canal and factory workers from the 1870s and 1880s, two or three units each, often with a porch or stoop and a single narrow street door.

Numbered-street row houses

Brick and brownstone row houses on the streets from about 7th to 14th, many dated 1901 or earlier, with a stoop to the parlor floor and often a garden entrance below it.

1930s brick walk-ups

Four-story apartment buildings, most dated 1931, with an outer street door, a vestibule and an inner door, and four to eight apartments up one stair.

Avenue store buildings

Two and three floors of apartments over shops on Third and Fourth Avenues, with the residents' door squeezed between storefronts and a cellar shared with the store.

Factories turned studio buildings

Brick and concrete industrial buildings on and near Second and Third Avenues, now rented as studios and workshops, with steel doors, freight entrances and long runs.

Rezoned lots under construction

Infill and tower projects on the blocks rezoned in 2021, where the entry system is drawn on plans and installed alongside the other trades before anyone moves in.

Canal-side frame tenements: light walls, old casings and a porch to think about

A frame tenement is the easiest Gowanus building to get cable through and the hardest to get a good door on. The stud bays in its walls usually run clear from the cellar toward the upper floors, so a new pair for each unit can be fished up inside a wall or a closet with a few small openings at the top and bottom. That keeps labor in the lower part of each range, and it is why a first talk-back system for a three-unit frame house so often comes in near the bottom of our video range.

The door is the problem. A house that has stood since the 1880s has settled, and its street door has usually been rehung, re-keyed and repaired around the lock more than once. An electric strike needs sound wood to hold its screws and a door that meets the frame squarely so the latch drops in every time. When the wood around the keeper is split or patched, a reinforcing plate or new jamb section comes first, and we price it on the quote rather than finding it on installation day.

Many of these houses have a porch, sometimes with original trim, in front of the door. The door station belongs where a visitor naturally stops and where it stays dry, which is usually on the house wall or the door casing under the porch roof, fastened to solid backing rather than to shingles or siding that move with the seasons.

Numbered-street row houses: a stoop, a vestibule and often a second front door below

The row houses on 8th, 9th, 10th, 11th, 12th and 13th Streets usually enter at the parlor floor, up the stoop and through a vestibule with an outer and an inner door. Below the stoop is a garden door that may belong to a rental, a home office or nobody. A buzzer plan has to decide, for each household, which door its visitors use and which release they need.

For a two-family with an owner upstairs and a garden tenant, the cleanest answer is usually one station with two clearly labeled buttons at the gate or the foot of the stoop, a release on each household's own door, and a station inside each unit. If the garden apartment may one day be folded back into the house, as the 8th Street owners in the 6sqft feature planned for, we leave the second button and its cable in place so it can be retired without opening a wall.

Brick party walls are left alone. In attached row houses we route cable inside your own building, up an interior partition, a closet stack or the stair, and if one run has to follow a party wall it goes in a slim raceway on your side.

1930s brick walk-ups: put the panel at the door visitors can reach

The four-story walk-ups the city dates to 1931 are the buildings most often cited for intercoms in Gowanus, and the most common layout fault we see is not electrical. The panel sits on the wall between the outer and inner doors, and the outer door is locked. The Union Street review quoted above described exactly that: "The buzzer only works on the 2nd door and the 1st door is always locked," and "The intercom was past the 1st door."

The fix is to decide which door is the security door and put the panel outside it. If the outer door locks, the panel goes on the street face beside it and the release goes on the outer door; the inner door can stay on a key, or be released too. If the outer door is meant to stand open by day, the panel can stay in the vestibule and the inner door takes the release. What a building cannot have is a locked outer door with the only buttons behind it.

These buildings are solid masonry with plaster on the stair walls, and the old bell riser usually climbs inside a shaft or behind the stair. We meter those pairs first. Where they test sound, a two-wire system reuses them and nothing in the hall has to open; where they do not, new cable follows the old route or a surface raceway painted to the wall.

Avenue store buildings: the residents' door is not the shop's door

On Third and Fourth Avenues the residents' door often sits a few feet from a shop entrance, and the two have been wired together at some point, sharing a cellar transformer or even a bell circuit. We separate them. The apartments get their own door station, release and battery-backed power supply, mounted where the super or owner can reach it without the shop being open, and the shop's buzz-in door, if it wants one, is a separate job on its own power.

The cellar is usually the store's. We agree access with the shop before the visit so the cable from the street door to the stair does not depend on someone's opening hours, and we keep it clear of the store's own equipment and stock. Where the avenue is loud, the door station is chosen for its speaker volume and mounted at a height where a visitor can speak into it rather than over it.

Factories turned studio buildings: one street door, dozens of tenants

Former factories along Second and Third Avenues rent space to artists, makers and small firms, often with dozens of tenants behind one entrance and a freight door used for deliveries. A row of buttons does not scale well there. A directory or code panel lists every studio by name or number, can be updated when a studio changes hands, and can ring a phone for a studio-holder who works off-site.

Steel doors and steel frames are common. They take an electric strike well when the frame is prepared properly, and a magnetic lock only with the sensor release, exit button and fire-alarm release that the building code requires. Long runs from the entrance to the farthest studio are planned on tray or along beams, labeled at both ends.

Rezoned lots under construction: decide before the walls close

On a building still going up, the entry system costs least when it is drawn on the plans: a back box and conduit at the door station, a path to the head-end, a dedicated location for the power supply above the cellar, and boxes at each apartment station or a decision that residents will answer by phone. We work from the architect's drawings with the general contractor and the electrician, pull and test cable when the other trades do, and return to mount and program equipment once finishes are in.

Gowanus adds one decision most Brooklyn sites do not. New York YIMBY reported in 2015 that the twin buildings at 363 and 365 Bond Street were designed with no cellar at all because the site is prone to flooding, and the city's flood-resistant construction rules expect electrical equipment in flood zones to sit at or above the design flood elevation. So the entry system's power and network gear belongs on a floor above the design flood elevation, with only the door station itself at street level.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Where a Gowanus Walkthrough Starts, and Why It Is Not the Panel

When our technician arrives at a Gowanus building, the first ten minutes are spent on the sidewalk, not in the cellar. The question is simple: where would a stranger stop? On a frame house near the canal it may be the porch steps; on a row house on 11th Street, the foot of the stoop or the iron gate; on a 1930s walk-up, the outer door of the vestibule. If the existing buttons are anywhere else, the plan starts by moving them.

Next comes the count. The technician counts mailboxes, bell buttons and apartment doors and asks who actually lives where, because in this neighborhood the paperwork and the building often disagree. A house recorded as two units may have a garden tenant, an owner's studio and an upstairs rental; a studio building may have thirty tenants behind one door and four more sharing one space. Every household or studio that receives visitors needs its own button or directory entry.

Then the doors, outer and inner. Each is opened fully and let go. It should swing shut, latch on the first try and not bounce. A door that has to be pulled closed defeats any buzzer, so its closer, hinges and latch are written down before equipment is discussed.

Only then the cellar. The technician finds the old bell transformer, notes how high it sits off the floor and whether the cellar is reached from the hall or only through a shop or an apartment, and meters each old pair to the floors. On low Gowanus blocks the height of that transformer matters: if it sits where water has stood before, the new supply goes higher.

Conversions and the Law

When a Gowanus Building Changes Its Apartment Count, the Front Door Changes With It

New York's entry rules are written around two numbers: the year 1968 and eight apartments. The Attorney General's Residential Tenants' Rights Guide sums them up. Multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 "must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at all entrances," and if they have eight or more apartments they "must also have a two-way voice intercom system from each apartment to the front door." Buildings from before 1968 "also must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom system if requested by a majority of the tenants." The guide adds that those doors "must be kept locked at all times, except when an attendant is on duty."

In Gowanus that matters because buildings keep crossing those lines. The neighborhood's older walk-ups were built long before 1968, so many owners have never been required to install an intercom, and nobody has asked. A conversion changes that conversation. When a store becomes an apartment, a cellar is legalized, an industrial building on a rezoned block is turned into homes, or a house is split, the building is "converted" in the sense the rules use, and the count after the work is what counts. Your architect or expediter, who files the conversion with the Buildings Department, is the right person to confirm which rule your building falls under.

The practical advice is the same whatever the answer. Put the buzzer into the conversion. While walls are open for a new apartment, its cable, its station and its button on the door panel cost a fraction of what they cost later. If the old system cannot take another apartment, the conversion is the natural moment to replace it, and a single new system for the whole building is easier to keep working than an old one with a new apartment grafted on.

Conversions run the other way here too. Brownstoner's 2024 feature on a Gowanus corner building followed a renovation that "transformed the building into a single-family dwelling with four bedrooms and four baths" from two rentals over a store. A house that becomes one home again does not need a multi-unit panel, but it often still wants a door station at the street, a release and a way to answer from upstairs, and the old buttons and handsets should come out cleanly rather than being left dead on the wall.

How we plan a buzzer around a Gowanus conversion

  • Count after, not before. We size the panel and the power supply for the number of households when the work is finished, plus any unit the owner expects to add or remove in the next few years.
  • Cable while the walls are open. Runs to new or changed units are pulled and tested during the renovation, before plaster or drywall closes, and labeled for the station we will mount later.
  • Every entrance on one plan. Converted buildings often gain or lose a door, such as a former shop entrance that becomes an apartment door. Each one is either put on the system or set to lock and stay locked from outside.
  • Paper for the owner. At handover we leave a written test sheet listing every household, what was tested and the result, for the owner, the architect or the managing agent to keep with the building's records.
Rules at the Door

Landmarks, Shared Entrances and Tenant Data: Rules That Reach a Gowanus Front Door

Three sets of rules come up on Gowanus installations more often than elsewhere in Brooklyn, because of what this neighborhood has become in the last few years.

Individual landmarks among the old industrial buildings

Gowanus has no large historic district, but the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted unanimously on October 29, 2019 to designate five industrial buildings ahead of the rezoning: the Gowanus Pumping Station and Gate House at 196 Butler Street, the Brooklyn Rapid Transit Company Central Power Station Engine House at 153 2nd Street, the Montauk Paint Manufacturing Company Building at 170 2nd Avenue, the ASPCA Rogers Memorial Building at 233 Butler Street and the Somers Brothers Tinware Factory at 238–246 3rd Street. The Coignet Building at Third Avenue and Third Street has been a landmark since 2006. Any of them that gains tenants needs an entry system that suits a landmark.

The Commission's permit guidebook chapter on health and safety work is written for exactly this. Its staff can issue permits for intercom panels, which "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," with "a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel" per opening. Equipment should not be "attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces," should be fastened into mortar joints or plain masonry, and visible conduit is limited to "a maximum of approximately ten inches." The guidebook also says "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of equipment do not require a permit." We size the station small, plan the cable to arrive from inside, and give the owner the cut sheets and photos the application needs.

One entrance for every apartment in a mixed-income building

The rezoning's new buildings mix lottery apartments with market-rate ones, and a 2015 state rule for buildings that receive tax or zoning benefits says that "affordable units shall share the same common entrances and common areas as market rate units." For an entry system that means one street door, one directory and one standard of station or phone answering for every household behind that door. We design new-building systems that way from the start, and leave questions about any particular building's regulatory agreement to its counsel.

Phone apps and the Tenant Data Privacy Law

Many of the new buildings, like Union Channel, open with a phone app and keep fobs as a spare. When an older Gowanus building moves to app or code entry, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to class A multiple dwellings that use smart access. The owner must get tenants' express consent in writing or through the app, must destroy authentication data within 90 days of collecting it unless it is anonymized, and may not require smart access to enter a tenant's own apartment. We set systems up so that a resident who does not want the app can still get in with a fob, a code or a key, and we leave the consent paperwork and the data policy to the owner and the owner's lawyer.

Studio buildings, including the below-market studios that Arts Gowanus negotiated with 11 developers, roughly 30,000 square feet in all according to Commercial Observer, where one entrance serves many studios and visitors arrive by appointment.

Offices and practices in converted buildings

Design studios, small firms and practices in former industrial space, where a receptionist releases a suite or floor door and the street door stays on the building's own entry system.

What a Gowanus business door needs from its buzzer

Most commercial jobs here come down to three choices. The first is the release: a fail-secure electric strike on a steel or wood frame, which stays locked if power fails and never stops anyone leaving, or for a glass storefront door in an aluminum frame, a strike or release made for that frame. The second is where the answer happens: a desk station at the counter, a phone for an owner who is often out on a job, or both. The third is power: a dedicated low-voltage supply with battery backup, mounted where the business controls it rather than in a shared cellar.

For studio buildings and workshops, the freight entrance usually matters as much as the front door. A call button at the loading door that rings whoever is expecting a delivery saves a studio-holder from waiting by the door for a truck, and a code for regular drivers can be limited to working hours.

Rules that apply to business entry installations in Gowanus

  • Free exit always. A door on a path of egress must open from inside without a key or special knowledge. Allegion's guide notes that with an electric strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure, which is why strikes suit most Gowanus business doors.
  • Magnetic locks only with their safeguards. The International Building Code, on which New York's codes are based, allows sensor-release electromagnetic locks only with a sensor on the egress side, a manual "PUSH TO EXIT" device 40 to 48 inches above the floor that cuts power directly, release on loss of power and release when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate.
  • Fire-rated doors keep their rating. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes so they stay latched in a fire; we use hardware listed for the door's rating and do not cut a labeled fire door in a way that voids it.
  • Reachable controls. The 2010 ADA Standards set a high forward reach of 48 inches; we mount call buttons, keypads and exit buttons within reach of a wheelchair user.
  • Landlord approval in writing. In a leased space the door, frame and storefront usually belong to the landlord, and in one of the designated landmarks the Commission's guidance applies to anything on the exterior. We need the owner's written approval before cutting a frame.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Gowanus: Questions Answered

Gowanus's oldest houses and walk-ups

Our block near the canal was built as wood-frame tenements in the 1880s, and our three-unit house has a deep front porch with its original trim. Should the door station go at the porch steps or on the house beside the door?

Usually on the house, under the porch roof, beside the door. That spot is dry, it is where a visitor ends up anyway, and it keeps the station and its cable off the porch posts and trim, which are the parts of an old frame house that are hardest to repair and easiest to spoil with drilling. The cable comes straight through the house wall behind the station rather than running across the porch.

The exception is a porch that is closed off from the street, for example by a gate or a locked screen door. Then visitors cannot reach the house door, and the station belongs at the gate or the top of the steps, on a solid post or a small mounting plate, with the release on whichever door actually keeps people out. We decide it standing on the sidewalk with you, at dusk if possible, because the porch light and the shadows decide how easily a visitor finds the button.

Our 1931 brick walk-up has four apartments and no working intercom at all, so tenants meet couriers and repair crews on the stoop. Is a building this size worth a full talk-back system, or just bells and a release?

For four apartments, talk-back is worth it, and the price difference is smaller than most owners expect. Bells and a release let a tenant open the door blind; talk-back lets them ask who is there, which is what stops strangers following a "delivery" in. On our Brooklyn list, an audio system with a station in each of four apartments and a new release usually sits in the $400 to $900 range for a building this size; adding video at the door and in the apartments moves it into the $700 to $1,800 range.

The bigger variable is the cable. A 1931 walk-up usually has an old bell riser from the cellar to each floor. If those pairs meter sound, a two-wire system reuses them and the job stays near the bottom of its range; if they do not, new cable follows the stair, and the quote says so before you approve. Either way the outer door has to close and lock on its own, or the new system protects nothing.

Our eight-apartment walk-up predates 1968 and nobody has ever asked for an intercom. If we put one in now, does the street door also have to start meeting the self-locking rule?

The Attorney General's guide ties both to the same trigger for older buildings: "self-locking doors and a two-way intercom system if requested by a majority of the tenants." Installing an intercom voluntarily is not, by itself, the event the guide describes, and a lawyer can tell you how the rules read for your building's history. In practice the question answers itself: a buzzer on a door that does not self-close and self-lock lets anyone in behind the first visitor, so we never install one without fixing the door.

Once the system is in, the owner should expect to keep it working. The Housing Maintenance Code treats a building's existing intercom as something to be maintained, and the 33 intercom violations HPD has written in Gowanus are almost all orders to repair systems that already existed. Our written test sheet at handover gives you a record of every apartment working on the day we finished.

We are adding a third household to our 1880s frame house by finishing the attic. Can the existing two-button bell be extended, or should the whole front door be redone?

It depends on what the two buttons are connected to. If they are plain bells with a release button upstairs, there is nothing to extend into talk-back, and a new three-button station with a station in each household is the better buy. If there is already a two-wire talk system that meters sound, many of those systems accept another station and another button, and the attic unit is a modest addition.

The attic itself is the easy part of the cable route in a frame house, because the run can come up through the floor or a closet while the walls are open. Tell us when the attic walls are framed, and we will pull and label the cable before they close, then come back to mount the station once the room is finished.

New buildings on the rezoned blocks

Our new building near the canal has lottery apartments alongside market-rate ones. Can the affordable units be given a simpler intercom or a separate door?

Not if the building receives the tax or zoning benefits the 2015 state rule covers. That rule says "affordable units shall share the same common entrances and common areas as market rate units," and in practice that means one street door, one directory and one way of answering for everyone behind it. We design the entry system so every apartment gets the same station or the same phone answering, whatever its rent.

Some buildings offer upgrades that residents choose and pay for themselves, such as a second indoor monitor. Whether that is appropriate in a regulated building is a question for the building's counsel and its regulatory agreement, and we will price it as an option only once the owner has that answer.

Management at our new Gowanus rental wants residents to come and go by phone app, with fobs only as a spare. What does city law say about tenants who don't want the app?

The Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, covers class A multiple dwellings that use smart access. It requires the owner to get each tenant's express consent in writing or through the app, to destroy authentication data no later than 90 days after collecting it unless it is anonymized, and it bars an owner from requiring smart access to enter a tenant's own apartment. The owner's lawyer should confirm how it applies to the building's street door and amenity doors.

The system design is simpler. We set app-based panels up so that every resident can also get in with a fob, a code or a key, and so that visitors can still call an apartment from the door. Union Channel, which Bisnow visited before it opened in 2025, used exactly that mix of an app with fobs as backup, and it is the approach we recommend for any Gowanus building moving to app entry.

Our new building has a street entrance on the avenue and a second residents' door onto the public path being built along the canal. Which one gets the directory panel?

The street entrance. Visitors, couriers and ride-shares arrive from the street address, so the directory panel, the camera and the delivery code belong at the door with that address. The door onto the canal-side path is a residents' door: a reader or keypad for people who live there, a self-closing, self-locking door and, if you want it, a call button that rings the front desk or the management office rather than a full directory.

The rezoning requires many canal-front owners to build public walkways, so that second door faces the public all day. We choose vandal-resistant hardware for it, keep its controller inside, and make sure it locks the moment it closes, so the path never becomes a back way into the building.

Studios, landmarks and the avenues

Our studios are in one of the industrial buildings the Landmarks Commission designated in 2019. What can a new entry panel look like, and where can it go?

Small, plain and beside the door. The Commission's guidebook says an intercom panel "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," allows one intercom panel per opening, and asks that its design and finish "be compatible with the architecture of the building." It should be fastened into mortar joints or plain masonry, not "through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces," and visible conduit is limited to about ten inches.

For a studio building with many tenants, that usually points to a slim directory or code panel rather than a long column of buttons, set into or beside the door opening, with its cable entering from inside. We prepare the cut sheets and photos the application asks for, and we do not cut or drill the exterior until the permit is in hand. Work that stays inside the building, like new cable to each floor, can usually proceed while the application is reviewed.

Our studio building's freight entrance on the side street is where artists load in large work and materials. Can it get a call button that rings whichever studio is expecting the delivery?

Yes. A small directory or code station at the freight door can list the studios and ring the one the driver names, on the studio-holder's phone or a station inside. The studio-holder releases the freight door from upstairs or from wherever they are, and the door relocks when it closes. Regular suppliers can be given a code that only works during the hours you set.

Freight doors are usually heavy steel, so the release has to be rated for the door and the closer has to be strong enough to pull it shut after a cart goes through. We check both on the walkthrough, because a freight door that is propped open for every delivery undoes the front door's security.

Our eight-family walk-up on Fourth Avenue is losing renters to the new buildings around the corner, which have video and phone answering. What would a video system for eight apartments come to on your Brooklyn list?

An eight-apartment building is at the point where our list moves from the small-building ranges into the multi-unit range. A video door station with a button or directory for eight apartments, a video or audio station in each, phone answering for those who want it and a new release usually starts around $2,500; it climbs with new cable through masonry, door and frame work, or a second entrance brought onto the system. If the old riser meters sound and the door is in good shape, it stays near the start of that range.

Video answers the comparison renters are making. On Fourth Avenue the traffic noise makes audio-only entry hard work, and a picture at the door lets a tenant decide without shouting over a bus. We quote with and without phone answering, and on wired stations there is no monthly fee at all.

We have thought about putting a smart lock on our walk-up's street door instead of a buzzer system. What would our tenants lose?

The ability to let anyone in without going downstairs. A smart lock on the street door lets people with codes or phones in, but a visitor, a courier or a repair crew still has to be met at the door unless every tenant hands out codes, which defeats the point of a locked door. It also gives nobody a way to see or speak to the person outside.

A buzzer system does both, and it can sit alongside a code or fob for residents if that is what you liked about the smart lock. Many systems we install include a keypad or reader on the same door station, so tenants get keyless entry and visitors still ring the right apartment.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Gowanus

Ask a search summary or an AI assistant about installing a buzzer and it answers with national figures from cost sites and vendors' blogs. They are not invented, but they describe a generic American building or a Manhattan lobby, not a three-unit frame house by the canal or a 1931 walk-up on Union Street. Here are seven claims we see repeated, each traced to its source and checked against what we find in Gowanus.

"Intercom installation runs between $1,500 and $15,000"

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost page gives that spread, with an average of $2,500, wireless systems from $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired systems from $1,000 to $15,000. It blends room-to-room intercoms in suburban houses with apartment entry systems, which are different jobs with different parts.

For Gowanus the low end is too high for the smallest buildings and the high end too low for the largest. A two-family row house with a sound bell pair can get an audio system with a new release in our $400 to $900 range. A studio building with dozens of tenants and new cable to several floors can run past $10,000.

A range that wide is not useful for planning. The number that matters is the range for your building type, which is why we quote from photos and a household count rather than a national spread.

"Expect $1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door"

Swiftlane's apartment intercom guide puts modern IP or video systems at "$1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door." Swiftlane sells cloud entry, and per-door pricing suits a product that charges per door.

Most Gowanus buildings we wire have one door that matters, and what moves the price is the number of households behind it, the state of the riser and the condition of the frame. A walk-up with an outer and inner door does not cost twice as much; usually only one of the two gets the release.

Where extra doors do appear, such as a freight door on a studio building or a canal-path door on a new one, they usually need a reader or a call button and a closer, not a second full panel, so the cost does not multiply the way a per-door figure suggests.

"Software subscriptions add $5 to $15 per unit per month"

The same Swiftlane guide says cloud subscriptions "commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month," and Habitat's 2017 report on smartphone buzzers quoted an install of about $5,000 to $7,000 plus $2.50 per unit a month, paid by residents in that building.

Those fees are real for cloud systems, and they are often presented as unavoidable. They are not. A wired system with stations in each apartment has no monthly charge at all, and for a four- or eight-family walk-up that is a meaningful difference over the years the system lasts.

Where phone answering genuinely helps, such as an owner who lives outside Brooklyn or a studio-holder who is rarely in, we add it and show any subscription as its own line, so the owner can see the monthly cost next to the one-time price.

"Wireless intercoms need no building wiring"

ButterflyMX's call box guide describes its intercom as one that "requires no building wiring." Its own apartment buzzer guide is more careful: wireless systems may avoid some runs to apartments, but "the entrance panel, power supply, controller, and lock still need connections."

That second sentence is the one that matters at a Gowanus door. The release in the frame is wired whatever the stations are, it needs a dependable power supply, and in a brick walk-up the signal to upper-floor apartments has to get through masonry and plaster that block radio.

We use wireless where a run truly cannot be made and say so on the quote. In a frame tenement, where cable climbs a stud bay in an hour, wired is often no dearer and far more dependable.

"Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY"

HomeAdvisor says exactly that, and for a single-family house with a plug-in chime it is true enough. A few Gowanus owners who turned a multi-unit house back into one home are well served by a doorbell they install themselves.

A multi-household street door is a different matter. Cutting an electric strike into an 1880s frame, sizing a power supply, keeping the egress side free and making every household's release work is where do-it-yourself jobs go wrong, and a strike that holds the door half-latched is worse than none.

If you have bought equipment already, we install it as labor-only work, priced by phone or text from the make, model and photos of the door.

"Only buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom"

Many summaries reduce New York's rule to that line. The Attorney General's guide is more specific: the eight-apartment requirement applies to buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968, and older buildings "also must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom system if requested by a majority of the tenants."

Both halves matter in Gowanus. Most older walk-ups here were built around 1931, so the majority-request rule is the one that applies to them. And the neighborhood is full of conversions, from stores to apartments and from factories to homes, which is exactly when the post-1968 rule can come into play.

The record also shows what happens after installation. HPD's 33 intercom violations in Gowanus are orders to fix systems that existed, including in small buildings. Once a building has a buzzer, it is expected to work.

"If the old wiring is in good condition, it can be reused"

Aiphone's upgrade guide says that, and it is correct. The problem is the "if." Summaries tend to treat it as a likely outcome, when ButterflyMX's own small-building guide warns that wire condition "cannot be confirmed from a photo, the age of the building, or the look of a wall station."

In Gowanus we find every condition: sound pairs in a 1931 riser, bell wire chewed in a damp cellar, and runs cut during renovations nobody recorded. A house that has been through as many conversions as some here may have three generations of wire in the same wall.

So we meter every pair before pricing. Pairs that test sound are reused at no extra cost; those that fail are named on the quote with the price of replacing them, so the figure you approve does not change on installation day.
