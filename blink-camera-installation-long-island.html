The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Blink Camera Installation

Long Island is not one neighborhood but two counties stitched together by parkways, and a Blink camera behaves differently in almost every corner of it. The state's own county profiles describe Nassau as two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets packed into 453 square miles, and Suffolk as ten towns spread across 2,373 square miles. The 2020 census counted 1,395,774 people in Nassau and 1,525,920 in Suffolk. This page is the hub for both. When we use one place as a close-up example, it is usually the Nassau Hub, the stretch of Uniondale and East Garden City around the Nassau Coliseum on Hempstead Turnpike, because its mix of postwar houses, apartment complexes, office towers and one of the busiest retail strips on the island captures most of what a Blink job on Long Island can involve. But the same questions come up in a Levittown ranch, a Smithtown colonial, a Long Beach condo and a seasonal house on the East End, and we have written this page so each of them gets a straight answer.

Start with the houses, because they decide almost everything about a wireless camera. The U.S. Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey five-year estimates count 477,860 housing units in Nassau, of which 364,529 are one-unit detached houses, and 579,790 units in Suffolk, of which 463,095 are detached. The median home in Nassau was built in 1955; the median home in Suffolk in 1970. Levittown is the textbook case of the older stock: Keri Murray Architecture records 17,447 homes built there between 1947 and 1951, ranch houses on concrete slabs with expandable attics, hot-water radiant heating pipes in the floor and no garage. A Newsday analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026, quoted in a Newsday post, found that more than 5,000 of the houses sold had been built in the 1950s and 1960s. Those are the homes people buy Blink for. They are finished, lived in and often already renovated, and nobody wants to open their walls to run camera cable. A battery camera on a bracket, a plug-in camera on a shelf and a doorbell that uses the old doorbell wires look like the obvious answer, and very often they are.

The catch is that those same houses put real obstacles between a Blink camera and the box that talks to it. Most Blink outdoor cameras and the Blink battery doorbell do not connect straight to your Wi-Fi; Blink's own support pages say the Outdoor 2K+ and the Battery Doorbell 2K+ need a Sync Module, a small hub that plugs in indoors and relays the cameras to the router. Blink states that its Sync Modules can communicate with cameras up to 100 feet away, and it tells owners to keep the module away from thick walls and from concrete, brick, stone and metal. In real use the distance is shorter. SafeHome's review of Blink reported a strong connection only within about 70 to 80 feet once walls and obstacles were in the way. A Long Island lot puts all of those obstacles in a row: a router in a finished basement behind a concrete-block foundation, a kitchen full of appliances, an exterior wall, a driveway, and a detached garage at the far end.

The Nassau Hub shows how common that layout is. NeighborhoodScout reports that single-family detached homes make up 69.74 percent of Uniondale's housing units and that 55.45 percent of its housing was built between the 1940s and the 1960s. Homes.com describes the area as divided by the Hempstead-Bethpage Turnpike, with East Garden City to the north busy with commerce and condos, townhouses and apartments, and the southern part mostly single-family homes, Cape Cods and minimal traditionals, often with detached garages and private driveways. NeighborhoodScout also counts 19.83 percent of Uniondale's homes in large apartment complexes or high-rises and puts ownership at 62.02 percent, with 37.98 percent renting. In plain terms: a lot of Long Island households want a camera on a garage that sits well beyond the Sync Module's comfortable range, and a lot of renters want cameras they can put up without upsetting a landlord or losing a deposit. Both situations are where a technician earns his keep on a product that is sold as do-it-yourself.

Then there is the reason people buy the cameras in the first place. In its police report for the week ending September 15, 2026, Patch Garden City listed a resident who reported packages taken from the front porch of the home, with detectives investigating, and in the same report noted that detectives were looking into damage to a Flock license-plate camera on Clinton Road. News 12 Long Island ran doorbell-camera video of a thief taking a package off a porch in Massapequa Park mid-delivery. CBS New York reported on November 25, 2025 that the Suffolk District Attorney had charged 14 men in a porch-theft ring that worked 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, between October 2023 and February 2025, allegedly hacking into systems to learn when Verizon and AT&T devices were being delivered and taking more than 200 phones and tablets. ABC7 New York reported a Central Islip man caught on video using a knife to destroy a doorbell camera at a home. And Long Island Life & Politics reported that Nassau burglaries rose 5 percent, from 234 to 245, in January through May of 2026 compared with the same months of 2025, even while overall property crime fell. Back in January 2024, ABC7 quoted police in a multi-agency burglary task force saying the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways.

Weather is the other Long Island factor, and it hits a battery-and-Wi-Fi system in particular ways. When Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, ABC7 reported almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers in the dark at the peak. A battery camera keeps its charge in an outage, but the router and the Sync Module plugged into the wall do not, so the cameras have nothing to report to. In August 2024 the state Department of Financial Services described the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County, the failure of the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam and flood damage to more than 2,000 residents and business owners. Winters are the quieter problem. A battery seller's guide notes that cold weather, weak Wi-Fi signal and frequent alerts all reduce battery performance, and that lithium batteries hold their output across a far wider temperature range than alkaline cells. A camera on the north side of a house, aimed at a busy street, on the edge of the Sync Module's range, in February, is the camera whose batteries die first.

Finally, the rules, which are not the city's rules. Long Island has no HPD, no 311, no Department of Buildings and no Landmarks Preservation Commission; those are New York City agencies, and none of their data applies to a house in Hicksville or a shop in Patchogue. Permits come from the town, village or city where the property sits. New York's recording law is the same island-wide: the Digital Media Law Project describes it as a one-party consent law, and New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes recording video inside a bathroom, bedroom, changing room or hotel room without the occupant's consent a class E felony. Police camera registries are local: the Suffolk County Police SHIELD program lets residents and businesses register the location of their cameras, and the Garden City Police run their own voluntary registry. We explain all of this below, plainly, because it affects where we point your cameras and how we set their audio.

We also want to be honest about what our research could not find. We searched for Reddit threads, Yelp reviews and local news about Blink installations specifically on Long Island and found none. We found no Long Island study of how often Blink cameras fail or drop offline. Where this page says what we expect to find on a property, it is drawing on the building types in the public record and on how wireless cameras behave in houses of that age generally, not on a survey of Long Island homes. The only way to know your signal is to stand at each spot and measure it, which is the first thing we do.

Long Island facts that shape a Blink install

  • Mostly detached houses: 364,529 of Nassau's 477,860 housing units and 463,095 of Suffolk's 579,790 are one-unit detached homes, per the Census Bureau's 2023 five-year estimates.
  • Older in the west, newer in the east: the median home was built in 1955 in Nassau and 1970 in Suffolk, which means more original doorbell transformers in the west and more two-story colonials with attached garages in the east.

  • Outages take the network down, not the batteries: Isaias left almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers without power at the peak, ABC7 reported, and a Blink camera without a powered router has nowhere to send its clips.

What owners often add once the cameras are up. After a Long Island house has a Blink doorbell and a couple of outdoor cameras working, the next request is usually reach: a camera on the garage or the back gate that the Wi-Fi could not cover the first time. Sometimes that means moving the Sync Module or adding a second one; sometimes it means improving the Wi-Fi itself. Say so when booking, so we bring what that part of the property is likely to need rather than coming back a second day.

Router to Garage: Where Blink's Signal Runs Out on a Nassau or Suffolk Property

Almost every Blink problem we expect on Long Island comes back to one measurement: how far, and through what, each camera has to reach to get to the Sync Module, and how far the Sync Module has to reach to get to the router. Blink publishes the outer limit; SafeHome's testing gives a more realistic one. Everything between the camera and the module cuts into that distance, and Long Island houses are full of things that cut into it. The detached garage problem

The single most common reason we expect a Long Island Blink owner to call is a camera on a detached garage that works for a day and then keeps going offline. It is a distance problem dressed up as a camera problem. A Cape Cod on a typical lot might put the garage sixty or eighty feet behind the house, which is already at or past the strong-connection range SafeHome measured, and the signal has to pass through the house's back wall, the garage wall and whatever is parked between. A battery camera on the edge of range also works harder: it retries, it wakes up more, and its batteries drain faster. Blink's own support guidance is that a second Sync Module can help cameras with weak signal. Sometimes that is the answer. Sometimes the better answer is moving the existing module to the back of the house, into a window that looks at the garage. And sometimes the Wi-Fi itself does not reach the back of the house well enough to feed a module there, in which case the real fix is better Wi-Fi. We take a reading at the garage before we mount anything there, so you know which of those three you need before you pay for it.

Concrete-block foundations and finished basements

A lot of Long Island routers live in the basement, where the cable line comes in. A restoration contractor working in Uniondale describes the local Cape Cods and ranches as sitting on concrete-block foundations, with basements finished in the 1960s and 1970s. Blink tells owners to keep the Sync Module away from concrete, brick and stone, and a block foundation is all three of those in one wall. A Sync Module sitting next to a basement router may have a perfectly good connection to the router and a poor one to every outdoor camera. We almost always move the module upstairs, to a spot with a clear path to the outside walls the cameras are on, and confirm the module still connects well to the router from there. The same contractor's warning that postwar Capes and ranches frequently contain asbestos materials is one reason we avoid drilling into old basement walls and ceilings unless there is no other choice, and why a Blink system, which rarely needs any interior drilling at all, suits these houses so well.

Metal garage doors, metal siding and metal doors

Blink's guidance names metal as a signal blocker, and a blog on Blink doorbells notes that obstructions such as walls, brick and metal doors both shorten the Sync Module's range and increase battery drain. On a 1970s colonial with an attached garage and a steel overhead door, a camera inside the garage aimed at the door may struggle more than one outside on the eave. On a house with a steel front entry door, a battery doorbell mounted on the frame can be fighting the door itself. We test those spots specifically, and where the reading is poor we look at mounting a few inches away on wood trim, moving the Sync Module or switching a doorbell to wired power if the house allows it.

Crowded Wi-Fi in condos and townhouses

In the condos, townhouses and apartment complexes of East Garden City and across the island, distance is rarely the issue; the issue is how many other networks share the air. A Blink Mini 2K+, which Blink says connects directly to Wi-Fi without a Sync Module, has to compete with every router through the walls. We look at how crowded the neighborhood's Wi-Fi is, where your router sits relative to the camera and whether your router's settings are helping or hurting, and we tell you plainly if the problem is the router rather than the camera. Exterior cameras on a condo or townhouse are also an association question before they are a technical one, which we cover in the building section below.

Stand where the camera will go and look at your phone

The simplest test is free. Walk out to the garage, the back gate or the side yard with your phone and see how many bars of your home Wi-Fi it shows. A phone is more sensitive than a battery camera, so if your phone struggles out there, a camera will struggle more. If your phone shows a strong signal at the garage, the job is probably a matter of Sync Module placement. If it barely holds on, the job may need better Wi-Fi at the back of the house. Either way, telling us what you saw saves a good part of the visit.

Decide what you actually want to see

Before you think about cameras, think about events. Do you want a face at the front door, the car in the driveway overnight, a delivery on the side porch, the gate to the pool, or the back of the garage where the bikes are kept? Each answer suggests a different spot and a different camera. On a corner lot or a street with heavy foot traffic, a camera that sees the whole sidewalk will alert you every time someone walks past and drain its batteries; one aimed at the walkway and the porch gives you the deliveries and the visitors without the noise. Send us a list of what matters to you, in order, and we will match cameras to it rather than the other way around.

Know your internet plan and your router

Blink sends clips over your internet connection, so the upload side of your plan matters. Most home plans have plenty for a handful of cameras, but a house that already streams, works from home and runs other cameras can feel it. Note the name on your router and whether it is the provider's box or one you bought. If it is more than a few years old, or it sits inside a metal cabinet or behind a TV, those details help us judge whether the router is part of the problem before we start moving cameras around.

Fixing a camera to vinyl, shingle, brick and stucco

A Blink camera weighs very little, so the question is not whether a wall can hold it; it is whether the screws will stay tight through wind, frost and a thief's tug. On vinyl siding we avoid screwing through the siding panel alone, because vinyl is meant to move with the temperature; we look for the trim, the soffit or a siding mounting block that ties into the sheathing behind. On wood shingle and clapboard we fix into the sheathing and framing, not the shingle face, and we seal the screw holes. On brick, which is common on older Long Island fronts and chimneys, we drill into the brick face rather than the mortar joint and use anchors made for masonry. Stucco takes a masonry bit and slow drilling so the surface does not spall around the hole. On all of them we prefer a spot under an eave or porch roof, because Blink's own setup advice and Asurion's guide both point to sheltering outdoor cameras from direct rain and extreme temperatures where possible.

Postwar doorbells and the transformer question

The wired Blink doorbell does not run on whatever happens to be connected to the button. Blink's support pages say the Video Doorbell needs a chime system that provides 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz and 40 VA maximum. If the app shows a "Wired power not supported" message during wiring and the doorbell is not connected to a Sync Module, Blink says the existing chime wiring is not compatible, and the doorbell falls back to Event Response mode. When Blink fits a business, and when it does not

A Blink camera wakes up when it senses motion, records a clip and goes back to sleep to save its batteries. Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Licensing. New York's licensing regulation, 19 NYCRR 195.2, requires a security and fire alarm installer's license to install or service a closed-circuit camera system used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Nassau home improvement licensing. Nassau County says it is against the law to operate a home improvement business there without a license, and that homeowners can check a contractor through its Office of Consumer Affairs. The county also states that anyone working only on commercial buildings does not need that license, which covers office suites but not houses.
  • Suffolk licensing. Suffolk County Code Chapter 563, Article XI, requires a license from the county's Office of Consumer Affairs for regulated trades, but does not apply in towns or villages that have their own licensing or in second-class towns that opt out. We confirm which applies by address.
  • Audio in the workplace. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, but a camera that records employees' or customers' conversations when nobody recording is part of them is a different matter. Most businesses are safest with audio off.
  • Private areas stay private. Penal Law section 250.45 makes recording video in a bathroom or changing room without consent a felony. No camera of ours points into a restroom, a changing area or anywhere similar.
  • Landlord and building rules. Office towers with key-card access, like The Omni, and managed retail centers have their own rules on exterior devices, drilling hours and contractor paperwork. Send us the property manager's requirements with the booking.

Your Questions Answered

Blink Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Signal, distance and the Sync Module

The garage sits at the back of our lot, maybe 75 feet from the house. Will a Blink camera out there stay connected?

It might, and the only honest way to know is to measure at the garage before mounting. Blink lists its Sync Module range at up to 100 feet, but SafeHome's testing found a strong connection only within about 70 to 80 feet once walls and obstacles were involved, and a garage at 75 feet sits right on that edge with two exterior walls in the way. We take a live reading there first. If it is strong, the camera goes up with the module where it is. If it is marginal, moving the module to a window facing the garage often solves it; Blink also says a second Sync Module can help weak cameras. If the house's Wi-Fi itself does not reach the back of the house, that has to be fixed first, and we will show you the reading so you can see why.

Does every Blink camera need a Sync Module, or can some connect on their own?

It depends on the model. Blink's own guidance is that the Mini 2K+ connects straight to Wi-Fi without a Sync Module, while the Outdoor 2K+ and the Battery Doorbell 2K+ need one. Many Long Island households end up with a mix: Minis indoors on the Wi-Fi and battery cameras outdoors through the module. One Sync Module serves many cameras, so you rarely need more than one unless the property is large or spread across buildings. Tell us the exact models you own or plan to buy, and we will tell you whether your kit is complete before the visit, which saves a second trip.

Our router is in the basement of a house on a block foundation. Is that why the outdoor cameras keep missing events?

Very possibly. Blink tells owners to keep the Sync Module away from concrete, brick, stone and metal, and a concrete-block foundation wall puts the module, the router or both behind exactly that material. Many Cape Cods and ranches on Long Island sit on block foundations with finished basements, which is where the cable line and router usually end up. A module beside a basement router may connect well to the router and poorly to every outdoor camera. We usually move the module to the main floor, on the side of the house where the cameras are, then confirm it still talks reliably to the router. If the router itself cannot reach upstairs well, the Wi-Fi needs attention before the cameras will behave.

Power, batteries and doorbells

How often will the batteries in an outdoor Blink camera need changing through a Long Island winter?

There is no fixed answer, because battery life depends on how often the camera wakes, how strong its signal is and how cold it gets. A battery supplier's guide notes that cold weather, weak Wi-Fi and frequent alerts all reduce battery performance, and that lithium batteries hold their output across a very wide temperature range while alkaline cells lose significant capacity in the cold. A camera on a quiet back corner with a strong signal can last a long time; one aimed at a busy sidewalk on the edge of range, in February, will need batteries far sooner. We set motion zones and sensitivity so the camera wakes for what matters, place the module so the signal is strong, and recommend lithium cells for outdoor cameras.

Our 1955 house still has its original doorbell and chime. Will a wired Blink doorbell run on it?

Only if the transformer delivers what Blink asks for. Blink's support pages say its Video Doorbell needs a chime system providing 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz and 40 VA maximum, and that a "Wired power not supported" message means the existing wiring is not compatible, in which case the doorbell defaults to Event Response mode if it is not connected to a Sync Module. We measure the transformer and look at the chime before you buy or mount anything. If it is in range, the doorbell goes on the existing wires. If not, you can use a battery doorbell, Blink's plug-in adapter for the Wired Doorbell 2K+, or have the transformer replaced, which may be an electrician's job depending on where it sits.

If PSEG power goes out during a storm, do the Blink cameras keep recording?

The battery cameras keep their charge, but they need a powered Sync Module and a working internet connection to save clips to the cloud and send alerts. When Isaias hit in August 2020, ABC7 reported almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers without power at the peak, and in an outage like that the router and module go dark with everything else on the wall. A battery backup under the router, the module and the provider's modem can keep the system reporting through a short outage, provided the provider's service outside is still up. For long outages, local storage on the Sync Module does not help if the module itself has no power. We explain what your setup will and will not do in an outage before you rely on it.

Permits, police and the law

Does my town or village require a permit before Blink cameras go on my house?

For a typical residential Blink install, usually not, but Long Island has dozens of towns, villages and cities, each with its own building department, so we check by address rather than promise. The Town of Hempstead's code, for example, requires a building permit for work that must conform to the state's Uniform Code or Energy Code, which a battery camera screwed to an eave normally does not trigger. Separately, the installer matters: New York requires a security installer's license for camera systems used to detect intrusion or theft, and Nassau requires home improvement contractors working on houses to hold a county license. Ask any installer for both before you book.

Can my Blink doorbell record audio of people talking at the door without breaking New York law?

New York's eavesdropping law is a one-party consent law, as the Digital Media Law Project explains, which means a recording is lawful when at least one person in the conversation consents. When you are talking to someone at the door through the doorbell, you are a party. The risk is a doorbell or camera picking up conversations between passers-by or neighbors when nobody in the household is involved; a legal Q&A on Justia advises disabling audio unless a consenting participant, including you, will be part of any recorded conversation. Video has its own limits: Penal Law section 250.45 makes recording inside bathrooms, bedrooms and similar rooms without consent a felony. We set audio to your choice after explaining this, and we never aim a camera into a neighbor's windows.

Should we register our Blink cameras with the Suffolk police or the Garden City police?

It is your choice, and it does not hand anyone access to your cameras. The Suffolk County Police SHIELD Camera Registry Program lets residents and businesses register the location of their video systems; the department says it does not have access to those cameras, registration is free, and detectives may ask a participant to check footage for a particular date and time, with retrieval at the owner's convenience. SHIELD also runs a separate Emergency Remote Camera Access program that partners can opt into or out of. The Garden City Police run their own voluntary registry and state that it does not give them access to privately owned systems. We found no county-wide Nassau police registry in our research.

Nassau makes residents register alarm systems. Does that cover a Blink camera system?

We could not confirm that it does. Nassau County requires residents served by the Nassau County Police Department to register their alarm systems, and Suffolk runs its own Alarm Management Program with a registration fee and, at the time of our research, a mail-in application because its online portal was down. Those programs are aimed at alarms that call for a police response. A Blink camera system is self-monitored: it sends alerts to your phone and does not dispatch anyone. If you add a monitored alarm to the house, registration almost certainly applies; for cameras alone, check with the department that serves your address.

How to read the list for a Long Island house

Take a Cape Cod in Uniondale with a doorbell, a camera over the front porch, one on the driveway side and one on a detached garage. On the Nassau list, that is the doorbell line, three outdoor camera lines and, if the garage reading is weak, either a Sync Module repositioning or a second module. If the doorbell transformer measures out of range, the doorbell line changes to the battery or adapter version. Move the same layout to a 1970s colonial in Suffolk with an attached garage and good doorbell power, and the Suffolk list applies, the garage line is often simpler, and the back-yard camera may be the one that needs the extra module. FAQ

Long Island Blink Camera Installation FAQ

How long does a Blink installation visit usually take at a Long Island house?
+

A doorbell and one or two cameras on a house where the signal is good and the doorbell power is right is a short visit. A full house with a detached garage, a doorbell that needs its transformer checked and a second Sync Module takes longer, mostly because of the signal readings at each spot before anything is mounted. Tell us the number of devices and the layout when you book and we will give you a realistic window for your property.

Do you come out to the North Fork and South Fork, or only western Nassau?
+

We cover both counties, from the Queens line to the East End. Visits far out in Suffolk are planned around a route so the drive is used well, which is why we ask for the town or hamlet up front. Seasonal houses there are often easiest to schedule before the house closes for the winter or when you are opening it in spring.

Are Blink cameras worth having for a Nassau or Suffolk homeowner?
+

For many households, yes, especially in finished postwar houses where nobody wants walls opened for cable. Porch theft is a real Long Island problem, from the Garden City porch report in September 2026 to the Suffolk ring that worked 31 communities, and a doorbell plus a couple of outdoor cameras gives you alerts and clips without wiring. Where you need continuous recording of a busy driveway or a large property, a wired system may be the better buy, and we will tell you honestly which fits.

If I move out of my Long Island rental, can my Blink cameras come with me?
+

Yes. Blink devices stay on your account, so they can be taken down, moved to your new home and re-added there. If the cameras were set up on no-drill mounts, removal leaves nothing behind. If any were screwed into the building with the landlord's permission, we can take them down and leave the holes neat, and set them up again at your new address on the same visit or another one.

Does a Blink outdoor camera bundle always include the Sync Module it needs?
+

Not always, which is why it comes up on deal forums. Blink says its battery outdoor cameras and battery doorbell need a Sync Module, while the plug-in Mini does not. Some bundles include a module and some single cameras do not. Send us a photo of the box or the model names before the visit and we will confirm your kit is complete, so we do not arrive to find a camera with nothing to connect to.

What should we sort out on our home Wi-Fi before the technician arrives?
+

Make sure you know your Wi-Fi network name and password, can log in to your Blink account or are ready to create one, and know where the router and the provider's modem sit. If you have a separate guest network, tell us which network you want the cameras on. If you recently changed routers or providers, mention it, because cameras set up on the old network will not reconnect by themselves.

Which Blink camera is simplest for a condo or apartment unit on Long Island?
+

Blink's own renter guide points to a plug-in indoor camera such as the Mini 2K+ for living areas, which connects straight to Wi-Fi without a Sync Module, and a battery doorbell for the front door where the building allows it. In a condo or townhouse complex, anything on the exterior usually needs the association's approval first, so most owners start indoors and add exterior devices once the board agrees.

Can Blink cameras go up in a rental without drilling, so the security deposit is safe?
+

Usually, yes. Indoor Minis sit on shelves or windowsills, outdoor battery cameras can sit on removable mounts in some spots, and there are door-clamp doorbell mounts. Those mounts have limits; one listed Blink no-drill doorbell mount fits only inward-opening doors between 0.82 and 2.08 inches thick with a small gap at the edge, so we measure your door first. Photograph the walls before and after installation for your own records.

How do you stop a Blink doorbell or camera from being knocked off or smashed?
+

No camera is impossible to damage, as the Central Islip case ABC7 reported, where a man destroyed a doorbell camera with a knife, showed. We make it harder: outdoor cameras go high enough under eaves to be out of easy reach, doorbells are fixed into solid framing with the screws Blink supplies or stronger ones, and a second camera covers the doorbell so that if someone attacks it, the attack itself is recorded and saved to the account.

Can a Blink camera be mounted on vinyl siding or cedar shingles without causing leaks?
+

Yes, with care. We avoid fixing through a loose vinyl panel alone and look for trim, soffit or a mounting block tied into the sheathing behind it. On cedar shingle or clapboard we fix into the sheathing and framing and seal the holes. Wherever possible we choose a spot under an eave or porch roof, which keeps rain off both the camera and the screw holes.

Do we need to pay for a Blink plan to keep recorded clips?
+

That depends on how you want clips stored. Blink sells cloud plans, which security.org notes include 60 days of cloud storage, and some Sync Modules can also save clips locally to a USB drive. Each approach has trade-offs: cloud storage keeps clips if a module is stolen or damaged, while local storage avoids a subscription. We explain both and set up whichever you choose on your account.

Will you ever tell us not to use Blink?
+

Yes. If you need continuous recording of a busy driveway, a camera aimed at constant sidewalk traffic, coverage across a large commercial floor, or a system you cannot afford to have go offline, a wired camera system is usually the better tool, and we will say so before you buy anything. Blink is good at what it does, and part of doing the job well is being honest about where it is not the right fit.

What do you leave behind so we can manage the system after you go?
+

A short written note: where the Sync Module sits and why, which camera is on which battery type and when it was installed, how the doorbell is powered and what the transformer measured, and which phones receive alerts. On a Long Island house where the router sits in a basement corner and the garage camera is the one most likely to cause trouble, that note saves a lot of guessing later.
