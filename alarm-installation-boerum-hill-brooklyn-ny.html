The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Alarm Installation

Boerum Hill is a small grid of mostly three-storey rowhouses squeezed between Downtown Brooklyn and Gowanus. The usual definitions put its northern edge at Schermerhorn Street, its eastern edge at Fourth Avenue, its western edge at Smith or Court Street and its southern edge at Warren or Wyckoff Street, and most of what stands between them was built from about 1840 to 1870. The neighbourhood got its present name only in 1964, when Helen Buckler of the newly formed Boerum Hill Association took it from the colonial Boerum family farm; before that, people called it South Brooklyn or North Gowanus. Today it sits in Brooklyn Community District 2 and is policed by the 84th Precinct, which also covers Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn, DUMBO and Vinegar Hill.

For an alarm installer, that age is the whole story. A house built in the 1850s has walls of brick finished with lime plaster on wood lath, ceilings with cornices and medallions nobody wants cut, floors that have been sanded so many times the boards are thin, and windows that are often still the original wood sash, weighted and roped inside their frames. There is usually a garden level a few steps below the sidewalk with its own door under the stoop, a parlor floor reached by the stoop, one or two bedroom floors above, a cellar below and a roof hatch at the top of a ladder. Every one of those levels has at least one way in. The work is deciding how to watch each of them without making the house look or feel like an office.

The burglary record in the neighbourhood points to the same openings. Brooklyn Paper’s police reporting from one week in July 2008 describes a burglar who broke a front-door lock on Pacific Street between Nevins Street and Third Avenue and left with the front and rear windows open; a penthouse on Dean Street whose door was forced; and an intruder who pulled the screen off a basement window on Bergen Street between Bond and Hoyt before the woman inside chased him off. In the Dean Street case, the resident learned of the break-in because her alarm company called her at 7 pm. In 2014 the same paper reported a burglar who climbed a fence behind a clothing store on Smith Street between Pacific and Dean, broke the glass in the back door, reached in to open it, and damaged the alarm panel. In 2016 a thief got into a deli at Warren and Bond Streets through an unlocked cellar door and then a locked basement door.

The pattern has not changed much with time. DNAinfo reported in January 2017 that the 84th Precinct had recorded 13 burglaries between December 5 and January 1, more than triple the previous four weeks, that most took place during the workweek when apartments and homes were empty, that in most cases the doors were forced, and that police suspected some burglars had climbed fire escapes to unlocked windows. In March it reported 15 burglaries in February against four the February before. In September 2024 Brooklyn Paper reported that the front doors of two Boerum Hill businesses, Blank Street Coffee and Piccola Cucina Casa, were smashed on the same day, while noting that burglaries in the precinct that year were running roughly level with 2023. None of those reports describes a sophisticated crime. They describe garden windows, rear windows, back doors, cellar doors and a keypad that was easy to reach — the places a good alarm design starts.

Two further facts shape what we install. First, a large share of the housing is landmarked: the Boerum Hill Historic District was designated on November 20, 1973, and the Commission added roughly 288 buildings in a 2018 extension along Dean, Bergen, Pacific, Wyckoff and Atlantic. That makes the front façade the wrong place for an exterior siren box, a conduit run or a camera arm, and pushes almost everything inside or to the rear. Second, many houses are now an owner’s duplex or triplex with a rental floor, so one alarm often has to serve two households who come and go on different schedules and should not hold each other’s codes. A system that ignores either fact ends up switched off within a year.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Boerum Hill’s Architecture

Boerum Hill has a short list of building types, but each one asks something different of an alarm. These are the ones we plan around.

Greek Revival and Italianate rowhouses

The 1840s–1870s houses that make up most of the district: brick bearing walls, lime plaster on lath, a stoop with a garden door beneath it, a cellar below and a roof hatch above. Four or five levels, each with its own openings.

The State Street row

Twenty-three landmarked houses at 291–299 and 290–324 State Street, between Smith and Hoyt, built from 1847 to 1874. Narrow, deep houses where the rear windows matter as much as the front door.

Second Empire houses on Bergen Street

The 1880s row the Commission noted in the 2018 extension, with mansard top floors whose dormer windows open toward neighbouring roofs and need their own contacts.

Neo-Grec flats buildings

Small 1880s apartment buildings with a shared street door and stair. Individual apartments get their own alarms; the shared door and cellar are the owner’s concern.

Owner-plus-rental conversions

Rowhouses split into an owner’s duplex or triplex and a garden or top-floor rental. Two households, separate entrances, one electrical service and one sensible place for the panel.

Mixed-use buildings on Atlantic Avenue

Two- to four-storey buildings with a shop at street level and apartments above, many inside the 2018 extension between Hoyt and Nevins. The shop and the flats need separate systems or separate areas.

Running cable through plaster and lath

In a nineteenth-century rowhouse the question is never whether cable can be hidden; it is how much time the hiding is worth. Lime plaster on wood lath cracks when it is drilled carelessly, and the cavities behind it are interrupted by fire blocking, old gas pipe and the occasional brick header. So we look for the routes the house already provides: the space behind the baseboard, the cavity beside a chimney breast, a closet stacked above a closet, the chase that the plumber or electrician used before us, the gap between the cellar ceiling joists that reaches every garden-floor wall. A contact on the garden door is almost always wired from the cellar below. A contact on a parlor window usually is too, by drilling up through the floor inside the window jamb. Higher than that, each wired run has to earn its place.

Where a run would mean opening a cornice or chasing a finished wall, we use a wireless device instead and say so on the plan. A typical Boerum Hill design ends up mixed: wired contacts and the keypad on the garden and parlor floors, where the cellar gives easy access and where most break-ins start; wireless contacts and detectors on the bedroom floors and the roof hatch; and the panel, power supply and communicator in the cellar or a closet near the electrical service. This is the same hybrid approach our Brooklyn hub page describes for brownstones, adjusted house by house.

Floors, stairs and the roof hatch

The vertical layout of a rowhouse decides the zoning. We usually treat the garden floor and cellar as one group, the parlor floor as a second and the upper floors and roof hatch as a third, so that the house can be armed with people asleep upstairs while the lower floors are fully protected. The stair hall is where a single motion detector does the most good: anyone moving between floors has to pass it. The roof hatch deserves particular care, because on a row of joined houses it is reachable from every neighbouring roof. It gets a contact of its own, wired if the top-floor ceiling allows, and its wireless signal, if it uses one, is tested from the hatch itself to the panel four floors down before we leave.

Windows, shutters and the rear wall

The back of a Boerum Hill house faces a block interior of private yards and extensions, and on many blocks the rear windows are more exposed than the front. The garden-floor windows and door to the yard get contacts in every design. Rear parlor windows that can be reached from an extension roof, a fence or a fire escape get contacts too, while upper rear windows that cannot be reached without a ladder may be left to interior motion coverage. Where the parlor floor still has interior folding shutters, the decision between a contact on the shutter and a contact on the sash depends on how the household actually uses them; we discuss both in the questions below. Where an owner wants more than an alarm at the garden windows, the LPC guidebook allows staff-level permits for simply designed window security bars at cellar, basement and parlor-floor windows on houses with a stoop.

Cellars, areaways and the space under the stoop

The cellar is both the best friend and the weak point of a rowhouse alarm. It is where the panel, power supply and communicator usually live, and the joists overhead give a route to every garden-floor wall. It is also an opening in its own right: many houses have a door from the areaway under the stoop into the cellar, a rear cellar door to the yard, or old coal-chute and window openings at sidewalk level. Each of those gets a contact, and the cellar stair gets a motion detector so that someone who gets in below cannot walk up into the house unnoticed. We mount the panel away from the cellar entrances, high on a dry wall rather than under the water pipes, so that a leak or an intruder does not reach it first.

Flats buildings and mixed-use buildings

In a small flats building, each apartment is its own alarm customer, with the panel inside the apartment and contacts on the apartment door and any fire-escape window. The owner’s system, if there is one, watches the cellar door, the boiler room, the roof door and the rear yard. In a mixed-use building on Atlantic Avenue, the shop below and the apartments above share a façade and sometimes a hallway but not a schedule, so the shop gets its own panel or its own partition, with its own codes, its own monitoring account and its own reports, and the residential door is never part of the shop’s arming sequence.

Owners who are protecting the openings of an old house often want to see the stoop and the yard as well. Our Boerum Hill security camera installation page explains how cameras fit under the same landmark rules, and the two systems can share one app.

Landmark District

Alarm Equipment and the Boerum Hill Historic District

Almost everything an intrusion alarm needs lives indoors, which is good news on a landmarked block. Contacts, motion detectors, keypads, interior sirens, the panel and the communicator are all inside the house, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates the exterior. The decisions that touch the Commission are the few pieces that might go outside: an exterior siren or strobe, a camera on the façade, the conduit that would feed either, and security bars or gates on the garden-floor openings.

The Commission’s Permit Guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment does not single out alarm bells or sirens, but its general criteria are clear enough to design to. Equipment should not go on or in front of a primary façade unless there is no other option, or the location is required by law; where it must, it belongs in a discreet spot such as beneath a band course, under the stoop or behind an areaway wall or planting. It must be as small as possible and painted to blend in. It may be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, but never through cast iron or onto decorative surfaces. Conduit should be concealed by passing through plain masonry behind the device; where that is impossible, staff may approve roughly ten inches of visible conduit. Cameras on small buildings are limited to two per 25 feet of street frontage.

For a rowhouse alarm, that points to a simple rule: keep the siren inside. A good interior siren on the stair hall is loud enough to fill the house and to be heard by neighbours through party walls, and an exterior box adds little that justifies a permit application and a hole in the brownstone. Where an owner or insurer does want an exterior sounder, we look first at the rear façade, which is usually plain brick and not visible from the street, and we prepare a short description and marked-up photo of the location, which is what Commission staff or an expediter will ask for. We do not drill decorative stone, we do not run raceway up a front wall, and we do not touch cast iron.

Bars and gates are the other exterior question. The guidebook lets staff approve window security bars at cellar, basement and parlor-floor windows where the house has a stoop, and security gates at openings below or under the stoop, provided they match historic ironwork or are simply designed, attach at limited points to the frame or surround, and avoid continuous perimeter framing. An existing historic gate under the stoop must be kept. Bars and contacts are not alternatives: bars slow a person down, and the contact on the window behind them tells you someone tried.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation on Atlantic Avenue and Smith Street

Boerum Hill’s commerce runs along two streets. Smith Street, on the western edge, is a restaurant row lined with small dining rooms, bars, cafés and boutiques in the ground floors of three- and four-storey buildings. Atlantic Avenue, on the northern side, is a long design and retail corridor; the Atlantic Avenue Business Improvement District, approved by the City Planning Commission in January 2011, runs from Fourth Avenue to the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway and counted 738 tax lots and more than 400 businesses when it was formed. Every fall since 1974, apart from 2001, the avenue closes for the Atlantic Antic street festival. Hoyt, Bond and Nevins Streets carry studios, small offices and corner stores between them, and the area near Third and Fourth Avenues has larger new buildings with ground-floor retail.

What those businesses have in common is a building type. A Smith Street restaurant usually has a glass front, a long narrow room, a kitchen at the back with a door to a yard or alley, and a cellar reached from inside and often from a sidewalk hatch. An Atlantic Avenue shop has a deep display window, a stockroom behind, and sometimes a roll-down gate in front. The front is visible from the street and gets the attention; the back and the cellar are where the recorded break-ins in the neighbourhood actually came in.

So a commercial design here starts at the rear. The back door gets a heavy-duty contact and the room behind it a motion detector. The cellar door and any sidewalk hatch get contacts that work in damp, gritty conditions. The front glass gets glass-break detection tuned to the size of the window, and the roll-down gate, if there is one, can get a gate contact so that the alarm knows whether the gate is down. The panel itself goes somewhere a burglar cannot reach in the entry delay, and the keypad is kept away from the back door. Counter panic buttons, opening and closing reports by user, and a schedule that alerts the owner if nobody arms the system by a set time round out most shop installs.

Restaurants add two concerns. Kitchens are hot and greasy, so detectors go where heat and steam will not reach them and smoke detection for the kitchen is planned with the fire alarm side rather than the burglar side. And staff turnover is high, so every employee gets an individual code that can be deleted the day they leave, which is also what makes the opening and closing reports worth reading.

Compliance notes for Boerum Hill businesses

  • Installer licensing — New York State’s General Business Law Article 6-D and 19 NYCRR Part 195 require anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems to hold a Department of State license; our license number is #12000287431.
  • Owner installs — the same law exempts a property owner who installs an alarm on their own property with their own employees, which is why a shop owner can fit a DIY kit; a hired installer cannot work unlicensed.
  • Landmarked storefronts — the 2018 extension brought Atlantic Avenue between Hoyt and Nevins into the historic district, including historic storefronts; exterior sounders, cameras and conduit there follow the LPC general criteria described above.
  • Fire alarm work — smoke and heat detection in commercial kitchens and assembly spaces falls under the NYC Fire Code and Building Code and is designed separately from the burglar alarm; see our fire alarm page for Boerum Hill.
  • Egress — alarm contacts never lock a door. Anything that electrically locks an exit, such as a magnetic lock on a rear door, is an access control question governed by the Building Code’s egress rules.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Boerum Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions Boerum Hill owners and shopkeepers ask once the conversation moves past brand names: what the Landmarks Preservation Commission allows, how to get through plaster, how to share one house with a tenant, and how to protect the back of a shop.

Landmark façades, windows and shutters

Does an outdoor siren or strobe on a landmarked Boerum Hill house need Commission approval, and where can it go instead of the front?

Any new equipment on the exterior of a designated building falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and its guidebook says equipment should not go on or in front of a primary façade unless there is no other option. In practice we rarely need an exterior sounder at all: an interior siren mounted high in the stair hall is loud enough to fill all four floors and carry through party walls.

If an insurer or owner insists on an outside sounder, the rear wall is the first choice. It is usually plain brick, not visible from the street, and the cable can come straight through a mortar joint behind the box. We supply a marked-up photo and a short description of the location and device for the permit application, and we keep the box small and painted to match the brick.

Can our original 1850s wood windows get alarm contacts without routing pockets into the sash or the moulded frame?

Yes. Old double-hung sash moves in a frame that is rarely square, and drilling recessed contacts into it removes original wood that cannot be replaced. On these windows we normally use a slim surface contact: the magnet goes on the meeting rail or the sash edge, the switch on the stop or jamb, both small and painted to match, and the screw holes are tiny and reversible.

Wireless versions of the same contact need no cable at all, which suits upper floors. On the garden and parlor floors, where the cellar gives easy access, we can feed a wired surface contact from below through the jamb. We check that the sash closes far enough for the magnet to line up in humid summer weather, because a swollen sash is the most common reason old-window contacts fault.

Our parlor-floor windows have folding interior shutters. Should the sensor go on the shutter, the sash or both?

It depends on whether the shutters are closed every night. A contact on the shutter only means something when the shutters are shut; if they fold back into their boxes most of the time, the alarm cannot be armed with a shutter open, which households find annoying. A contact on the sash protects the window whether the shutters are open or closed.

For households that do close their shutters, the sash contact plus a motion detector in the room is usually enough. We avoid screwing hardware into the shutter panels themselves, which are often original and carefully restored, and we never mount anything on the shutter boxes’ decorative faces.

Are window bars on the garden floor allowed in the historic district, and do they replace the need for window sensors?

The LPC guidebook lets staff approve window security bars at cellar, basement and parlor-floor windows on houses with a stoop, provided they match historic ironwork or are simply designed, attach at limited points to the frame or surround and avoid continuous perimeter framing. Security gates are allowed at openings under the stoop, and an existing historic gate there must be kept.

Bars do not replace sensors. Bars make entry slower and noisier; a contact on the window behind them tells you that someone has already tried, and a glass-break detector in the room catches a pane broken between the bars. On garden-floor windows facing a yard, we treat bars and contacts as two layers of the same plan.

Getting wire through a nineteenth-century house

How do you get alarm cable from the cellar to the third floor of a rowhouse with original plaster cornices and no open walls?

Mostly, we don’t try to. Cable goes where the house already has a path: up a closet stack, beside a chimney breast, through an old plumbing or electrical chase, or behind baseboards along an inside wall. Garden-floor and parlor-floor devices are usually wired up through the floor from the cellar, which is quick and invisible.

Above the parlor floor, if no clean route exists, we switch to wireless devices rather than cut plaster. You see the decision on the written plan for each device, with the reason. That keeps the cornices and medallions intact and keeps the price down, because the expensive part of a wired retrofit is the hour spent fishing one cable through a blocked cavity.

The panel is in the cellar and the roof hatch is four floors up. Will a wireless sensor up there reach it?

Sometimes not, and that worry is a real one. In a 2008 Brownstoner forum thread on brownstone alarms, a fire-alarm installer warned that transmitters on a roof hatch often fail to reach a receiver on a lower floor through four levels, while another owner reported no range problems in a two-storey house. Both can be true; it depends on the house.

We settle it with a measurement, not a guess. Before we commit to a wireless hatch contact, we read the signal level at the panel from the hatch itself. If it is weak, we add a wireless repeater on a middle floor, move the receiver higher in the house, or run one cable for the hatch alone through the top-floor ceiling. The hatch is the opening joined rooftops make easiest to reach, so it is never left to chance.

We are gutting the garden and parlor floors next year. What should the contractor leave behind so the alarm can be wired later?

A renovation is the cheapest moment to wire a rowhouse. Ask for a cable from a chosen panel location in the cellar or a closet to each exterior door frame, to each window opening on the open floors, to the stair hall ceiling for a motion detector and siren, and to the keypad spot beside the door you will use most. Leave a cable up the open wall cavity toward the upper floors and the roof hatch as well, even if those floors are not being touched.

Send us the drawings before the walls close and we will mark exact positions and cable types on them, so the electrician or our crew can pull everything in a day. A labelled pre-wire turns a later installation into a few hours of mounting devices.

Owners, tenants and the back of the house

We live in the lower duplex and rent the top floor. Can one panel arm our floors without arming the tenant’s?

Yes, most current panels support partitions, sometimes called areas. The owner’s floors, the cellar and the yard door become one area; the rental floor becomes another; each has its own keypad or app access and its own codes. The tenant can arm and disarm their floor without ever seeing the owner’s zones, and the owner can be away for a week with the lower floors armed while the tenant comes and goes.

Shared spaces such as the stoop door and the stair hall need a decision: usually they belong to whichever area is armed last, or they stay unarmed so nobody trips them. We draw the areas on the floor plan with you before anything is installed.

Who should hold the alarm codes when the garden apartment turns over, and how do we make sure a former tenant’s code stops working?

Each person should have their own user code, never a shared household code, so that deleting one person removes only their access. When a tenant moves out, the owner deletes that tenant’s codes and app login from the owner’s administrator account the same day, and the panel’s event log shows that the old code has not been used since.

We set the owner up as the only master user and show them how to add and remove users from a phone during handover. If the tenant’s area has its own keypad, the new tenant chooses a fresh code at move-in. Nothing needs to be rewired.

Our rear yard backs onto the yards of the Pacific Street houses behind us. Which openings at the back actually need sensors?

Every door to the yard and every window that someone could reach from the ground, a fence, an extension roof or a fire escape. On a typical Boerum Hill house that means the garden-floor door and windows, often the parlor-floor rear windows above a one-storey extension, and any window beside a fire escape. Brooklyn Paper’s 2008 report on a Pacific Street burglary between Nevins and Third Avenue noted that both the front and rear windows were found open afterwards.

Upper rear windows that are out of reach without a long ladder can be covered by a motion detector inside instead. We stand in the yard during the walkthrough and look at the back of the house the way an intruder would before deciding.

Shops on Atlantic Avenue and Smith Street

A burglar on Smith Street once broke in through a back door and damaged the alarm panel. How do we stop that from defeating our shop’s alarm?

The case was reported by Brooklyn Paper in 2014: a burglar climbed a fence behind a clothing store between Pacific and Dean, broke the back-door glass, reached in to unlock it and damaged the alarm panel. Two things defeat that approach. First, the panel and communicator go somewhere hidden and out of reach, not beside the door; only a keypad sits near the entry, and a keypad is not the system.

Second, a communicator that reports the moment the entry delay starts, and a monitoring service that treats a delay that never ends in a disarm as an alarm, means smashing the keypad or panel after entry no longer stops the signal. We also shorten the entry delay on back doors that staff do not use for opening up, and put the opening keypad at the front.

Our Atlantic Avenue store has a sidewalk cellar hatch. How should that be protected?

The cellar is often the weakest part of a storefront. In 2016 Brooklyn Paper reported a thief who entered a deli at Warren and Bond Streets through an unlocked cellar door and then broke through a locked basement door. We protect a sidewalk hatch with a heavy-duty contact rated for wet, gritty conditions on the hatch doors, plus a contact on the door from the cellar into the shop and a motion detector at the foot of the cellar stair.

If deliveries come through the hatch during business hours, the cellar can be a separate zone that stays disarmed while the shop is open and arms with the rest of the building at closing, so staff are not fighting the alarm every morning.

Can the alarm tell us which employee disarmed it each morning, and alert us if nobody arms it at closing?

Yes. With individual user codes, the panel logs who disarmed and armed the system and when, and the app or monitoring service can send an opening and closing report to the owner. A closing schedule sends an alert if the system has not been armed by a set time, which catches the night someone forgets.

The same logs settle questions after a loss. When a code is used outside normal hours, the owner knows which person to call. When staff leave, their codes are deleted from the owner’s phone that day.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Boerum Hill

We could not view Google’s AI Overview answers from the environment this page was researched in, so the claims below are ones owners repeat to us after asking chatbots or reading national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is checked against the houses and rules of Boerum Hill.

“Wireless sensors work anywhere in the house.”

Wireless devices are excellent, and most of our rowhouse designs use them. The claim breaks down at distance and density. A four-level house with brick party walls, plaster ceilings and a panel in the cellar is a long way, vertically, from a roof-hatch contact.

Brownstone owners have argued about this for years; one 2008 Brownstoner thread includes an installer warning about roof-hatch transmitters and an owner with no problems at all. The honest answer is that it varies house by house.

That is why we read signal strength from each wireless device at installation and add a repeater or a single cable where a reading is marginal, rather than trusting a range figure printed on the box.

“You can’t add security equipment to a landmarked house.”

The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates exteriors, and its guidebook sets out conditions under which staff can approve cameras, intercoms, lights, bars and gates. It is a set of placement rules, not a ban.

An intrusion alarm is almost entirely interior: contacts, detectors, keypads, sirens and the panel never touch the façade. The decisions that involve the Commission are the few optional exterior pieces.

On Boerum Hill houses we keep the siren inside, put any exterior device on the rear or in a discreet spot, avoid decorative stone and cast iron, and hide conduit — the approach the guidebook’s general criteria describe.

“A video doorbell is basically an alarm system.”

A doorbell camera watches one door, and only when someone approaches it. It does nothing about the garden window facing the yard, the roof hatch or the cellar door, which is where the neighbourhood’s reported break-ins have come in.

An alarm is a set of detectors on every opening that matters, a siren, and a communicator that reports even when nobody is looking at a phone. A doorbell is a useful part of that system, not a substitute for it.

We often add a doorbell on the same platform as the panel, so the stoop is visible and the alarm can be armed or disarmed from one app.

“A typical home alarm costs the national average.”

National cost guides average across ranch houses, apartments and new construction. Those averages say little about a nineteenth-century rowhouse with four levels of openings and plaster walls.

In Boerum Hill the main price drivers are the number of floors with reachable windows, how much can be wired from the cellar, whether there is a rental floor needing its own area, and whether the house needs a cellular communicator replaced or added.

Our published Brooklyn prices, which Boerum Hill pays at the base rate, are on this page, and the written quote after a walkthrough is fixed.

“You have to sign a long monitoring contract to get an alarm installed.”

Some companies subsidise equipment and recover the cost through multi-year monitoring agreements, which is where this belief comes from. It is a business model, not a rule.

We sell the installation outright. Monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and owners who prefer self-monitoring through an app can do that instead.

Owners who already have a contract with another company should read its cancellation terms before switching; we can install around an existing contract’s end date.

“Once the alarm is connected, the communicator never needs replacing.”

Alarm communicators ride on public networks that change. AT&T shut down its 3G network on February 22, 2022, despite requests from the alarm industry for more time; industry figures cited at the time put millions of alarms on 3G connections.

Panels that relied on those radios, or on copper phone lines that have since been replaced, now show communication trouble or report nothing at all. Many older Boerum Hill systems still have keypads that look fine and communicators that are silent.

On every takeover we send a test signal to the monitoring station before we leave, and we replace any communicator that cannot reach it on a current network.

“An outside siren box is the best burglar deterrent.”

An exterior sounder is a visible sign that a house has an alarm, which is why guides recommend one. On a landmarked rowhouse front it is also new equipment on a primary façade, which the Commission’s criteria steer away from.

A loud interior siren in the stair hall is heard throughout the house and by neighbours through party walls, and it is the sound an intruder inside actually has to deal with.

Where a visible signal is wanted, a small window decal or a discreet rear-wall sounder does the deterrent job without a permit question on the front of the house.

Our Installation Process

A three-contact garden-floor system and a partitioned four-floor house follow the same seven steps. In Boerum Hill, the walk through the house matters more than the box on the wall.

1. Phone walk-through photos

Send photos of every door, the garden and parlor windows, the cellar door or hatch, the roof hatch and the front of the house. From those we sketch which openings need contacts and where a detector can cover a room.

2. Walk every floor, the yard and the roof

A technician walks the cellar, each floor, the rear yard and the hatch, notes which windows can be reached from outside, finds cable routes through chases and closets, and checks whether the house is in the historic district.

3. Written floor-by-floor plan and Brooklyn price

Each device is marked on a plan as wired or wireless with the reason, partitions are drawn for owner and tenant floors, and a fixed written price comes from our published Brooklyn figures before any deposit.

4. Wire the lower floors from the cellar

Garden-floor and parlor-floor contacts, the keypad and the stair-hall detector are wired up through the floor from the cellar, and the panel, power supply and communicator are mounted out of reach.

5. Fit wireless devices where plaster says no

Upper-floor windows, dormers and the roof hatch get wireless contacts and detectors, with a repeater added where the four-floor distance to the panel needs one.

6. Program users, areas and schedules

Every person gets an individual code, owner and tenant areas are set up, shops get opening and closing schedules, and the owner becomes the only master user on the app.

7. Test from the roof hatch down and hand over

Every device is tripped from the top of the house down with signal levels recorded, the monitoring station confirms a test signal, and the owner receives the device list, codes guide and app login.

After handover: a seasonal five-minute check

Old houses move with the weather. Twice a year, put the system in test mode and open each door and window on the garden and parlor floors to confirm the keypad sees it; summer swelling and winter shrinkage are when old sash contacts drift. Check the battery status of wireless devices on the upper floors and the roof hatch before winter. Review the user list and delete anyone who no longer needs a code. And once a year, ask the monitoring station to confirm it received your test signal, which proves the communicator is still talking.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test From the Top of the House Down

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular customer. On a rowhouse, the most useful test happens at the very end of the job and takes about twenty minutes. With the system in test mode, one technician starts at the roof hatch and works down, opening every protected opening and walking past every detector, while a second watches the panel in the cellar and reads off the signal level of each wireless device as it reports.

The roof hatch and the top-floor rear windows come first, because they are furthest from the receiver and the most likely to be marginal. Anything that reports weakly gets fixed there and then, with a repeater, a better mounting position or a short cable, rather than being noted for later. Then the garden door is opened and left open while someone tries to arm the system, to confirm the panel refuses or warns, and the communicator sends a test signal that the monitoring station confirms by phone.

The reason is simple. Most complaints about alarms in tall old houses — a sensor that drops out in winter, a hatch that never reports, a keypad that cannot reach anybody — come from devices that were never tested from where they actually sit. The handover sheet lists every device with its reading.

FAQ

Boerum Hill Alarm Installation FAQ

How long does a typical Boerum Hill rowhouse alarm installation take, and does someone need to be home?
+

A garden-floor or two-floor system usually takes one working day; a partitioned four-floor house with wired lower floors can take one and a half to two days. Someone with keys needs to let us in and be available at the end of the job for the handover, when we program codes and show the app, but you do not need to stay home the whole time.

What happens to the alarm during a power outage on our block?
+

The panel switches to its standby battery and keeps protecting the house and reporting through its communicator; wireless sensors run on their own batteries regardless. How long the battery lasts depends on its size and how many powered devices it carries, so we size it for the system and show you on the keypad how to see that the house is on battery. When power returns the battery recharges by itself.

Does the alarm still need a landline to reach the monitoring station?
+

No. Current systems report over a cellular communicator, an internet connection, or both for backup. Many older Boerum Hill panels were connected to copper phone lines or to 3G radios that stopped working after AT&T shut its 3G network on February 22, 2022, which is why we send a confirmed test signal to the monitoring station on every job.

Can the same system warn us about water in the cellar or a pipe about to freeze?
+

Yes. Flood sensors beside the boiler, water heater and washing machine, and low-temperature sensors in the cellar and on the top floor, report through the same panel and app as the intrusion sensors. In an old rowhouse with an unheated cellar or a cold top floor, those alerts can save a ceiling.

Can the alarm arm itself when the last person leaves the house?
+

Many current platforms can remind you or arm automatically using the phones of household members, a schedule, or both. We set it up conservatively in houses with tenants, so that the owner’s floors arm on the owner’s schedule without affecting the rental floor.

We have scaffolding going up for façade work. What should we do with the alarm?
+

Do smoke and carbon monoxide detectors have to be part of the burglar alarm?

How do we change the master code ourselves after installation?

Will the keypad chime every time a door opens, and can a tenant turn that off?

Chimes are set per zone and per partition. Owners often want a chime on the garden door and the yard door while they are home; tenants can have their own keypad’s chime turned off without affecting the owner’s floors.
