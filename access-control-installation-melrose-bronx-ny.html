Melrose, Bronx

Second-Generation Upgrades · Staged Changeovers in Occupied Buildings · Desk Stations · No Monthly Fees

Melrose was rebuilt one building at a time. Since the city approved the community’s own Melrose Commons plan in 1994, thousands of affordable apartments have gone up between Park Avenue and St. Ann’s Avenue, most of them developed by nonprofit groups, and each building opened with the entry system its developer chose that year. The oldest of those systems are now twenty to thirty years old. Parts are discontinued, the original installers are gone, and the people who knew the passwords have moved on. Replacing a lobby system in a full building, with a front desk, social-service staff, a community room and residents who need to get home tonight, is different from installing one in an empty shell. That is the work this page is about.

The Neighborhood

Why Melrose Properties Need Access Control

On a map Melrose is a tidy rectangle. Wikipedia bounds it by St. Ann’s Avenue on the east, East 149th Street on the south, Park Avenue on the west and East 163rd Street on the north, with Melrose Avenue and Third Avenue as its main streets and the old shopping district called the Hub at its southern corner. It lies in Bronx Community Board 1 and in ZIP codes 10451, 10455 and 10456, and it is patrolled by the NYPD’s 40th Precinct, whose station house now stands at 567 East 149th Street, on the corner of St. Ann’s Avenue. Housing Authority property in the neighborhood is covered by Police Service Area 7 on Melrose Avenue. Neighborhood names in the South Bronx overlap, and some of the buildings described on this page are called Melrose by one source and Mott Haven, Morrisania or the Hub by another; we say so where it matters.

The history explains the buildings. According to the NYC Parks history of Melrose Commons Park, the area was part of the Morris family’s colonial land grant, was sold off from the 1850s, and became one of the Bronx’s largest commercial and entertainment centers after rapid transit arrived in 1908. By the 1970s and 1980s the frame and brick houses that had lined its streets were gone, lost to disinvestment and arson. Wikipedia adds that the Third Avenue El, one of the original draws to the neighborhood, was taken down in 1973, and that much of the original housing stock had disappeared by the 1980s.

What came next is unusual. In the late 1980s the city drew up a Melrose Commons Urban Renewal Area that would have cleared some 30 blocks for middle-income housing, without asking the more than 6,000 people who lived and worked there. Residents organized as We Stay/Nos Quedamos, chaired by Yolanda García, and, NYC Parks records, persuaded the city to adopt a revised plan in 1994 that kept affordable housing, shops and community space at the center. A case study of the plan counts the 168 community meetings held in a single year to write it, notes that the city already owned 71 percent of the lots it needed, mostly through tax default, and reports that more than 2,800 affordable units had been built within the renewal area, by “dozens of (mostly non-profit) developers and architects,” each building financed from its own patchwork of tax credits and subsidies.

Local writer Ed García Conde, in a 2017 Welcome2TheBronx article archived by WHEDco, put the count higher: more than 3,500 units of new affordable rentals, two- and three-family townhouses and condominiums since 1993, representing over $650 million in development, most of it inside the 30-block zone, plus Via Verde and St. Ann’s Terrace just outside it. He also recorded that Melrose Commons was selected in 2010 as the first LEED-certified neighborhood in New York State. The two figures cover different areas and dates, so we quote both rather than choosing one.

The result is a neighborhood where most residential buildings are not old in the way Bronx buildings usually are. They are fifteen, twenty or thirty years old, each built by a different team, each opened with the door hardware, intercom and credential system that its developer bought at the time, and each run today by an owner, a managing agent and often a social-service provider who were not in the room when that system was chosen. A 1990s townhouse, a 2000s rental building, Via Verde (completed in 2012 at Brook Avenue and East 156th Street, with 151 low-income rentals and 71 middle-income co-ops in one complex), and the newest supportive buildings all sit within a few blocks of each other. Their entry systems are at very different points in their lives.

Newer projects keep arriving. Bronx Commons, on the west side of Elton Avenue between East 162nd and East 163rd Streets, combines 305 affordable apartments, including homes for people who have experienced homelessness and domestic violence, with the Bronx Music Hall, a performance space of roughly 250 seats, and retail along 163rd Street. La Central, at 556 and 600 Bergen Avenue, opened its first two buildings in 2021 with nearly 500 income-restricted apartments, a YMCA, a BronxNet production studio and a rooftop farm, after Breaking Ground and Comunilife finished a 160-unit supportive and low-income building on the same site in 2019. And in October 2024 the city opened Melrose North, 171 apartments on NYCHA’s Morrisania Air Rights campus beside the Melrose Metro-North station, 103 of them supportive homes for formerly homeless New Yorkers, with front-desk security, a community room, intercoms in every apartment and an arts education space run by DreamYard.

Public housing is the other half of the picture. Wikipedia lists five NYCHA developments in Melrose and describes the neighborhood as having one of the highest concentrations of public housing in the borough; a 2017 NYU oral-history exhibit describes the Melrose Houses, completed in 1952, as more than 1,000 apartments home to about 2,300 people. Those doors are the Housing Authority’s, not ours, and this page does not offer to fix them. But they matter to private owners nearby, because the city’s own review of public housing doors, discussed below, is the clearest public record of how entry systems fail when maintenance falls behind.

Put together, Melrose’s access control needs are less about a first installation than about a second one: replacing, rescuing or extending systems that came with the building, in buildings that stay occupied the whole time, for owners who answer to lenders, agencies, residents and service partners at once. A lobby door here may serve market-rate co-op owners and formerly homeless tenants, a concert crowd and a case manager, a delivery driver and a youth program. The rest of this page explains how we plan for each of them.

Repairs

Lobby Reader Dead or a Door Stuck Unlocked in Melrose?

Systems that came with a building tend to fail in a familiar order. First the battery in the power supply gives out, so the first storm-related outage leaves the street door unlocked or dead. Then readers start dropping cards because a connector corroded or a cable was crushed when someone hung a bulletin board. Then the controller’s clock drifts, schedules open the community room at the wrong hour, and nobody can fix it because the software runs on a laptop that left with a former superintendent. Ring (646) 490-0629, tell us which door is doing what, and we give you an arrival window on the call. If a door is failing to let people out, or failing to release on the fire alarm, we fix that first and diagnose the rest afterwards.

We work on systems that other companies installed, including proprietary ones a developer’s subcontractor put in twenty years ago. If the original panel can be kept going cheaply, we say so; if it is time to plan a replacement, we make the building safe tonight and write up the options afterwards. Residents should report problems to the building’s management office or front desk first; we take instructions from the owner or the managing agent.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Melrose’s Architecture

Melrose’s building stock was laid down in a few distinct waves, and each wave takes entry hardware differently. Below are the building types we plan around, described from the sources on this page and from what anyone can see from the street; no building named here is an AESS client, and naming one is not a claim that we have worked there.

Renewal-era rental buildings, 1990s to 2000s

Mid-rise brick and block buildings with a single lobby, a rear service door, a cellar and a roof bulkhead, built under the Melrose Commons plan with public financing. Each opened with whatever entry equipment its developer chose at the time. In our experience the cable in buildings like these is often reusable, while electronics of that age usually are not.

Two- and three-family townhouses

Welcome2TheBronx counts two- and three-family townhouses among the renewal’s new homes. Typically they have a front stoop door, sometimes a separate entrance for the rental unit and a rear yard. Each owner decides alone, but the houses on a block were often built by one developer with the same doors and frames.

Mixed-income complexes with rentals and co-ops

Buildings like Via Verde, where low-income rentals and middle-income co-op apartments share a site. Two ownership groups, two sets of rules and sometimes two managing bodies share courtyards, roofs and doors, which has to be reflected in who can change what.

Supportive and service-rich buildings

Buildings where a share of the apartments house formerly homeless residents or survivors of domestic violence, with a staffed front desk, offices for an on-site provider and visitor procedures. Melrose North and parts of La Central and Bronx Commons are recent examples of this model.

High-performance and Passive House buildings

Melrose leads the borough in green-certified buildings, according to Wikipedia, and Melrose North is Passive House certified. Their entrance doors are heavier and more tightly sealed, with vestibules designed for air, not just security. Hardware has to respect the seal.

Older walk-ups, churches and storefront buildings

The tenements and commercial buildings that survived, and four churches Wikipedia dates to before 1900 on Elton Avenue, East 150th Street and East 156th Street. Masonry openings, wooden doors and no conduit: careful retrofits, done door by door, with the door repaired first.

How Melrose’s materials change the work

Hollow metal frames in block walls. Many apartment buildings of this period set steel frames in concrete block, grouted solid at the lock side. That is good news for strength and bad news for a retrofit: a new strike pocket in a grouted frame means drilling out grout, and a cable that was never pulled to that frame has to be surface-run in a raceway or fished through a hollow cell above the header. When the original builder did run cable to the frame, we reuse that path.

Aluminum and glass storefront entrances. Many newer lobbies use narrow-stile aluminum doors with glass sidelights. Their stiles leave little room for a lock body, so electrified hardware is chosen from the small family made for those doors, and the power transfer goes through the hinge or a concealed loop. Maglocks are tempting on glass doors, and we explain below why they are rarely our first choice.

Sealed doors in high-performance buildings. A Passive House or LEED building relies on its entrance doors closing fully against their gaskets. A strike that holds the latch a little off the stop, or a closer set too soft so the door drifts, costs the building energy as well as security. We set closers, strikes and position switches together and test that the door seals as well as locks.

Masonry and wood in the older buildings. In a walk-up or a church, the door is often the problem before the lock is. Frames are out of square, hinges sag and weatherstripping has been painted over. We repair or rehang the door first, mount devices into mortar joints rather than face brick where we can, and keep visible hardware off historic surfaces where the owner cares about the facade.

Ceilings and risers in the newer buildings. Where corridor ceilings are drywall rather than lay-in tile, new cable runs need access panels or careful fishing. Where the original builder left an empty sleeve or riser between floors, our cable goes through it; where not, we plan the shortest exposed route and finish it in paintable raceway.

The Melrose Problem

The Second System: Replacing What the Rebuild Installed

Every building put up during the Melrose renewal came with a first entry system. Some were simple buzzer panels with an electric strike on the lobby door. Some were early card or fob systems, often proprietary, with a controller in a closet and software on a single desktop computer. The newest buildings opened with networked panels that call residents’ phones. What they have in common is that the owner did not choose them with twenty years of operation in mind: they were chosen by a developer and a general contractor working to a budget line, installed by a subcontractor, and handed over at the end of construction along with a binder that may or may not still be in the building.

Twenty years is roughly where that equipment runs out. Readers and controllers keep working long after their makers stop selling them, but each repair gets harder: the replacement board comes from a reseller, the software no longer installs on a current computer, and the one technician who knew the system has retired. Meanwhile the building has changed around the system. The managing agent is not the one who was there at opening; a social-service contract has changed hands; the community room has a new tenant; residents carry phones that could open the door if the system knew how. A second system is a chance to fit the entry hardware to the building as it is now run, not as it was drawn.

It is also harder than a first installation, for one plain reason: the building is full. In a new building we can switch every door over in a day and nobody notices. In a Melrose building with two hundred households, a front desk staffed around the clock, a ground-floor provider seeing clients and a program running after school, the old system has to keep working until the new one is proven, door by door. The sections below set out how we do that. They describe AESS practice, not anything a source says about a particular Melrose building.

Signs that a first-generation system is near the end

  • Nobody can log in. The administrator password left with a former employee or the original installer, so residents cannot be added or removed, and fobs belonging to people who moved out still work.
  • Parts come from resellers. The manufacturer no longer makes the readers, in-unit stations or controller boards, and each repair means a search for used stock.
  • The software lives on one old computer. If that machine fails, the schedules and the resident list go with it.
  • The batteries have never been changed. Power supplies that are years past their battery date leave doors either unlocked or dead in an outage.
  • The doors themselves are tired. Closers leak, strikes have been shimmed, and the lobby door is propped because it no longer latches reliably.
  • The building has outgrown the system. There is no way to give a case manager or a program operator limited access without handing out a master fob.

What is usually worth keeping

Cable is the most valuable thing an old system leaves behind, because replacing it in an occupied building means opening finished walls. If a cable tests good end to end, has enough conductors for the new reader and lock, and runs through conduit or a protected path, we reuse it. Door frames and hinges are usually kept and repaired. Electric strikes are sometimes kept if they are the right function and voltage. Readers, controllers and power supplies are almost always replaced: they are the parts that age, and a new controller rarely talks to old readers in any useful way.

Lobby intercom panels are a judgment call. A panel that still works, whose in-unit stations are still sold and which the building is happy with, can sometimes stay and be joined to the new controller for the door release. More often the panel is the reason for the project: its stations are no longer made, half the apartments have dead handsets, and residents would rather the call came to their phones.

Commercial

Business Access Control at the Hub, on Third Avenue and Melrose Avenue

The southern corner of Melrose is the Hub, where East 149th Street, Willis, Melrose and Third Avenues converge. In March 2026 the Bronx Times reported on the Third Avenue Business Improvement District’s commercial district needs assessment: the Hub is home to more than 600 businesses, about 17.5 percent of storefronts were vacant, and merchants and shoppers named public safety and cleanliness as their top priorities. The BID said it planned dozens of new security cameras with state Downtown Revitalization Initiative funds and more than 50 new light fixtures through a city grant. Street cameras and lighting are the BID’s job; what happens at each shop’s own doors is the owner’s.

Third Avenue and 149th Street

Ground-floor stores with roll-down gates, a narrow staff door and a basement stockroom reached by an inside stair or a sidewalk entrance. Opening staff arrive early; the reader belongs where they can use it with the gate still half down, and the stockroom door needs its own schedule.

Melrose Avenue

Smaller shops, offices and service businesses with apartments above, where the shop entrance and the residential stair door are side by side. The two must never share a credential, and the residential door must still meet the owner’s obligations to tenants.

Courtlandt and Elton Avenues

Mixed blocks of renewal-era housing, churches, community gardens and small institutions. Doors here often belong to nonprofits and congregations whose volunteers change every year, which makes credentials that expire on their own worth having.

Around Lincoln Hospital

NYC Health + Hospitals/Lincoln, at 234 East 149th Street between Park and Morris Avenues, lists 665 beds in operation. The hospital itself is outside our scope, but nearby practices, pharmacies and offices need staff doors, records rooms and after-hours entry that are simple to manage.

Venues and program space

The Bronx Music Hall, YMCA and studio space at La Central, and DreamYard’s arts space at Melrose North bring evening crowds into buildings that are also homes. Public doors, staff doors and residential doors have to be planned as three separate groups.

Ground-floor retail in new buildings

Bronx Commons plans 22,000 square feet of retail on 163rd Street and La Central includes retail space. Where a shop shares a building with apartments, the line between the landlord’s system and the tenant’s should be written into the lease before the shop opens.

The rules a Melrose entry system has to meet

  • Self-closing, self-locking entrance doors. New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking doors at the street, yard and cellar entrances of class A multiple dwellings built or converted after 1968, and an intercom at the main entrance of buildings with eight or more apartments, with voice communication and remote release. Roof doors must close by themselves but must not lock against people leaving.
  • Electrified egress doors. The NYC Building Code §1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9 set the conditions for sensor-release and magnetic locks: free exit on the egress side, release on power loss and on fire-alarm activation, and a manual release button mounted 40 to 48 inches above the floor that holds the door unlocked for at least 30 seconds.
  • Tenant data. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) applies to owners of multiple dwellings using keyless entry: a written privacy and retention policy for tenants, minimum data, no sale of the data, no tracking of tenants outside the building, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. Biometric data is covered.
  • Listed equipment. We install access control equipment listed to UL 294 and power supplies suited to the locks they drive, so the building’s insurer and the fire-alarm contractor see recognized products.
  • Agreements beyond the code. Many Melrose buildings were financed with public money and carry regulatory agreements, lender requirements and service contracts of their own. We do not interpret those documents, but we produce the records an asset manager usually asks for, and we raise any question that should go to the owner’s counsel or agency contact.
A Public Record

What the City’s Public Housing Door Review Teaches Private Owners

In the summer of 2022 the city Comptroller’s audit bureau reviewed building doors at 262 NYCHA developments. THE CITY reported that 57.9 percent of the buildings checked were unsecured, with entry doors open, locks broken or both, nearly three times the 23.5 percent found in 2018. In the Bronx the figure was 63.5 percent. The Comptroller’s letter to NYCHA pointed to the city’s requirement for automatic self-closing and self-locking doors, and the Housing Authority said it would repair the doors identified.

AESS does not work for NYCHA, and nothing here is a comment on any particular development, in Melrose or anywhere else. We cite the review because it is the largest public inspection of residential entry doors in the city, and its lesson applies to every owner: entrance doors fail mostly through ordinary wear and deferred repair, not through clever attacks. A closer that no longer pulls the door shut, a strike knocked out of line, a lock broken and not replaced, a door propped because it sticks. Electronics cannot fix a door that does not close.

That is why every Melrose replacement we plan starts with the doors, and why we recommend that owners put a short monthly door check in the super’s routine: does each controlled door close and latch by itself from fully open, does the reader respond, does the desk see the door as closed. A written check takes ten minutes. Finding out at two in the morning that the lobby door has been unlatched for a week costs far more.

Q&A

Access Control in Melrose: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners, managing agents, service providers and shopkeepers ask us about Melrose buildings. The answers describe how we work; where a rule or a source is involved, we say which.

When the Original System Wears Out

Nobody on our current staff knows where the access controller is or how the doors are wired. What does a survey of an older system involve?

A survey is a day or two of detective work. We start at the lobby door and follow its cable back: through the ceiling, down a riser or along the cellar, until it reaches a controller or a terminal block, usually in a locked closet, a meter room or the super’s workshop. We do the same from every door that has a reader, keypad, strike or contact. Along the way we photograph and label each device, read the model numbers, and note anything that has been spliced or disconnected.

At each door we test what the hardware actually does: whether the door closes and latches from fully open, whether the lock releases when the reader or the desk tells it to, whether it releases when power is cut, and whether exit is free from inside. We test the cable where we can, and we read a sample fob or card to identify its format. At the head-end we check the power supplies and their batteries and try to reach the software.

The written result is an inventory, one line per opening, with what is there, what it does, what condition it is in and what we recommend. It ends with three options priced at our Bronx rates: keep and repair, partial upgrade, or full replacement. The survey is free; many owners use the report to set a capital budget even if the work happens a year later.

Parts for our lobby panel are no longer made. Can the readers, strikes and cable already in the walls stay when the controller is replaced?

The cable often can. Strikes sometimes can. Readers rarely should. Cable that tests good, has enough conductors for the new devices and runs through a protected path is worth keeping, because pulling new cable through finished walls in an occupied building is the most disruptive and expensive part of any job. Electric strikes can stay if they are the right function for the door, fail-secure or fail-safe as the code and the door require, and run at a voltage the new power supply can provide.

Old readers are a different matter. Many first-generation readers speak an older wiring protocol and read only low-frequency credentials that are easy to copy. A new controller can often be set to accept them, which is useful during the changeover, but leaving them in place permanently keeps the weakest part of the old system. Our usual plan reuses the cable and frames, keeps strikes that pass testing, and replaces readers and controllers together.

Should an aging system be replaced in one project or door by door as each piece fails?

Replacing door by door as things break sounds cheaper, and in a small building with two or three doors it can be. In a larger building it usually costs more in the end. Each emergency repair is paid at service rates, each one hunts for an obsolete part, and the building ends up with two half-systems that need two sets of software and two sets of credentials. Residents carry two fobs; the desk watches two screens.

The approach we recommend is one planned replacement, carried out in stages. The controller and power go in first, beside the old equipment; doors move over in groups as budget and building life allow; and the old equipment is removed only when the last door has moved. That spreads the cost over months if the owner needs it to, without leaving the building with a permanent patchwork. If the budget truly allows only part of the job this year, we start with the head-end and the doors that fail most, so later stages simply add readers to a controller that is already there.

How do we re-badge a two-hundred-apartment building without residents queuing in the lobby for days?

By scheduling it like an appointment, not a giveaway. Before enrollment starts, the managing agent gives us the current rent roll, and we preload every household into the new system with its apartment number, so issuing a credential takes a minute rather than a form. Residents get a notice with a choice of sessions, mornings, evenings and a weekend, held in the community room rather than the lobby. Each household signs for its credentials, and anyone who misses every session can collect them from the management office later.

Phone credentials cut the queue further: residents who choose them receive an invitation and enroll themselves, and only people who want a physical fob need to attend. During the whole period the old credentials keep working, so nobody is locked out for missing a session. On the switch-off date, anyone still using an old fob finds it no longer works and goes to the office, where their new credential is already waiting.

Several Organizations, One Building

A nonprofit sponsor owns our building, a management firm runs it and a service provider has offices on the ground floor. Who should be allowed to change door schedules?

As few people as can do the job, in writing. We suggest one owner-side administrator, usually in the managing agent’s office, who can change schedules and add or remove anyone, and a backup at the sponsor. The service provider’s office manager gets limited rights: adding and removing the provider’s own staff, and nothing else. Front desk officers can release doors and issue temporary visitor codes, but cannot change schedules. Every change is logged with the name of the person who made it.

The decision belongs to the owner, and it should be written down: a one-page access policy that says who holds which rights, who approves a new door schedule, and what happens when a contract ends. When a provider or managing agent changes, as they do over twenty years, the policy tells everyone what to hand over. We set the system up to match whatever the owner decides and leave a copy of the policy with the as-built drawings.

Our case managers sometimes need to reach a tenant’s floor quickly. How should their credentials differ from the maintenance staff’s?

Their doors and hours differ, and their records are handled differently. Maintenance staff typically need the service door, the cellar, the roof, mechanical rooms and every residential floor during working hours. Case managers usually need the lobby, their own office suite, the elevator and residential corridors, during their program’s hours and sometimes at night when on call. Neither group should carry a master credential that opens every door at every hour.

Apartment doors are a separate question. In most buildings we work in, entry to an apartment remains a matter of the lease, the law and the provider’s own procedures, and the access system controls only the common doors. Where an urgent wellness check is needed, the system’s job is to get staff to the right floor without delay and to record who went where; the decision about entering an apartment stays with the people trained to make it.

Our front-desk officer can see the main door but not the side and rear entrances. What should the desk station show?

Every door a person can come in by, on one screen. A good desk station lists each controlled entrance with its status, shows the camera image for that door beside it, raises an alert when a door is forced or held open too long, and lets the officer release a door with one click or a button on the desk. When someone calls from the lobby panel or a side-entrance call station, the call and the picture arrive at the desk together.

The details matter at three in the morning. Alerts should be few and meaningful, so officers do not learn to ignore them: a refuse room door held open for two minutes on collection night is normal; the cellar door opening at 3 a.m. is not. The log should be easy to read at shift change. And the desk should still be able to release the doors if the network drops, which is one reason we keep door decisions on the local controller rather than in a cloud service alone.

Some apartments are set aside for survivors of domestic violence. What can the entry system do when a resident says a specific person must not get in?

It can make sure that person never holds a credential, keep the resident’s name off the public directory, and help desk staff recognize a request they should refuse. The first two are settings: any credential associated with that person is disabled, and the lobby directory can show apartment codes instead of names for the whole building or for chosen households. The third is procedure: the desk receives the instruction from management, not from a resident at the counter, and visitor calls for that apartment are confirmed with the resident before anyone is let in.

What the system cannot do is stop someone from following another resident through the door, and it is not a substitute for a court order, the police or the provider’s safety planning. We set up the technical side as the owner and provider direct, keep the records as limited as the law requires, and make sure that only the people who need to know can see which apartments have special instructions.

Ground Floors That Serve the Public

The arts space on our ground floor lets out a few hundred people at once after a show. How do we keep that crowd from following residents through the lobby door?

By making sure the crowd never passes the residential door at all. The best arrangement gives the venue its own street entrance and exits that discharge to the street, with no unlocked connection into the residential lobby. Where the two share a vestibule or a corridor, the door between them stays locked and controlled, with a reader on the residential side only, and the venue’s staff use their own credential on a schedule that ends when the building closes.

On show nights, staff at the venue doors and a camera on the residential door, watched from the desk, do more than any lock. Exits from the venue must always open freely for the audience; the control is on the doors that lead into the rest of the building. We plan the doors with the venue operator and the owner together, because the venue’s fire exits and the residents’ security have to work at the same time.

A youth program uses our community-facility rooms after school. How should that door behave once the program staff leave for the night?

It should lock itself at the end of the program day, stay locked to everyone except building staff, and tell the desk if someone opens it later. We set the program door to a schedule that matches the program’s hours, unlocking for arrivals if the operator wants and otherwise opening only for staff credentials. At closing time the schedule locks it automatically, so nobody depends on the last person remembering.

The connecting door between the program space and the residential part of the building deserves attention too. Children and parents should reach the program from the street without passing through residential corridors, and program staff should not need residential access to do their jobs. Where the building was designed with a shared corridor, a controlled door and a clear schedule keep the two apart.

Our building shares a campus with a public housing development, and a compactor room serves both. Who should control that door?

Whoever is responsible for the room under the agreement between the owners, and the agreement should say so. HPD’s release on Melrose North describes exactly this kind of arrangement: a new interior compactor built into the private building so that NYCHA could keep servicing its own residents. Where a room serves two owners, the access plan has to follow the legal one: who holds the key, whose staff may enter, who repairs the door.

Technically, the simplest answer is often a door with its own reader on a small separate controller, with each party’s staff enrolled by the party responsible for them, and a log both can request. We can set that up, but we take instructions on who controls it from the owner, in writing, after the owners have agreed among themselves.

Several of us bought townhouses on the same block when they were new. Is there any advantage to upgrading our doors together?

Usually, yes, if the houses were built alike. Townhouses from one developer tend to share the same door, frame and stoop design, so one survey covers all of them and the hardware can be ordered once. A single visit to several houses on one block saves travel and setup time, and we pass that saving on in the per-house price we quote. Neighbors also end up with the same equipment, which makes it easier to help one another with an app or a code.

Each house remains a separate system and a separate contract, with its own owner as administrator; nobody’s door depends on a neighbor’s. If one owner wants a keypad and another wants video entry on the same door style, both are fine. What matters is that the survey, the ordering and the installation days are coordinated.

Our Hub storefront has a roll-down gate over the door. Where does a reader go so opening staff aren’t standing on the sidewalk with the gate half up?

Ideally, nowhere on the street side. Where the gate covers the whole storefront, the best arrangement is a separate staff door beside the gate, or a gate with a pass door, fitted with a reader or keypad and an electric strike, so staff can come in, lock the door behind them and raise the gate from inside. Gate motors and their controls are a specialist trade, and in many buildings the gate belongs to the landlord. We coordinate with the gate company and the landlord on any change to the operator, and we keep our part to the staff door, the reader and the schedule that tells the owner who opened up and when.

Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Melrose

Generic advice about building entry, whether from an AI assistant or a cost-guide website, tends to assume an empty building and a single owner. These are claims we hear repeated that do not fit Melrose well. We do not attribute any of them to a particular tool or site.

“Replacing an access system is a one-day swap.”

In an empty building, perhaps. In an occupied Melrose building, the swap itself may be quick, but surveying the old system, repairing doors, enrolling hundreds of residents and running old and new credentials side by side takes weeks of planning. Rushing that work is how residents end up locked out on a Friday night.

“If a building has a front desk, the doors don’t matter.”

A desk officer can watch only what the desk can see and release only what is wired to the desk. Side doors, cellar doors and program entrances are where people come in unnoticed. The desk works best when every entrance reports to it and the doors close and lock by themselves.

“Any fob is more secure than a key.”

Many older low-frequency fobs can be copied with an inexpensive device. A building that replaces keys with copyable fobs, and never removes the fobs of people who move out, has traded one problem for another. The credential format and the habit of disabling old credentials matter more than the switch from metal to plastic.

“Access control costs the same everywhere in New York City.”

Published estimates often quote a single per-door range. Real prices depend on the doors, the cable already in the walls, the number of credentials and whether the building stays occupied. Our own Bronx prices are listed below, and a Melrose replacement that reuses good cable can come in well under a first installation of the same size.

“Tenant data rules only apply to luxury buildings with face recognition.”

Local Law 63 of 2021 applies to owners of multiple dwellings who use keyless entry of any kind, including ordinary fobs and phone credentials, not just biometrics. A replacement project is the natural time to adopt the required privacy and retention policy, because the new system can be set to keep only what the law allows.

“A magnetic lock is the strongest choice for a lobby.”

A maglock holds well while it has power and lets go completely when it does not. The Building Code surrounds it with release requirements for exactly that reason. On most Melrose lobby doors an electrified lock or strike that fails secure on the outside and always allows free exit is the better answer.

What moves a Melrose quote up or down

FAQ

Melrose Access Control FAQ

Will residents need new fobs, or can new readers accept the ones they carry now?
+

It depends on what the old fobs are. Many first-generation systems use a 125 kHz proximity format that modern readers can still read, so a staged changeover can keep the old fobs working for a few weeks while residents are re-enrolled. Where the old credential is easily copied, we recommend replacing it, because keeping it means keeping its weakness. We read a sample fob during the survey and tell you which case you are in before you buy anything.

Does replacing an older system mean opening finished walls and ceilings?
+

Usually not. In most buildings put up during the Melrose renewal, the door cables run through conduit or above corridor ceilings, and the new controller can reuse them if they test good. We open a wall only where a cable is broken, missing or too short, and we tell you before the work starts which openings will need patching, so the super can plan the painting.

Can the new system load the old resident list instead of someone retyping every name?
+

Often, yes. If the old software can export its user list, we clean it up and import it, matching names to apartments and removing people who have moved out. If the old system cannot export, or nobody can log in to it, we build the list from the rent roll the managing agent provides. Either way the list is checked against current leases before it goes live.

Does AESS work on NYCHA buildings in Melrose?
+

No. Doors, locks and intercoms in NYCHA developments, including the Melrose Houses, belong to the Housing Authority and are maintained through NYCHA. We work for private owners, nonprofit sponsors, co-ops, condominiums and businesses, including privately developed buildings that stand on land leased from NYCHA. If you are a NYCHA resident with a broken door, report it to NYCHA through its own repair request channels.

Our building is certified Passive House. Will an electric strike or a heavier closer hurt the door's air seal?
+

It should not, if the hardware is chosen with the door. Passive House entrance doors depend on their gaskets and on closing fully, so we match the strike or electrified lock to the frame and the weatherstripping, set the closer to pull the door firmly into the seal without slamming it, and use a door position switch so the desk knows when a door has been left ajar. We coordinate any frame work with the building’s architect or energy consultant first.

Can one broken apartment intercom station be replaced without changing the lobby panel?
+

Usually, if the in-unit stations are still made or a compatible model exists. We identify the system from the lobby panel and the station’s label, then replace the single unit. If the manufacturer no longer makes stations for that system, the building has to decide whether to stock compatible used units or plan the lobby replacement, and we explain the trade-off in writing.

Who trains the super and desk staff on the new system, and what do they get to keep afterwards?
+

We do, on site, at handover and again after the first few weeks of use. Training covers adding and removing residents, issuing temporary codes, reading the event log, what to do when a door will not lock, and who to call. Staff keep a printed quick guide, a door-by-door list of what each opening does, and the as-built drawings, filed in the building office.

What does it cost to replace only a worn-out lobby panel and keep the rest of the system?
+

On our Bronx price list, a video intercom panel with fob access that replaces an old buzzer or panel is $1,500 to $3,000 installed, and a single door with a reader and lock is $1,500 to $2,500, plus sales tax. If the rest of the old system cannot talk to the new panel, the job becomes a controller replacement and moves into the multi-door range. We confirm which it is during the survey.

Our original system still sends alerts to a company we never hired. How do we cut that link?
+

Start by finding out what the link is: a cloud account, a monitoring contract or a phone line programmed into the panel. We identify it during the survey. The owner then cancels or transfers any contract in its own name, and we change the system so alerts and administrator access go only to the people the owner names. If the old panel is being replaced, the link ends with it.

How far in advance should tenants be told that the entry system is being replaced?
+

We suggest at least two weeks for a notice explaining what is changing and when, then a reminder a few days before. The notice should say how residents will get new credentials, what happens if they miss the sign-up hours, and who to call on the day. We provide a template in English and Spanish that the managing agent can adjust.
