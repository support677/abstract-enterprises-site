The Neighborhood

Why Parkchester Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Parkchester was built around its front doors. When the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company laid out the complex on the grounds of the old New York Catholic Protectory, which had occupied the site from 1865 to 1938, its planners, led by Richmond H. Shreve, whose firm had worked on the Empire State Building, arranged the housing as 51 clusters of buildings with 171 separate building entrances across 129 acres, with only about 27 percent of the land built on. The first 500 families moved into the southern part of the complex in March 1940, and by 1943 every one of the 12,271 apartments was occupied. Each of those 171 entrances serves dozens of households, which means each one is a door that hundreds of people open, close and wait at every day. When the system at a single Parkchester door fails, it rarely fails for one family alone.

The ownership behind those doors has changed several times. MetLife signed an open occupancy pledge and in the same year sold the whole complex to a syndicate headed by Harry B. Helmsley, whose condominium conversions created the Parkchester North Condominium and the Parkchester South Condominium. In 1998 the restoration of the complex began, and Parkchester Preservation Management today runs a rental portfolio of more than 6,000 apartments scattered through the buildings, alongside the units that individual owners hold in the two condominiums. For a door problem, that layering matters. The entrance hardware and the panels are common elements, Parkchester South's own service pages say that common-area repairs are made through its Service Department at no additional cost to owners, and residents who rent from a unit owner are told to go to that owner for interior service. An outside company cannot simply walk up and open a lobby panel here, and an honest page about buzzer repair in Parkchester has to start from that.

Then came the biggest change to Parkchester's entrances since the buildings went up. According to Comelit's own case study, a modernization of the complex's intercom infrastructure was launched in September 2024 because the old wiring had become obsolete, and replacing it with conventional intercoms would have taken years of work and significant investment. Instead, over about nine months, all 171 buildings were fitted with Ultra Touch entrance panels, 1456B gateways and lifetime app licences, installed by Total Security and managed through the app and the property-management software. Comelit describes it as the world's largest app-based IP video intercom, covering 12,271 apartments and roughly 48,000 residents. The help site the condominiums point to, ParkchesterIntercom.com, explains what that means for a resident: the panel reaches you through the Comelit app, through an ordinary call to a phone number on file with management, or both, and on a phone call you press *9 to unlock the door. The same site tells residents who never received their invitation to check the spam folder.

The change solved one problem and created a new kind of failure. On February 5, 2025, in the middle of the rollout, News 12 Bronx reported on a Parkchester couple who said they had been unable to buzz in guests or deliveries for months; without a working intercom, they said, deliveries of medication were often missed, and getting home health aides and other medical professionals upstairs was a struggle. Parkchester Management told the station the system was working and that residents with problems should contact them. Both things can be true at once. A panel can be working perfectly while a particular apartment's call goes to a number nobody answers, lands in a phone's spam filter, or reaches an app that was never signed into. In a complex where many residents are older, live alone or rely on aides, a door that will not call the right phone is not an inconvenience. It is the difference between a prescription arriving and a prescription going back to the pharmacy.

So our work in Parkchester splits into two clear halves. Inside the complex we work for residents and unit owners on everything that belongs to them: the phone that is supposed to ring, its call-blocking and spam settings, the app and its sign-in, the email address the invitation went to, the home Wi-Fi the app depends on, and the *9 step that has to reach the panel as a tone. When the fault is on the condominium's side, we tell you so plainly and give you a written description of exactly what failed, when, and from which phone, which is what a Service Department needs to find a fault quickly. Outside the complex, in the walk-ups and elevator buildings on Starling Avenue, Unionport Road, White Plains Road, Castle Hill Avenue and East Tremont Avenue, in the Stratton Park blocks to the west and in the shops along the commercial streets, buzzers are ordinary wired systems with strikes, transformers, risers and handsets, and we repair them from the street door to the top floor.

Before You Call

A Ten-Minute Check for Complex Residents

None of the steps below need tools, and none of them touch the condominium's equipment. They are the same checks we make first when we visit an apartment, written out so that a resident, or a relative helping a resident, can try them before booking anyone. If they fix the problem, good. If they do not, write down what you tried; that list is the first thing management or a technician will ask for.

1. Ask the neighbours first

Find out whether other apartments in your entrance are getting their door calls. If several people are affected at once, stop here and report it to management with the date, the time and the entrance address. That pattern points to the panel, the gateway or the door, none of which you can or should touch. If only your apartment is affected, keep going.

2. Confirm what management actually has on file

The phone route uses a phone number on file with Parkchester's management, and the app route uses an email address on file. Ask management, in writing if you can, which number and which email they hold for your apartment. Many failed calls are simply going where the record says they should: to a landline that was disconnected, a phone that was replaced, or the number of a relative who no longer lives with you. Only management can change that record, and the help site's distinction between the two condominiums' management offices and Parkchester Preservation Management's matters here, so make sure you are asking the right office for your building.

3. Look for the invitation

If you want to use the app, search your email, including the spam and junk folders, for an invitation from Comelit. ParkchesterIntercom.com warns about exactly this. If it is not there, the email on file may be wrong or the invitation may never have been sent, and that is a question for management rather than for your phone.

4. Make sure your phone is allowed to ring

Modern phones are good at silencing calls they do not recognise. On an iPhone, a setting called Silence Unknown Callers sends calls from numbers that are not in your contacts straight to voicemail. Android phones have their own call screening and spam protection, and carriers add filters of their own. Focus modes, do-not-disturb schedules and sleep settings can also hold back a call. If you can see the number the door call comes from in your recent calls or voicemail, save it as a contact with a name such as "Front door" so the phone treats it as someone you know.

5. Test the *9 from the phone you really use

On a phone call, the door opens when you press *9. That only works if the tones actually leave your phone. Answer the next door call on the handset itself, open the keypad on screen and press the keys firmly. If you usually answer through a car, a smartwatch, earbuds or a speakerphone dock, try once without them; some of those devices do not pass keypad tones the way the phone does. If the tones go through and the visitor still cannot get in while neighbours' unlocks work, note it for management.

6. Check the app's basics

If you use the app, confirm you are still signed in, that the app is allowed to send notifications, that it is not being put to sleep by battery-saving settings, and that the phone has a working internet connection when you are at home. An app that cannot reach the internet cannot ring. A weak Wi-Fi signal in a back bedroom, far from the router, can be enough to delay a call until the visitor has given up.

7. Write down what happened

Keep a short log: date, time, who was at the door, what the visitor saw on the panel, what your phone did, and whether *9 or the app unlock was tried. Two or three entries like that turn a vague complaint into a fault someone can find, and they are exactly what we put into the written note after a visit.

The shorter buildings, which Wikipedia lists at eight storeys with floors numbered from M (Main) upward and a single elevator. Today the entrance panel calls phones rather than wall stations. The taller buildings, listed at thirteen storeys with paired elevators. Thick brick and concrete inside these buildings can weaken a home Wi-Fi signal from room to room, which matters if you rely on the app rather than an ordinary phone call. A rear bedroom is often where app calls arrive late.

More than 6,000 rental apartments managed by Parkchester Preservation Management, spread through the same buildings. Renters use the same panels and the same phone or app routes; the record of who receives the call for a rental apartment is kept by management.

Units Owned to Rent Out

Apartments that individual owners lease to tenants. Parkchester's information pages tell those tenants to contact the unit owner for interior service, so the owner becomes the link between a tenant who cannot be reached at the door and the management office that keeps the directory.

Private Walk-Ups and Elevator Buildings

The multi-unit buildings around the complex, many unrelated to it, on the streets toward Castle Hill Avenue, Unionport Road, White Plains Road and East Tremont Avenue. Rules and practical limits for business doors here

  • Common elements inside the complex. Parkchester South's service pages say common-area repairs are made through its Service Department and that private arrangements with members of its staff are prohibited. A shop inside a complex building should confirm with its landlord which parts of its door are the shop's own before anyone, including us, changes them.
  • Electrically locked egress. Where a business door uses a magnetic lock rather than a strike, building code conditions on electrically locked egress doors require that people inside can always get out, typically with a sensor or a release that cuts power to the lock. When we repair a door like that, we test the exit side before we test the buzzer.
  • Reach heights. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, the high forward reach for a control is 48 inches at most. A replacement call button or release on a shop door goes within reach, not wherever the old one happened to be.
  • Line-voltage work. NYC's Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work at line voltage. Our work stays on the low-voltage side; a new outlet or circuit for a door supply is named as an electrician's job in the quote.
  • Apartments above shops. In a private building with homes upstairs, the residents' door is subject to the city's self-closing and self-locking requirements. HPD treats a defective self-closing door as a class C immediately hazardous violation with 14 days to correct, so a shop's work should never leave that door unable to lock.

A note on shared storefronts. When two or more businesses share one Starling Avenue space, the back door buzzer is often wired to whichever counter was there first. Before we repair it, we ask who should be able to let deliveries in, and we label the release buttons so the next tenant does not inherit a mystery.

Door Buzzer Repair in Parkchester: Questions Answered

These are the questions that only make sense in Parkchester, where most front doors now call telephones. Each is answered in full here. The shorter, practical questions about booking and coverage are in the FAQ further down.

Calls that never arrive

I switched phone carriers and kept my number, and since then the lobby has not reached me. What changed?

Your number stayed the same, so the directory record is probably still correct, which points to the new carrier or the phone. Carriers apply their own spam and call-filtering services, and a call from a number they flag can be blocked or labelled as spam before it rings. A new phone set up from scratch may also have call-silencing switched on by default. Check the carrier's call-protection settings in its app or account, look in your phone's blocked and spam lists for the door call, and save the calling number as a contact if you can see it. If none of that brings the call back, confirm with management that your number is still on file exactly as you think it is.

Visitors say the panel is ringing me, but my phone shows no call at all. Could a setting on my own phone be hiding it?

Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons we find. On an iPhone, Silence Unknown Callers sends calls from numbers not in your contacts directly to voicemail, and they show up only in your recent calls list. Android phones can screen or filter suspected spam, and focus modes, bedtime modes and do-not-disturb schedules can hold calls back on either kind of phone. Look at your recent calls and voicemail around the time the visitor rang; if the door call is there, the call is reaching you and the phone is silencing it. If there is no trace at all, the call may be going to a different number, which is a directory question for management.

I answer, press *9, and the visitor is still standing outside. Is that my phone or the door?

Test the phone side first, because it is quicker. The *9 has to travel back to the panel as keypad tones. If you answer through a car system, a smartwatch, wireless earbuds or a speakerphone accessory, some of those do not send keypad tones the same way the phone does, so answer once on the phone itself, open the on-screen keypad and press star, then nine, deliberately. If the tones go through and the visitor still cannot open the door, ask whether a neighbour's *9 works at the same entrance. If theirs works and yours does not, write down the date and time and report it. If nobody's works, the fault is at the panel or the door, and that is for management.

I never received the email invitation to the app. Where is it, and who sends it?

Start with your spam or junk folder; the residents' help site specifically tells people to check there. The app route depends on your email address being on file with management, so if the invitation is not anywhere in your mailbox, the address they hold may be an old one or contain a typing error. Ask management which address they have for your apartment and whether an invitation can be sent again. Until then you can still receive door calls as ordinary phone calls, as long as your phone number is on file, and open the door with *9.

My father has a flip phone and no internet. Can the door still reach him?

According to the residents' help site, yes. Besides the app, the system can place a regular phone call to any phone number on file with management, and the resident presses *9 to unlock. That works on a basic phone or a landline, as long as the number on file is the one he answers and the phone can send keypad tones. The things to check are practical: that his phone is loud enough to hear from the rooms where he spends time, that it is not set to silent, that the door's calling number is saved as a named contact if his phone supports contacts, and that someone has practised the *9 with him once or twice while a family member waits at the entrance.

When I report a door-call failure to management, what details help them find the fault fastest?

Give them the building address and entrance, your apartment, and a specific event: the date and time a visitor rang, what the visitor saw on the panel, what your phone did, and whether you tried *9 or the app unlock. Parkchester's information pages tell residents who rent from a unit owner to contact that owner for interior service, so the request lands with you. A neighbour says the panel works fine for her while it fails for me. What does that tell us?

It tells you the shared parts, the panel, the building's gateway and the door, are very probably working, because they are the same for both of you. What differs between two apartments is the directory record, the phone or app at each end, and each home's internet connection. The app shows me the visitor on video, but the visitor cannot see me. Is my camera broken?

Probably not. The residents' help site describes the video as one-way, so you see the person at the panel while they hear you without a picture of you. Does an app door call use my phone's data, and what happens when my data runs out?

An app call needs an internet connection, which means either your home Wi-Fi or your mobile data. If you are away from Wi-Fi and out of data, the app cannot ring. An ordinary phone call from the door does not depend on data, which is one reason the help site offers both routes. If you rely on the app and your data plan is tight, keep the phone on your home Wi-Fi when you are in, and ask management whether your phone number can also be on file so that a call still reaches you.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Parkchester

Google's AI Overview could not be viewed from the system we used to build this page, so nothing below is presented as its wording. Instead, here are seven claims about buzzers and intercoms that appear in vendor articles, cost guides and general advice, the kind of thing a chatbot is likely to repeat, each checked against what is known about Parkchester's doors.

Claim one: "If the buzzer doesn't ring, the wiring is broken"

For a conventional system, that is a reasonable first guess. Bell wire corrodes, joints loosen, and a riser cut during a plumbing repair can silence a whole line of apartments. It is how we approach the private buildings around the complex.

Inside the complex it is usually wrong. The intercoms there call telephones, and the residents' own help site warns people to check their spam folders for the invitation. A door call that never reaches you may be going to an old number, waiting in a phone's spam filter, or failing on a weak home Wi-Fi connection while every wire in the building is fine.

So the first step in a Parkchester apartment is not a meter. It is your recent-calls list, your contacts and a conversation with the neighbour across the hall.

Claim two: "App intercoms need no building wiring"

One vendor's article on apartment call boxes says such an intercom "requires no building wiring." That makes app systems sound like they float free of the building.

Comelit's description of Parkchester tells a different story. Every building received entrance panels and 1456B gateways, the building's data is stored locally in its gateway on a closed network, and the doors still have to release and lock. Panels need power and a connection, and doors still have strikes, closers and latches.

What app intercoms remove is the cable to each apartment's wall handset. They do not remove the building's own equipment, and when that equipment fails, every resident in the entrance feels it at once.

Claim three: "Every apartment building in New York City must have an intercom"

Vendor guides to the city's intercom law usually say the requirement generally applies to class A multiple dwellings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968, and that older buildings need closer review. NYC rule 1 RCNY §42-01(d) is written the same way.

Parkchester's original buildings went up between 1938 and the early 1940s. For pre-1968 buildings, the New York Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says self-locking doors and a two-way intercom are required if a majority of the apartments request them, and owners may recover the cost under the Multiple Dwelling Law.

The complex has an intercom regardless, and a new building such as the nine-apartment one permitted on Guerlain Street falls squarely under the post-1968 rule. The point is that the date of the building matters, and blanket statements skip it.

Claim four: "Any intercom technician can fix your lobby panel"

General advice assumes the person with the problem can hire whoever they like. In a privately owned walk-up, the owner can.

In the complex, Parkchester South's service pages say common-area repairs are arranged only through its Service Department and are included in the common charges, and the complex's panels were installed by a named contractor and are managed through the vendor's software. An outside technician opening those panels would be working on someone else's equipment.

That is why we say plainly what we will not touch in Parkchester, and why our written report to management is part of the service rather than an afterthought.

Claim five: "Most residents use the app every day, so the system works for everyone"

Comelit states that over 75 percent of Parkchester residents now use the app daily. That is the vendor's own figure, and even taken at face value it leaves a large minority who use phone calls, use nothing, or have not been able to make either work.

The News 12 report from February 2025, about a couple who could not buzz in guests or medication deliveries for months, came while management said the system was working. Both can be accurate. A system can work well in aggregate and fail for a particular household because of a record, a phone or a setting.

Statistics about adoption say nothing about your apartment. Testing your own call path does.

Claim six: "Your landlord has to fix the buzzer"

For a tenant in an ordinary rental building, the owner is responsible for keeping the intercom and the door in repair, and that is the right starting point in the private buildings around the complex.

In the complex the picture has more layers. Common-area repairs belong to the condominium. A tenant renting from a unit owner is told to contact that owner for interior service. A Parkchester Preservation renter deals with that management office. And the directory record that decides which phone rings is kept by management, not by whichever landlord signed the lease.

Knowing which office holds which piece is often the fastest repair there is.

Claim seven: "A new system means the old door problems are gone"

A modern panel replaces the electronics at the door. It does not replace the closer that pulls the door shut, the latch that must catch, or the fob system, which the residents' help site says is separate and unchanged.

Residents' complaints about faulty lobby door locks, reported by the Bronx Times in 2024, are about exactly those mechanical parts, and they predate and outlast any calling system.

When a resident tells us "the buzzer is broken," we always ask whether the door itself closes and locks. In the complex, a door that does not is for management to fix; in a private building, it is often the real repair.
