The Neighborhood

Why Dyker Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Dyker Heights began as a sales pitch. In October 1895 Walter Loveridge Johnson started laying out a speculative suburb on his late parents’ land at the top of a hill in the old town of New Utrecht, advertising it as “the handsomest suburb in Greater New York.” The best houses went up around Eleventh Avenue and 82nd Street, about 110 feet above sea level, the highest natural point in southwest Brooklyn. Johnson’s restrictions, reported by The Wall Street Journal in 1899, required plots of at least 60 by 100 feet and houses set well back from the street. Those rules lapsed in 1915, but their legacy is the reason an alarm here looks different: Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as detached and semi-detached one- and two-family homes, many with driveways and private yards, with very few apartment buildings. A burglar in an attached rowhouse district has two faces of the building to work with. Here there can be four.

The record of break-ins on the hill bears that out. In March 2013 the Brooklyn Eagle reported a run of house burglaries mostly between 73rd and 79th Streets from 11th to 13th Avenues, with jewelry, iPads, laptops and in one case $5,000 in cash taken; the 68th Precinct’s advice was to lock doors and windows, put jewelry in a safe and consider alarms and good cameras. In the summer of 2019 police sought a man linked to at least 15 burglaries in Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights between July 10 and August 28, about $265,000 in cash, jewelry and electronics in all; CBS New York reported he got in through fire escapes, unlocked windows and doors, once by putting a ladder to a back window while the family slept and once by pulling an air conditioner out of a window. In September 2022 two Dyker houses a few blocks apart, near 12th Avenue and 71st Street and near 13th Avenue and 67th Street, were entered through the basement on consecutive evenings, the first through a broken basement window at about 8:50 p.m.

None of those entries was through a front door at the top of a stoop. They came through the openings a detached house has more of: basement windows at grade, upper windows at the back that a ladder reaches, window air-conditioner openings, and the side and rear doors along a driveway. An alarm in Dyker Heights has to be planned from the back and sides of the lot first, which is the opposite of how a kit is usually laid out.

Then come the two conditions that are peculiar to this neighborhood. Large sections of the hill are still fed by overhead electrical lines, which Community Board 10’s district manager told the Eagle are “susceptible to wind and sleet”; residents reported at least three outages in 2019 before the December 1 failure that darkened about 3,600 customers. And every December the whole neighborhood, not just one block, lights up, bringing tour buses to a drop-off on 86th Street, visitors on the sidewalks until late at night and, for the owners, a month when the house is the most looked-at it will be all year. This page is about an alarm that copes with both.

Dyker Heights at a glance: the facts that shape an alarm

  • Founded October 1895 by Walter L. Johnson; of the roughly 150 houses he built, Wikipedia says about half survive, the rest replaced by Mediterranean-style villas, condos and semi-detached or attached homes.
  • A 159-block rezoning in 2007 (approved 48–0 by the City Council on July 25, 2007, per CityLand) limited parts of Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton to detached one- and two-family homes, others to detached and semi-detached homes, and the rowhouse blocks to a lower bulk.
  • Repeated power failures: more than 6,000 Con Edison customers lost power one July 2018 afternoon between 10th and 13th Avenues; more than 1,200 lost it when overhead wires fell at 10th Avenue on February 28, 2019; about 3,600 lost it for roughly three hours on December 1, 2019 (Brooklyn Eagle).
  • The 68th Precinct, at 333 65th Street, reported 96 burglaries for the whole precinct in 2018, after an 88.6% fall in crime across all categories since 1990 (Wikipedia, citing NYPD CompStat).
  • The December displays run from Thanksgiving into the first days of January, with mid-December the busiest, and are concentrated in the mid-80s between 11th and 13th Avenues; tour buses are directed to 86th Street.

Trouble Light After an Outage, a Dead Siren or a Panel That Stopped Calling: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Dyker Heights

Most of the repair calls we get from houses on the hill trace back to the last power failure. A backup battery that was already tired runs flat during a three-hour outage and never fully recovers, so the keypad shows a low-battery or power-trouble light for weeks afterward. A communicator that still depends on a landline goes silent when the phone service on the same poles is interrupted. An old panel that rebooted badly when the power came back has lost its programming, or stays in a trouble state nobody knows how to clear. Each of these is usually a one-visit fix: a fresh battery sized for the panel, a cellular communicator in place of the phone line, or a reset and a new program.

The other cluster comes in November and early December. Outdoor motion sensors start tripping once the yard fills with inflatables and figures, a gate contact gets knocked out of line by a decorator’s ladder, or a door contact on the garage stops reporting after extension cords were pushed under the door for a month. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Dyker Heights’ Architecture

The 2007 rezoning recognized what anyone who walks the hill can see: Dyker Heights is several neighborhoods of housing in one. Each type below takes a different alarm, and we describe them as types, not as any particular address.

The Johnson-era originals

Large frame houses from 1895 to the early 1900s, some still with deep porches, bays and towers. The Brooklyn Eagle’s 1899 description of a typical house gave ten-foot ceilings on the first floor, five rooms on each of the first two floors, four more on the third and a full cellar. That means many openings, three floors of them, and cellar windows at grade. The Saitta House on 84th Street, built in 1899 and on the National Register since 2007, is the best-known survivor of the type.

1920s and 1930s detached houses

Brick and stucco houses built as the first estates were broken up; Wikipedia notes that one of the original 1895 houses was demolished in 1928 and replaced by seven fully detached single-family homes. These typically have a side driveway, a garage at the back and a basement with small windows at grade.

Postwar semi-detached two-families

Pairs of houses sharing one wall, with a driveway on the open side and a rental floor upstairs or below. Three exposed sides instead of four, and often two households whose alarm needs differ.

The rebuilt villas

Large new houses that replaced older ones before the 2007 rezoning, often with wide glass doors to the yard, two-story foyers, balconies and an attached garage. Big glass, open interiors and many exterior lights.

Rowhouse blocks at the edges

Attached houses on the blocks the 2007 rezoning placed in its rowhouse district, closer in character to Bay Ridge and Bensonhurst than to the estates at the top of the hill. Front and back only, with a yard behind.

Condos and small walk-ups

The handful of apartment buildings and newer condo buildings, where an alarm protects one unit and the shared doors belong to the board or the landlord.

Methods by house type

Original frame houses. Old balloon framing usually gives clear wall cavities from cellar to attic, so a wired system is often realistic without opening plaster, with cable fished up beside chimneys or inside closets. Where a run is blocked, wireless contacts fill in, and recessed contacts are fitted into old sash only where the wood is sound. A hybrid panel handles both. Porches and porch roofs make second-floor windows reachable, so they are treated as ground floor.

Brick and stucco houses of the 1920s and 1930s. Masonry walls and plaster on lath slow cable down above the first floor, so we wire the basement and first floor from below through open joists and use wireless for the upper floors.

Rebuilt villas. Walls of glass and sliding doors call for acoustic glass-break detection backed by contacts on every operable panel. Two-story foyers and open plans let one motion detector watch a large space, but they also put balconies and their doors within reach of a ladder, and those get contacts. Many of these houses have attached garages with an interior door, which is wired as an entry door, not ignored.

Wires on Poles and Short, Frequent Outages: Building an Alarm That Keeps Reporting When the Power Drops

Most of New York City gets its power underground. Dyker Heights, in many places, does not. When the Brooklyn Eagle covered the December 1, 2019 outage, it noted that many sections of the neighborhood still have above-ground wires installed decades ago and that only a few spots had been buried. The Dyker Heights Civic Association’s president called outages “a regular occurrence.” That year alone residents reported at least three before December, including the evening of February 28 when a pair of overhead wires fell at 72nd and 81st Streets on 10th Avenue, darkening more than 1,200 customers from 80th to 86th Streets between Seventh and 12th Avenues. Con Edison’s position, as quoted by the Eagle, is that burying lines would cost millions of dollars per mile, and that overhead lines are cheaper and faster to repair. So the wires are likely to stay, and an alarm on the hill has to be designed for power that sometimes goes away.

Designing for that comes down to four decisions, and we make all four on every Dyker Heights job.

1. A battery sized for the real load, not the panel alone

Every alarm panel has a standby battery, but it has to carry the keypads, the communicator, any wired motion detectors and, if the alarm trips, the siren. A battery that is fine for a small apartment system can run short in a three-floor house with several keypads and a dozen wired devices. We add up the load, fit a battery that covers it with room to spare, and record the installation date on the panel door so it is replaced on schedule, not after it fails during an outage.

2. A communicator that does not depend on the poles

The phone and cable lines on a Dyker Heights block usually share the poles with the electric lines. An alarm that reports over a landline, or over a home internet connection whose modem and router have no battery, goes silent at the same moment the power fails. We fit a cellular communicator that runs from the panel’s own battery, so the alarm can still send events to the app and the central station during an outage. Internet can be added as a second path, but it is never the only one.

3. Alerts for the power itself

For a family away for the holidays, knowing that the power is out matters almost as much as knowing a door has opened: the boiler may stop, the sump may stop, and the house is dark on a block where every other house is lit. We program the panel to report AC power loss after a short delay, so a flicker does not wake anybody, and to report again when power returns. If the outage is long enough to run the battery down, a low-battery report follows.

4. Protection from what happens when the power comes back

The return of power after a fault can be rougher on electronics than the outage. We put the panel’s transformer on a surge-protected outlet, avoid sharing a circuit with motors, boilers or holiday display power, and use a panel that keeps its programming in non-volatile memory, so a hard restart does not wipe the zone list.

Thanksgiving to New Year’s: Alarming a House the Whole City Comes to See

Dyker Heights’ Christmas lights have been a draw since at least the 1980s, when Lou Singer began running tours of “designer lighting” in southern Brooklyn, and by 2000 Conan O’Brien was filming a segment here. Wikipedia calls the neighborhood “an epicenter of professionally-hung Christmas lights”: most displays are hung by local decorating companies at costs that Wikipedia, citing a 2013 Brooklyn Eagle article, puts anywhere from $1,000 to $20,000 or more. The season formally starts on Thanksgiving and runs until the first days of January, and Brooklyn Paper reported that visitor numbers had returned to their pre-pandemic level of hundreds of thousands each winter.

For residents, that month has a security side the brochures leave out. In 2017 CBS New York reported residents at a community board meeting describing visitors ringing doorbells around 11 p.m. to ask to use a bathroom, people relieving themselves on private property, garbage and noise, and owners unable to get out of their own driveways. In 2016 police set aside tour-bus-only spaces on 86th Street and limited parking for residents from 3 p.m. to midnight until the new year, FOX 5 reported. In November 2018, after a snowstorm, a handful of homeowners found the heavy-duty power cords to their displays cut and the copper inside taken, according to the Eagle. And in November 2016 a homeowner’s camera recorded a man walking into the yard, cutting the twine on a life-size inflatable Santa with a knife and leaving with it along Bay Ridge Parkway.

A seasonal arming mode instead of switching things off

Owners often switch the alarm off for December because outdoor sensors keep tripping. We program two away modes instead. The normal one arms everything. The seasonal one arms every door, window and interior motion detector exactly as usual, but bypasses outdoor motion sensors and any outdoor beam that faces the sidewalk or the front lawn where people gather. You pick the mode in the app or at the keypad; there is no reprogramming to do in November and none in January.

Interior coverage where the crowd cannot see in

During the season, lit front rooms are on show until late at night, and the back of the house is darker by comparison. The rooms that matter are the back rooms, the basement and the upstairs bedrooms where jewelry is kept, which is what the 2013 and 2019 burglaries targeted. We make sure the rear doors, basement windows and upper back windows are on the alarm, and that at least one interior motion detector covers the path from the back of the house to the stairs.

Display power that never touches the alarm

A large display can draw heavily from outdoor outlets and temporary runs, and a decorator’s cord pushed under a garage door can hold a contact open or pull it out of line for weeks. We check which circuit feeds the panel, move it off any circuit used for the display if needed, and fit garage and gate contacts where a cord or a ladder will not disturb them. If a display controller or timer has its own power monitoring, it stays separate from the alarm; the alarm’s job is the house.

Codes for the decorators, and an end date

Crews often need the yard, the garage and the side gate in November and again in January. Instead of sharing your master code, we create a separate user code that disarms only the garage and yard areas, works only during the days you choose, and expires on its own when the job is done.

The doorbell at 11 p.m.

A video doorbell lets you talk to whoever rings without opening the door, and when it is linked to the alarm app, the clip sits alongside the alarm history. We place it so it sees the porch steps, not the whole sidewalk, so it is not recording every passer-by, and we set its motion alerts for the porch only.

Basement Windows, Ladders and Air Conditioners: Covering the Openings Dyker Heights Burglars Have Used

Put the reported Dyker Heights entries side by side and a pattern appears. The September 2022 pair came through basements in the evening, one through a broken basement window. The 2019 suspect used a ladder against a back window while residents slept, removed a window air conditioner, used fire escapes, and walked in through windows and doors that had been left unlocked. The 2013 spree took jewelry, tablets, laptops and cash from houses between 73rd and 79th Streets. What these have in common is that they avoided the front of the house.

Basement and areaway windows

Every basement window gets a contact if it opens and a glass-break detector covering it if it does not, and the basement room behind them gets a motion detector. Where window wells or areaways put a window below grade, the contact goes on the sash, not on a grille, because grilles are often removable from outside.

Upper windows at the back

A window counts as reachable if a ladder, a porch roof, a garage roof, a deck rail or a fence can bring someone to it. On a detached house with a garage at the back, that often includes second-floor windows on two sides. Those windows get contacts even though they are upstairs.

Windows that hold an air conditioner

An installed window unit leaves the sash raised and the gap blocked only by the unit and its side panels. We fit a second magnet so the sash is still supervised in the partly open position, and a contact or shock sensor on the unit’s frame or a glass-break detector covering the room, so pulling the unit out trips the alarm.

Side and back doors along the driveway

These are the doors used every day, which also means they are the ones most often left unlocked. They get contacts with an entry delay for family members, and the keypad goes inside the door the family actually uses, so arming is easy and therefore actually done.

Business Alarm Installation in Dyker Heights

Dyker Heights is mostly houses, so its businesses are concentrated on a few streets, and the 2007 rezoning cut the depth of the commercial overlays on Fort Hamilton Parkway, 11th Avenue and 13th Avenue to keep shops from spreading into residential blocks. The result is narrow strips of storefronts with homes directly behind and often directly above them. 13th Avenue, Dyker Heights Boulevard

Co-named Dyker Heights Boulevard between 86th Street and Bay Ridge Avenue in 2001, and described by Wikipedia as the neighborhood’s only commercial district and its de facto center, with many Italian-owned businesses. Two incidents on this avenue shaped how we alarm its shops: in December 2024 a pharmacy was forced open with a pry tool shortly before 6 a.m., and in August 2018 a bodega near 74th Street was entered through its front gate around 5 a.m.

86th Street

The southern edge of the residential grid and, every December, the drop-off point for charter and tour buses visiting the lights, with buses waiting near the golf course. For a store that keeps longer hours in the season, that changes when the alarm should arm and who should be told if it does not.

11th Avenue

A mix of storefronts and community buildings, including the area around 11th Avenue in the 60s where the Coptic Egyptian community and St. George Coptic Orthodox Church, established in 1973, are based. Where a building combines a ground-floor space with an apartment above, the two get separate alarm areas on one panel.

Fort Hamilton Parkway

The neighborhood’s north-eastern commercial edge, with small shops, offices and services between residential blocks; the Brooklyn Public Library’s McKinley Park branch is at 6802 Fort Hamilton Parkway. Where a shop’s rear door opens onto a yard behind houses, that door is treated as the main risk.

Offices inside houses

Where a house on a residential block also holds a professional office with its own entrance, the office goes on a business area with opening and closing reports and the family rooms on another, both on one panel, so staff never need the family’s code.

What we fit in a Dyker Heights storefront

The 2024 pharmacy entry is the case we design against: a pry tool on the door before dawn. A contact on the door only reacts once the door has moved. We add a shock sensor on the frame or the glass that reacts to the force of a pry bar or a hammer, so the alarm and the alert begin while the attacker is still working on the door, not after. Behind the gate, the glass gets an acoustic glass-break detector, and the interior gets a motion detector covering the back room, the pharmacy or stock area and the office. Safes and controlled-medicine cabinets get their own contact or vibration sensor on a 24-hour zone, so they stay protected even while the store is open.

For stores that open early, we pay attention to the hour before opening. The panel can send an alert if the alarm is disarmed outside normal hours, and a separate alert if no one has armed it by a set closing time. In December, for stores on 86th Street and 13th Avenue that stay open later, a seasonal schedule moves the expected closing time so the owner is not pinged every night by a reminder the season has made wrong.

Rules that apply to every Dyker Heights alarm

  • Siren cutoff. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of stopping, and to stop, within fifteen minutes of activation. Every siren we install is programmed to do so, which matters on residential blocks where a store’s siren is heard by the families above and behind it.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a company that installs burglar or fire alarms for others to hold a Department of State license (General Business Law Article 6-D); a homeowner doing work on their own house does not need one. A legitimate installer will give you the number without being asked twice. AESS works under #12000287431.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide. NYC HPD requires smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied, which covers many Dyker Heights two-families with a rented floor. Linked detectors can also report through the alarm.
  • No city alarm permit claimed. We found no primary source for a New York City burglar-alarm permit requirement, so we do not charge for or claim one.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Dyker Heights: Questions Answered

Overhead lines, outages and the panel

The electric and cable lines come to our house overhead. Could someone cut them to knock the alarm out?

Cutting them would take out your lights and internet, but it should not take out the alarm. That is the point of a cellular communicator running on the panel’s own battery: there is no wire outside the house that carries the alarm’s signal, so there is nothing on the pole or the service drop to cut. We also program the panel to report the loss of power, so if the power to one house goes while the rest of the block stays lit, you get a message saying so. Old systems that report over the phone line are the ones vulnerable to a cut wire, and replacing that communicator is usually the first thing we recommend on the hill.

Our part of Dyker lost power several times in one year. Can the alarm tell us the moment it goes out while we are away, and again when it comes back?

Yes. The panel watches its own power supply. We set a short delay, a few minutes, so a flicker does not send a message, and then it reports “AC power lost” to the app and, if you have monitoring, to the central station. When power returns it reports that too, which tells you how long the house was dark. If an outage lasts long enough to run the battery down, a low-battery report follows.

After the last outage, a few appliances never worked again. Does an alarm panel need protecting from what happens when the power comes back?

It is worth doing. We plug the panel’s transformer into a surge-protected outlet, keep it off circuits shared with the boiler, motors or the holiday display, and use panels that store their programming in memory that survives a hard restart. Those three steps cost little and are the difference between a panel that comes back on its own and one that needs a service call to reprogram.

The lights season

From Thanksgiving to New Year’s people stand outside our house until midnight. Does that make outdoor sensors and camera alerts useless for a month?

The ones that face the sidewalk, yes, if they stay armed. That is why we program a seasonal away mode that keeps every door, window and interior detector armed while bypassing outdoor sensors aimed at the front. Sensors that watch the back of the lot, the side driveway behind a gate or a rear garage usually stay armed, because visitors have no reason to be there. Camera motion alerts are trimmed the same way: porch and side gate only.

Visitors have rung our bell late at night asking to use the bathroom. Can the doorbell and the alarm deal with that without us opening the door?

A video doorbell lets you answer from your phone or an indoor screen and decline through the speaker, and the alarm stays armed the whole time because nobody opens a door. We aim the doorbell at the porch steps so it records whoever comes up to the door rather than everyone walking past, and in the app its clips sit next to the alarm events, so if someone tries a door after being turned away, you can see who it was.

A decorating company hangs our display. Can their crew open the yard and garage while the house itself stays armed?

We create a decorator code that disarms only the garage and yard areas, works only on the dates you set and expires by itself. The house itself stays armed while they work outside. The app logs each time the code is used, so you know when the crew arrived and left, and you can delete the code early from your phone if the job finishes ahead of schedule.

Neighbors had their display cords cut for the copper. Can the alarm warn us if the display loses power in the middle of the night?

Not by watching the display directly; the alarm is not wired to the lights and should not be. What it can do is watch the places a thief has to go to reach the cords: an outdoor beam or motion sensor along the side of the house or across the yard, a contact on the side gate, and a camera covering the outlets. For the back and side of the lot those can stay armed all season, so someone kneeling by the outlets at 3 a.m. trips an alert even though the front sensors are bypassed.

If we travel after Christmas while the display is still lit, should we leave the alarm in the seasonal mode or the full away mode?

If the display timer switches the lights off at night, use the full away mode for those hours, since nobody gathers at a dark house; if the lights must stay on, use the seasonal mode and check that every interior detector is active. Add the power-loss alert, a temperature sensor near the boiler or the coldest pipe, and a contact on the basement door. Give one neighbor or relative a code of their own so the central station has someone local to call, and tell the decorators not to come while you are away unless their code is still valid.

The way break-ins here have happened

One burglar here put a ladder to a back window while the family slept. Which upstairs windows on a detached house count as reachable?

More than most owners expect. Any window above a porch roof, a garage roof, a bay or a deck rail is reachable without a ladder, and with one, every back and side window on the second floor is. A ladder left in the yard or the garage makes it easier still. We put contacts on all of those, and program a night mode that arms them along with the doors while leaving bedroom-floor motion off, so the house is protected while you sleep in it.

Another entry was made by pulling out a window air conditioner. Can a window that holds a unit half the year still be alarmed?

Yes. We add a second magnet so the contact still reads the sash as closed at the height the unit holds it, which means opening it further trips the alarm. Then we cover the unit itself: a glass-break detector in the room hears the side panels or glass break, or a shock sensor on the frame reacts to the unit being forced. In winter, when the unit comes out, the window goes back to a normal contact without any reprogramming.

Two Dyker houses were broken into through the basement in 2022, both in the evening. What does a basement window actually need?

A contact on every window that opens, glass-break coverage for fixed ones, and a motion detector in the basement room. Evening matters: at 8:50 or 9:30 p.m. many families are home upstairs with the alarm off. For that reason we suggest an evening mode that arms the basement and the perimeter while you are home, so the family does not have to arm the whole house to protect the floor they are not using.

Houses, garages and yards

Our house is one of the 1890s originals, with a deep porch, a tower room and a cellar under the whole house. Where are the weak points on a house that age?

Three, usually. The porch, which hides the front windows from the street and puts a roof under the second-floor windows. The tower or bay rooms, which add several windows close together, often above that porch roof. And the cellar, which on these houses runs under the whole footprint with windows at grade on several sides. All three get contacts or glass-break coverage; the cellar also gets a motion detector. Old frame walls usually let us wire much of this without opening plaster, with wireless filling the gaps.

We rebuilt the house with a wall of glass doors to the yard. Contacts, glass-break or both?

Both. A contact on each sliding or folding panel tells you if it is opened. An acoustic glass-break detector covering the room tells you if a pane is smashed without the panel moving, which a contact alone cannot detect. A wide opening also gets a motion detector inside, because a large glass wall is one opening a burglar can step straight through.

Our driveway runs down the side of the house to a separate garage at the back. Can the garage be on the same alarm?

Usually, yes. The garage becomes its own area with a contact on the overhead door and the side door and, if tools or a car are worth protecting, a motion detector inside. The question is the signal: a brick or stone house between the panel and the garage can weaken a wireless sensor. We test the signal from the garage on the visit and either add a wireless repeater or bury a low-voltage cable along the driveway. Either way you can arm the garage separately from the house.

Stores and offices

The pharmacy break-in on 13th Avenue was done with a pry tool. Can an alarm react while a door is being forced, before it opens?

Yes, with a shock or vibration sensor on the door frame or the glass. A door contact only reacts once the door has moved, which with a pry bar can be the last step. A shock sensor reacts to the blows and the strain of forcing, so the siren and the alert start while the attacker is still outside.

We stay open later in December for the visitors. Can the alarm’s closing reminder follow the season without reprogramming?

Yes. The panel can hold two schedules, and you switch between them in the app. The normal one expects the store armed by your usual closing time and sends a reminder if it is not; the seasonal one moves that time later for the weeks you choose. Disarm alerts outside opening hours keep working in both, so an unexpected opening at 5 a.m. still reaches you.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Dyker Heights

Google’s AI Overview was not reachable from the environment this page was built in, so the claims below are not quotations from it. They are the stock statements found across chatbot answers, the national cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and advertising for ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe. Each is checked here against what has actually happened on the hill.

“The backup battery keeps your alarm working in a power outage.”

The panel, yes, for a while. But the claim skips the parts that do not run on that battery: the router and modem many systems use to report, the cameras that confirm an alarm, and any landline service that shares the poles with the electric lines. On a hill where overhead wires have fallen and outages have darkened thousands of customers for hours at a time, an alarm that keeps its lights on but cannot send a message is not working.

The fix is not exotic: a battery sized for the whole system, a cellular communicator running from it, and a power-loss alert so you know the outage happened. Those three things are what “works in an outage” should mean.

Ask, too, how old the battery is; ours are dated and tested at handover.

“Most burglars come in through the front door.”

As a national generalization, it may be. It does not describe the Dyker Heights incidents in the local press. The 2022 pair came through basements; the 2019 suspect used a ladder to a back window, pulled out an air conditioner, used fire escapes and walked through unlocked windows and doors; the 2013 spree targeted houses between 73rd and 79th Streets without any report of front doors being forced.

A plan that starts with the front door and a couple of front windows protects the side of a Dyker Heights house that is most visible from the street and least likely to be used.

We start with the back and sides of the lot and the basement, and the front door gets its contact last.

“Add outdoor motion sensors to stop burglars before they reach the house.”

On a quiet suburban lot, outdoor sensors can give early warning. On the lights blocks in December, an outdoor sensor that faces the street will trip constantly from Thanksgiving to New Year’s, and the usual result is an owner who switches the whole alarm off.

Outdoor detection still has a place here: along a back fence, down a gated side driveway, around a rear garage. It needs to be zoned so the street-facing parts can be bypassed for the season without disarming anything else.

That decision is made at installation, not in late November.

“A starter kit with a few sensors covers a typical home.”

Kits are sized for an average house with a front door, a back door and a handful of ground-floor windows. A Dyker Heights house on its own lot may have a front, a side door off the driveway, a back door to a deck, a basement with several windows at grade, three floors of reachable windows thanks to porches, and a detached garage at the back.

Buying enough extra sensors to cover that is possible, but the kit’s base station often cannot reach a rear garage through brick, has no seasonal mode and reports only over home Wi-Fi.

A kit already bought can still be used; we install it labor-only and say what it leaves uncovered.

“Wireless sensors work anywhere in the house.”

Wireless works very well in frame houses and over short distances. Thick brick and stone walls, metal-framed glass and the distance from a panel in the front hall to a garage at the end of a driveway can all weaken the signal enough to cause missed reports or supervision trouble.

We test every wireless location with the panel before mounting the sensor, and where the signal is weak we add a repeater or run cable.

Often the answer is a hybrid of both.

“Alarm installation costs about the same everywhere.”

National cost guides publish national averages, and those blend in houses and labor markets unlike southern Brooklyn. They also tend to price the equipment and ignore what drives labor here: the number of exposed sides, basement windows, a garage at the back, and an old house where cable has to be fished rather than stapled.

Our prices are the published AESS Brooklyn list, shown in full lower on this page, and the figure for your house is written down after one visit.

No national average, and no lights-block surcharge.

“A camera does the same job as an alarm.”

The 2016 inflatable-Santa theft is the counterexample. The homeowner’s camera captured the whole thing, which helped tell the story afterward, but it did not stop the thief walking into the yard and leaving with the display.

A camera records. An alarm reacts while something is happening and tells someone. In a neighborhood where the house is on show for a month and the yard is full of valuable decorations, the two work best together, with the camera clip attached to the alarm event.

On a tight budget, the alarm comes first.

Field Notes: Walk the Lot and Look Up Before Choosing the Panel

This is how our technicians survey on the hill, not the story of any particular house. Before counting sensors, the technician walks every side of the lot: the front, along the driveway, the back yard and the garage. On the way he looks up. Only then does he go inside to choose the panel’s place: away from the front door, on a circuit with nothing else heavy on it, close enough to the middle of the house for wireless sensors to reach the far corners and, if there is one, the garage. The order matters because on this hill, the outside of the house decides the alarm.

We are thinking about a standby generator because of the outages. Should the alarm be connected to it?

It can be, but the alarm should not depend on it. The panel’s own battery and cellular communicator carry it through an outage whether or not the generator starts. Can the alarm switch on the yard and porch lights when a sensor trips at night?
+

Yes, with a panel that supports home automation and a smart switch or relay on the lighting circuit. When an alarm or an outdoor zone trips after dark, the lights come on, which shows a person they have been noticed and gives any camera a better picture. In the seasonal mode we leave this off for the front lights, since the display is already lit.

Our front yard has an iron fence with a gate between stone piers. Is a sensor on the gate worth it?
+

Often, yes, if the gate is normally kept closed. A weatherproof contact on the gate tells you when it opens, and on the seasonal mode it can be set to chime only, so you know when a visitor or the decorators come in without the alarm going off. If the gate is left open all day, an outdoor beam across the path or a camera does the job better.

Our back yard is large by Brooklyn standards. Are outdoor beam sensors along the back fence worth the cost?
+

They can be, on a deep lot with a fence line that nobody should cross at night. A pair of photoelectric beams warns you before anyone reaches the house. They need a clear line, so shrubs and garden furniture have to stay out of the beam, and they are best on their own zone so you can switch them off when the family is using the yard.

Can the keypad go by the side door off the driveway, since nobody uses the front door?
+

Yes, and that is where we usually put it. A keypad by the door the family actually uses means the alarm gets armed every time, and the entry delay can be shorter. The front door still gets a contact, and a second keypad or a fob can be added upstairs for the night mode.

My father is hard of hearing. Can the alarm alert him with a light or a vibration instead of a siren he might not hear?
+

Yes. We can add a strobe that flashes with the siren, place a flashing indicator in his room, and set his phone or a wearable to vibrate with alarm and entry alerts. How many keypads does a three-story Dyker Heights house need?
+

Usually two: one by the door the family uses most, and one on the bedroom floor for setting the night mode and for an emergency button at the top of the stairs.
