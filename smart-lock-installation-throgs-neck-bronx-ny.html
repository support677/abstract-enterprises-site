The Neighborhood

Why Throgs Neck Homes Need Smart Lock Installation Built for the Peninsula

Throgs Neck — spelled with one G on the bridge and two by many people who grew up here — is a narrow spit of land in the south-east Bronx. Westchester Creek runs down its west side, the East River and Long Island Sound wrap its south and east, and Baisley Avenue and the Bruckner Expressway close it off to the north. That geography explains most of what we find at the front door. Almost everything here is a house rather than an apartment, a lot of those houses face open water, and a surprising number of them stand on ground the owner leases rather than owns.

The housing grew in layers. Through the nineteenth century the peninsula was farms and then waterfront estates. In the 1920s those estates were cut up into row homes and densely packed bungalow lots, and summer colonies took shape along the shore. Silver Beach Garden began as a colony of bungalows whose owners held their houses but rented the land underneath. A church youth campsite to the north grew into Edgewater Park. Over the following decades the cottages were winterized, given basements and sewer connections, and lived in year-round. Meanwhile the inland streets filled with attached and semi-detached one- and two-family houses, many of them now home to an owner on one floor and a tenant or relative on another.

The result is a neighborhood where the question “whose door is this?” has an unusual answer. On the street grid in Schuylerville or the blocks off East Tremont Avenue, the homeowner owns house and lot outright and decides what goes on the door. In Silver Beach, the proprietary lease each household signs with the Silver Beach Gardens corporation has, at least since the corporation took title in the 1970s, said a bungalow cannot be changed, modified or altered without the corporation’s permission. Edgewater Park, a cooperative since 1988, enforces its own building guidelines across 675 houses on a single city zoning lot. None of those rules, as far as we have been able to find, mentions door locks by name — but they are the reason we ask which part of the peninsula you are in before we recommend anything.

Then there is the water. The Department of City Planning counts roughly 278 of Edgewater Park’s homes, just over 40 percent, inside the 1% annual chance floodplain, some of them in the V Zone where storm surge can arrive with waves of three feet or more. A lock mounted at knee height on a ground-floor door in that zone is a different purchase from the same lock on a Silver Beach cottage that the State Tax Commission described as sitting on a promontory about a hundred feet above the river. Salt spray, wind-driven rain and a storm door that slams every time the breeze shifts are everyday conditions, not edge cases.

Finally there is space, or the lack of it. On Edgewater Park’s typical 30-by-50-foot lots, city planners measured side yards of two to five feet, with eaves in places touching the house next door. Side doors open into those slots. Wi-Fi from one house reaches into the next one’s kitchen. A keypad that would be invisible on a suburban lot is in plain view of the neighbor’s window. That is why a Throgs Neck smart lock plan starts with a walk around the house.

Throgs Neck facts that change a lock job

  • Bungalow co-ops on leased ground: Edgewater Park has 675 single-family homes on about 55 acres with roughly 3,000 residents and became a cooperative in 1988; Silver Beach Gardens was incorporated on August 30, 1972 and leases house sites to bungalow owners under proprietary leases (NYC Department of City Planning; NYS Tax Commission decision, 1983).
  • Floodplain: about 278 Edgewater Park homes — just over 40 percent — lie in the 1% annual chance floodplain, with just under 100 in the Coastal A Zone and 167 in the A Zone (Department of City Planning, Resilient Neighborhoods).
  • Tight lots: typical Edgewater Park lots measure about 30 by 50 feet, side yards run two to five feet, and one case-study lane in the city’s report had a 12-foot roadbed (Bronx Times, December 2015; Department of City Planning).
  • Owners and older residents: in NYU Furman Center figures for the Throgs Neck/Co-op City district, a little under half of households own their home and roughly one resident in five is 65 or older.
  • Break-ins are not the main story: the 45th Precinct, which patrols the peninsula from 2877 Barkley Avenue, reported 108 burglaries in 2022, with crime across all categories down 67 percent since 1990.
  • Main street: the Throggs Neck Business Improvement District, created in January 2019, covers 27 block faces of East Tremont Avenue with 281 ground-floor retail businesses (NYC Small Business Services).

What we look at on every Throgs Neck door

  • The ground the house stands on: owned lot, co-op land, or leased site — it decides whether you need to tell anyone before we drill, and who keeps a key afterwards.
  • The storm door: how deep the gap is, whether the storm door has its own lock, and whether its closer is strong enough to stop it banging into the new hardware.
  • The door’s exposure: facing the water, the street or a side passage; under a porch roof or open to rain; in or out of the floodplain on the city’s maps.
  • The frame: houses on old fill and former stilt foundations move, so we check that the latch and bolt still meet the strike squarely before a motor is asked to push a bolt into a misaligned hole.
  • The door itself: thickness, backset and existing bores; old rim-lock and chain holes; whether it is a solid door or a lightweight cottage door that needs a reinforcement plate.
  • The radio path: where the router is, how many neighbors’ networks crowd the same channel, and whether a bridge or Thread border router can sit within a few feet of the lock indoors.
  • The people: who lives upstairs, who visits, who looks after a parent, and who must never have a code.

Rules that shape a Throgs Neck business door

  • Exit without a key: under the city’s 2022 Building Code, an egress door must open from the side people leave by with no key, no special knowledge and no special effort (section 1010.1.9). Codes belong on the street side only; staff should never need one to get out.
  • One motion to unlatch: the same chapter limits how many operations it takes to release a door, which is why we fit locks whose inside lever or thumb-turn retracts everything at once.
  • Reachable hardware: as a guide we follow the 2010 ADA Standards, which put operable parts between 34 and 48 inches above the floor and call for hardware that works without tight grasping, pinching or twisting.
  • Tenant data: if the building above your shop has apartments and you add keyless entry for residents, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act requires a written data retention and privacy policy for smart access systems in covered buildings.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Throgs Neck: Questions Answered

Questions that come from the way Throgs Neck is built and lived in, each answered in full.

Lots, lanes and signal

With a dozen neighbors’ Wi-Fi networks packed into our bungalow lane, will the lock keep dropping offline?

It can, if the lock relies on Wi-Fi and has to shout across the house to reach the router. Many Wi-Fi smart locks only use the 2.4 GHz band — Schlage, for example, lists a 2.4 GHz network as a requirement for its Encode deadbolts — and 2.4 GHz has only three channels that don’t overlap. On Edgewater Park lots measured at about 30 by 50 feet, with side yards of two to five feet, your lock may be closer to three neighbors’ routers than to your own. Add a door that faces outward and a router at the back of a long, narrow house, and you get a lock that shows “offline” for an hour and then reappears.

The fix is usually not a stronger router. It is moving the radio work indoors and close to the door. August, for instance, tells owners to keep its Connect Wi-Fi bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock. A Z-Wave or Thread lock talking to a hub or border router in the front room avoids the 2.4 GHz scrum altogether for the short hop, and only the hub needs a good path to the router — which can be a cable. Bluetooth-only locks never drop off Wi-Fi because they never join it; you simply lose remote access.

At the walkthrough we stand at the door with a phone, see how many networks are visible and how strong yours is, then choose. Before we leave we lock and unlock from mobile data on the lane, not from the living room, so you know remote access works from where you will actually use it.

Our side door opens onto a passage barely two feet wide — is a keypad even sensible there?

Sometimes it is the best door in the house for one, and sometimes it is the worst. In its favour: it is the door you use with shopping bags, it is out of sight from the street, and a keypad there means the front door can stay deadbolted for weeks. Against it: in a passage that narrow you may be standing with your back against the neighbor’s wall, the keypad may be at the height of their kitchen window, and there may be no light at night. A keypad you can’t see to use, or one the neighbor can watch you use, is not an improvement on a key.

We check four things. Can you stand square to the door and see the keypad with the door closed? Does the door swing into the passage, and if so does the outside housing clear the neighbor’s wall at full swing? Is there power nearby for a small light, or does a backlit keypad do the job? And is this door also somebody’s way out — a basement tenant’s or a second exit from the kitchen — in which case the inside must stay a plain thumb-turn that opens in one motion.

If the answers are poor, a lock you open from your phone or with a fingerprint, with no visible keypad, usually suits that door better. Working space is rarely the problem for us: a right-angle drill and a short bit bore a deadbolt hole in a passage you could barely turn round in.

Does a keypad on the front door tell people walking the lanes that there is something worth taking?

We have not seen evidence that it does, and a keypad tells a passer-by very little that a brass deadbolt doesn’t: there is a lock on this door. The more real risk in Throgs Neck’s close-packed lanes is the code, not the keypad. If the neighbor’s window or the path along the promenade gives a clear view of your hand, a code can be learned simply by watching, and smudges on the four most-used buttons give the rest away on a glossy keypad.

So we position and set up against watching rather than against attention. Many current locks let you type extra digits before or after the real code, so an onlooker sees a different sequence each time. Touchscreen keypads that light up all digits make smudge patterns less telling. We can angle a surface-mounted keypad, or put it on the side door rather than the front. And every person gets their own code, so if one leaks you change one code, not the whole household’s.

If a visible keypad still bothers you, there are smart locks with no keypad at all: they look like an ordinary deadbolt outside and open from your phone, a fob or a key. Pair one with a doorbell camera and the door looks no different from any other on the lane.

Water, wind and salt

Salt water reached our lock in a storm surge — can it be dried out, or does it have to be replaced?

Plan on replacing the electronics. The Electrical Safety Foundation’s guidance on water-damaged equipment lists batteries among the items that must be replaced, and NEMA’s guide to evaluating water-damaged electrical equipment notes that saltwater residue is particularly damaging because it is both corrosive and conductive; for electronic products such as signaling and protection systems it says to replace the components or return them to the manufacturer. A smart lock is a small circuit board, a motor and a battery pack in a housing that is weather-resistant, not submersible. A lock that seems to work a week after a surge can fail months later as corrosion creeps along the board.

What to do straight away: take the batteries out so nothing is powered while wet, open the door with the mechanical key if it turns freely, and photograph the lock and the waterline for your insurer before anything is removed. Don’t try to dry the board with heat. Don’t keep using a cylinder that feels gritty — sand and salt grind it.

When we come out, the door often matters as much as the lock. A wood or composite door that sat in water can swell, and a frame on fill can shift; we re-check the latch and bolt alignment before fitting a new lock, so the new motor isn’t straining from its first day. Where the door sits low in Edgewater Park’s floodplain, we also talk about whether the replacement should be a simpler, cheaper keyed-and-keypad model rather than the top-of-the-range unit that drowned.

We fitted tracks for removable flood shields either side of the front door — will a keypad and handle still clear the barrier?

Usually, but only if we measure the tracks before choosing the lock. Flood shields are a dry floodproofing measure — the Department of Energy’s building guidance lists installing shields to cover openings such as doors and windows as one strategy — and on existing homes in places like Edgewater Park owners add them as extra protection. (FEMA does not accept dry floodproofing as compliance for new or substantially improved homes in the floodplain; elevation is the route there.) The shield panel slides into channels fixed to the masonry or jambs either side of the door, and its brackets and the panel itself can foul anything that projects from the outside face of the door.

The parts that project are the exterior keypad housing and, if you change it, the handle or lever. We measure the depth from the door face to the inside of the shield channel, and the height of the shield’s top edge. The keypad and its electronics ideally sit above the shield top, so on the day a surge is forecast the panel goes in without the lock in the way and the circuit board is not the lowest thing on the door. A slim keypad deadbolt and a low-profile pull handle normally fit; a bulky lever-set with an integrated keypad sometimes doesn’t.

Remember that with the shield in place nobody is using that door. Keep the side or upstairs door’s code working for storm days, and keep the mechanical key somewhere other than the car.

Wind off the Sound keeps slamming our storm door — will that knock the smart lock out of adjustment?

It can, in two ways. If the gap between the storm door and the entry door is tight, a storm door that blows shut hits the outside keypad housing; a few months of that cracks housings and loosens the screws that hold the two halves of the lock together through the door. And every slam shakes the entry door in its frame. Smart deadbolts are less forgiving of a bolt that no longer lines up with its strike than a manual one, because a person turning a key feels the bind and lifts the door, while a motor just stalls and reports a jam.

So on a Throgs Neck job with a storm door we fix the storm door too. That means adjusting or replacing its pneumatic closer so it shuts firmly but slowly, adding a wind chain or stop so a gust can’t throw it past its hinges, and checking that it clears the new lock with room to spare. On the entry door we tighten the hinges, set the strike so the bolt slides in without touching, and fit the long screws that reach the framing.

If the storm door has its own lock and you leave it locked, a keypad behind it is useless from outside. In that case we talk about which door the household actually opens day to day, and set the system up around that one.

The people at the door

The kids come back from the beach with wet, sandy hands — will a fingerprint reader still let them in?

Not reliably. Fingerprint sensors read the ridges of a clean, reasonably dry finger; Kwikset’s own support advice is to remove excess lotion, sweat or debris before scanning, and to try again if fingers are very dry. A wet, salty, sandy fingertip fails more often, and sand is abrasive on the sensor glass. Summer in Edgewater Park, Silver Beach or by the yacht club means a lot of exactly that.

Fingerprint locks still work well for families here if they are set up for it. We enroll two or three fingers per child, including one they are less likely to have been digging with, and we teach a quick wipe on a towel or shirt before the press. More importantly, every fingerprint lock we fit also has a keypad or a key, so a child whose finger isn’t reading has a code to fall back on. For younger children, a small fob on a lanyard or bag strap avoids both wet fingers and forgotten codes.

Keep the sensor clean. A soft cloth and plain water every week or two in summer, more often on a door that faces the water, stops a film of salt and sunscreen from building up. Don’t use solvents or abrasive cleaners on it.

Mom set up the smart lock and has since passed away — how do we take control of it without her password?

There are two routes, and the right one depends on whether you need anything stored in her account. If you only need the lock to work for the family, a factory reset is the clean answer: it wipes every code, fingerprint and phone invitation and returns the lock to its out-of-the-box state, ready to be set up under a new owner’s account. Schlage’s support pages, for example, say a factory default reset removes all codes and virtual keys programmed to the lock. Resets are done from the inside of the door, typically with a button or a battery-out sequence in the interior unit, which is part of the point — nobody can do it from outside.

If you need her entry history, or the lock is tied into a wider account with cameras or a doorbell, contact the manufacturer’s support first and ask what they need from the family. Do that before resetting, because a reset can’t be undone.

When we do this for a family, we reset the lock, set up a new admin account in the name of the person now responsible for the house, give a second family member admin rights so this never happens again, re-enroll everyone who still needs access, and change any code she had shared with neighbors, aides or contractors over the years. In Edgewater Park or Silver Beach, where the house may be passing through the cooperative or corporation’s transfer process, we can also leave the lock with keys only, so the next owner starts fresh.

Neighbors post doorbell clips on the Ring Neighbors app — if our lock is linked to the same account, what do they see?

Only what you choose to post. Ring says that only content a Neighbors user chooses to share is publicly accessible in the Neighbors app. Linking a smart lock to your doorbell account does not publish your lock’s unlock history, codes or schedule to the neighborhood feed. When a Throgs Neck family shared doorbell video of teenagers kicking in their front door in November 2025, neighbors reportedly recognised the same group from other homes — that kind of sharing is a deliberate post, not something the lock does on its own.

The privacy questions worth asking are closer to home. Who is a shared user on the account? Ring’s shared-user feature gives family, roommates and others device access without your login, and on many systems a shared user can see activity too. A relative you added years ago may still be watching the door. Before posting a clip yourself, watch it through once: a video that shows someone keying in the door code is giving that code to everyone who watches.

At handover we go through the account with you — shared users, notification settings, whether the lock and doorbell are linked at all — and remove anyone who shouldn’t be there.

Burglary is fairly low in the 45th Precinct — is a smart lock about security here, or about keys?

Mostly about keys, and that is a perfectly good reason. The 45th Precinct reported 108 burglaries in 2022, across an area that also takes in Co-op City and City Island, and crime in all categories there has fallen by about two-thirds since 1990. For many owners the trigger isn’t a break-in at all. It is being tired of cutting keys for a tenant, a dog walker, a cousin who checks on Dad and a contractor who never gave the last one back, and of not knowing how many copies of the front-door key exist.

A smart lock solves that problem completely: codes per person, removed in seconds, with a record of when each was used. What it does not do on its own is make the door stronger. The incident reported by News 12 in November 2025, in which teenagers kicked in a Throgs Neck front door, was a reminder that a door fails at the frame, not at the keypad. A smart deadbolt is only as strong as the strike plate and the screws holding it.

So every door we fit gets the security basics whether you asked or not: a strike plate fixed with long screws into the framing, a bolt that fully extends, hinges checked, and a frame we will tell you honestly about if it is soft or split. That combination, a sound frame plus controlled codes, covers both reasons people buy these locks.

Shops on East Tremont Avenue

A Throggs Neck deli was burgled through a forced basement door — what should our shop do about its own cellar door?

Treat it as a front door that nobody is watching. In May 2020 the Bronx Times reported that two burglars got into a Throggs Neck corner deli at about 3:50 in the morning by forcing open a basement door, and left with roughly $7,200 from two safes. Cellar and basement doors are often the weakest openings on a shop: older, out of sight, secured by a padlock or a single cheap deadbolt, and rarely checked at closing.

For a cellar door that staff use for deliveries and stock, a heavy-duty keypad deadbolt or a keypad with an electric strike gives each employee a code, logs who opened it, and can be set so it is never left unlocked overnight. The lock is only part of it: a steel door, a reinforced frame and strike, and a door contact tied to your alarm do as much as the keypad. If the cellar is a workspace where staff are sometimes alone, the inside must open in one motion without a key or code, whatever is on the outside.

If the cellar is reached through sidewalk doors rather than an ordinary door, a smart lock is usually the wrong tool; those hatches are better served by heavy hasps, a contact sensor and a camera. We will tell you which you have before quoting.

Our shop’s steel back door has a push bar on the inside — can a keypad go on the outside without taking the bar off?

Yes, and the push bar should stay exactly where it is. A panic bar or exit device is the hardware that lets everyone inside leave with a single push, which is the behaviour the Building Code expects of an egress door; nothing we add should change that. What changes is the outside. Most exit devices are sold with a choice of outside trims — a blank plate, a pull, a key cylinder, or a lever that can be locked and unlocked electrically. Fitting keyless entry means adding the right outside trim, or an electric strike in the frame, so the door opens from the alley for a valid code while the bar on the inside works as before.

The details depend on the bar. A rim device that latches into a strike on the frame can usually take an electric strike made for that device, wired to a keypad on the wall. A bar with rods running to the top and bottom of the door usually needs an electrified trim or a kit from the bar’s own maker, because a strike only controls one latch point. Either way we read the make and model off the device before ordering anything, because trims and strikes are not interchangeable between brands.

On East Tremont Avenue these back doors open onto yards and service alleys that nobody sees at night, so we pair the keypad with a door contact that tells your alarm, or your phone, whenever the door is left open after closing. Staff each get a code; the bar never needs one.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Throgs Neck

We couldn’t capture Google’s AI Overview from our build environment, so instead of inventing what it says, here are the claims that national cost guides, retailers and lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr repeat most often — and what they leave out for a house on this peninsula.

“It’s a 30-minute screwdriver job”

On a modern door with a standard bore, a square frame and a strike that already lines up, swapping a deadbolt for a smart one can be quick. That is the case the box describes. National guides price it that way too: LawnStarter’s national figures put labor at $50 to $110.

Throgs Neck doors are rarely that door. Cottage doors that started life on a summer bungalow can be thinner or softer than modern entry doors. Frames on fill and old stilt foundations drift. Many doors carry the holes of two or three earlier locks, and a storm door sits a few inches in front of the whole assembly.

The time goes into making the door right for the lock: reinforcing a soft edge, re-setting a strike, adjusting a storm-door closer, deciding where a bridge lives. That is why we price house doors flat after seeing them, rather than quoting a national labor figure that assumes none of it.

“Smart locks are weatherproof”

They are weather-resistant, which is not the same thing. Yale, for example, gives its Smart Keypad an IP55 rating, which means protected against dust and against jets of water — a good rating for rain, not a promise about standing water.

Edgewater Park has roughly 278 homes in the 1% annual chance floodplain, some in the V Zone where surge comes with waves. A lock on a low front door there may see salt water at the height of its circuit board, and no residential smart lock we install is built for that.

Weather resistance also fades with salt. Gaskets dry out, the finish pits, and contacts corrode. On waterfront doors we look at finish, gasket fit and mounting height, and we tell owners plainly to plan on replacing the electronics after a surge.

“Set up the Wi-Fi bridge anywhere your router reaches”

The router is the easy half. The hard half is the lock-to-bridge hop, which is usually Bluetooth and short. August’s own instruction is to keep its Connect bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock.

In a narrow Throgs Neck house the router often sits in a back room, and the bridge gets plugged in next to it because that is where the outlet is. The lock then works when you test it on day one and drops offline in humid weather or when the neighbors’ networks get busy in the evening.

We place the bridge or border router inside, near the door, then check the bridge’s own path back to the router, and we test from mobile data outside. If no outlet is close, we say so and suggest a lock whose radio suits the house.

“Fingerprint is the easiest way in for the whole family”

For an adult coming home from work with dry hands, it often is. For a family that spends summer between the house and the beach, fingerprint readers misread wet, sandy fingers, and manufacturers’ own support pages tell users to clean and dry their fingers first.

Older relatives can have fainter ridges, and a reader that fails three times in a row teaches people to stop trusting the lock and leave the door on the latch.

We set fingerprint locks up as one credential among several: fingerprints for those whose prints read well, a code for everyone, a fob for small children, and a key kept somewhere sensible.

“Auto-lock makes every door safer”

Auto-lock is excellent on a front door that is always pulled shut behind you. It is less helpful on a door that doesn’t close reliably — for example one behind a storm door that the wind keeps nudging, or a side door that sticks in humid weather.

A deadbolt that throws while the door is not fully closed hits the frame, jams, and in many locks then reports a fault and stops trying. You come home to a door that looks locked and isn’t.

Before turning auto-lock on, we make sure the door closes and latches by itself every time, then set a delay long enough for real life: a minute for a door you carry beach chairs through, less for the front door. Locks that can sense whether the door is closed are worth the extra cost on a door that the wind plays with.

“If you own the house, you can change any lock you like”

On most of the peninsula, yes. In Silver Beach, where owners hold their bungalows under proprietary leases that have required the corporation’s permission for changes, and in Edgewater Park, where the cooperative enforces its own building guidelines, owning the house is not the whole story.

Nobody we know of has written a rule about smart locks in either community. But generic advice that never mentions the cooperative leaves owners to find out the hard way that someone else had a say.

We ask where the house is before we suggest anything, offer a lock that reuses the existing bore where that helps, and give you a written scope to show the office if you choose to.

“A smart lock stops break-ins”

A smart lock controls who has a way in. It does nothing on its own about a door being forced. In November 2025 News 12 reported that teenagers kicked in the front door of a Throgs Neck home; the weak point in a kick is the frame and the strike, whatever is on the door.

Some retail listings imply that an app, an alert or a camera link is the security. Alerts are useful, but by the time one arrives the door is already open.

Every lock we fit gets a strike secured with long screws into the framing and a fully extending bolt, and we will point out a split or soft frame rather than hang an expensive lock on it.

Porch, Side Passage, Basement: Every Door of a Throgs Neck House

A Throgs Neck house usually has more doors than its size suggests, because cottages grew porches, two-families grew separate entrances, and basements gained their own way in. Before recommending a single lock we walk all of them, because the door that most needs a smart lock is often not the one with the house number on it.

The front door, and the porch in front of it

Many bungalows had their open porches enclosed long ago, so the original front door now sits inside a glazed or sided porch, and the porch door is what faces the lane. That leaves two doors in a row with two different jobs. The outer porch door keeps weather and parcels in; the inner door is the real security line, because the porch walls are usually lightweight. We generally put the smart lock on the inner door, keep a simple keyed lock on the porch door, and make sure both can be opened from inside in one motion. Where the porch door is the only one that latches reliably, we fix the inner door first.

Edgewater Park’s cooperative changed its bylaws after the city’s Resilient Neighborhoods work so that covered porches and stair turns could be built when homes are elevated. If your house is likely to be lifted one day, the front door you have now may not be the front door you have in five years; a lock that reuses the existing bore moves to the new door cleanly.

The side door in the passage

With side yards measured by city planners at two to five feet in Edgewater Park, and not much more on many attached-house blocks inland, side doors open into slots where you can touch both walls. They are often the household’s everyday door and the one most likely to be left on the latch. A keypad there has to be visible to you and not to the neighbor’s window; the bolt has to throw against a frame that may never have been reinforced; and the inside must open with one simple motion because this is frequently the second exit from the kitchen.

The basement door

The state’s 1983 description of Silver Beach noted that its former summer bungalows had long since gained basements, and the city’s Edgewater Park report found a considerable number of homes there have them too. Basement doors under a stoop or down a few outside steps are usually solid but neglected, and in the floodplain they are the first opening to take water. We advise a keyed lock rather than an electronic one on a basement door below the expected flood level, and a smart lock only where the door sits high enough and someone actually uses it. If anyone sleeps or works down there, the inside always opens without a key.

The rear door to the yard

Rear doors in Throgs Neck open onto small yards, bulkheads or the back of the next lane. They get less weather than the front and less foot traffic than the side, which makes them a good place for a lock with auto-lock and an open-door sensor: the door you walk out of to hang washing should lock itself when you come back in.

The second entrance of a two-family

Where the upstairs flat has its own street door, it gets its own lock and its own codes, administered by the owner with the tenant holding a key. Where both households share one front door and split at a hall inside, the shared door gets codes for each household and the unit doors stay under each household’s control. City law requires the owner of a rented unit to provide a key lock on the unit’s entrance door and at least one key; a code-only lock on a tenant’s door does not meet that on its own.

Flood Zone

Smart Locks in the Edgewater Park Floodplain: Height, Backup and Recovery

The Department of City Planning’s Resilient Neighborhoods study describes Edgewater Park as exposed to coastal storm surge, wave action and high flood elevations, with most homes built before floodplain rules existed and many still below today’s design flood elevation. City Limits reported in 2019 that only one Edgewater Park property had obtained permits to elevate since Hurricane Sandy. In practical terms that means most of the 278 floodplain homes still have their front doors where they always were, close to grade, a short walk from the Sound. Here is how that shapes a lock.

  • Know your zone before you buy. The city’s figures put just under 100 Edgewater Park homes in the Coastal A Zone and 167 in the A Zone, with others in the V Zone. If you don’t know which yours is, the city’s flood maps will tell you; we ask, because it decides how much money it makes sense to put on a low door.
  • Keep the electronics high. On a door that has taken water before, the circuit board and batteries are worth more above the old waterline than below it. On many locks the keypad sits higher than the deadbolt’s centre line; a lever-set with an integrated keypad puts the electronics at handle height, lower than you might expect.
  • Always a mechanical backup. Every lock we fit on a floodplain door has a working key cylinder, and the key lives somewhere that won’t flood with the house — with a relative inland, or upstairs.
  • A second door for storm days. When a surge is forecast the front door may be shielded or simply not used. The door you will use that day, often an upstairs or side door, should have a code that works.
  • After the water. Take the batteries out, don’t power anything up wet, photograph before removal, and plan on new electronics. A lock that has been under salt water is not one to trust with the house, however well it seems to work the next morning.
  • Elevating later. If the house will be raised, the entry will move up a stair, possibly with a turn and a covered porch. A lock that fits a standard bore moves with the door; we leave the old lock with you so the original door can go back to keys if it is ever reused.

Two Households, One House

Running Codes for Owners, Tenants and Relatives Under One Throgs Neck Roof

The Furman Center’s figures for the Throgs Neck/Co-op City district show a little under half of households owning their home and about one resident in five aged 65 or older. On the peninsula that often means an owner on one floor, a tenant or grown child on another, and a parent who needs a carer to get in.

  • One admin, one backup admin. The owner holds the master account; a second family member gets admin rights too, so the house never depends on one phone or one person.
  • A code per person, not per household. When a carer changes or a relative moves away, you delete one code and nobody else has to learn a new number.
  • Tenants keep a key. Codes are a convenience on top of the key the law expects a tenant to have for their own entrance, not a replacement for it.
  • Schedules for helpers. Cleaners, aides and contractors get codes that work only on their days and hours; many apps send the owner a note each time one is used.
  • Logs used fairly. An owner can see when each code opened a shared door. We set the system to record what you need for the house and suggest you tell tenants what is logged.
  • A paper list. At handover we leave a written list of every code, every admin and every key, kept inside the house rather than on the fridge by the door.

For the building’s shared street door in a three-family house, which under the Multiple Dwelling Law is a multiple dwelling, a keypad with electric strike or an intercom upgrade may suit better than a residential smart lock; our Throggs Neck intercom installation page covers that side.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Peninsula Door Before Choosing a Lock

How our installers size up a Throgs Neck door — our general working method, not an account of any one job

We start with the ground, not the door. Which side of the Cross Bronx Expressway are we on, is this a co-op lane or a city street, how far is the water? That tells us whether to ask about permission, how hard the weather will be on the hardware, and whether to talk about flood height at all.

Then the storm door. We open it and let it go. If it slams, the closer gets fixed before anything else, because a new lock behind a slamming storm door is a cracked keypad in a season.

Then the swing test on the entry door: open it, let it go, watch it latch. On houses that have settled on old fill, the strike is often a few millimetres off; a person with a key compensates without noticing, but a motor doesn’t.

Then the phone. We stand at the door and count the Wi-Fi networks we can see. In a bungalow lane that number can be high, and it decides between a Wi-Fi lock, a hub-based lock and a lock that never touches Wi-Fi.

Last, the people: who uses which door, who needs a key by law, who needs a code by Tuesday, and who has to stop having one.

FAQ

Throgs Neck Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Is a Throgs Neck job priced any differently from the rest of the Bronx?
+

No. There is no peninsula or travel supplement: Throgs Neck jobs use the Bronx figures, which match the Brooklyn base. For a shop or entrance door that means $1,500 to $2,500 installed for a keypad or fob reader with electric strike, or $250 to $450 for a new strike alone. House doors get a flat written price once we have seen them.

If a code leaks while we are out at work, can we change it from the phone?
+

Yes, on any lock with Wi-Fi, a bridge or a hub. Delete or edit the code in the app and the lock stops accepting it within moments. Bluetooth-only locks need a phone near the door to make the change, so if you travel or work far from the peninsula, choose a lock with remote access or ask us to add a bridge.

If a coastal storm is forecast for our appointment day, will you reschedule?
+

Yes. If a storm watch or warning covers the peninsula on your appointment day we will call to move the visit to the next open window. If the storm damages your door, call us and we will come out to secure it once it is safe to travel.

Will drilling our door disturb the family on the other side of the party wall?
+

Only briefly. The drilling is through your door, not the shared wall, and boring a deadbolt hole or a strike pocket takes minutes. Most of the visit is quiet work: fitting, adjusting and programming. If your neighbors have a baby or a night-shift worker, tell us and we will plan the loud few minutes around them.

Our front door has a dog flap cut into it. Does that weaken a smart lock?
+

It can. A large pet flap near the lock can let someone reach through and turn the inside thumb-turn, which no smart lock prevents. We check the distance from the flap to the lock; if it is within arm reach we suggest moving the flap, fitting a smaller one lower down, or using a lock whose interior turn has a guard.

How many physical keys do we end up with, and where should they go?
+

Most keyed smart deadbolts come with two keys. We hand them over labelled, suggest one stays inside the house and one with a trusted person off the peninsula, and recommend against keeping one in the car. In a two-family house the tenant also gets a key to any lock on their own entrance.

Do you fix smart locks that a handyman or another company installed?
+

Yes. We diagnose and repair locks we did not fit, including ones nobody in the house can administer any more. Often the fault is the door rather than the lock, such as a strike that has drifted, so we check alignment, batteries, cables and the account before replacing anything.

Will you help register the lock’s manufacturer warranty in our name?
+

Yes. For a lock we supply we can complete the maker’s online registration with you at handover, or show you where to do it, so the manufacturer has the purchase date on file. Our own parts terms for AESS-supplied products are set out in the service agreement.
