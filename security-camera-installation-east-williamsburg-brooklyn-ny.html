The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Security Camera Installation

East Williamsburg is still an industrial district that people have moved into, and almost every camera problem here comes from that overlap. The area was laid out in 1835 as the Third District of the Village of Williamsburgh, which then reached east to Bushwick Avenue and Flushing Avenue, and it joined the City of Brooklyn in 1854. Dutch landowners left their names on Devoe, Meserole and Vandervoort. By the 1920s, CityNeighborhoods notes, this was one of the densest industrial zones in New York, packed with low brick factories, corrugated-steel warehouses, rail spurs and heavy-timber or reinforced-concrete lofts. The eastern half became the East Williamsburg In-Place Industrial Park, one of eight such parks in the city, roughly bounded by Newtown Creek, the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway and Flushing Avenue, with a business group now called Evergreen working with its companies since 1982. Corcoran describes the neighborhood as split by Morgan Avenue: the eastern side, out to Newtown Creek, zoned for industry, and commercial and residential blocks to the west.

The split is not as clean on the ground. In the 1990s, owners of empty factories around Bogart Street and the Morgan Avenue L station turned them into lofts, and Untapped Cities describes the McKibbin Street buildings at 248 and 255 McKibbin as "art dorms" that came to house roughly 400 people. A 2003 study cited by City Limits found that about 500,000 square feet of industrial space in the area had been illegally converted to homes in only a few years. Some of those buildings later entered the state Loft Law process; others became creative offices, like the Paper Mill at 456 Johnson Avenue, built in 1931, and the warehouse at 215 Moore Street. Netflix opened a 170,000-square-foot studio with six sound stages at 333 Johnson Avenue in 2021. Brooklyn Steel holds 1,800 people in a former steel fabrication plant on Frost Street, and in November 2026 Pacha New York opened on the old Brooklyn Mirage site at 140 Stewart Avenue. Meanwhile the warehouses on Morgan Avenue, Varick Avenue and Cook Street keep shipping food, signs and building supplies. A single block can hold all of it, and each kind of tenant needs cameras for different reasons.

The reported crime here follows the buildings rather than the residents. When burglaries in the 90th Precinct rose 31 percent in the first half of 2015, to nearly 170, DNAinfo reported that commercial burglaries drove the increase while residential burglaries were falling. Police said burglars entered businesses from the roof overnight or broke the gates and locks that owners found damaged in the morning, and Graham Avenue was one of the corridors hit. Brooklyn Paper reported four burglaries at 35 Meadow Street, a three-story converted factory a block from English Kills, probably over a weekend when the workers had gone home; there was no forced entry and no security system, and the deputy inspector called the building's security very weak. During Bushwick Open Studios in 2012, five larcenies and a robbery were reported in and around 56 Bogart Street, from a phone at a front desk to a ring taken from a jewelry studio upstairs. In May 2026, News12 reported that thieves took generators from two food trucks outside 599 Johnson Avenue about ten minutes apart, and police released surveillance images to find them. Empty weekends, shared doors, roofs that connect and valuable things left in studios and vehicles are the pattern, and that is what a camera plan here has to answer.

The same sources also show what makes a camera useful rather than decorative. The officers who described the 2015 rise gave specific advice: ceiling cameras are easy to beat with a hooded sweatshirt, so put a camera low and angle it up, and leave lights on, because infrared night vision distorts faces. Brooklyn Paper noted that at one Bogart Street gallery, a landlord added cameras after a laptop theft and they reportedly scared the thief off when he came back months later. And the city now lets businesses choose to share their camera feeds with their nearest precinct, under a pilot announced in May 2024. A camera that sees the roof hatch, catches a face under a hood, records in color under a working light and can export a clip quickly does far more than a dome on the ceiling of a dark loading bay. We found no source ranking East Williamsburg blocks by crime and we do not offer one; the 90th Precinct, which DNAinfo described as covering South and East Williamsburg between Metropolitan and Maspeth avenues to the north and Flushing Avenue to the south, has its station at 211 Union Avenue.

East Williamsburg facts that shape a camera plan

  • Businesses, not homes, drove the 2015 rise: nearly 170 burglaries in the 90th Precinct by July 5, 2015, up 31 percent, mostly commercial, with entry from rooftops and through broken gates and locks, per DNAinfo.
  • Police camera advice: mount covert cameras low and angled upward, and keep lights on rather than relying on infrared, from the same 90th Precinct briefing.
  • Weekends are the gap: the 35 Meadow Street burglaries likely happened after workers left for the weekend, in a building with no security system.
  • Studios hold valuables: laptops, phones, wallets and jewelry were taken during open studios at 56 Bogart Street in 2012.
  • Vehicles are targets too: two food-truck generators worth about $2,500 each were taken outside 599 Johnson Avenue on May 2, 2026.
  • Big venues bring big crowds: Pacha's plan for 140 Stewart Avenue lists more than 200 cameras and a capacity of 7,600, down from 9,600 at the Brooklyn Mirage, according to its Community Board 1 presentation.

What East Williamsburg owners often add next. Once the roof, the gate and the street door are covered, loft owners usually ask whether the cameras can be tied to the door buzzer so a resident sees who is ringing, and businesses ask whether a card reader on the freight door could log who opened it. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an intercom or access control upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the entrance, so the ceilings and walls only get opened once.

Many East Williamsburg buildings have more than one kind of occupant under one roof. A former factory on Bogart Street may have rental lofts upstairs and a fabricator on the ground floor. A Morgan Avenue warehouse may be split into six small industrial units with one shared loading dock. A building that was illegally converted in the 1990s may now be part of the Loft Law process, with tenants who have legal protections and an owner who has a list of code work to finish. Loft Law buildings: cameras that fit the legalization work

The Loft Law, Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law, has been on the books since 1982. It treats converted buildings like the McKibbin Street Lofts as interim multiple dwellings and requires owners to bring them up to code, and the Loft Board has even revoked registration for basement units in one McKibbin building. A camera job in a building like that should not get in the way of the code work. We keep cameras and cable off exit paths, seal every opening through a rated wall or floor with rated firestop, and route cable so it does not have to be torn out when a corridor is rebuilt. Where the owner already has architect's drawings for the legalization, we ask for them and place the recorder and cable where the plans leave room, rather than in a closet that is about to become a stair enclosure.

Shared studio floors: one camera in your space, not the hall

Studio buildings around Bogart, Thames, Ingraham and Moore streets are often run by one master tenant who sublets dozens of small rooms. A tenant who rents one of those rooms can usually have a camera inside it, aimed at their own door and their own work, on their own account. The shared corridor, the kitchen, the freight elevator and the roof are for the building or the master tenant to decide. We are glad to put the corridor and roof cameras in for whoever controls them, and to give each studio holder a view of only the stretch of corridor outside their own door if the building agrees, but we will not hang a tenant's camera in a shared hall without that yes.

Multi-unit warehouses: who owns the dock and the roof

The bigger warehouses on Morgan Avenue and Varick Avenue are sometimes leased whole, like the 22,100-square-foot building at 154 Morgan Avenue that Urban Umbrella took in 2023, and sometimes split into several small bays. In a split building, each tenant controls its own bay and roll-up door, while the dock, the yard gate and the roof usually stay with the landlord. Since the roof is where the 2015 burglaries came in, we recommend the landlord cover the roof and dock and the tenants cover their own bays, and we can put both on separate recorders or on one recorder with separate accounts, whichever the lease and the people involved prefer.

Avenue walk-ups: the shop below, the apartments above

The three- and four-story tenements on Graham Avenue, Grand Street and the side streets usually have a store at street level with its own lease and a separate door for the apartments. The store controls its own space and the inside of its gate. The landlord controls the facade, the apartment entrance and the stairs. We put the store's cameras on the store's recorder and the building's cameras on the landlord's, and we make sure the store's sidewalk view does not double as a camera on the apartment door without the landlord's agreement, because tenants reasonably want to know who is watching their entrance.

Roofs, Gates and Hoods

Planning East Williamsburg Cameras Around How Break-Ins Here Actually Happen: Over the Roof, Through the Gate, and Under a Hood

Most camera plans start at the front door and work outward. In East Williamsburg we start from the other direction, because the sources tell us how people got into businesses here, and the front door was rarely the way. In 2015 the 90th Precinct described burglars coming in from rooftops overnight and breaking gates and locks. At 35 Meadow Street the door was probably jimmied with no sign of force. At the open studios on Bogart Street, things went missing from inside a building full of visitors. On Johnson Avenue in 2026, the targets were generators on parked trucks. Each of those needs a camera in a different place, with a different job, and none of them is the camera most people buy first.

The roof: hatches, bulkhead doors and the next building over

Rows of low industrial buildings and lofts here often share party walls and sit at similar heights, so someone who gets onto one roof can sometimes walk to several. A roof hatch, a bulkhead door or a skylight is then the way in, and inside a warehouse or a loft the hatch usually opens straight into a stair or a top-floor corridor. We put a camera inside the building looking up at the hatch or at the bulkhead door from the stair below, where it sees a face coming down and cannot be reached from the roof. Where the owner wants the roof itself watched, a camera under the bulkhead roof or on a parapet bracket covers the approach, and we keep it off neighbors' roof decks and windows. A hatch camera costs little compared with the stock or equipment below it, and it is often the one camera nobody thought to ask for.

Roll-up gates and storefront grilles: the camera behind the steel

A camera mounted outside above a store's roll-up gate looks at the gate, and a person cutting or prying it will have their back turned and, often, their hood up. On Graham Avenue and Grand Street we usually pair two cameras: one outside, set to see the sidewalk approach and the gate from an angle so it catches faces walking up, and one inside behind the glass, low and aimed at the gate from within, so if the gate comes up the first thing in the picture is a face at eye level. On industrial roll-up doors we do the same thing with the yard: an outside camera along the building face toward the door, and an inside camera on the door from the bay. With the gate down at night, the inside camera is the one that cannot be blinded with spray paint.

Hoods, hats and high cameras: why the face camera goes lower

The officers' advice in 2015 was direct: a camera on the ceiling is easy to defeat with a hooded sweatshirt, so mount it low and angle it upward. That is why the face camera at a door or counter in our East Williamsburg plans usually sits lower than people expect, at head height or below, often beside the door frame or under the counter lip, in a vandal-resistant housing. It does not replace the overview camera up high, which shows what someone did and where they went; it adds the one picture that shows who they were. We set both cameras to the same clock so an officer can line up the overview and the face shot in seconds.

Lights on, not infrared: getting color at night

Police also warned that infrared night vision distorts faces. In a dark loading bay, under a gate or on a side street with weak streetlights, an infrared camera turns everything gray and can flatten a face into a white shape. We prefer to light the area properly and use cameras that keep color in low light, so a jacket, a pickup truck or a hat stays the right color. Where the owner can leave a light on over the gate or the dock, we set the camera for that light; where that is not possible, we choose a camera with its own white light that comes on only when someone is there, and we aim it away from apartment windows across the street.

Vehicles at the curb: trucks, vans and food carts

In the Johnson Avenue case, the thieves went from one truck to the next in about ten minutes and left in a white pickup, and police had stills to work from. For a business that parks trucks, vans or food carts outside, we aim one camera along the curb where the vehicles sit, zoomed so a person at the side of a truck fills a good part of the frame, and a second at the corner or driveway where a getaway vehicle would pull out, to catch its shape, color and, in good light, its plate. The second camera often matters more to investigators, because it records the direction people left.

How each building type changes the camera work

The honest answer to most camera questions here is that it depends on what the building is made of, so the walk-through always includes looking up at the ceiling, knocking on walls and finding out where the old freight elevator, the stair and the roof hatch are. The notes below are what we look for in each type.

Heavy-timber lofts: follow the beams and protect the wood

CityNeighborhoods notes that many of the neighborhood's 1920s lofts were built of heavy timber or reinforced concrete, and loft buildings like 59 Bogart Street and 315 Seigel Street, each listed as built in 1931 with 51 units on three floors, come from that era. In a timber building, the cleanest cable route is usually along the side of a beam in a small metal conduit or raceway, painted to match if the owner wants, rather than fished through walls that may be original brick on one side and new drywall on the other. Camera mounts go into solid timber with the right screws, never into a decayed end of a beam, and we stay away from sprinkler pipes that often run along the same beams. Long corridors in these buildings need cameras that can see 100 feet or more without losing faces, so we usually use two cameras per corridor, one at each end, rather than one wide camera in the middle.

Concrete lofts and factories: drill once, in the right place

Reinforced-concrete buildings, from the Paper Mill at 456 Johnson Avenue to the McKibbin Street buildings, have flat slab ceilings and thick columns. Concrete gives a rock-solid mount, but every hole costs time and noise, and some slabs carry reinforcing that should not be cut. We plan every anchor and every opening with the owner first, use existing openings and pipe chases where they exist, and run cable on the surface in conduit where a building has no drop ceiling. Wi-Fi cameras rarely work well through concrete floors, which is one of the reasons we wire cameras in these buildings rather than relying on wireless.

Steel warehouses: height, distance and weather

The industrial sheds east of Morgan Avenue are tall and wide. A camera at the eave of a 30-foot wall looks down on the tops of heads, so we mount the overview cameras high and the face cameras at the doors low, as police advised. Runs from a far corner of a yard or the back of a long building to the recorder can pass the distance a normal network cable carries, so we add a switch partway or use a fiber link. Exterior cameras go in weather-rated housings, cable enters the building through sealed openings, and where a building is in a flood-prone spot, such as 200 Morgan Avenue, which StreetEasy lists in Hurricane Evacuation Zone 1, we keep the recorder and switch above the floor rather than in a cellar.

Walk-up tenements and stores: cellar routes and separate systems

On Graham Avenue and Grand Street, the store usually has the cellar, and the cellar ceiling is the easiest route from the front of the building to the back. For the store's cameras, that is ideal. For the landlord's building cameras on the apartment door and stairs, we often run up through the stair hall instead, so the building's cable does not depend on a commercial tenant's cellar key. Each system gets its own recorder, or one recorder with strictly separate accounts, and every opening through a floor or a rated wall is firestopped before we leave.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk an East Williamsburg Loft or Warehouse, Starting on the Roof

This describes how we approach these buildings, not one particular job. We ask to go up first. On the roof we look at the hatch or bulkhead door, any skylights, the parapets and how the roof meets the buildings on either side, because that tells us whether someone could arrive from next door and where a camera inside would see them come down. We note where the internet service arrives, since in parts of the industrial park it comes from a rooftop antenna rather than a cable in the street, and that antenna's cable is often the easiest path down. Coming down the stair, we look at each floor's corridor from end to end, count the doors and measure the length, because a long loft corridor often needs a camera at each end. We note what the ceiling is made of, timber, concrete or a new drop ceiling, where the sprinkler pipes run, and which walls are rated. On the ground floor we look at the street door, the freight door and any roll-up doors, from inside and outside, and stand where a person would stand to pry each one, to see where a low face camera would get them. We step outside and look along the sidewalk in both directions, at the corner, and at where trucks or cars park. Then we find a dry, locked spot above the cellar for the recorder, switch, modem and battery unit, and we ask who will watch which cameras: the owner, a managing agent, each business, each studio holder. By the time we leave, we know the camera count, each cable length, where the equipment lives and who sees what, and the written quote comes from that.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in East Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Four businesses rent space in our converted factory and each locks its own door, but nobody is responsible for the shared hallway and stair. Who should pay for and watch those cameras?

Usually the building owner, because the hallway, stair, street door and roof belong to the building, not to any one tenant. That is also where the 35 Meadow Street burglaries show the gap: several businesses were hit in one building with no security system, likely over a weekend, and no single tenant could have covered the shared spaces alone. We suggest the owner put the shared spaces on one recorder, give each business a view of the corridor outside its own door, and let each tenant add cameras inside its own unit on its own account. If the lease makes tenants share building costs, the owner can pass the cost through as the lease allows.

Our studio building takes part in an open-studios weekend, with the street door propped and hundreds of strangers inside. How should the cameras be set for those two days?

Treat it as a different building for the weekend. During Bushwick Open Studios in 2012, Brooklyn Paper reported five larcenies and a robbery in and around 56 Bogart Street, including a phone taken from a front desk and a ring from a jewelry studio upstairs. Before the event we check that every corridor and stair camera records continuously rather than on motion, add a temporary camera at any front desk or sign-in table, turn off phone alerts that would fire every few seconds, and make sure each studio holder knows how to pull a clip from their own camera. Studio holders should lock away laptops and small valuables, because a camera records a theft; it does not stop someone walking out with a phone.

We park two food trucks overnight on Johnson Avenue. Can one camera on our commissary wall watch both, or does each truck need its own?

One wide camera can show that something happened to both trucks, but it will rarely show who did it. In the May 2026 thefts outside 599 Johnson Avenue, the thieves took generators from two trucks about ten minutes apart and drove off in a white pickup, and police had to work from still images. We would put a zoomed camera on the side of each truck where the generator or the service window sits, and a third on the corner or driveway the trucks face, to catch the getaway vehicle's color and shape. If the trucks park on the street rather than on your lot, the cameras go on your own wall, aimed at your own curb, and we keep the view off the buildings across the road.

The landlord's new camera in our loft corridor sounds a siren every time someone walks past. Can the siren be turned off without turning off the camera?

On most cameras with a built-in siren, yes. A Brooklyn tenant described exactly this on Reddit in 2026, and one reply identified the camera as a Reolink and said the siren can be switched off in the app without affecting recording. Sirens and flashing lights make sense on a yard or loading dock after hours, not in a residential corridor where people move furniture and receive deliveries all day. If you are the tenant, the setting is the landlord's to change, so ask them or their managing agent. If you are the landlord, we can set the siren to sound only during the hours a space should be empty, or turn it off entirely, during a setup visit.

Our Grand Street store wants to join the city program that shares camera feeds with the precinct. Does that let police watch our store whenever they like?

Not as the city described it. When the pilot was announced in May 2024, News12 reported that businesses could choose to share their camera feeds with their nearest precinct and choose when to share them. What that means in practice depends on the program's current terms and on your recorder, so we would read the enrollment terms with you before you sign up. On our side, we can set up a separate account on the recorder that sees only the cameras you choose, such as the sidewalk and the door, never the stock room or the office, and that you can switch off at any time.

Pacha says it will run more than 200 cameras at 140 Stewart Avenue. Will any of that help the street outside our building on a club night?

Do not count on it. A venue's cameras are planned around its own entrances, lines, floors and frontage, and their footage belongs to the venue. Its plan also mentions post-event patrols, shuttles to the Morgan Avenue and Jefferson Street stations and better lighting on walking routes, which may help the blocks nearby. If your building is on one of those walking routes, cover your own street door, any recess or loading bay where people gather, and your own stretch of sidewalk, recording continuously on event nights. If something happens outside, a venue may have useful footage too, but whether to share it is the venue's decision.

Our warehouse has a row of skylights over the main floor. Do those need cameras of their own, or is the floor camera enough?

The floor camera is usually enough if it is placed to see the area under the skylights, rather than the skylights themselves. A camera looking straight up at a skylight is blinded by daylight much of the day and shows little at night. A camera on a column or wall, angled across the floor below the skylights, records anyone dropping in and where they go next. If the roof has a hatch or bulkhead door, that is the more likely way in, and it does get its own camera, looking up from the stair or ladder below. On a building whose roof joins its neighbors, we also suggest the owner ask about the condition of the skylight frames themselves, because a camera records a break-in but a sound frame prevents one.

Police here said infrared night vision distorts faces. Our loading dock is dark after the evening shift. Should we leave a light on all night instead?

If you can, yes. That was the 90th Precinct's own advice in 2015, and it matches what we see: under infrared, faces and clothing turn gray and flat, while under ordinary light a color camera shows a red jacket as red. A dusk-to-dawn LED fixture over the dock is usually the cheapest improvement you can make to your footage, and the camera is then set for that light. Where a light cannot stay on, for example because it would shine into apartments across a narrow street, we use a camera with a small white light that turns on only when it detects a person, which also tells the person they have been seen.

Our building lost power for hours when there was a manhole fire on Montrose Avenue. How long will the cameras keep recording in an outage like that?

That depends on the battery unit, and it is better to plan for it than to guess. Greenpointers reported that the February 2026 fire at 101 Montrose Avenue left hundreds of Williamsburg residents without power. A typical battery backup sized for a recorder, a switch and a modem will carry a small system through a short outage, not through many hours. For longer outages we size the unit to the load, put the cameras that matter most, such as the street door and the roof hatch, on the protected switch ports, and set the recorder and cameras to restart on their own and keep their settings when power returns. We test that by cutting the power before we leave.

Privacy and Permission

Lofts, Studios, Shops and Neighbors: The Privacy Rules Behind an East Williamsburg Camera Job

East Williamsburg puts a lot of people close together in buildings that were never designed for privacy. A loft corridor may pass 20 front doors. A studio floor may have rooms with glass partitions. A walk-up's front windows may look straight into a warehouse office across a narrow street. Cameras have to work around all of that, and the rules we follow are simple to state.

Common areas are the owner's to cover. New York courts have treated building hallways as places where tenants do not have a reasonable expectation of privacy. In Otero v. Houston St. Owners Corp., decided in 2012, a court dismissed tenants' privacy claims over a landlord's hallway cameras, noting that no cameras were placed in private rooms. That supports an owner covering corridors, stairs, lobbies and mailrooms. It does not make it wise to aim a corridor camera straight into one tenant's doorway, so we place corridor cameras to look along the hall, and we mask the part of the picture that would see into an open apartment.

Inside a unit is the occupant's business. A loft tenant, a studio holder or a store can generally put a camera inside their own space, aimed at their own rooms, door and property. They usually need the owner's permission to mount anything in a shared hall, on the roof or on the facade, and leases often say so. We put those cameras on the occupant's own account, separate from the building's.

Neighbors, windows and the street. Outdoor cameras here are framed on the owner's own door, gate, frontage, curb, yard and roof. Where an apartment window, a neighbor's roof deck or a stretch of someone else's yard falls in the picture, we mask it out in the camera settings. Cameras go on the owner's own building, fence or post, never on a city lamppost, tree or sign.

Who watches, and who can export. In a mixed building, the hardest question is often not where the cameras go but who sees them. We set up accounts by person and role: an owner or managing agent with full access, each business or studio with its own cameras and the corridor outside its door, a super with live views but no exports, and a separate account for police sharing only if the owner joins a program. Every account can be switched off in a minute when someone moves out, and footage leaves the recorder only when the owner chooses to export it. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, block, concrete or a rated wall is sealed: against water outside, and with rated firestop inside. In Loft Law buildings, we keep cable and equipment clear of the paths and assemblies the legalization work depends on.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in East Williamsburg

AI search summaries and cost sites such as HomeAdvisor and Angi give general answers that read well and fit almost anywhere. These are the claims we hear repeated most often, and how they hold up in East Williamsburg's buildings.

"Professional camera installation in Brooklyn costs between $680 and $2,145."

Those are HomeAdvisor's figures for Brooklyn: an average of $1,416, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and a full spread from $160 to $3,500. They are a fair picture of a few cameras on a house. They say little about a three-story timber loft with 51 units and two long corridors per floor, a warehouse with five roll-up doors and a yard, or a venue with bars, a line and a cash room.

What drives an East Williamsburg quote is distance and structure. A loft corridor that needs a camera at each end, a roof hatch three floors above the recorder, a yard gate past the reach of one cable, or a concrete slab that needs every opening drilled and sealed all add labor that a national average cannot see.

So we do not quote from an average. We price after photos or a walk-through, on our Brooklyn rate, with each camera, cable run and conduit length on its own line, and we explain which lines a smaller budget could drop first.

"Mount cameras high, out of reach, so nobody can tamper with them."

High mounting is common advice, and it is half right. A camera up high is hard to knock down and gives a good overview of a room or a yard. But the 90th Precinct told DNAinfo in 2015 that ceiling cameras are easily beaten by a hooded sweatshirt, and recommended mounting covert cameras low and angled upward instead.

In a neighborhood where most reported burglaries in that rise were commercial, at gates and roofs, a camera that only shows the top of a hood at the moment of entry wastes the most important seconds of the recording.

Our plans here pair the two: a high overview for what happened and where people went, and a low face camera at each door, gate or counter in a vandal-resistant housing. Both run on the same clock so the clips line up.

"Night vision means you do not need lighting."

Most cameras do switch to infrared in the dark, and an infrared picture is better than nothing. AI answers often stop there. Police here went further and said infrared distorts faces, and advised leaving lights on.

Docks, gates and side streets in the industrial park can be very dark after the last shift leaves, and an infrared image of a person in dark clothing at a dark gate is often just a gray shape. Color is lost, so a jacket, a truck or a hat cannot be described.

We plan lighting with the cameras. A steady light over a gate or dock, or a camera with its own white light that turns on for people, gives color footage at night. We aim both away from apartment windows across the street.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for any building."

For one room in one loft, a Wi-Fi camera can be fine. For a whole building here, wireless runs into two problems. Concrete slabs, brick party walls and steel framing block Wi-Fi between floors and across long buildings, and a corridor camera 150 feet from the router may drop out every evening.

The second problem is the internet itself. NYCEDC listed the North Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg industrial business zones among the city's broadband "digital deserts" when it launched Connect IBZ, and some buildings get service from rooftop wireless equipment. Cloud-dependent cameras lose their recordings when that link goes down.

We wire cameras to a recorder inside the building, so recording continues with no internet at all, and use the internet only for viewing from a phone and, where wanted, an off-site copy of selected clips.

"Installing camera wiring in New York City needs an electrical permit and an electrician."

That was a common answer for years and some summaries still give it. New York City changed the rule. Local Law 128 of 2024, in effect since December 21, 2025, says an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when the work is done by a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person.

Camera cable falls into that low-voltage category. The installer still needs the right state license for security work, and the building still needs every opening sealed and firestopped properly. A new 120-volt outlet for a recorder is still electrical work for a licensed electrician.

On an East Williamsburg job, that means the permit question rarely holds up the cameras, while the license, firestopping and, in Loft Law buildings, coordination with the legalization work still matter.

"Big venues with hundreds of cameras make the surrounding blocks safer for everyone."

It is easy to assume that a room like Pacha, with a plan for more than 200 cameras, or Brooklyn Steel nearby, covers the streets around it. A venue's cameras are planned for its own entrances, lines, floors and frontage, and the footage is the venue's to keep or share.

The residents who spoke at Community Board 1 in April 2026 described crowds, trash and late noise in Bushwick and East Williamsburg, and the plan answered with patrols, shuttles and lighting rather than cameras on other people's buildings.

If your building sits on a walking route between the venues and the Morgan Avenue or Jefferson Street stations, your own street door, recesses and sidewalk are still yours to cover, and recording them continuously on event nights is the part that is in your control.

"East Williamsburg is just part of Williamsburg, so the same camera package fits."

Many summaries fold East Williamsburg into Williamsburg or Bushwick, and Wikipedia notes the name has partly fallen out of use. That leads to camera advice written for brownstones and new condos.

The buildings here are different: an in-place industrial park, converted timber and concrete lofts, Loft Law buildings, studio floors sublet by the room, creative offices, music venues and walk-up tenements over shops. A package designed for a townhouse stoop does not cover a roof hatch, a roll-up gate or a long loft corridor.

We plan each building by what it is and who controls it, which is why the written quote often looks nothing like the next one on the same block.

FAQ

East Williamsburg Security Camera FAQ

A Bogart Street gallery's visible cameras reportedly scared off a thief who came back, yet police advised low covert cameras. Which approach suits our studio building?
+

Both, for different jobs. Brooklyn Paper reported that cameras a landlord added at a Bogart Street gallery after a laptop theft seemed to deter the thief when he returned, which is the value of a camera people can see. The 90th Precinct's advice in 2015 was about identification: a camera on the ceiling is easy to beat with a hood, so a low camera angled upward catches the face. In a studio building we usually put clearly visible cameras at the street door, the stair and each corridor, and add a smaller low camera beside the door frame at the most likely entry points, so the visible ones discourage and the low ones identify.

Our building's studios are sublet by the month through a master tenant. Who controls the corridor footage, the building owner or the master tenant?
+

Whoever the lease gives control of the common areas, which is something we ask both parties to confirm in writing before installing. In many sublet studio buildings the master tenant runs the floors day to day and wants the corridor cameras, while the owner keeps the roof, the street door and the facade. We can put the master tenant's corridor cameras on one account and the owner's building cameras on another, on one recorder or two. Individual studio holders get only their own camera and, if both parties agree, a view of the corridor outside their own door. Exports are limited to the accounts that control each set of cameras.

Our internet arrives from an antenna on the roof rather than a cable under the street. Can the camera cable follow the same route down?
+

Often, yes, and it is frequently the easiest path. NYCEDC's Connect IBZ program described fixed-wireless service in the North Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg industrial business zones that reaches customers from rooftop-mounted equipment, so many buildings already have a cable route from the roof to the network gear. If that route has space, the roof-hatch camera and any roof cameras can share it in their own cable, and the recorder can sit near the network equipment. We keep camera cable separate from the provider's own wiring and do not open or move the provider's equipment, which belongs to the provider.

Sprinkler pipes run along the timber beams in our loft corridors. Can the camera cable be tied to them to save time?
+

No. We never fasten camera cable to sprinkler piping or its hangers, and we keep cameras clear of sprinkler heads so the spray pattern is not blocked. Cable in a timber loft runs along the side of a beam in its own small conduit or raceway, or on its own supports, so it can be worked on later without anyone touching the sprinkler system. This takes a little longer than tying to a pipe, but it keeps the fire protection untouched, which matters in any occupied loft and especially in a building working through Loft Law code compliance.

We rent our space to production crews who sometimes ask that nothing be recorded on set. Can certain cameras be paused for a shoot without switching off the whole system?
+

Yes. Most recorders let you set a recording schedule or a privacy mask per camera, so the cameras inside the stage can be paused or blacked out for the hours of a shoot while the freight door, gear cage, loading area and street door keep recording. We set this up so that a production manager can apply it from the app for a booked period and it returns to normal by itself afterward, rather than relying on someone remembering to switch it back. The schedule is logged, so the owner can see later when recording was paused and by whom.

If our warehouse is broken into over a weekend, how fast can we hand a usable clip to the detectives on Monday?
+

Within minutes, if the system was set up for it and someone has practiced once. At handover we have the owner export a test clip while we watch, so on the day it matters they know to pick the camera, the start and end times, and save the original file to a USB stick or phone, with the recorder's own player if one is needed. The recorder clock is synced to a time server, so the times on the clip match what investigators expect. If nobody at the business is comfortable doing it, call us; we can usually walk you through it by phone or come and do it, and we never reset a recorder holding evidence.

Who at our bar should be able to see the cash-room camera, and who should not?
+

As few people as practical. We put the cash room, the safe and the office on their own camera group with their own account, usually for the owner and one manager, while bar staff and door staff see the floor, the bars and the entry on their own accounts. Exports from the cash-room cameras are limited to the owner's account. That keeps counting and deposit routines private, protects staff from suspicion when a count is off, and means the footage that matters most in a dispute can only be handled by the people responsible for the money.

Can a camera go inside the cab of our loft building's old freight elevator?
+

Usually, but not by us alone. A camera in an elevator cab needs its signal and power to travel with the cab, normally through the elevator's traveling cable or a wireless link designed for elevators, and any work inside the hoistway or on the cab is for a licensed elevator contractor. We plan the camera, the recorder connection and the account, and coordinate with the building's elevator company for the hoistway part. Where that is not possible right away, a camera at each landing door facing the elevator records who goes in and who comes out at every floor.

Many residents in our concrete loft building work from home. Can the drilling be kept to certain hours?
+

Yes. Drilling into concrete slabs and columns is the loud part, and it is a small share of the job. We map every anchor and opening at the walk-through, then schedule the drilling in blocks at times the owner agrees with residents, such as late morning, and do the quieter work of pulling cable, mounting cameras and setting up the recorder around them. Residents get notice of which floors will be noisy on which days. Where the building has existing openings or pipe chases we can use, the amount of drilling drops further.

A 14-story light manufacturing and office building has been proposed at 410 Morgan Avenue. If we sign a lease in a building still under construction, when should we raise cameras?
+

Before the ceilings in your space close. In a new building, the cheapest time to run camera cable is while framing and ceilings are open, so ask the landlord early what the building will provide at the shared doors and loading areas, and ask for a pathway and a power circuit for your own recorder in your space. If you send us the tenant drawings, we can mark camera locations and cable routes so your fit-out contractor leaves room, and come back to mount cameras once the finishes are in. Trying to add the same cameras after move-in usually means opening finished ceilings.

Williamsburg Houses converted to private management under the city's PACT program. Who should residents ask about cameras in their halls and grounds?
+

The development's current property manager. Williamsburg Houses, a city landmark since 2003, has been under a RAD/PACT conversion since December 2021, which moves day-to-day management to a private partner while the buildings remain public housing. Cameras on its doors, halls and grounds are decisions for that management, and any exterior change on a landmarked building also has to fit landmark rules. A resident's own options are inside their apartment, aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door, and we can help with that. For building cameras, residents usually do best raising it through their tenant association.

Our loft corridors use motion lights that go dark between people. Will the corridor cameras miss someone walking in the dark stretch?
+

They might catch the person but lose the face, which is the usual problem. When a motion light snaps on, a camera needs a second or two to adjust from infrared to color, and those first seconds are often when someone comes through the door. We usually recommend keeping a low-level light on in the corridor, or setting the motion lights to dim rather than switch off, so the cameras always have something to work with. Where the lighting cannot change, we choose cameras that handle low light well, place them so a person walks toward them, and test the corridor at night before we leave.
