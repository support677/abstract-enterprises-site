The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need Access Control

Bushwick is Brooklyn Community District 4, the 1.83 square miles that Wikipedia bounds by Flushing Avenue, Broadway, the Queens line and the Cemetery of the Evergreens, spread across the 11206, 11207, 11221 and 11237 ZIP codes and policed by the 83rd Precinct from 480 Knickerbocker Avenue. Community Board 4’s own history of the neighborhood describes its housing in a few lines: two- to six-family houses, large apartment houses from the 1920s, and the mansions that brewers and doctors built on Bushwick Avenue between about 1880 and 1913. Add the factories near Morgan Avenue that became lofts and studios from the 1990s on, and the stores under the elevated tracks on Myrtle Avenue and Broadway, and you have every kind of door we install on, often on a single block.

What those doors have in common is that many of them have not been looked after. When the Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development compared the city’s neighborhoods, it found Bushwick’s buildings carried more serious housing-code violations on average than any other, with 284.5 immediately hazardous violations for every 1,000 apartments; the next neighborhood, Sunset Park, had 191. Not all of those are doors, and the study does not break them out. But they describe a housing stock where repairs wait, and a street door is one of the repairs that tenants feel first.

The local reporting we checked is almost entirely about doors that failed. In April 2022, at about 6:25 in the morning, a man forced the front door of an apartment building at 10 Goodwin Place with tools and pried open several mailboxes inside, 1010 WINS reported. In September 2019 a thief opened the gate beside the stoop at 11A Eldert Street, walked into the gated area where about five packages were lying on the ground, and left with three of them, about $2,200 worth of clothing, according to Patch. In December 2022 News 12 aired a Bushwick tenant’s complaint that her building’s doors would not latch: “There is no protection.” And a former tenant of 273 Linden Street wrote on a building review site that the front door “lock has been broken for 3 years now and still hasn’t been fixed,” that packages were being stolen, and that the second door was locked while the first stood open, so anyone could walk into the vestibule.

Bushwick’s burglary history points at the same place. In 2015 the 83rd Precinct had 457 burglaries, the most of any precinct in the city and about 50 more than East New York, Brooklyn Magazine reported from NYPD figures. The precinct’s commanding officer at the time, Maximo Tolentino, said many of them involved no forced entry at all, and put it bluntly: “you just can’t assume that every neighbor is going to be neighborly.” A door that relocks itself every time it closes, whoever forgot to turn the key, is the most direct answer to a burglary that needed no tools.

The shops have their own version of the story. Over a few weeks in December 2020 and January 2021, BKLYNER and News 12 reported a string of break-ins at Bushwick businesses: at Chess and the Sphinx on Knickerbocker Avenue the burglar cut the gate and “slithered through,” at Foster Sundry the security gate was pried back far enough to squeeze in, and at Risk Gallery & Boutique the front glass was smashed with bricks and rocks. A roll-down gate that nobody knows is being attacked until the morning is a door problem, too.

None of this calls for fear, and we do not sell with it. It calls for doors that close and latch on their own, credentials that can be cancelled one at a time instead of keys that travel for decades, and a record of which door opened and when. It also calls for care, because in Bushwick the person who wants the system and the people who live behind the door are often different people. A landlord replacing keys with fobs in a rent-stabilized six-family is bound by the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law and by the conditions state housing regulators have set in fob cases, and we plan around both from the first visit.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Bushwick’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware, and Bushwick has seven kinds that come up again and again. Below is what each one usually needs at the door, and why.

Three-story six-family walk-ups

Brick buildings, typically with two apartments a floor, and a street door that often opens straight onto the stairs. One controlled street door, a cellar door on the same system, and the mailboxes moved or protected.

1920s apartment houses

Larger brick buildings with a lobby, an entry vestibule and sometimes a courtyard or a rear yard door. Two vestibule doors, a package space, cellar and roof rules, and a video panel that replaces a long row of buzzer buttons.

Frame two- and three-family houses

Early wood-frame houses, many now clad in siding, with a stoop or a side entrance. A sealed surface-mount station, a strike set into a solid section of a wood jamb, and separate codes for each household.

Linden Street row houses

The 32 brick and brownstone houses of the historic district, built between 1885 and 1901. Small readers and stations placed where Landmarks staff will accept them, cable through the areaway or cellar rather than the facade.

Bushwick Avenue mansions

Large brewer-era houses divided into apartments, with a grand front door, deep cellars and sometimes a carriage-house entrance at the back. A shared street door, a directory station, and each apartment kept on its own credentials.

Converted factory lofts and studio buildings

Concrete and steel buildings near Morgan Avenue with freight elevators, loading doors and dozens of separate tenants. Networked readers, floor-by-floor rules and an administrator who is not the only person who can cancel a lost fob.

Storefronts with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on the shopping streets, many under the Myrtle Avenue and Broadway els, with a shop door, a narrow residential door beside it and a shared cellar. Two separate schedules on one system, and the residential door never left on a daytime unlock.

What the materials mean for the install

Bushwick brick is mostly forgiving. We fix stations and readers into mortar joints rather than the face of a brick, so if the hardware ever changes, what is left is a joint to repoint rather than a broken brick on a hundred-year-old front. On a six-family, the brick beside the street door is often already pocked by old buzzer plates, and we reuse an existing opening where we can, so the facade ends up with fewer holes than it started with. Cable from a cellar to a street door usually goes up through the floor of the entry, inside the frame or in a slim raceway on the inside face of the wall, never across the front of the building.

The frame houses are the delicate case. A wood jamb that has been bored for four or five generations of locks does not have much wood left where a strike goes, so we fill or splice the jamb first. Siding hides the condition of the sheathing underneath; we mount a station on a flashed block, seal the single hole the cable needs, and keep the device out of the path of water coming off a stoop canopy. In the loft buildings it is the reverse problem: concrete and steel take any amount of abuse, but every hole is a drilling job, and fire-rated stair and corridor doors cannot be cut in ways that void their rating. There the lock often goes into panic hardware or the frame, using the preparation the door already has.

Two Doors, One Weak Link: Fixing the Bushwick Vestibule Before Adding Fobs

The tenant review of 273 Linden Street describes a problem we see on Bushwick buildings constantly, and it is worth spelling out. The building has two entrance doors, an outer one on the stoop and an inner one into the hall. The inner door locked. The outer door did not, and had not for three years. That sounds like half a working entrance, but in practice it is less than half: the space between the two doors is where the mailboxes are, where couriers leave parcels and where someone can wait out of sight until a resident opens the inner door. The reviewer’s plea was simple: “PLEASE FIX FRONT DOOR.”

Adding a fob reader to the inner door of a building like that changes nothing a thief cares about. So on a two-door entrance we decide, door by door, what each one is for. Usually the inner door carries the controlled lock, because it is the door between the street and the apartments, and it is the one whose frame and closer we can keep in good order. The outer door gets a working closer and latch so it shuts on its own, and a contact that tells the system when it has been standing open. Where the mailboxes and packages sit between the two doors, the outer door may get the lock instead, with the inner door on a simple latch, so that the vestibule itself becomes a protected room. Which arrangement is right depends on where the mailboxes are and who needs to reach them.

The legal side is plain. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires the entrance doors of class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to be self-closing and self-locking, and the city’s Administrative Code sections 27-2041 and 27-2043 set the lock rules for apartment entrance doors. A door that does not latch is a repair the owner owes the tenants whether or not anyone ever installs a reader. We price that repair separately and do it first, because a strike on a door that does not close true is money spent on a door that still does not lock.

The mailbox wall after the Goodwin Place burglary

The April 2022 burglary at 10 Goodwin Place, where the front door was forced with tools and several mailboxes pried open, is a reminder that the mailbox bank is a target in its own right. In many Bushwick walk-ups the boxes sit on the wall immediately inside the street door, sometimes with no vestibule at all, which puts them one forced door away from the sidewalk. When we plan a street door, we plan the mailbox wall with it. Sometimes the answer is a stronger strike and a door reinforced at the latch side; sometimes it is moving the line of control so the boxes sit behind a second locked door. A contact on the street door that reports a forced opening at 6:25 in the morning will not stop the first pry bar, but it means the owner hears about it before the tenants come down for the mail.

Mail carriers and couriers still have to reach the boxes and the package shelf. Where the boxes are behind a controlled door, the carrier’s access is arranged with the owner and the post office, and couriers can be given a time-limited code that opens only the outer door, not the inner one. The Eldert Street theft in 2019, where a thief simply opened the gate beside the stoop and took parcels lying on the ground, is the other half of the same lesson: anything left in front of the first locked door is effectively on the street.

Keys to Fobs in a Rent-Stabilized Walk-Up: What the Law and the Regulators Expect

A large part of Bushwick’s housing is rented, and a large share of the street-door jobs we are asked about are owners moving a building from metal keys to fobs or phone entry. That is a good change when it is done properly, and a source of disputes when it is not. Three sets of rules shape it.

The first is the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, which HPD summarizes on its website. An owner of a multiple dwelling with a smart access system, which includes fobs, RFID cards, phone apps and biometrics, must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, obtain express consent in writing or through the app before collecting the data the system needs, collect only the items the law lists, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell the data or use it to harass or evict a tenant. The law also says an owner cannot require a smart access system to get into a tenant’s own apartment. We hand the owner a draft policy and consent form that match the system we install, so the paperwork is ready before the first fob is issued.

The second is the conditions state housing regulators have attached when they approved a fob system in a rent-regulated building. In one DHCR ruling summarized by a landlord-tenant law publication, the agency let the owner replace metal entry keys with fobs on conditions that included metal keys still working for emergencies, entries but not departures being recorded, a security camera watching the entry system, a 48-hour battery backup, a free fob for every occupant plus up to four for guests or employees, replacements capped at $25, accommodation for tenants who cannot use electronic devices on the Sabbath, and no equipment inside the apartments. That was one ruling about one building, not a rule for every case, but it is the clearest published picture of what the regulator looked for, and we design rent-stabilized buildings to meet it.

The third is what tenants have won when an owner pushed too far. In 2019 tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville, a 700-apartment building, fought their landlord’s plan to replace key fobs with facial recognition; the Department of Buildings told them it was not prohibited but could only supplement a key-based system. In Hell’s Kitchen, tenants who sued over app-controlled smart locks reached a preliminary settlement in 2019 that required the landlord to give them physical keys. Neither case is in Bushwick, but both are why we do not install a street door in a rental building that opens only with a phone.

How we roll fobs out in an occupied building

Notice goes up before anything changes, with the privacy policy and the consent form attached. On the switchover day the old keys keep working while every household collects its fobs, and the old cylinder is kept as an emergency key lock rather than removed. Each person gets their own fob, so a lost one is cancelled without touching anyone else’s. The intercom keeps working throughout, because a building with eight or more apartments erected after 1968 must have a two-way voice intercom from the front door to each apartment, as Habitat’s summary of the Multiple Dwelling Law notes, and fobs do not replace it. Entry logs are kept short and visible only to the people who need them.

Studio Buildings Near Morgan Avenue: Open Around the Clock, Locked Every Time

North of Flushing Avenue and around Morgan Avenue, Bushwick runs into the industrial blocks that Untapped Cities described in 2012 as “Morgantown,” part of the East Williamsburg Industrial Park, where factories had been turning into lofts since the 1990s and the McKibbin Street lofts alone housed about 400 people. Brownstoner reported in 2016 on more conversions of former factories, a paper mill and a brewery into offices and studios in the same blocks. Today a building like 195 Morgan Avenue, 79,352 square feet, is marketed to artists, makers and creative businesses with “24/7 secure building access” and an intercom.

Twenty-four-hour access and a secure building are not in conflict, but they are only both true if every person who comes in at three in the morning has their own credential. A studio building usually has dozens of tenants, each with assistants, collaborators and visitors, on leases of very different lengths, behind a street door, a loading door and a freight elevator that all lead to the same corridors. Keys and shared codes do not survive that; within a year nobody knows who holds what.

The arrangement that works is a reader on each entrance to the building, a reader or keypad on each studio floor, and individual credentials with end dates that match each lease. Tenants can be given their own administrator rights for their own studio doors, so a sculptor can let in an assistant without calling the landlord, while the building owner keeps control of the street and loading doors. The loading entrance, where a steel personnel door sits beside a roll-up, gets a heavy-duty strike and a door contact, because it is the door most likely to be propped open during a delivery and forgotten.

Two rules matter more here than anywhere else in Bushwick. Stair and corridor doors in these buildings are usually fire-rated, so locks and cable have to respect the rating. And every door on a path of exit must open from the inside without a key, card or code. Under the NYC code as Lori Greene’s code commentary describes it, a magnetic lock released by a motion sensor has to unlock when the fire alarm activates, while one released by a switch in the door hardware is treated differently. We usually avoid magnetic locks on studio exits altogether and use strikes or electrified panic hardware, which keep the inside free all the time.

Linden Street’s 32 Row Houses: Entry Hardware in Bushwick’s First Historic District

On May 9, 2023, the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted unanimously to make Linden Street, between Broadway and Bushwick Avenue, Bushwick’s first historic district and the city’s 156th. Brooklyn Paper counted 32 brick and brownstone row houses built between 1885 and 1901 in Queen Anne, Neo-Grec, and Romanesque and Renaissance Revival styles. The commission drew the line carefully: the row houses are in, but the apartment buildings and an empty lot on the same block are not. The commission’s chair, Sarah Carroll, explained what designation means in a sentence: “We regulate changes to protect historic building fabric.”

For an owner of one of those 32 houses, a keypad or a door station on the front is a change to the historic fabric, and it belongs in the Landmarks application rather than after it. In practice that means choosing the smallest device that does the job, placing it where it is least visible from the street, often on the side of the stoop or inside the areaway rather than on the facade beside the door, and running cable from the cellar instead of across the stone. The original door stays; the strike goes into the frame on the hinge-opposite side, and nothing is cut into carved woodwork.

For an owner of one of the apartment buildings on the same block, the answer is different, because those buildings were left outside the district. Their entrances follow the same rules as any other Bushwick walk-up. We still recommend the same quiet hardware on a block that the city has just recognized for its architecture, but the decision is the owner’s, not the commission’s.

Storefronts behind roll-down gates, with back and cellar doors that need staff schedules and an alert when they open after closing.

Shops under the M train’s elevated structure, many in buildings with apartments upstairs. The usual job splits a shop door and a narrow residential street door so each keeps its own hours on one system.

Wyckoff Avenue

The corridor toward Myrtle–Wyckoff and the Queens line, with food, retail and the ground floors of newer buildings. Back doors to service areas and stockrooms that staff from several shifts share.

Broadway

The border street under the J and Z, hit hardest in the 1977 blackout according to Community Board 4, now storefronts, offices and new buildings. Upstairs offices whose street door must let clients in by appointment without being left unlocked.

Morgan Avenue and the industrial blocks

Fabricators, warehouses, studio buildings and event spaces in former factories. Steel personnel doors beside roll-ups, freight entrances, and buildings open to tenants at every hour.

Side-street cafes, galleries and bars

Small businesses in the ground floors of walk-ups, often run by a few people with keys. Moving those keys to individual codes, and keeping a gallery’s public hours separate from its storage room.

What the winter 2020–21 burglaries say about shop doors

The BKLYNER report on that string of break-ins is useful because it records how each burglar got in. At Chess and the Sphinx the gate surrounding the store was cut. At Foster Sundry the security gate was pried back and the burglar squeezed through. At Risk Gallery & Boutique, bricks and rocks broke the front glass. None of those is a door a reader would have stopped: the attack came through the gate or the glass.

What an access system can do on a storefront is narrower and still worth having. A contact on the roll-down gate and on the door behind it can tell the owner, at three in the morning, that the gate has moved while the shop is armed as closed, rather than leaving the discovery for the opening shift. The back and cellar doors, which are the ones a burglar inside the store would use to carry things out, can be on individual staff codes with a schedule, so they are locked outside business hours and every opening is recorded. And a camera aimed at the gate, tied to the same timeline, shows what happened in the seconds the contact reports. We are honest that a cut gate and a broken window are a job for stronger gates, glass protection and an alarm as much as for access control, and we will say which one your shop needs first.

Rules that apply to Bushwick business doors

  • Exit doors always open. The NYC Building and Fire Codes require free egress from occupied spaces. An electrically locked door on a way out has to release when power fails, and a magnetic lock needs a release that works without a key, card or code. Commercial magnetic locks hold with 600 to 1,200 pounds of force, so getting their release right is not optional.
  • Apartments above the shop. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking. A shop that shares a building with apartments cannot leave the residential door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building has apartments and the shared door uses fobs, an app or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law’s policy, consent and retention rules apply to the residential side. A shop’s own staff door is a separate matter.
  • Fire alarm tie-ins. Where a lock releases on the fire alarm, the integration should be done by someone holding the FDNY Certificate of Fitness S-98, and the release tested at least once a year with the results written down.
  • Accessible controls. Readers, keypads and intercom controls are mounted no higher than 48 inches above the floor, with clear floor space in front, and door opening force kept to five pounds, as the city’s accessibility rules expect.
  • Permits. Department of Buildings filing can come into play for work on fire-rated doors, magnetic locks or major electrical work. We tell you at the site visit whether your job is one of those, and price the filing separately.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a license from the Department of State to install or service security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Bushwick: Questions Answered

Walk-ups, vestibules and the rental side

The mailboxes in our six-family sit right inside the street door, and the boxes were pried open after a break-in. Where should the reader and the boxes end up?

Behind the same locked line. If the street door opens straight onto the stairs and the boxes are on the wall beside it, the street door is the only barrier, so it gets the strongest strike the frame will take, a reinforced latch side, and a contact that reports a forced opening. If there is room for a second door or a short vestibule, the boxes can sit inside it, with the carrier’s access arranged with the owner and the post office and couriers given codes that open only the first door.

What we avoid is a new reader on an inner door while the boxes stay in an open hallway in front of it. The burglary at 10 Goodwin Place in April 2022 went through a forced front door to the mailboxes; the plan should start from that door.

The inner of our two entrance doors locks, but the outer door has been broken for years. Does one working door satisfy the law?

Not in the way tenants need it to. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires a newer class A building’s entrance doors to be self-closing and self-locking, and a broken outer door that lets anyone into the vestibule, where the mailboxes and parcels usually are, is exactly the complaint the tenant of 273 Linden Street made in a public review. Whether an inspector writes up the outer door depends on the building and its doors, so we do not promise an outcome.

What we do is fix the outer door to close and latch, give it a contact so the system knows when it is open, and put the controlled lock on whichever door protects the mailboxes. The repair comes first and is priced on its own.

Our building has a long list of open HPD violations. Should the door violations be cleared before we spend money on fobs?

Yes. Bushwick buildings carry more serious violations on average than any neighborhood ANHD compared, and where a violation names the entrance door, the intercom or the self-closing hardware, fixing it is the owner’s obligation regardless of any upgrade. A fob reader installed on a door with an open self-closing or lock violation does not correct the violation, and a closer or latch that fails later can put the building back where it started.

Send us the violation numbers and their wording. We price the corrective work on the door first, then the access control, and give you a written record of what was done for the certificate of correction you file with HPD.

Our landlord is replacing the street door keys with fobs. Does a metal key still have to open the door?

In the published DHCR ruling we rely on, the regulator approved a key fob system on the condition that metal keys still work for entry in an emergency, along with a 48-hour battery backup. In separate cases, the Department of Buildings told Brownsville tenants in 2019 that facial recognition could only supplement a key-based system, and Hell’s Kitchen tenants who sued over app-controlled locks won a settlement giving them physical keys. Those decisions are specific to their buildings, so check your own lease and any DHCR order for yours.

On every rental building we install, the street door keeps a keyed cylinder for emergencies, and no apartment door is made to open only by app or fob.

As a tenant, what can I do if the building’s new fob system keeps locking me out and nobody fixes it?

Write to management with the dates and times it failed, and keep copies. Attorneys quoted by Brick Underground on exactly this situation said a tenant has a right to get into their apartment at all times; one put it this way: “If you’re being deprived of the ability to get into your own apartment, your lease is being breached.” They suggested a 311 complaint to HPD, asking management for another way in, such as a staffed door or a conventional lock, and, if tenants act together, an HP action in Housing Court for an order requiring 24-hour access.

We work for owners and managers, not against tenants. If your landlord calls us to repair a failing system, the first thing we restore is a reliable way in.

Houses, mansions and the historic block

Our apartment house is on the Linden Street block but was left out of the historic district. Does Landmarks review a new entry panel on our front?

Based on how the designation was drawn, no. Brooklyn Paper’s report on the May 2023 vote says the district covers only the 32 row houses on both sides of Linden Street between Broadway and Bushwick Avenue, and that the apartment buildings and an empty lot on the block were excluded. A building outside a district’s boundary is not regulated as part of it. Check the commission’s map for your lot before relying on that, because boundaries are drawn lot by lot.

Your door still has to meet the ordinary rules for a multiple dwelling. We would still choose a compact panel set into the mortar joints, since it sits beside some of the best-preserved fronts in the neighborhood.

Our Bushwick Avenue mansion was divided into five apartments. It has one grand front door and a carriage-house door at the back. Which door gets what?

The front door is the shared street door, so it gets the controlled lock, a directory station that calls each of the five apartments, and a fob for each resident. A heavy original door usually takes a strike in the frame without touching the door itself, or an electrified mortise lock if the frame cannot be cut. The carriage-house door is the one that tends to be forgotten; if residents use it for bikes or parking it gets its own reader on the same system, and if nobody does it gets a contact and stays locked.

Each apartment door inside stays on its own key or code, managed by its tenant. The controller goes in the cellar on the common circuit, never on one apartment’s meter.

Our 1920s apartment house has a lobby, a long row of buzzer buttons and a rear yard door the super uses. What does a first phase look like?

Usually three things: a video entry panel with a fob reader that replaces the button row at the street, a strike on the inner vestibule door with the outer door repaired to close, and a reader on the rear yard door so the super and contractors stop carrying a key that has been copied for decades. The cellar, roof and package doors can follow in a second phase on the same controller.

Each apartment keeps its intercom connection to the street, because tenants rely on it and the Housing Maintenance Code expects a working intercom. Older riser cable is tested before we decide whether it can be reused.

Studios, lofts and the shopping streets

Our studio building advertises 24/7 access. How is a building open around the clock without its street door standing unlocked?

By making the door locked all the time and the credential the thing that is open around the clock. Every tenant, assistant and regular collaborator gets their own fob, card or phone key, valid at any hour but only to the building entrances and their own studio floor. Visitors are let in from the studio by phone or with a code that works for a set window. The street and loading doors relock the moment they close, and a contact reports either one held open.

If the building also hosts public hours, those run as a schedule on the lobby door only, with the studio floors still on credentials.

Every studio in our converted warehouse has a different lock, and the landlord wants one system. Who ends up controlling the studio doors?

Both sides, for different doors. The owner administers the street door, the loading entrance, the freight landing and the stairs, because those are building doors. Each studio tenant can be given their own administrator account for their own studio door, so they can add an assistant or cancel a lost card without waiting for the landlord. The owner keeps a sealed override for emergencies that shows in the log whenever it is used.

Put the arrangement in the studio leases, including what happens to a tenant’s credentials on the last day of the lease.

Burglars cut or pried the roll-down gates on several Bushwick shops one winter. Can our gate report an attack before anyone reaches the glass?

It can report movement, which is usually the earliest sign. A contact on the gate’s bottom bar or its guide rail tells the system when the gate lifts or is forced while the shop is closed, and a second contact on the door behind it confirms whether anyone got further. The alert goes to the owner’s phone and, if you have a monitored alarm, to the monitoring station. A camera on the gate records the same moment.

It cannot make a gate harder to cut. If your gate is old, thin or loose in its tracks, a better gate and an alarm may do more than access control, and we will tell you so.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Bushwick

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Bushwick door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Put a fob reader on the door and the building is secure.”

A reader secures a door only if the door shuts and latches by itself. On a two-door entrance, it secures only the door it is on.

The tenant review of 273 Linden Street describes the outer door left broken for three years while the inner door locked, with packages disappearing from the space between. A reader on the inner door would have changed none of that.

We begin every Bushwick job with the doors themselves: hinges, closers, latches and both halves of the vestibule. Electronics come after the doors close.

“Most break-ins mean a forced door, so buy a stronger lock.”

Some do. The Goodwin Place burglary began with a front door forced with tools, and a stronger strike and reinforced latch side help there.

But when the 83rd Precinct led the city with 457 burglaries in 2015, its commanding officer said many involved no forced entry at all. A lock nobody remembered to turn is not made safer by being stronger.

A strike that relocks every time the door closes fixes the forgotten lock, and a contact that reports a forced door covers the other case. Bushwick doors usually need both.

“A landlord can switch a building to app-only entry.”

Quick answers often treat phone entry as a simple upgrade an owner can impose. In New York City it is not that simple.

The Tenant Data Privacy Law says an owner cannot require a smart access system to get into a tenant’s own apartment and sets rules on consent and data. A published DHCR ruling kept metal keys working for emergencies, and Hell’s Kitchen tenants won physical keys in a 2019 settlement.

We install phone entry as one option among several, never the only way through a residential street door.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a lock off the shelf. That is the price of one part, not of a working door.

On our Brooklyn list, one controlled door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500. A walk-up or studio building with several doors on one cloud-managed system is $4,000 to $15,000.

Bushwick pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for the age of the building, the neighborhood or the distance from our Troy Avenue office.

“Magnetic locks are the easy choice for any door.”

Magnetic locks are simple to mount and very strong, holding with 600 to 1,200 pounds of force on commercial models, which is why answers recommend them.

That strength is the problem on an exit. NYC code treats a magnetic lock released by a motion sensor differently from one released by a switch in the door hardware, and the sensor-released kind must unlock when the fire alarm activates. On a studio stair door or a walk-up exit, a mistake traps people.

We use strikes and electrified panic hardware on Bushwick exits wherever we can, and when a magnetic lock is the right answer, we test its releases and write the results down.

“Facial recognition is the newest, safest entry.”

Biometric readers appear in many recommendations for apartment buildings. In New York City they carry particular legal weight.

The Tenant Data Privacy Law lists biometric identifiers among the data that may be collected only if the system uses them, with consent and retention rules attached. In 2019 more than 130 Atlantic Plaza Towers residents in Brownsville opposed a plan to replace their key fobs with facial recognition.

For a Bushwick rental building, a fob or a code does the job without collecting anyone’s face. We do not recommend biometrics on residential street doors.

“Studio and warehouse buildings just need a keypad and a shared code.”

A shared code is cheap and quick, and many answers stop there for small commercial buildings.

In a converted factory near Morgan Avenue with dozens of studios, a shared code is known to every former tenant, assistant and party guest within months, and there is no way to cancel one person without changing it for everyone.

Individual credentials with lease end dates cost little more per person and are the only way a building can honestly advertise around-the-clock access and a secure door at the same time.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Let the Door Go Before Touching the Wiring

This is how we start on any Bushwick walk-up, not a story about a particular building. Before we look at a buzzer panel, a reader or a cellar, we open the street door fully and let go of it. Then we do the same with the second door of the vestibule, if there is one. A door that drifts shut and latches by itself tells us the closer, hinges and strike are sound. A door that stops an inch short, or bounces off the latch, tells us the first line on the quote will be a repair, not a device.

Then we stand in the vestibule and look at where things are: the mailboxes, the package shelf, the intercom plate, the light. That is where the reported trouble in this neighborhood happens, from pried mailboxes to parcels walking out of a gated stoop. Only after that do we go down to the cellar to find a clear wall for the controller, a circuit that belongs to the owner, and a straight run back up to the door. The device on the wall is the last decision, and on most Bushwick buildings the smallest part of the price.

FAQ

Bushwick Access Control FAQ

What does a fob reader on a Bushwick six-family’s street door cost, and what if we add the cellar door?
+

On our Brooklyn list one controlled street door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500. A keypad on the cellar door is $1,500 to $2,000 on its own, and when both doors go on one controller the job moves into the multi-door line, which starts at $4,000. A sagging street door or a dead closer is priced separately as a repair. Sales tax is added and the figure is fixed in writing after the visit.

Will the fob system record when I leave the building, or only when I come in?
+

On a street door with a reader outside and a plain handle or exit button inside, only entries are recorded, because nobody taps a fob to leave. In the DHCR fob ruling we rely on, the regulator allowed entries to be recorded but not departures. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law also limits the time and method of access to security use only. We set rental buildings up to log entries, keep the log short, and never add a reader on the inside of a residential street door.

If I drop my fob on Knickerbocker Avenue, can whoever finds it tell which building it opens?
+

Not from the fob itself, on the systems we install. A fob carries a number that means something only to your building’s controller, and in the DHCR ruling we cite the fob was not allowed to show the tenant’s name, address or photo. Report the loss to the super or management and the fob is cancelled within minutes from the app, without changing anyone else’s. Do not tag a key ring with your address.

Is a magnetic lock overkill for the door of a single studio inside a warehouse?
+

Usually, yes. Commercial magnetic locks hold with 600 to 1,200 pounds of force, and they bring release rules with them under the NYC code, including unlocking on the fire alarm when they are released by a motion sensor. For a studio door an electric strike or an electrified lever does the job, stays free to open from inside at all times, and costs less to keep compliant. Magnetic locks make sense on a few doors, such as glass doors with no room for a strike, and we test their releases every year where they are used.

Who tests the fire alarm release on our building’s locked doors, and how often should it happen?
+

The connection between a door lock and a fire alarm should be made and tested by a technician holding the FDNY Certificate of Fitness S-98 for fire alarm work, and the release should be tested at least once a year, with the result written down and kept. Some compliance guides also recommend quarterly checks. We coordinate with your fire alarm company, test every release during installation, and leave a written record you can show an inspector.

Does a fob system in a rental building need a camera on the entrance as well?
+

No general rule says every fob door must have one. In the DHCR ruling we rely on, though, a security camera monitoring the entry system was one of the conditions of approval, and a camera on the vestibule is the most common pairing we install in Bushwick anyway, because it shows what happened at the moment the system logs a forced or held door. If you add one, aim it at the door and the mailboxes, not into anyone’s apartment.

Our building is studios only, with no apartments. Can the street door stay unlocked during business hours?
+

Often it can, because the Multiple Dwelling Law’s self-locking rule for residential entrances does not apply to a building with no dwellings. The door can unlock on a schedule while the building is staffed or open to the public and lock automatically at closing, with tenants using their own credentials after hours. If anyone actually lives in the building, legally or not, treat the street door as residential and keep it locked. Check your certificate of occupancy and your insurer’s conditions too.

Our landlord wants a photo of every adult in the apartment before handing out fobs. Is that allowed?
+

In the DHCR ruling we rely on, photos of each adult occupant linked to their fob were one of the approved conditions, and tenants had to show identification that the landlord could not record. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law separately limits what a smart access system may collect to a listed set of items and requires written consent and a privacy policy. Ask management for the written policy before you agree. On systems we set up, we collect only what the system needs.

Can we buy the reader and lock ourselves and pay you only to install them?
+

Yes, as long as the hardware is a listed product that suits the door.
