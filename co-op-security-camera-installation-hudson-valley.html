Where the Co-ops Are

Most Hudson Valley Co-ops Sit in Westchester, and That Shapes Every Camera Plan

Ask a board in Hartsdale and a board in Poughkeepsie what a "co-op" looks like and you will get two very different pictures. Cooperative apartment ownership north of the city line is lopsided. Westchester County holds the great majority of it, the river counties above it hold a scattering of complexes, and two of the six counties barely have any at all. Before we talk about lenses or recorders, it helps to know which of those markets your building belongs to, because the size of the corporation, the age of the buildings and the distance to the nearest neighbor all change how a co-op security camera system gets designed.

The sales numbers make the imbalance plain. OneKey MLS recorded 1,581 co-op closings in Westchester in the twelve months through October 2026, more than the 1,250 condominium closings in the same county over the same stretch, at a twelve-month co-op median of $240,000 (OneKey MLS Westchester, Oct. 2026). Rockland logged 71 co-op closings over its own twelve-month window (OneKey MLS Rockland, Oct. 2026). Dutchess logged 42 (OneKey MLS Dutchess, Q3 2026). Orange County closed six co-op sales in the third quarter of 2026 (OneKey MLS Orange, Q3 2026), and Putnam closed two (OneKey MLS Putnam, Q3 2026).

Ulster County is the honest exception, and we would rather say so than pretend otherwise. OneKey counted a single co-op sale there in the twelve months through September 2026 and no co-op inventory at the end of that period (OneKey MLS Ulster, Q3 2026). In July 2025 the county executive described a planned 15-unit limited-equity cooperative in Kingston as something that "would be the first cooperative ownership structure in the County" (Ulster County, July 3, 2025). That building has not been built. So we do not publish a separate Ulster co-op camera page, and we will not invent Ulster co-ops to fill one. If your Ulster property is a condominium or a homeowners association, our condo pages cover it, and if a housing cooperative does open there, we will gladly quote its cameras from the Ulster price page.

What this means in practice: a typical Hudson Valley co-op camera project is a Westchester project. It is a prewar elevator building a short walk from a Metro-North platform, a garden complex in Yonkers or Mount Vernon with several buildings around a lawn and a parking field, or a postwar mid-rise along a commercial avenue in New Rochelle or White Plains. The smaller co-op markets of Rockland, Dutchess and Orange lean toward 1960s and 1970s garden complexes with a laundry building, a lot and sometimes a pool. Putnam's handful tends to be a single small building run by volunteer officers. Each of those has its own camera logic, and we describe all of them below. For intercoms, entry panels and fobs, our Hudson Valley co-op security systems page covers the whole building; this page stays with cameras.

The figures we plan around

  • 1,581 Westchester co-op closings in twelve months through October 2026, against 1,250 condo closings; 510 co-ops were on the market in October (OneKey MLS). Turnover that steady means new shareholders arriving every month who have never seen the building's camera rules.
  • Dutchess co-op closings rose 44.8% to 42 over the twelve months through September 2026, at a median of $114,000 (OneKey MLS). Budgets in the upper valley are smaller, so phasing matters more.
  • Rockland's co-op median fell to $140,000 over the twelve months through October 2026, from $165,000 a year earlier (OneKey MLS).
  • Orange County's co-op median was $167,000 in the third quarter of 2026, up 4.7% (OneKey MLS).
  • Yonkers counted 211,569 residents in the 2020 Census (NYS Comptroller), well under the 325,000 line that decides which state housing law applies, a point we come back to in the law section.
Building Types

Five Kinds of Hudson Valley Cooperative, Five Different Camera Jobs

We sort Hudson Valley co-ops into five groups before we ever take out a tape measure. The groups are not legal categories; they are how the buildings behave when you try to put cameras on them. A board that knows which group it is in can read a proposal much more critically, because it knows where the money should be going.

1. Prewar elevator buildings near the station

Southern Westchester grew up around the railroad, and some of its earliest apartment buildings were built for commuters. Bronxville's own published history describes apartment buildings of the 1910s, with shops on the ground floor, rising within its one square mile (Village of Bronxville). Garth Road, on the Eastchester side of Scarsdale, has thirteen residential buildings that are "nearly all cooperatively-owned" and sit "just south of the Scarsdale Harlem Line Rail Station," built between 1926 and 1966 (Scarsdale10583, Feb. 6, 2026). Buildings like these have a formal lobby, a small elevator, a cellar with storage cages and a laundry, and often a rear service door onto an alley or a parking court. The camera work looks a lot like city work: a handful of well-placed interior cameras, a tidy recorder closet, cable hidden in old plaster and molding. The difference is the exterior. Where a Manhattan building has a sidewalk, these buildings often have a driveway, a rear lot and a garage, and those outdoor spaces take as much of the budget as the lobby.

2. Large multi-building campuses

At the other end of Westchester's range are campuses with hundreds of apartments in several buildings. Habitat magazine described the county's largest co-op as 778 units in five buildings, with no doormen (Habitat, Feb. 17, 2022). A campus without a doorman leans on its cameras for the jobs a doorman would do: seeing who came in behind a resident, which van left a pile of boxes in the vestibule, who propped the side door. The camera system for a campus is really several small systems, one per building, tied together over fiber or wireless links so the management office can see everything from one screen. Distance, not camera count, drives the price.

3. Postwar garden complexes

This is the dominant co-op type in Rockland, Orange and Dutchess, and it is common in lower Westchester too. Two- and three-story buildings sit around lawns, with a central laundry room, parking fields and sometimes a pool. Listings give a sense of the stock: a garden-style co-op in New Windsor built in 1960 with on-site laundry (Coldwell Banker listing), a 1965 complex in Cornwall with a parking lot, a common laundry and a playground behind the buildings (Raveis listing), and a 1970 co-op in Suffern with assigned parking (Raveis listing). We name these as examples of the building type only; none is a client. Garden complexes have many entrances, usually one per stair hall, and very little interior common space. The cameras go outside: building entrances, the laundry, the lot, the dumpster enclosure, the pool gate.

4. Townhouse and single-family co-ops

A smaller group of Hudson Valley cooperatives are not apartment buildings at all. Orange County listings viewed in November 2026 included a townhouse-style co-op in New Windsor and a ranch-style single-family co-op in Monroe (Homes.com, Orange County co-ops). Each household has its own front door, so there is no lobby to watch. The corporation still owns the roads, the mailbox shelter, the community building and the grounds, and that is where the cameras go. Front porches belong to the households under the proprietary lease, and the board usually leaves those alone.

5. Small village and small-city buildings

The last group is the single building of a dozen to forty apartments in a village center or a small city: a masonry walk-up in Poughkeepsie or Beacon, an elevator building in Peekskill, a building over storefronts in Brewster. Dutchess listings in November 2026 sat mostly in Poughkeepsie, Hopewell Junction, Beacon and Wappingers Falls, a few of them marked "near train" (Homes.com, Dutchess County co-ops). These co-ops are often self-managed by a few volunteers, with no super on site, and their camera systems need to be the simplest of all to run: four to eight cameras, one recorder, a phone app for two or three officers, and health alerts so a dead camera does not go unnoticed for months.

Commuter Buildings

Metro-North Co-ops: Lots, Garages and Buildings That Empty Out at 7 A.M.

A large share of the region's co-op stock was built, bought and sold on the strength of the train. Hartsdale co-op listings advertise being "just steps from the Hartsdale Metro-North station" (Homes.com, Hartsdale co-ops). Garth Road's co-ops sit just south of the Scarsdale station. Tarrytown and Beacon listings mention the train in the same breath as the laundry. Poughkeepsie is the northern terminus of the Hudson Line (Poughkeepsie station), and in Orange County the Port Jervis Line stops at Harriman, Salisbury Mills, Campbell Hall, Middletown and Otisville (NJ Transit, Port Jervis Line).

Commuter co-ops have a rhythm that a camera plan should respect. Between roughly six and nine in the morning, a building sends most of its working shareholders out the door and leaves behind a lobby with nobody in it, a parking area full of cars that will not move until evening, and a package shelf that fills up by noon. Deliveries, contractors and visitors arrive during the hours when the fewest people are home to notice them. In a doorman building in Manhattan, a person sees all of that. In a Westchester co-op with no doorman, the cameras are the only witness.

The parking area

Most Hudson Valley co-ops own a parking field or a row of garages, and many own both. Lot cameras have two very different jobs, and boards often ask one camera to do both and get neither. An overview camera, mounted high on a building corner or a pole, records what happened in a section of the lot: which car backed into which, who walked between the rows at night. An identification camera, mounted low and close at the entrance lane, records the face of a driver and, with the right lens and lighting, a plate. We plan the lot as a few overview zones plus one identification point at each way in. That is far cheaper than trying to get detail everywhere, and it answers the question police actually ask: who came in, and when.

Commuter lots add a twist. Cars that sit all day are what people tamper with, and the damage is usually discovered in the evening by an owner who has no idea when it happened. Recording that is searchable by motion in a marked zone around a parked row lets the managing agent find the moment in a few minutes instead of scrubbing through ten hours of footage. We set those search zones during commissioning and show the agent how to use them.

Detached garages and carports

Many garden co-ops and prewar buildings have a block of garages separate from the apartments. Garage blocks are unheated, often unlit and sometimes a long way from any network equipment. We feed them with outdoor-rated cable in conduit, or with a point-to-point wireless link where trenching is impractical, and we choose cameras rated for cold starts so they come back on their own after an outage in January. A single wide camera on the garage apron usually covers every door in the block.

The lobby at mid-morning

Packages are the biggest mid-day problem in commuter co-ops, and the numbers in Westchester show both the scale and the effect of attention. In November 2024 Yonkers police arrested three people near Bronx River Road with "several open Amazon packages with the contents removed" that had been taken from a building lobby (ABC7, Nov. 2024). By June 2026, News 12 reported a 50% drop in package-theft reports at the Yonkers First Precinct and about 13 reports in White Plains so far that year against 18 in the same period of 2025, and quoted White Plains Deputy Public Safety Commissioner Joseph Castelli: "Cameras are still good" (News 12 Westchester, June 9, 2026). A lobby camera placed to see the face of anyone standing at the package shelf, rather than the tops of their heads, is the single most useful camera in a commuter co-op.

Camera Placement

Where Cameras Belong in a Hudson Valley Co-op

Every co-op camera plan we write starts from the same question: which places does the corporation own and control, and which belong to a shareholder under the proprietary lease? Cameras go on the first and stay away from the second. Inside that rule, the order of priority depends on the building type, but the list of candidates is fairly constant across the valley.

Every entrance people actually use

Not every entrance on the drawings, every entrance people use. In a garden complex that might be twelve stair-hall doors and a back gate to a shopping center that residents have worn a path to. In a prewar building it is the front door, the service door, the garage door into the cellar and, often, a door to the trash area that never quite latches. One camera inside each well-used door, looking at the faces of people coming in, does more than any number of cameras on the outside walls.

Mail and packages

The mailbox wall and the package area, whether that is a shelf, a locker bank or a separate room, get a camera at face height or slightly above. Where a garden co-op has a separate mailbox shelter on the grounds, it gets its own outdoor camera with lighting, because those shelters are dark after five in December.

Laundry rooms and laundry buildings

Laundry rooms collect complaints about damaged machines, missing clothes and arguments. A camera here covers the door and the machines and nothing else. Audio stays off; New York's eavesdropping law makes recording conversations without a party's consent a felony (Penal Law §250.05).

Storage, bike rooms and the cellar

Cellar storage cages and bike rooms are where burglaries in co-ops most often go undiscovered for days. A camera at the door to the storage area, plus one looking down the main aisle, is usually enough. We do not point cameras into individual cages; the aisle and the door tell the story.

Elevators

Hudson Valley elevator co-ops can put a camera in the cab, but the video has to leave the moving car somehow. We use wireless elevator camera links that need no new traveling cable. Anything inside the elevator controller or the car's own wiring is the elevator contractor's work, and we coordinate with that contractor when the job needs it.

Pools, playgrounds and community buildings

Garden co-ops with a pool or a playground ask us about cameras there more than about anything else in summer. The pool gate and the pool deck are common areas, and a camera can cover both. Playground cameras deserve more thought, because parents may ask the board who can see video of their children; the footage policy section below covers who should. Community rooms and clubhouses get a camera at the entrance and, where the board wants it, one in the main room.

Grounds, sheds and the edges

The maintenance shed, the dumpster enclosure, the generator pad, the back gate and the entrance drive are the places where a garden co-op loses equipment and gets illegal dumping. They are also the places farthest from power and network, so each one is a small engineering decision: run cable, run fiber, use a wireless link, or decide the risk does not justify the cost.

Where a co-op camera never goes

  • Into any apartment, through any window, or onto any balcony, terrace, porch or yard that a shareholder holds under the proprietary lease.
  • Into a restroom, a shower or a room where people change; New York makes surveillance there a crime (Penal Law §250.45).
  • Into a staff locker room or changing room. New York Labor Law says "No employer may cause a video recording to be made of an employee in a restroom, locker room, or room designated by an employer for employees to change their clothes, unless authorized by court order" (Labor Law §203-c).
  • Anywhere with audio switched on, unless the board's counsel has approved it in writing.
Across the Campus

Getting Video From the Far Buildings to the Recorder

In a city co-op, the hardest cable run is usually up a riser. In a Hudson Valley co-op it is usually across a lawn. A garden complex can have buildings two hundred yards apart, separated by parking, a stream or a slope, and the price of a co-op security camera system there depends more on how the video crosses that ground than on what the cameras cost.

Copper inside, something else between buildings

Inside a building, each camera gets its own network cable carrying both power and video, and a single run can go about 100 meters, or 328 feet, before the signal needs help. Between buildings, copper is the wrong answer for two reasons: distance, and lightning. A long copper run across open ground is an antenna for a nearby strike, and it carries the surge straight into the recorder. So between buildings we use one of three paths:

  • Fiber in conduit. Immune to lightning, good for any distance a co-op is likely to have, and the most durable choice. It needs a trench or an existing empty conduit, which is why it costs the most up front.
  • Point-to-point wireless. A small dish on each building, aimed at the other. No trench, quick to install, and fine for most campuses with a clear line of sight. Trees are the enemy: a link aimed in March can fail when the leaves come out in May, so we aim and test for the summer canopy, not the bare branches.
  • A recorder in each building. For a property where neither fiber nor wireless is practical, each building records its own cameras and the office views them all over the network. If the link between buildings goes down, nothing stops recording.

Rock, wetlands and frozen ground

Trenching is where Hudson Valley jobs most often run into the land itself. More than nine-tenths of Putnam County sits on Hudson Highlands bedrock, according to the county's own hazard profile (Putnam County), and similar ledge turns up across northern Westchester, Rockland and Orange. Wetlands are regulated too: the Town of Carmel, for example, regulates wetlands of an eighth of an acre or more along with a 100-foot buffer (Town of Carmel). When a route crosses ground like that, we price the wireless option alongside it so the board is not surprised by a change order. Frozen ground is the third limit; we plan any new trenching for the months when the ground can be dug.

Surge protection and grounding

Every outdoor run that enters a building gets surge protection where it enters, and every pole camera's run gets protection at both ends. Equipment racks get bonded to the building ground. None of this is visible after the job, and it is the part of a Hudson Valley proposal most often left out of cheap bids. We list it line by line so the board can see it is there.

Power we need, and power we do not provide

Cameras inside the network take their power from the network switch, so most cameras need no outlet at all. The switch and the recorder need ordinary outlets, and a pole far from any building may need a circuit. We do not do electrical work. When a location needs a new outlet or circuit, we mark it, give the co-op's licensed electrician the requirements and schedule around them.

Weather and Power

Storms, Lightning and Long Outages: Keeping a Co-op's Cameras Recording

The night a co-op most needs its recordings is often the night the power goes out. A dark building with doors that may not latch, a lot full of cars and nobody watching is exactly when someone tries a door. Hudson Valley outages are not rare, and the utility record from 2026 alone makes the case for planning around them.

  • On June 7, 2026, Con Edison reported more than 5,000 Westchester customers without power, in places including Bronxville, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle, Scarsdale, White Plains and Yonkers (Con Edison, June 2026), which is a fair list of the county's co-op towns.
  • After the July 4, 2026 storms, Con Edison said Westchester "experienced the greatest impact," with the largest outages in Greenburgh, Yonkers, Elmsford, Dobbs Ferry and Rye (Con Edison, July 5, 2026).
  • Orange & Rockland said more than 47,000 customers had been affected by storms since July 1 (Orange & Rockland, July 6, 2026).
  • In Dutchess, the same July 4 storm left Central Hudson reporting more than 33,000 outages and NYSEG more than 7,400 (Dutchess County, July 2026).
  • Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 knocked out 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers (Daily Voice, Aug. 5, 2020), and Putnam recorded 75,093 lightning pulses in 2020 (Earth Networks).

Battery backup sized to a real outage

Every recorder and every network switch that feeds cameras goes on a battery backup unit in our proposals. The question is how long it should run. A small unit will carry a recorder and a switch through a short flicker and a clean shutdown. Riding through a multi-hour outage takes a larger battery, or the co-op's generator if it has one. We size the battery to the load we are installing and tell the board in writing roughly how long it should last, so the decision about a bigger unit is the board's and not a surprise.

Generators

Some Hudson Valley co-ops added standby generators after long outages. If yours has one, the recorder and the camera switches should be on a circuit the generator carries. Connecting to a generator circuit is electrical work for the co-op's electrician; our part is telling the electrician which outlets need it and confirming the equipment restarts correctly after a transfer.

Lightning

Lightning damage to a camera system usually arrives through a long outdoor copper run: a pole camera in the lot, a cable to a garage block, a feed to the pool house. Fiber or wireless between buildings, surge protection at both ends of every outdoor copper run, and a properly bonded rack remove most of that risk. No system is strike-proof, and a direct hit will damage whatever it hits; that is what the warranty and insurance are for.

Cold, snow and ice

Orange County dug out from about 18 inches of snow in Monroe in January 2026, with officials reporting up to two feet in some areas (News 12, Jan. 26, 2026). Outdoor cameras on co-op buildings need to be rated for the cold, mounted where icicles and roof slides will not hit them, and aimed slightly down so snow does not pile on the lens shield. Recorders stay indoors in a heated space; an unheated garage or a pump house is the wrong place for a hard drive.

Law and Privacy

New York Rules for Co-op Cameras Outside the City

This is general information with sources, not legal advice. The co-op's counsel should review the camera policy and the notice to shareholders before anything goes up. That said, boards moving north from city co-ops, or managing agents with buildings on both sides of the line, often carry city assumptions with them, and several of those assumptions are wrong in the Hudson Valley.

The governing documents come first

A co-op's own bylaws and proprietary lease decide who controls the common areas, and therefore who decides where cameras go. New York courts give boards wide room for decisions made in good faith for the corporation's purposes: the Court of Appeals set that standard for co-op boards in Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp. in 1990 (NY Court of Appeals). That standard applies statewide, so a Hudson Valley board's camera decision, documented in minutes and made for the building's security, stands on the same footing as a Manhattan board's.

Video in common areas

An attorney quoted in The Cooperator, Ricardo M. Vera, explained that New York does not recognize a common-law right of privacy in public or common areas, while audio recording may run into the Penal Law's eavesdropping article (The Cooperator). In practical terms: video of lobbies, halls, laundry rooms and lots is ordinary; sound is where the risk is. We install with audio off.

Shareholders' own cameras

Shareholders often want their own video doorbell or a camera aimed down the hall. Writing about video doorbells, Habitat quoted attorney Debra Guzov: "you can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways" (Habitat, Feb. 20, 2023). A board that installs building cameras should decide its policy on shareholder cameras at the same meeting, so the two do not end up in conflict.

The city's laws stay in the city

New York City co-ops live under the Multiple Dwelling Law and the city's own Administrative Code. Hudson Valley co-ops do not. The state's Multiple Residence Law applies "to all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population and to all towns and villages" (Multiple Residence Law §3), which takes in every city in the six counties, Yonkers included. City-only rules, such as the 2021 local law on smart access systems in New York City multiple dwellings (NYC Local Law 63 of 2021), do not follow a camera system north. Boards should not cite city rules in a Hudson Valley camera policy, and should not assume a city permit exemption or requirement applies either; the town or village building department is the place to ask.

Who may install

New York licenses security and fire alarm installers statewide through the Department of State (General Business Law §69-m), and the department's guidance treats closed-circuit and video systems used to detect or monitor intrusion as covered work (NYS Department of State). Our license is NYS #12000287431. Putnam County's electrical board, on its own low-voltage technician application, notes that it "does not include installation of Fire & Burglar Alarm Systems, CCTV and Access Control as that is licensed by the state" (Putnam County, Apr. 2025). Line-voltage electrical work is a different trade with county rules of its own: Westchester requires electricians to hold a county trade license (Westchester Consumer Protection), and Rockland's code defines electrical contracting to include signaling systems on 50 volts or more (Rockland County Code ch. 250). We do not do electrical work; the co-op's electrician does.

Historic districts

A few co-ops sit in village or city historic districts. Rules differ by municipality. Beacon's preservation chapter, for example, excludes from "alteration" electrical work and "any minor, accessory, utility or security-related changes that do not substantially detract from the historic property" (City of Beacon Code ch. 134). Other villages review anything visible from the street. Small cameras on secondary walls, painted to match, are the safe default; we check with the local office when a front facade is involved.

Footage and Control

Footage Policy, Passwords and Who Gets to Touch the Equipment

A camera system is only as trustworthy as the rules around it. Shareholders accept cameras far more easily when they know who can see the video, how long it is kept and what happens when someone asks for it. Boards protect themselves the same way.

Retention

FirstService Residential, writing for New York condo and co-op boards, says footage is generally kept for 30 to 90 days, recommends logging every request for and every viewing of footage, and advises that residents not be given footage without board and counsel approval (FirstService Residential, Sept. 23, 2024). We size the recorder's drives to the period the board picks, so the policy and the hardware match.

Who can see what

We set up named accounts, not a shared login. A typical Hudson Valley co-op gives live view to the super or the management office, playback and export to the managing agent and one or two officers, and administrator rights to one board-designated person plus a sealed backup. When a director leaves or an agent changes, their account is removed, and nothing else has to change.

Vendor access: the lesson from Westchester's largest co-op

In 2022, Habitat reported that the 778-unit Westchester co-op mentioned above found delivery-access devices installed inside its intercoms without the board's authorization; the agreements had been "signed by somebody with no authority," and the devices were discovered by the agent's own intercom company. The managing agent's president put the lesson simply: "At least ask permission" (Habitat, Feb. 17, 2022). The same thing can happen to a camera system. Our handover gives the board a written list of every device on the camera network, with its location and serial number, and the administrator account sits with the board. Any device that later appears on the network without being on the list is something the board did not approve.

Police requests

Police in Westchester, Rockland and the upper counties ask co-ops for footage after thefts and accidents. The managing agent or a designated officer should export the clip, log the request and keep a copy. A written procedure, agreed before the first request arrives, keeps the super from improvising. We show whoever the board designates how to find and export a clip in under five minutes.

Signs

Whether signs are needed is a question for the co-op's counsel. Either way, signs that say the building is recorded deter people and cut down on complaints, so we recommend them at the main entrances and the lot.

Board Process

Taking a Camera Plan Through a Hudson Valley Co-op Board

Most co-op camera projects in the Hudson Valley die in committee, not in the budget. A director raises cameras, the board asks for a proposal, the proposal is a single price with no map, and the question gets put off until the next incident. A plan built to be voted on avoids that.

Who runs it

Small co-ops usually hand the project to one officer, often the president or treasurer. Larger corporations use a security or capital improvement committee and the managing agent. Either works, as long as one person is our point of contact, keeps the minutes and owns the final camera map.

What the board should see before the vote

  • A marked site plan or aerial showing every camera, what it looks at and what it deliberately does not.
  • Where the recorder will live, how it is backed up on battery and how video gets there from the far buildings.
  • The retention period and the footage policy, in plain words.
  • A line-item price that separates cameras, cabling, links between buildings, surge protection, battery backup and labor, and that lists what is excluded (electrical work, trenching through rock if found, patching and painting).
  • Our license number and the warranty terms.

Paying for it

Boards pay for cameras from the operating budget, the reserve fund or an assessment, depending on the size of the job and what the governing documents allow; that choice belongs to the board and its accountant. Some boards phase the work: the entrances and the packages first, the grounds the next year. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval. In Westchester, boards can also draw on the Westchester Cooperative and Condominium Advisory Council, which The Cooperator described as representing "more than 400 co-ops and condos in Westchester and the Mid-Hudson Region" (The Cooperator), for peer advice on capital projects.

Telling shareholders

The notice to shareholders should be short: which common areas will be recorded, that cameras do not look into apartments or private outdoor space, that audio is off, who can view footage, how long it is kept, and the installation dates. Attaching the camera map heads off most questions. The co-op's counsel should approve the final text.

Insurance paperwork

The agent's office will want proof of our coverage with the cooperative corporation and the agent listed. That certificate comes from our broker after the board picks us, not with the bid, and we fill out whatever vendor packet the agent's office uses at the same time.

Turnover

Explaining the Cameras to Every New Shareholder

With more than 1,500 co-op apartments changing hands in Westchester in a single year, a camera policy that the board explained once, at installation, is soon unknown to a large share of the building. New shareholders arrive with assumptions from wherever they lived before: that they can ask the super for a clip, that they can put a camera over their own door, or that nobody watches the lot. Most camera complaints we hear about from boards trace back to someone who simply never saw the rules.

The fix costs almost nothing. Put a one-page camera summary into the package new shareholders receive at closing or move-in, alongside the house rules: which common areas are recorded, that audio is off, how long video is kept, who to ask for footage and how, and the building's position on shareholders' own doorbells and hallway cameras. Post the same page in the laundry room or mail area. When the camera map changes, update the page.

The move-in itself is also worth a thought. Movers prop doors and block the lobby for hours, and an elevator padded for a move is one where the cab camera may be covered. Boards that require move-in reservations can ask the super to check, afterward, that every camera's view is clear and that no door was left wedged open.

Older Systems

Keep, Rebuild or Replace the Camera System You Already Have

Many Hudson Valley co-ops already own a camera system, often an analog one put in fifteen or twenty years ago, with a DVR in a cellar closet and a monitor in the office nobody watches. Not all of it has to go.

When a repair is enough

If most cameras work, the picture is acceptable and the board can get into the recorder, a repair visit may be all that is needed: a failed camera, a dead drive, a broken power supply. Our Hudson Valley security camera repair page covers that work.

When the cable can stay

Old coax runs often test fine. Where they do, a new recorder and new cameras can sometimes reuse them, which spares the walls of a prewar building. Where the cable fails testing, it is replaced, and that is priced before the board signs.

When the whole system goes

If the recorder is locked with a password nobody knows, the cameras are blurry at night, or the system cannot be viewed remotely, a replacement usually costs less over five years than nursing it along. Bringing in parts from different eras

Some co-ops want to keep a newer camera bought a few years ago while replacing the rest. New network recorders accept many cameras from other makers; we check compatibility during the walk and say which ones can stay.

Board Questions

Questions Hudson Valley Co-op Boards Bring to the Walk

Most of our shareholders take Metro-North and leave their cars in our lot from early morning to evening. What should the lot cameras be able to show?

Two things, from two different kinds of camera. Overview cameras on building corners should cover every row, so that when an owner finds a scrape or a broken window at 7 P.M., the agent can search that row's motion events for the day and find the moment. An identification camera at each lot entrance, mounted low and close, should catch every driver and vehicle coming in. Together they answer "when" and "who." Expecting one high camera to do both usually gets you a picture that shows a car but not a face.

Westchester's largest co-op found devices inside its intercoms that the board never approved. How do we keep unapproved hardware off our camera system?

Keep the administrator account with the board, keep a written device list, and require that every vendor's work be approved in writing before it starts. Our handover includes a list of every camera, switch and recorder with its location and serial number. The recorder can show the board what is connected; anything not on the list deserves a question. When a contractor needs access later, give them a temporary account and remove it when the work is done.

Our garden co-op's pool opens only in summer. Should its cameras record all year?

Yes, in most cases. A closed pool behind a fence is exactly where winter trespass, vandalism to the pool house and dumping happen, and the cameras cost nothing extra to leave running. What can change by season is who sees them. Many boards give the pool committee live view from Memorial Day to Labor Day and take it away in the fall. If the pool house has a changing room or restroom, its cameras stay outside that room all year.

Our cleaning contractor's staff change clothes in a basement room. Which rooms must cameras stay out of?

That room, for a start. New York's Labor Law bars an employer from video recording employees in a restroom, locker room or room designated for changing clothes without a court order, and the Penal Law separately makes surveillance in restrooms and changing rooms a crime. A camera in the corridor outside, aimed so it cannot see through the doorway, is fine. If the co-op's own employees use the room too, the same rule protects them. Ask counsel about anything in a gray area before the camera goes up.

A shareholder who works from home says the hallway camera makes her uncomfortable. Can recording be limited to certain hours?

Technically, yes; recorders can follow a schedule per camera. Practically, we advise against it, because the hours a camera is off become the hours someone takes advantage of. A better answer is usually to show the shareholder exactly what the camera sees, confirm it does not look into her door when open, and walk her through the footage policy: who can view, when and why. If the board still decides to schedule that camera, we set it up and note it in the handover.

With no super and no office on site, who should be able to see the camera picture?

In a small self-managed co-op, give live view and playback to two or three officers, not to everyone on the board and never to all shareholders. Set the system to send a health alert to one officer when a camera or drive fails. Give one officer the administrator login and keep a sealed copy with the corporation's records. That keeps the system watched without making it anyone's full-time job.

After a storm, how would we know a camera was knocked out of aim?

Most current cameras can raise a "scene change" or tamper alert when the view suddenly changes, which catches a camera turned by wind, hit by a branch or covered by snow. We turn that alert on for outdoor cameras and send it to the people the board names. It is also worth having the super glance at the recorder's grid view the morning after a big storm; it takes thirty seconds.

Our prewar halls are lined with steam pipes and radiators. Can camera cable run near them?

Near, but not on. Network cable should not be strapped to steam pipes or run behind radiators, where heat degrades the jacket over time. In older Westchester buildings we route along ceiling lines, through closets, behind crown molding or in slim raceway painted to match, and keep a gap from heat sources. The walk tells us where the cleanest path is.

We are replacing the roof next year. Should cameras on the roofline wait?

Usually, yes, for any camera that would be mounted on the parapet, the roof edge or the eaves, because roofers will have to take it down. Cameras inside, at entrances or on poles can go in now. We can run the cable for the future roofline cameras during the first phase so that mounting them later is quick.

Icicles hang over our main entrance every February. Will they ruin the picture?

They can, if the camera sits under the drip line. Icicles in the frame block faces and set off motion alerts as they melt, and falling ice can knock a camera out of aim. We mount entrance cameras inside the vestibule where we can, or on a side wall clear of the eave, and aim them so the door, not the roof edge, fills the picture.

Our second building sits across a wetland we are not allowed to dig in. Can its cameras still reach the main recorder?

Yes. A point-to-point wireless link between the two buildings carries the video without any trench, as long as the two mounting points can see each other over the vegetation in full leaf. If they cannot, the second building gets its own recorder and the office views both over the network. Either way, no digging in the regulated area.

Teenagers gather in our stairwell after school. Should a camera watch the landing?

A camera at the stairwell door on the ground floor, looking at who enters, usually solves the problem without putting a camera on every landing. People behave differently when they can see a camera at the door. If damage keeps happening on a particular landing, one camera there, aimed at the landing and not at any apartment door, is reasonable. The board's notice to shareholders should mention stairwell cameras like any other.

Reality Check

What Online Answers Get Wrong About Co-op Cameras North of the City

"Co-ops are a New York City thing"

Westchester alone had 1,581 co-op closings in twelve months, more than its condo closings, per OneKey MLS. The upper counties have fewer, and Ulster has almost none, but the southern half of the valley is full of co-op boards making camera decisions.

"The Multiple Dwelling Law covers every apartment building in New York"

It does not. The Multiple Residence Law covers cities under 325,000 people and every town and village, which is the entire Hudson Valley. Answers that cite city codes for a Yonkers or White Plains co-op are pointing at the wrong law.

"Wi-Fi cameras save money on a big property"

Wi-Fi cameras save cable, then cost it back in batteries, dropouts and a building network that was never meant to carry twenty video streams. Across a campus, wired cameras in each building with fiber or a dedicated wireless link between buildings are more reliable and cheaper over the life of the system.

"Any electrician can install the cameras"

Camera installation is licensed by New York State as security work; even Putnam County's own electrical board points CCTV installers to the state license. Electricians handle line-voltage power. A camera job may need both, and they are different licenses.

"A battery backup is optional"

In a region where single storms in 2026 cut power to tens of thousands of customers, a recorder without battery backup is a recorder that goes dark exactly when the building is most exposed. Battery backup is the cheapest line in a proposal and the one we would remove last.

"Cameras stop package theft"

Cameras help police identify and arrest the people who take packages, and they deter some attempts, as Westchester police data suggests. They do not stop a determined thief from picking up a box. A locked package room or lockers, with a camera at the door, is what actually reduces losses.

From the Field

What Our Crews Check First on a Hudson Valley Co-op Walk

We have worked in the security trade since 2000 and run our own company since 2002, with thousands of jobs completed across New York City, Long Island and the Hudson Valley. The co-op walks north of the city line follow a routine that comes from that work. We do not name client buildings, but we can describe what we look for.

  • The recorder's future home. We look for a locked, heated, dry room with an outlet, ideally in the building closest to the middle of the campus. Boiler rooms and unheated garages are last resorts.
  • Every door people really use. We ask the super which doors get propped, which ones the delivery drivers prefer, and where residents cut through. That list beats the site plan every time.
  • Lines of sight in summer. On a November walk we look at the trees and ask what they look like in July, because a camera aimed through bare branches can be blind in leaf season.
  • Old cable worth keeping. We test existing coax and network runs on the spot when we can, because reusable cable is the biggest saving in older buildings.
  • Power at the edges. Garage blocks, pool houses, sheds and entrance signs often have no outlet. We note each one for the electrician.
  • Night lighting. We look at how lit each entrance and lot section is after dark, and decide where a floodlight camera beats a camera that relies on infrared.
  • Who will use the system. A self-managed co-op with three volunteer officers needs a simpler setup and clearer instructions than a campus with a full-time office.

Hudson Valley Co-op Security Camera FAQ

Why is there no separate co-op camera page for Ulster County?
+

Because Ulster has almost no housing co-ops today. OneKey MLS counted one co-op sale there in the twelve months through September 2026, and the county describes a planned 15-unit Kingston building as its first cooperative. If your Ulster property is a condo or an association, our Hudson Valley condo camera page covers it, and any Ulster co-op is quoted from the Ulster price page.

Do New York City's building laws apply to camera projects at Hudson Valley co-ops?
+

No. Hudson Valley co-ops fall under the state Multiple Residence Law, which covers cities under 325,000 people and all towns and villages, not the city's Multiple Dwelling Law or Administrative Code. State laws such as the Penal Law limits on audio and private spaces still apply everywhere, and the town or village building department answers local permit questions.

Will the cameras keep recording if the co-op's internet service goes down?
+

Yes. The recorder sits in the building and saves video to its own drives, so recording does not depend on the internet. What stops during an internet outage is remote viewing from phones and the office elsewhere. When the service returns, remote viewing resumes and nothing recorded in the meantime is lost.

Can cameras watch the co-op's backup generator or fuel tank?
+

Yes, and many Hudson Valley co-ops that added generators after long outages want exactly that. One outdoor camera on the generator pad or tank enclosure, with a light if the spot is dark, records anyone tampering with it or siphoning fuel. Keep it on battery backup so it records while the generator is starting.

Can video from our intercom entry panel be saved on the camera recorder?
+

Often, yes. Many current video intercoms can send their camera stream to a network recorder, so the door panel's view is kept with the rest of the footage. It depends on the intercom brand and model, so we check it on the walk and tell the board whether it works before anything is bought.

Can the board get an alert when a camera stops sending video?
+

Yes. We set up health alerts for lost video, a full or failing drive and a recorder that goes offline, sent to the people the board names, usually the super, the managing agent or one officer. That way a dead camera is found within a day, not months later when someone needs footage.

Can one recorder serve co-op buildings on opposite sides of a public road?
+

Usually, yes, with a point-to-point wireless link between the buildings, since running cable under or over a public road needs permission the co-op may not have. If the two buildings cannot see each other, each one gets its own recorder and the office views both over the network.

Will cameras work in an unheated detached garage block through the winter?
+

The cameras will, if they are rated for cold starts, and we choose models that are. The recorder will not do well there, so it stays in a heated building and the garage cameras connect back to it by cable in conduit or a wireless link. Surge protection goes on any copper run from the garage block.

How do you keep a camera on a windy riverfront wall from shaking?
+

By mounting it on the building itself with a short, rigid bracket rather than a long arm, choosing a compact camera with less surface for the wind to catch, and anchoring into solid masonry rather than siding where we can. A picture that wobbles in wind also triggers false motion alerts, so a steady mount pays twice.

Can the managing agent and the board have different levels of access?
+

Yes. Each person gets a named account with only the rights the board approves, for example live view for the super, playback and export for the managing agent, and full administration for one designated officer. Accounts can be changed or removed without affecting anyone else's.

Can one camera's recordings be kept longer than the rest?
+

Yes. Recorders let us set retention camera by camera, so the board can keep, for example, the lot entrance or the package area for 90 days while hallway cameras keep 30. Longer retention on a few cameras costs far less drive space than raising it for the whole system. We set it to the board's written footage policy.

How long will battery backup keep the cameras running in a power outage?
+

It depends on the size of the battery and how many cameras the switch powers. A standard unit carries a small system through short outages and a clean shutdown; longer ride-through needs a larger battery or a generator circuit. We put the expected runtime for the equipment we install in the proposal, so the board can decide whether to size up.
