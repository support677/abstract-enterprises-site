The Neighborhood

Why Bedford Stuyvesant Properties Need Access Control

The short version is that Bedford Stuyvesant has some of the oldest front doors in New York carrying some of the busiest households. Wikipedia counts roughly 8,800 buildings put up before 1900 and calls the neighborhood the largest collection of intact Victorian architecture in the country. Those houses were designed around one household and a key on a ring. Today the same house is often an owner on the garden and parlor floors with two rental apartments above, or three or four separate leases, or a family that inherited the house and lives somewhere else. Every one of those arrangements changes who should be able to open the door, and the old mortise lock cannot tell any of them apart.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s report on the Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district describes the pattern we meet most often: row houses with a shared first-floor entrance that leads through a vestibule into a hallway, round-headed double-leaf doors, areaways and raised basements, and, from the 1890s, French flats of four or five stories. A French flat is a small apartment building with one street door for every household in it. When that door does not lock, everyone in the building feels it, and when it locks with a key that has been copied for thirty years, nobody really knows who can get in.

The cost of getting this wrong was in the news this year. On March 18, 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported that tenants of a four-story building on Bedford Avenue had lived with broken front-door locks for at least four months. Tenants described trespassers sitting in the stairwell; the management company said an access card reader “was found to be inoperable and requires replacement”; HPD issued violations and used its Emergency Repair Program. One tenant, Morris Tapley, said: “I just want to be safe where I pay rent.” We cite that story for one engineering lesson, not to point at anyone: a reader is an accessory to a lock, not the lock itself. When the reader dies, the door should still latch and stay locked from the street. Much of this page is about building doors that way.

The city already expects it. HPD’s self-closing door rules require apartment and hallway doors to swing shut and latch by themselves, its inspectors check public areas including the building entrance, and a door that fails to self-close or latch is a class C immediately hazardous violation that the owner has fourteen days to correct. Under Local Law 71 of 2022 HPD also picks 300 buildings a year for proactive inspections. An access system that adds a strike but leaves a sagging door or a weak closer behind does not just fail the tenants; it fails the inspection.

The fourth reason is privacy. Bedford Stuyvesant has a great many small rental buildings, and New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law treats fobs, key cards, phone apps and biometrics in multiple dwellings as “smart access.” Owners who use them must get consent, collect only the minimum data, publish a retention and privacy policy, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it. A cheap app-based kit installed by a handyman usually ignores all of that. We set systems up so that the owner of a three-family can meet those rules without hiring a lawyer to read the app’s settings.

And the shops are a separate story again. The main shopping street, Fulton Street, runs through the middle of the neighborhood, and the Bed-Stuy Gateway Business Improvement District covers it from Classon Avenue to Troy Avenue, home to what BK Reader called “hundreds of businesses.” Many sit under apartments. Two Fulton Street burglaries in the news came in through the ceiling rather than the door: Patch reported a burglar who took about $10,000 from the registers of Mr. Liquor at 1555 Fulton Street in 2016 and left the same way, and News 12 reported in October 2025 that a man climbed a wall and broke through the ceiling of Fulton Pawnbroker, taking about $20,000 of merchandise. Access control on a storefront is worth having, but it has to be planned with the interior doors and the alarm, not as a single reader on the front.

What brownstone, brick and old wood mean for the install

Brownstone is a soft sandstone, and the carved surrounds, newel posts and lintels on these fronts are exactly what a careless installer drills through. We do not. Readers and stations go into mortar joints or plain masonry beside the doorway, or onto the wood of the door frame, which matches what the Landmarks Commission’s guidebook asks for in historic districts and is simply good practice everywhere else. A later change then leaves a joint to repoint, not a broken stone. Cable goes through the wall behind the device into the vestibule, so there is little or no visible conduit on the front.

Old wood doors and frames are the bigger question. A double-leaf door from the 1890s has usually had three or four generations of locks fitted to it, and the jamb where a strike would go is often bored, patched or split. Before quoting, we check whether the active leaf closes flat against its stop, whether the fixed leaf is held firmly by its bolts, and whether the jamb is solid where the strike body will sit. Where the wood is tired we say so, and the repair comes before the electronics. A strike in a split jamb is a strike that a shoulder opens.

French flats and walk-ups tend to have heavier street doors and cellar stairs that run under the stoop, which makes the cable route easy: down through the vestibule floor, along the cellar ceiling, to the controller. Newer buildings with aluminum storefront entrances are the opposite problem. Their stiles are narrow, so the electric lock usually goes into the panic bar or the frame header, and the reader goes on the wall beside the door rather than on the frame.

When the Reader Dies: Building a Bed-Stuy Door That Fails Locked

Every electronic part on a door will fail one day. Readers get kicked, keypads get wet, controllers lose their batteries, cloud services change their terms. The only question that matters is what the door does at that moment. In too many buildings the answer is that it stops locking, because the reader or the magnetic lock was the only thing holding the door shut, and somebody propped it or set the latch back while waiting for a repair. That is how a building ends up with an open front door for months.

We design the other way round. On a Bedford Stuyvesant street door the lock is a mechanical lockset with a latch that drops into the strike every time the door closes, and the electric part only decides whether that strike lets go. If the strike loses power it stays locked; the industry calls that fail-secure. The inside handle always opens the door by hand, so nobody is ever trapped, and the original key cylinder keeps working from the street. If the reader is smashed, the keypad floods or the controller dies, the door behaves like a good old-fashioned locked door until the part is replaced. Tenants carry a key for exactly that day.

Magnetic locks are a different animal and we use them carefully. A maglock holds a door only while it has power, so it is fail-safe by nature: when power goes, the door opens. The New York City Building Code allows them on exits only with a release built into the door hardware that frees the door immediately, with one hand, and releases on power loss, with sensors and push-to-exit buttons that meet the code. That is right for some shop exits and some interior doors. It is rarely right for the only lock on a residential street door, because a maglock that loses power or is unplugged leaves the building open, which is the very failure tenants complain about.

The second half of failing well is finding out. A reader that dies quietly on a Friday can sit dead for weeks if the only person who notices is a tenant who already has a key. The controllers we install report their own health: when a reader stops answering, a door contact shows the door held open for longer than it should be, or the controller drops off the network, the owner or manager gets an alert that day. For a building with a super, the alert goes to the super and the owner; for a three-family, it goes to the owner’s phone. Nobody has to walk past the door to learn it is broken.

The third half, if there is such a thing, is spare parts and records. We leave a written list of every part on the door with model numbers, label the cable at both ends and note where the controller and power supply sit, so that whoever services the door next can find them without opening walls. For buildings with eight or more apartments, where the state’s Multiple Dwelling Law expects a working intercom with voice and remote release at the street door, we can keep a spare reader on the shelf in the cellar, so a replacement is a visit, not an order.

None of this is exotic. It is the difference between treating access control as a gadget bolted to a door and treating it as part of a lock that has to work every day for a hundred-year-old building. The cost difference is small: a dead-latching lockset and a fail-secure strike are line items, not upgrades, and the health alert is a setting.

Owner Downstairs, Tenants Upstairs: Who Runs the Door in a Bed-Stuy Row House

The most common Bedford Stuyvesant arrangement we see is a row house where the owner lives on the garden and parlor floors and rents one or two apartments above. Everyone shares the stoop and the vestibule. Before anyone talks about hardware, we sort out three roles. The owner, as landlord, controls who can open the shared doors. Each household controls its own guests, cleaners, sitters and deliveries. And nobody, including the owner, gets more information about the tenants’ comings and goings than the law and good manners allow.

In practice that means the owner holds the administrator login for the street and vestibule doors and can add or remove anyone in an emergency. Each tenant gets their own codes or fobs, and on systems that support it, a household-level login that lets them create a code for a babysitter that ends on Sunday, without asking the owner. Apartment doors, if they are put on keypads at all, belong to the household that lives behind them; under Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c a tenant may add their own lock to their apartment entrance door, provided the owner gets a key on request, and an electronic lock on that door does not take that right away.

Once a house has three or more apartments it is a multiple dwelling, and New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to any smart access system on its doors. HPD’s summary is plain: tenants must give express consent, in writing or through the app; only a minimum list of data may be collected; the owner must publish a privacy and retention policy; authentication data must be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized; data cannot be sold; it cannot be used to track tenants outside the building, to track how often they come and go or who they are in a relationship with, or to harass or evict. A tenant cannot be required to use smart access to get into their own apartment. We configure systems with short log retention, no location tracking and no data sharing switched on, and hand the owner a plain policy to adapt and give to tenants.

Two-family houses are not multiple dwellings, but we follow the same habits there, because tenancies change and because it is good practice. The setup we steer owners away from is the single shared code, given to every tenant, every cleaner and every contractor over the years. Brownstoner’s Brownstone Boys described a Bed-Stuy house where an intruder may have used a front-door code “shared with many people”; nobody could say for certain how he got in, which is exactly the problem. Individual codes do not just keep people out; they tell you which one was used.

Houses that belong to a family but where nobody lives anymore need one more decision: who among the owners is the administrator. We suggest at least two, so that one person’s travel or illness never leaves the house without anyone able to change a code, and a written note of who holds which credential. When the house is sold, administrator access is handed to the buyer and every old code is removed on the closing date.

Readers on Landmarked Fronts: What Staff-Level Review Expects in Bedford Stuyvesant

A large part of Bedford Stuyvesant sits inside city historic districts: the Bedford–Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district designated in 2013, the Bedford Historic District designated in 2015, and Alice and Agate Courts, designated in 2009. Owners there are often told, wrongly, that nothing can go on the front. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s own Permit Guidebook says the opposite: its staff can issue permits for intercom panels, doorbells and security cameras when the installation meets published criteria, without a public hearing.

The criteria are practical. Intercom panels and doorbells must be installed within or next to a door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, and the guidebook allows one intercom panel and up to two light fixtures per door opening. Equipment must be fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or concrete paving, never through cast iron or onto decorative surfaces. Conduit should be hidden by passing through plain masonry behind the device; where that is impossible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match and fastened into mortar joints. Devices should be as small as possible and finished to blend with the building. Ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of existing equipment needs no permit at all.

Card readers and keypads are not named in that chapter, and the guidebook tells owners to contact the Commission about equipment it does not list. In practice that steers us toward two approaches on landmarked houses. The first is a single combined station, a camera, a call button and a reader in one housing, so that the doorway carries one device rather than three. The second is to put the reader on the inner vestibule door, out of view from the street, and leave the outer doors with their original hardware. Which is right depends on how the house is used: a one-family house can often live with the second, while a building with tenants and deliveries usually needs a call station outside.

Some limits we apply on every landmarked front, whether a permit is involved or not. We do not drill brownstone carving, iron railings, newel posts or the decorative surround of a doorway. We use the smallest housing that will do the job, in a finish chosen for the facade. We run cable through the wall behind the device, not across the front. And where a device must be visible from the street, we photograph the doorway before and after, because the owner may need those photographs for a Landmarks file. Where a staff permit is needed, the owner applies; we supply the product sheets, drawings of the mounting position and photographs that the application asks for.

On blocks outside the designated districts and away from individual landmarks, a reader beside the door needs no Landmarks review at all, but the same care costs nothing and protects what makes these houses valuable. A brownstone front is not the place for a big plastic box.

Ceilings, cellars and the door between the shop and the stairs

The two Fulton Street burglaries in the news share a detail that matters to anyone planning a shop’s access control. Patch reported that the burglar at Mr. Liquor came in through the ceiling of a store with two floors of apartments above it and left the same way; News 12 reported that the man who broke into Fulton Pawnbroker climbed a wall and went through the ceiling. In neither case was the front door the problem. A reader on the front door would not have changed what happened.

What access control can do in a building like that is limit how far someone gets once they are inside. A cash office, a stockroom or a back room with its own electric lock and contact means that a person who comes through the ceiling at midnight still faces a locked door, and that door opening outside business hours sets off the alarm and records the time. The door between the shop and the residential hallway, or the shared cellar, is just as important in the other direction: tenants should not be able to walk into the store, and the store’s staff should not be able to wander the apartment stairs. On one controller those are two separate user groups that never overlap.

Roll-down gates complicate the front. Most shop owners here lock a gate over the glass door at night, so the reader for staff has to be reachable with the gate down, usually on the side door or the back door. We plan opening and closing around the person who actually does it: the first employee in at seven, the owner who counts the till at closing, the delivery driver who needs the back room for ten minutes. Each gets their own code with the hours they work, and every opening is logged.

Rules that apply to Bedford Stuyvesant business and building doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path has to unlock when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and push-to-exit buttons must cut lock power and hold the door unlocked for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Shop exits always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Residential street doors. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires the entrance doors of class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 to be self-closing and self-locking, locked except when an attendant is on duty, with an intercom with voice and remote release for buildings of eight or more apartments. Older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. A shop in the same building cannot put the residential door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a building entrance, hallway or apartment door that does not swing shut and latch as a class C immediately hazardous violation with a 14-day correction period.
  • Tenant data. Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Bedford Stuyvesant: Questions Answered

Brownstones and row houses with an owner and tenants

We live on the garden and parlor floors of our brownstone and rent the two floors above. Who should hold the administrator login, and what should our tenants be able to see and change?

You, as the owner, should be the administrator of the shared doors: the stoop door, the vestibule and any cellar or yard door the tenants use. That lets you add a tenant on move-in day and remove them on move-out day without anyone else’s help. Each tenant household should be able to manage its own guests: create a code for a cleaner that works on Thursdays, or a phone invitation for a visitor that ends after the weekend, without calling you.

What tenants should not see is each other’s activity, and what you should not keep is a long history of when each tenant came home. We set log retention short, switch off anything that tracks location, and give each household its own view. If the house has three or more apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies and you will need tenants’ written or in-app consent and a short privacy policy; we provide a plain template to adapt.

Our late parents’ brownstone now belongs to four of us siblings, and none of us lives there. How do we share control of the doors without one sibling holding every key?

Give two of you administrator rights, not one and not four. Two means that one person’s travel, illness or falling-out never leaves the house without someone able to change a code, while keeping the number of people who can add users small enough to track. The other siblings get their own codes or phone keys to the house, which can be switched off if ownership changes, but cannot create new users.

If there are tenants, they get credentials that work only on the doors they use. Contractors, brokers and inspectors get codes with end dates, and the system’s log shows whose code opened the door, which ends the arguments about who let the plumber in. When a sibling sells their share or the house is sold, the departing owner’s access is removed on the closing date and the administrator logins are handed over in writing. A one-page record of who holds which credential, kept with the deed papers, saves a great deal of trouble later.

During our renovation the house looked empty, and someone walked through the upstairs and out the front door. Which openings besides the street door belong on the system before we move back in?

That is almost exactly what Brownstoner’s Brownstone Boys described in their Bed-Stuy house: an intruder walked around the upstairs unit and left by the front door, which was found ajar, and the likely ways in were the shared door code, an unlocked window or the roof hatch. Their fixes are a good list: a deadbolt on the front door, a lock on the inner vestibule door and double latches on the roof hatch. We would add electronic eyes to the openings people forget.

In a typical row house that means a contact on the roof hatch and the rear yard door, a contact or a lock on the garden-level door under the stoop, and the vestibule door on the same system as the street door so you can see when either is left open. The street door gets a strike that relocks every time. Contractors get individual codes that end when the job does, and once you move in, every renovation code comes off the system the same week.

Is there a point where a reader is the wrong answer for a one-family brownstone, and a good mechanical lock is better?

Yes. If one household lives in the house, nobody else needs regular access, and nobody loses keys, a high-quality mechanical deadbolt and a door that latches properly may be all the house needs. Access control earns its cost when the list of people grows: a dog walker, a home attendant, a contractor, a grown child who visits, a tenant in the garden apartment. It also earns it when you want to know that the door locked behind the last person out.

We say this on site when it is true. Sometimes the best first step is a new closer, a strike plate adjusted so the latch actually catches, and a lock cylinder that has not been copied for forty years. If, after that, you still want codes and an alert on your phone, the work we did on the door is not wasted; it is the foundation the reader sits on.

Walk-ups, French flats and the city’s door rules

Someone in our walk-up keeps pressing the little buttons on the edge of the old mortise lock so the street door never locks. Can a new system take that option away?

Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons a Bedford Stuyvesant street door stands unlocked. Many older mortise locks have stop buttons in the faceplate that hold the latch back, so the door can be pushed open from the street. Somebody sets them for a delivery and nobody unsets them. The fix is a lockset without that option, or with it disabled, combined with a fail-secure strike, so the latch is always thrown when the door closes and only the strike decides whether it opens.

Then the door contact does the rest. If the door is held or propped open for longer than a minute or two, the owner or super gets an alert, and the log shows when it happened. For deliveries, the answer is a short-lived code, a phone release from the tenant upstairs or a video call from the station at the door, so nobody needs to defeat the lock to receive a package.

HPD’s self-closing door inspections include the building entrance. Will adding a reader and an electric strike make our street door more or less likely to pass?

More likely, if it is done in the right order, and less likely if it is not. HPD’s test is mechanical: the door has to swing shut and latch by itself, and a defect that stops it is a class C violation with fourteen days to fix it. An electric strike does not change how the door closes, but a strike set slightly out of line with the latch, or a heavy closer replaced with a weak one to make the door easier for a reader user, can stop it latching.

That is why our first job on any street door is to adjust or replace the closer, check the hinges and set the strike so the latch drops in cleanly from every angle. Only then does the reader go up. A magnetic lock, by contrast, can hide a door that does not latch, because the magnet holds it while powered; when the power goes, the same door may swing loose. On a residential street door we prefer the strike and a door that latches on its own.

If HPD’s Emergency Repair Program has already replaced our building’s entrance lock, can we put access control on the door its contractor worked on?

Usually, yes. According to HPD, the Emergency Repair Program hires contractors to correct hazardous conditions and bills the owner, as News 12 reported in the Bedford Avenue case. Once that work is done, the door and its hardware are the owner’s to maintain like any other part of the building. What we check is what was actually installed: an emergency repair is aimed at making the door lock again quickly, which may mean a basic lockset and a new closer rather than hardware prepared for an electric strike.

If the frame is sound and the lockset is a type that works with a strike, we fit the strike, reader and controller around it. If not, we replace the lockset with one that does, keeping the closer and any frame repair the contractor did. Either way, the door should pass HPD’s self-closing test before and after our work, and we photograph it both times for the owner’s file.

Landmarked fronts

Our house in the Bedford Historic District already has an intercom panel beside the door. If the new reader goes in the same spot and the old panel comes out, does that still need a Landmarks permit?

The LPC guidebook says ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of existing health, safety and utility equipment does not need a permit, and it allows one intercom panel per door opening when a new installation is reviewed. A like-for-like swap of a panel in the same position, of similar size and finish, sits closest to the no-permit end. A larger device, a new location, new visible conduit or extra equipment on the front moves it toward a staff-level permit.

Because card readers and keypads are not specifically listed in that chapter, the safe course on a landmarked house is to ask the Commission before the work, with a photograph of the existing panel and the product sheet of the new one; the guidebook gives that route for unlisted equipment. We prepare the photographs and sheets, the owner sends them, and we keep the new housing at or under the size of the one it replaces.

Landmarks staff want equipment as small as possible. Does a smaller reader mean a less secure door?

No. The security of a controlled door lives mostly in the parts you cannot see from the street: the strike in the frame, the latch in the lock, the closer, and the controller in the cellar that decides who is allowed in. The reader is only the part that reads a credential. A slim mullion-style reader or a compact combined station reads encrypted fobs as well as a large panel does.

What a smaller housing can lose is features: a large screen, a full directory or a keypad. On a one- or two-family house that rarely matters. On a building with many tenants, the answer is often to keep the outside device small, a camera and call button, and put the full directory or keypad on the inner vestibule wall, where Landmarks review does not reach and weather does not either.

Shops on Fulton Street and the avenues

If a burglar comes into our Fulton Street store through the ceiling at night, can the access system keep him out of the back office?

It can make the back office a second locked door that records being opened, which is the realistic goal. Both Fulton Street break-ins in the news, at Mr. Liquor in 2016 and Fulton Pawnbroker in October 2025, came through the ceiling, so a front-door reader would not have mattered. An electric lock with a door contact on the office, stockroom or safe room, set to stay locked outside business hours, means someone who drops in from above still meets a solid door.

Tie that door to the alarm, so that forcing it or opening it after hours triggers the siren and the monitoring call, and add a camera facing it. Then the ceiling itself is an alarm question: a motion detector covering the shop floor, and where the landlord allows, a sensor in the ceiling space. Access control limits how far someone gets; the alarm and camera deal with the fact that they got in.

We lease a storefront on Fulton Street. Can we put our own access system on the shop’s doors, and does the landlord have to approve it?

Usually you can, and usually the landlord does have to approve it, because a strike or electric lock involves cutting into the door frame, which is the landlord’s property under most commercial leases. Read your lease’s alterations clause before you buy anything. What we provide for the landlord is a short description of the work, the product sheets and a statement that every exit will still open from inside without a key, card or code, which is usually enough.

Keep the system yours: a controller in your shop, your own administrator account, and nothing tied to the residential side of the building. If the landlord needs emergency access, give them a credential you can see in the log rather than a master key. When your lease ends, the lease decides whether the hardware stays or goes; we can remove it and leave the frame ready for a standard strike plate.

Our brownstone needs a reader at the stoop door and a keypad at the garden door. How is that priced on the Brooklyn list?

The stoop door is the main line: a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, the wiring and ten fobs runs $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. The garden door, if it is a personnel or service-type door with a keypad, falls in the $1,500 to $2,000 line. When both doors share one controller and the cable run between them is short, which it usually is in a row house where the cellar sits right under both, the combined job can come in below the sum of the two lines.

If you want both doors, a rear yard contact and a roof hatch contact managed from one app, the job moves toward the multi-door system line, $4,000 to $15,000, usually toward its lower end on a single house. Sales tax is added, and the exact figure is put in writing after the walkthrough.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Bedford Stuyvesant

Quick online answers about access control are built from national cost guides, vendor blogs and general advice, including sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, with the specific figures attributed only where we found them published, set against what a Bedford Stuyvesant door actually needs.

“Access control in NYC costs anywhere from $500 to $10,000.”

That range comes from ButterflyMX’s guide to access control in New York City, which also puts key fob and card systems at roughly $500 to $5,000 per door. Swiftlane’s cost guide gives $1,500 to $5,000 installed for a standard door. Both are honest about being wide.

The trouble is that a range that wide tells a brownstone owner nothing. What decides the price here is the door: whether the old jamb takes a strike, whether the cellar sits under the stoop, and whether the house has one household or four.

Our Brooklyn list is narrower because it is written for these buildings: $1,500 to $2,500 for one controlled door with ten fobs, $1,500 to $3,000 for video entry replacing a buzzer, and a written price after one visit.

“Install a card reader and the door is secure.”

A reader decides whether to release a lock. It does not hold the door shut. The Bedford Avenue building News 12 covered in March 2026 had a card reader; when it failed, the front door stopped locking for months.

Security on a street door comes from a latch that drops every time the door closes, a closer strong enough to pull it shut, and a strike that stays locked when power fails. The reader sits on top of that.

So we fix the door first, fit a fail-secure strike and a dead-latching lockset, and set an alert for a dead reader. A reader on a door that does not latch is decoration.

“Fobs are always safer than keys.”

NYCHA told CBS New York in 2025 that its layered access fobs and door systems are safer than traditional keys and locks. At Queensbridge in Queens, tenants said the new fobs did not work, some doors jammed, and the resident association counted 78 door complaints in a month on one side of the complex.

Fobs are safer than keys only when the hardware works, the door latches, the fobs cannot be copied and the rollout is handled carefully. Old 125 kHz fobs are easier to copy than a good key.

On a Bed-Stuy building we use encrypted credentials, keep a mechanical key for every tenant, and switch doors over only after every household has a working fob in hand.

“You can’t put anything on a landmarked facade.”

Owners in the Stuyvesant Heights and Bedford historic districts hear this often. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s Permit Guidebook says its staff can approve intercom panels, doorbells and cameras that meet published criteria, without a public hearing.

The criteria are specific: devices within or beside the door opening, one intercom panel per opening, fixed at mortar joints or plain masonry, never through cast iron or onto decorative surfaces, as small as possible, with conduit concealed.

We follow those rules on every front, prepare the photographs and product sheets an owner needs, and ask the Commission first about readers and keypads, which the chapter does not list.

“Expect a subscription of $5 to $10 per door per month.”

Swiftlane’s guide quotes that range for cloud-managed systems, and for some buildings a cloud plan is worth it: a manager with many buildings, a board that wants remote access from anywhere.

It is not a requirement. A one-family brownstone or a three-family house can run on a local controller with a phone app and no monthly fee at all.

We price both and let the owner choose. The Brooklyn list covers the installation; any cloud plan is a separate, optional line that is written down before the work starts.

“Lock the doors and the building is protected.”

Two Fulton Street break-ins in the news, at Mr. Liquor in 2016 and Fulton Pawnbroker in 2025, came through the ceiling. A Bed-Stuy brownstone’s intruder in 2019, by Brownstoner’s account, may have used a shared code, a window or the roof hatch.

Doors are one route in. Roofs, ceilings, windows and rear yards are others, and an access plan that ignores them gives a false sense of safety.

We put contacts on roof hatches and yard doors, lock interior doors that guard cash or stock, and tell owners plainly when the answer is an alarm sensor or a camera rather than another reader.

“The owner decides what the entry system records.”

In a one-family house that is true. In a building with three or more apartments, New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law sets the terms: consent, minimum data, a written retention policy, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and no sale of the data.

It also bars using smart access data to track how often tenants come and go or who they are in a relationship with, and a tenant cannot be required to use smart access to enter their own apartment.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Shut the Door Before You Wire It

This describes how we approach an old Bed-Stuy street door, not a particular customer. Before we open a catalog, we close the door. We let it swing shut from fully open and watch: does it close all the way, does the latch catch, or does it stop an inch short? Then we push it from the street side. On a 120-year-old double-leaf door, the fixed leaf often moves more than it should, because its top and bottom bolts no longer reach their keepers.

Next we look at the edge of the lock for stop buttons, and at the jamb for the scars of every lock fitted before. Then we go down to the cellar to find the spot under the vestibule where cable can come up, and a dry wall for the controller away from the boiler. Only when the door shuts and latches every time do we choose a reader. Most of the value in a good installation here is in that first half hour.

FAQ

Bedford Stuyvesant Access Control FAQ

What is a fail-secure electric strike, and why does a Bed-Stuy street door usually get one?
+

A fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power and releases only when the controller sends power to it. The inside handle still opens the door by hand at all times, so nobody is trapped. On a residential street door that means a power cut or a dead reader leaves the door locked from the street instead of open to anyone, and tenants use their keys until power returns.

Is a dead latch the same as a deadbolt, and which one works with a reader?
+

No. A deadbolt is thrown by a key or a thumb-turn and stays out until someone retracts it, so it cannot be released by a strike. A dead latch is a spring latch with a small plunger that stops it being pushed back with a card. A dead-latching lockset relocks every time the door closes, which is why it pairs with an electric strike on a shared street door.

Can a reader or keypad be finished to blend with a brownstone or brick front?
+

Yes. We choose the housing finish when we order, picking the darkest or most neutral option that suits the stone, brick or painted wood beside the door. We do not paint over the face of a reader or keypad, because paint can cover the reading surface or the keys. On landmarked fronts, the LPC guidebook asks for equipment that is small and finished to blend with the building.

Does New York State require a license to install access control on someone else’s property?
+

Yes. New York State requires a license from the Department of State to install, service or maintain security systems for others, and access control falls within that work. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems works under NYS license #12000287431. Ask any installer for their license number before work starts, and check that the name on the license matches the company on the contract.

Why do you ask for a photo of the inside of each door as well as the outside?
+

Because most of the decisions are made on the inside. The photo of the inside shows the closer, the lock, the hinges, whether there is a vestibule wall for cable and whether the door swings in or out. The street side shows the frame, the stoop and any carving or ironwork to avoid. With both, we can usually name the hardware and the price line before anyone visits.

Can a fob system be fitted to an 1890s Bed-Stuy building that has no modern wiring?
+

Yes. The access system brings its own low-voltage cable, so the age of the house wiring matters less than the route from the door to the cellar. Most row houses and French flats have a straight path down through the vestibule floor. The lock power supply needs an outlet nearby; if a new dedicated outlet is needed, a licensed electrician adds it before installation day.

We want fobs now. Can phone unlocking be added later without replacing the readers?
+

Usually, yes, if the readers are chosen for it from the start. Many current readers accept encrypted fobs, cards and phone keys in the same housing, so phone access later is a software change and a license, not new hardware. We name that option in the written plan, so a building that starts with fobs is not locked into them when tenants ask for phone entry.

Can tenants on the upper floors of a brownstone answer the street door from their own apartments?
+

Yes. A video station at the stoop door can ring each household separately, either on a phone app or on an indoor screen, and each household can release the door for its own visitor. A single video doorbell shared by everyone usually cannot tell the households apart. For a top floor four flights up, the phone app saves a long walk down to the stoop.

When we need more fobs a year from now, where do they come from and how are they added?
+

We supply additional fobs that match your system’s encrypted format, and the administrator adds each one to a person in the app, usually in under a minute. Because the credentials are encrypted and tied to your system, fobs bought from a kiosk or online will not work on the reader, which is the point. Keep the order record so lost fobs can be found and switched off.
