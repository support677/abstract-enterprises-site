The Neighborhood

Why Belmont Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Belmont's buildings were laid out for a crowded street life, and a camera system has to be planned for the same crowd. The neighborhood began as part of the Lorillard family's land. Six to Celebrate, the Historic Districts Council's neighborhood program, records that the Lorillard tobacco firm was founded in 1760, moved to this part of the Bronx in 1792, and moved its manufacturing to Jersey City in 1870. In 1888 the city acquired the eastern section of the Lorillard lands for Bronx Park, and the western section, today's Belmont, was subdivided for building. The Belmont BID's history notes that construction of the zoo and the botanical garden began in 1898 and drew Italian immigrants to live and work here. NYC Parks quotes a 1913 advertisement that boasted of the "phenomenal increase in value of real estate in the 'Italian' colonies," and from 1901 to 1973 the Third Avenue Elevated ran along the neighborhood's edge. What went up in those years still shapes every camera job we expect here.

CityNeighborhoods.nyc puts it simply: five-story walk-ups rose along Bathgate, Hughes and Crescent, while modest apartments over shops lined Arthur Avenue and East 187th Street. Wikipedia describes Belmont's housing as mostly five- and six-story walk-up and elevator buildings, most of them built before 1939. City land-use records for Community District 6 inside ZIP code 10458 back that up: of the 662 residential lots there with three or more units, 386 hold buildings dated 1901 to 1929 and another 114 date from the 1930s, while 153 were built from 1968 on. A building of that age was never wired for cameras. There is no conduit waiting in the wall, the front door opens straight into a vestibule, the stair is the only way up, the roof is reached through a bulkhead, and the cellar holds the electrical service and usually the internet line. Every one of those spaces is a possible camera point, and the cable has to reach all of them without passing through tenants' apartments.

The mixed-use building is the type that makes Belmont different from a street of single-family houses. A listing for 2476 Arthur Avenue, at the corner of East 189th Street, describes a four-story walk-up with 10 apartments and roughly 1,200 square feet of commercial space. The American Planning Association, naming Arthur Avenue between 184th and 187th Streets one of its Great Places in 2016, said all of the buildings on that stretch are mixed use. In a building like that, the store owner wants cameras on the shop door, the counter and the register. The tenants want to know who is in the vestibule and who took the package. The building owner wants all of it, plus the cellar and the roof. Those three sets of needs do not overlap neatly, and a camera system that gives everyone the same app login creates problems of its own: a tenant who can watch the store's cash drawer, or a shopkeeper who can watch tenants come home at night.

Who owns and lives in the buildings matters too. Homes.com notes that many owners of Belmont's multi-family buildings live in one of the units and rent out the others, so the person paying for the cameras is often also a neighbor of the people on them. Redfin listings include co-op apartments in walk-up buildings with an on-site super, and Belmont Arthur Avenue LDC, the community's housing and economic development agency since 1981, has rebuilt and managed housing here for decades. Then there are the students. Wikipedia notes that a large population of Fordham University students lives in off-campus and private apartment buildings in Belmont, and The Fordham Ram's April 2025 housing guide describes a student pocket bounded by Third Avenue, Crescent Avenue and Cambreleng Avenue, served by six realty companies, where students who graduate tend to leave at the same time. A building that turns over a whole floor every May needs camera accounts that can be closed and reissued without a service call.

The reasons owners call about cameras are visible in the local record. The Bronx Times reported in February 2024 that a burglar used a brick to break the glass door of Arthur Fashion, at 2415 Arthur Avenue, just before 6 a.m., and that the NYPD released surveillance video and images of the suspect. A month later the paper's police beat reported that in the 48th Precinct, which covers Arthur Avenue, burglaries were up 25 percent in early 2024, with robberies up 20 percent and grand larcenies up 23.7 percent. DNAinfo reported back in 2011 that robbers posing as customers were buzzed into Spinelli & Son Jewelers on Arthur Avenue. The picture in 2026 is mixed: Bronx.com reported on October 27, 2026 that major crime in the Bronx fell 11.9 percent in the first nine months of the year, while the 48th Precinct's own CompStat report for the week of August 31 to September 6, 2026 showed 200 burglaries year to date against 189 a year earlier. In an April 2026 case covered by Norwood News, nine of 252 car, tire, rim and catalytic converter thefts charged by the Bronx District Attorney were in the 48th Precinct, and prosecutors said many of the crimes were captured on surveillance video. Footage is how cases like these are built, which is why the aim and the export matter as much as the camera.

Belmont's streets also behave unlike most Bronx blocks. The Belmont BID, with its office at 2396 Arthur Avenue, employs private licensed security guards to patrol the district. In 2019 the Bronx Times reported that the BID wanted to buy the city-owned municipal parking lot between Arthur Avenue and Hoffman Street and was placing a guard there. Time Out described the 2021 Piazza di Belmont, when the stretch from East 188th Street to Crescent Avenue closed to traffic for outdoor dining on Friday and Saturday evenings and Sunday afternoons, and BronxBuzz lists 2026 street fairs including the Skenderbeu Fair on May 3 and Ferragosto on September 13 along Arthur Avenue between East 187th Street and Crescent Avenue. A storefront camera here may look at an empty sidewalk at 5 a.m. and a solid crowd at 7 p.m., and it has to be useful in both.

One Building, Three Viewers

Store Below, Tenants Above, Owner on the Third Floor: Who Watches Which Camera in a Belmont Building

The question that decides most Belmont camera designs is not which brand or how many megapixels. It is who is allowed to watch. Modern recorders let each user account see only certain cameras, and the system can be split so the store, the tenants and the owner each have their own view of the same building. Planning those accounts before the first hole is drilled changes where cameras go, where the recorder sits and how the internet reaches it. The store's cameras

A shop on Arthur Avenue or East 187th Street usually wants three views: the door, so every person who walks in is seen face-on; the counter, so the register and the hand at it are clear; and the back, where deliveries come in and staff go out. The February 2024 Arthur Fashion break-in, where the burglar broke the glass door with a brick before dawn, shows what the door camera is for. It has to see the glass and the person in front of it at the hour nobody is there, which means a camera inside the glass aimed out, not one high in a corner that sees the tops of heads. If the store is a tenant in someone else's building, its cameras can still record to the building's recorder on a separate account, or to its own recorder in the shop. Many shopkeepers prefer their own, so the footage of their business stays theirs if they move.

The tenants' cameras

Tenants care about the street door, the vestibule and the mailboxes. Whether they get to watch those cameras at all is the owner's decision, and there are reasons for both choices. Giving tenants a view of the vestibule lets them see who is ringing before going down five flights, and lets them check whether their delivery arrived. It also means a dozen phone logins that need managing, and in a student building, a fresh set every year. When tenants are given access, we create a single shared viewer account for the front door and vestibule only, or one per apartment if the owner wants to be able to remove one without disturbing the rest. Tenants never get the recorder's settings, the playback of halls outside other people's doors, or the cellar.

The owner's view

The owner, or the managing agent, sees everything the building's cameras record and holds the administrator account that can change settings, delete footage and add users. In an owner-occupied three-family that is usually one person with a phone. In a co-op walk-up it is a board decision, and the super may need a viewer account that can play back and export but cannot delete. We write down who holds each login before we leave, because the single most common problem with older systems we see is that nobody knows the administrator password any more.

Where the recorder lives

A recorder that sits on a shelf in the vestibule, or under the counter by the shop door, is the first thing a burglar unplugs on the way out. In a Belmont walk-up we usually put it in the cellar, in a locked metal enclosure near the electrical service and the internet line, on a battery backup so a short power cut does not stop recording. Where the cellar is damp or shared with the store, the owner's own apartment or a locked closet on an upper floor can work, at the cost of a longer cable run. The location goes in the written plan, and so does the route each camera cable takes to reach it.

Count the ways in, then the cameras

The right number of cameras follows from the number of ways into the building and the places where something of value sits. A typical Belmont walk-up has a street door, sometimes a separate cellar door or sidewalk hatch, a stair, a roof door and a rear yard reached from the cellar or the ground-floor apartment. A store adds its own front door, its counter and its back door. Walk the building and list every door and opening a person could use, then mark where packages wait and where bikes or strollers are kept. That list is the first draft of the camera plan, and it usually shows that two or three well-aimed cameras at choke points do more than a camera in every hall.

Light first, then aim

Many Belmont stairwells are lit by a single fixture per landing, and some fixtures are on timers or motion sensors that only come on when someone is already halfway up. A camera's own infrared can light a short hall, but it turns everything grey and flattens faces. Before we pick cameras for a stair, we look at each landing with the lights as they normally are at night. Where a bulb is out or a fixture faces the wrong way, fixing that is usually cheaper than buying a better camera, and the owner's lighting duty under the HPD guide has to be met either way. We point out what we see; the electrical work itself is for the building's electrician.

Security Camera Installation for Belmont's Architecture

Belmont's housing falls into a handful of types, and each one changes three things about a camera job: where the cable can travel, what the camera is fixed to, and who has to say yes. These are the types the public record and neighborhood guides describe here.

Five-Story Walk-Ups

The buildings CityNeighborhoods.nyc places along Bathgate, Hughes and Crescent, and listings like 2408 Cambreleng Avenue describe. One stair, a roof bulkhead, a cellar, no elevator shaft to use as a cable route.

Apartments Over Shops

The Arthur Avenue and East 187th Street type, like the four-story building at 2476 Arthur Avenue with 10 apartments over its store. Two owners' interests, one facade, and a ceiling between them.

Owner-Occupied Houses

Brick rowhouses and low-rise multi-family buildings that Homes.com describes, where the owner lives in one unit. Smaller systems, one decision-maker, tenants a floor away.

Co-op Walk-Ups

Walk-up buildings sold as co-op apartments with an on-site super, as Redfin listings show. Board approval, insurance papers, and a super who needs playback without the power to delete.

Elevator Rehab Buildings

Gut rehabs like Belartes Court, which the Alpert Group lists as 73 units in three five-story elevator buildings at 1892-94 Arthur Avenue and 1899 Belmont Avenue. Lobbies, elevators and more halls.

Buildings From 1968 On

The newer third of the stock, by PLUTO's count of lots built from 1968. More likely to have block walls, dropped ceilings and a building entry system already in place.

On walk-ups. A walk-up has no elevator shaft, and its halls are usually plaster laid straight on brick or block, which leaves no hollow wall to fish a cable through. The usual paths are a chase beside the stair where old pipes or wiring already rise, the exterior rear wall where cable can run neatly in a painted raceway up to the roof and down, or the cellar ceiling along to the front of the building. We avoid running anything through an apartment, because it means a tenant's consent, access on the day and a cable inside a home that is not the owner's to open. Every hole through a floor or a wall between apartments is sealed afterward with a fire-rated material, so the cable does not leave a new path for smoke between floors.

On mixed-use buildings. The ceiling of the store is the frontier. Above it are the apartments' floor joists, and through it run pipes, the building's wiring and sometimes the store's own ductwork. Camera cable from the shop has to go up into that space and across to the cellar stair, or down through the shop floor to the cellar directly. Which path works depends on what the shop has done to its ceiling over the years. We look from both sides, the store and the cellar, before deciding, and we schedule the noisy part around the store's hours. The facade belongs to the building owner, so even a store that owns its own cameras needs the owner's agreement to put one outside.

What a Belmont shop camera has to do

A store camera on Arthur Avenue works two shifts. Before opening and after closing, it watches an empty doorway, and the one thing it must do is show the face of anyone who tries the glass. During business hours and on festival days it watches a constant stream of customers, and what matters then is the counter, the register and the corners where stock walks out. Those are different jobs, and one camera rarely does both well. A camera high in a corner covering the whole floor is useful for knowing what happened, but it seldom shows a face clearly enough for anyone to recognize. The door camera should sit inside the glass at about head height, looking out and slightly down, so every person who enters walks straight toward it. The counter camera should look down at the register from behind the staff. The floor camera can then be the wide view. We plan a shop screen by screen with the owner and price each point separately, so a small store can start with the door and counter and add the rest later.

Rules and limits that shape a business camera

  • No general camera mandate for buildings. As security consultants Seneca Security explain it, there is no general NYC law requiring cameras in apartment buildings; landlords add them for tenants, insurers or after trouble.
  • Two claimed business rules we could not confirm. One vendor blog says a "Local Law 26" requires laundromats, pharmacies and electronics stores to keep cameras, and another says a "Local Law 50" requires posted notice of cameras. We could not verify either in city law, so we do not tell customers they apply; check with your own counsel or the agency that licenses your business.
  • No landmark district here. None of the Bronx historic districts on the city's designated list is in Belmont. The Historic Districts Council has proposed the Arthur Avenue Retail Market, Our Lady of Mount Carmel and Aquinas High School for consideration, and the BID said in 2018 it would ask the Landmarks Preservation Commission to protect a small area, but proposals are not designations. If that changes, exterior hardware would need review.
  • Licensing. New York General Business Law §69-m requires a license to install security or fire alarm systems, while the state's installer application says installing passive, non-detecting security devices does not require one. We hold NYS license #12000287431 either way.
  • Exits and egress. No camera, monitor or cable may block an exit path or cover an exit sign. We check this in restaurants and shops before fixing anything.
  • Power for monitors. A TV or monitor's own power cord may not be hidden inside a wall under National Electrical Code §400.12; we use an in-wall rated power and cable kit or a surface cover.
  • Insurance papers. Many landlords and managing agents ask for a certificate of insurance before contractors work in a store or building. Send the request when you book.

Field Notes: Walking a Belmont Mixed-Use Building Before Quoting Cameras

We only publish stories about a particular building when we can document them, so this is not about one address. It is the walk our technicians follow in the kind of building Belmont has on almost every commercial block: a shop on the ground floor, four or five floors of apartments, a cellar shared between the store and the landlord.

We start in the cellar, because that is where the recorder will most likely live. We find the electrical service, the internet line, and the wall between the store's side and the building's side. We look up at the ceiling for a path toward the front of the building. Then we go to the store and look at its ceiling from below, noting where the joists run. Out on the sidewalk we stand where a customer stands and look at the door glass, the awning and the windows across the street that a camera must never show. Then we climb. At each landing we note the light fixture, whether it was on, and where a pipe chase rises beside the stair. At the roof we check the bulkhead door and the view down into the rear yards. Back in the cellar, we sketch the cable routes and list every camera with who should see it. Only then do we write a price.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Belmont: Questions Answered

The recorder and who controls it

In a five-story walk-up with a store on the ground floor, which room should hold the camera recorder?

Usually the building's side of the cellar, in a locked metal enclosure near the electrical service and the internet line. That keeps it away from the street door and the shop floor, which are the two places a burglar passes on the way in and out, and it puts it on the shortest route to both the store's cameras and the stair. If the cellar is damp, flooded in heavy rain or shared openly with the store, the next best place is a locked closet in the owner's own apartment or the super's unit. That means longer cable runs to the roof and the shop, which we show on the quote, but it also means the recorder is behind two locked doors. We put the recorder on a battery backup in either location, and we never mount it in a vestibule, under a stair or beside a shop's cash register.

I live on the third floor of my own building. Can the tenants watch the front-door camera without seeing anything inside the shop downstairs?

Yes. The recorder holds each camera separately, and every user account is given only the cameras the owner chooses. A common setup in an owner-occupied mixed-use building is an administrator account for you that sees everything; a tenant viewer account that sees the street door and the vestibule live, with no playback of other areas; and a store account that sees only the shop's door, counter and back room, if the store's cameras share your recorder at all. The shop's cameras never appear in the tenants' app, and the tenants' halls never appear in the store's. We set this up on the day and leave you a written list of who can see what, so if a tenant moves out you can remove their access yourself.

Our co-op walk-up has a super living on site. Who should hold the main administrator login?

The board or the managing agent should hold it, not the super and not the installer. The administrator account can change recording settings, delete footage and add users, so it belongs with whoever answers for the building legally. The super usually needs a viewer account with playback and export, so he can find the clip of a broken door and send it to the managing agent or the police, but without the power to delete or change anything. We create both on the day, give the board the administrator details in a sealed handover, and write down which accounts exist. Seneca Security notes that building-wide camera upgrades in co-ops may need board approval in the first place, so get that resolution before we book.

When a group of Fordham students moves out in May, what changes on the camera system before the next lease starts?

Only the accounts, if they were set up properly. The Fordham Ram's 2025 housing guide notes that students in the blocks around Third, Crescent and Cambreleng Avenues tend to graduate and leave at the same time, so a building can lose every tenant viewer on one floor in a single week. If each apartment has its own viewer account, the owner deletes those accounts from the app and creates new ones for the incoming tenants; nothing on the cameras or recorder changes. If the previous owner gave everyone a shared password, that password should be changed when the group leaves, and every remaining user given the new one. We show owners how to do this themselves at handover, because it is not worth a service call twice a year.

Shop doors and counters

After a break-in like the one at Arthur Fashion, what camera position gives police a usable face, not just a shape?

A camera inside the glass, at roughly head height, aimed out through the door and slightly down. The Bronx Times reported that the Arthur Fashion burglar broke the glass door with a brick just before 6 a.m., which is the moment a door camera exists for. A camera mounted high in a ceiling corner looks down at the top of a hood; one inside the glass sees the face of anyone who approaches. Two details make it work. The camera should be set to cover a narrow field, roughly the door frame, so a face fills a good part of the picture instead of being a few pixels in a wide view of the sidewalk. And it needs to handle the light at dawn, when the street is brighter than the shop, which is where a camera with wide dynamic range earns its place.

We buzz customers in, the way jewelers on Arthur Avenue have done. Can a screen at the counter show who is at the door before we release it?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things a camera does in a buzz-in shop. DNAinfo reported in 2011 that robbers posing as customers were buzzed into Spinelli & Son Jewelers on Arthur Avenue, which is exactly the decision a counter screen helps with. We put a camera outside or just inside the door at face height, and a monitor at the counter that shows that camera full-screen at all times, not in a grid of small views. The person at the counter can see the face, hands and companions of anyone waiting before pressing the release. The monitor mounting is priced from our Bronx list; the camera and its cable are quoted with the rest of the system.

Our shop door looks out onto a bright street most of the day. Why do customers show up as dark silhouettes on the door camera, and how is that fixed?

The camera is setting its exposure for the bright street behind the customer, so the customer's face, lit only by the shop's interior, comes out dark. It is the most common complaint about storefront cameras. The fixes, roughly in order: choose a camera with true wide dynamic range, which balances the bright and dark parts of the scene; move the camera so the door is seen at an angle rather than straight against the daylight; narrow the field so the camera meters on the doorway, not the sky and the street; and, where needed, add light inside the entrance so faces are lit from the shop side. We test the door camera at the brightest time of the store's day before we leave, not only in the late afternoon when it looks fine.

Will a camera hold up in a restaurant kitchen next to a hot oven, or inside a walk-in cooler?

It can, with the right camera in the right spot, but kitchens are hard on electronics. Grease in the air coats the lens and the housing, heat near ovens and fryers shortens a camera's life, and steam fogs the dome. In a kitchen we choose a sealed camera rated for the temperatures involved, mount it away from the direct heat and the hood exhaust, and point it across the line rather than over the stove. Inside a walk-in cooler, the problem is condensation every time the door opens; a camera mounted outside the cooler aimed at the door usually serves better than one inside. Kitchen lenses need cleaning on a schedule, and we show staff how to do it without knocking the aim.

Streets, crowds and neighbors

When Arthur Avenue closes to cars for Ferragosto or weekend dining, what should a shop's outside camera be watching?

Your doorway, your tables and your glass, not the whole street. On festival days like Ferragosto, which BronxBuzz lists for September 13, 2026 between East 187th Street and Crescent Avenue, and on the car-free dining evenings Time Out described from East 188th Street to Crescent, the sidewalk in front of a shop fills with people, tables and stalls. A wide camera trying to watch the whole block in that crowd shows a sea of heads. A camera aimed at your own entrance and any outdoor seating you run shows who comes in, who leaves without paying and who leans on the window. If your outdoor seating moves into the roadway on those days, a second camera on the facade aimed at that area is worth considering, set up before the season rather than the morning of the fair.

Our outside camera has to look across the street. Can it be set so it never shows the apartment windows opposite?

Yes. Almost every recorder and camera we install supports privacy masks, which black out chosen parts of the picture permanently, both live and in recordings. On a Belmont block, where five-story walk-ups face each other across a narrow street, an exterior camera aimed at your own door will often catch windows on the other side. We aim the camera as tightly as the job allows first, then draw masks over any windows still in view, and check the result from the phone app. It is the considerate thing to do, and it also means your footage never becomes the subject of a neighbor's complaint. We include the masking on the written plan so you can see what is covered and what is blocked before we start.

My neighbor's new camera seems to look straight into our rear yard. What does New York law say about that?

New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a, sometimes called the backyard camera law, gives a residential owner or tenant the right to sue a neighbor who installs or uses video equipment to observe or record the private areas of their yard without consent. Belmont's rear yards sit close together behind the walk-ups, so a camera meant for one building's fire escape can easily take in the next yard. The first step is usually a conversation: most people do not realize what their camera sees, and a privacy mask or a slightly different angle solves it. If that does not work, it is a question for a lawyer, not for us. When we install cameras on your building, we aim and mask them so your own system never raises the same problem with your neighbors.

Rules, records and elevator buildings

The 48th Precinct asked for our video after an incident on the block. How do we hand it over without giving up the recorder?

You export a clip, not the machine. Every recorder we install can save a chosen camera and time range to a USB stick or send it through the app, usually in the recorder's own format with its player attached, or as a standard video file. Find the start and end time of the incident, export a few minutes either side, and give the detective the copy. Keep the original on the recorder, and do not reset or reformat it while the case is open. Write down the camera name, the date and time shown on the footage, and whether the recorder's clock was correct, because a clock that is ten minutes off confuses everyone later. At handover we show the owner and the super how to do an export themselves, and we set the recorder's clock to sync automatically.

We rent the store on East 187th Street. Do we need the building owner's permission to put a camera on the front of the building?

In nearly every case, yes. The facade, the awning frame and the brick belong to the building owner, and most commercial leases say alterations to the exterior need the landlord's written consent. Cameras inside your own store, aimed out through your own glass, are a different matter and usually fall within your space, but check your lease's alteration clause. If you want an exterior camera, send us the lease clause or ask the owner for a short written OK naming the camera position, and we will include the mounting method in that request so the owner knows how the brick will be fixed and patched later. If the building owner is also putting in cameras, it is often simpler to share the cable route and keep separate accounts.

Our building is an elevator rehab like Belartes Court. Can a camera go inside the elevator cab?

Often, but it is a job for the elevator company and us together. The camera itself is small and mounts in a ceiling corner of the cab, but its signal has to get from a moving cab to the recorder, either through the elevator's own traveling cable or over a wireless link designed for elevator shafts. Touching the traveling cable or working in the shaft is work for the building's licensed elevator contractor, and they usually need to be present. In five-story elevator buildings like the three that make up Belartes Court on Arthur and Belmont Avenues, a camera outside the elevator door in the lobby, aimed at people getting in, often answers the same question for much less cost. We price both options so the owner can choose.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Belmont

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or its People Also Ask boxes for this neighborhood from our build environment, so we will not pretend to quote them word for word. These are claims that turn up repeatedly in quick online answers about cameras in New York and in this part of the Bronx, checked against the public record and our own field practice. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that feed many of those answers; they give a rough idea of a national average, but not a price for a five-story walk-up on Hughes Avenue.

"New York City requires security cameras in apartment buildings."

It does not, as a general rule. Seneca Security, which writes about NYC building compliance, says there is no general city law mandating cameras in apartment buildings. What the law does require of owners, according to HPD's guide for owners, is to provide and maintain security measures, heat, hot and cold water, and good lighting.

Cameras get added for other reasons: tenants asking for them, an insurer asking for them, or trouble at the door. In Belmont that trouble is often at the shared vestibule of a walk-up or the glass of a ground-floor store.

The practical point is that a camera system is the owner's choice and the owner's design. Nobody is going to inspect it against a standard, so it has to be planned around what you actually need to see.

"Belmont is a historic district, so exterior cameras need Landmarks approval."

Belmont has the feel of a historic district, and AI answers sometimes assume it is one. It is not. None of the Bronx historic districts on the city's designated list, such as Mott Haven, Clay Avenue, Morris Avenue, Fieldston or Riverdale, is in Belmont.

The Historic Districts Council has listed the Arthur Avenue Retail Market, Our Lady of Mount Carmel and Aquinas High School as sites worth considering for designation, and the BID said in 2018 that it would ask the Landmarks Preservation Commission to protect a small area. Those are proposals.

So today, an exterior camera on a Belmont walk-up needs the building owner's agreement and a careful fixing into the brick, not a Landmarks filing. If any designation happens, we will update this page.

"City law requires every Bronx shop to have cameras and post signs."

Two specific claims circulate online. One vendor blog says a "Local Law 26" requires certain businesses, including laundromats, pharmacies and electronics stores, to keep cameras. Another says a "Local Law 50" requires building owners to post visible notice that cameras are operating.

We tried to confirm both against city law and could not. That does not prove they are wrong, but it means we will not tell a Belmont shopkeeper that either applies to them.

If you run a business type that is licensed by a city or state agency, ask that agency or your own lawyer what it requires. Many owners post a small notice anyway, because a visible camera sign discourages trouble at the door.

"You need every tenant's consent to put cameras in the halls."

Not for common areas. In New York City, landlords may install cameras in lobbies, hallways, stairwells, mailrooms and exterior areas of their buildings. What they may not do is place cameras where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as inside apartments or bathrooms.

The flip side is also true. Tenants generally have no right to put their own cameras in shared halls or lobbies without the landlord's permission, and a landlord is not obliged to share footage with a tenant.

In Belmont's walk-ups, where the owner may live upstairs from the tenants, these rules are worth explaining to everyone before the cameras go up. We aim stair cameras along the stair, not into apartment doorways.

"Anyone can put up cameras; there is no license involved."

The sources disagree, which is why short answers get it wrong. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license.

The state Department of State's installer application, on the other hand, says installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require licensure. Where a plain recording camera falls, and where a camera tied to a door release or an alarm falls, is not something a quick answer settles.

We sidestep the question by holding the license: our work is done under NYS license #12000287431. For owners, the useful step is to ask any installer for their license number and proof of insurance before they drill into your building.

"Bronx crime is falling, so cameras matter less in Belmont."

Borough-wide numbers hide a lot. Bronx.com reported on October 27, 2026 that major crime in the Bronx fell 11.9 percent in the first nine months of 2026. In the same year, the 48th Precinct's CompStat report for the week of August 31 to September 6 showed 200 burglaries year to date, up from 189.

The car-theft case Norwood News covered in April 2026 is a reminder of what cameras are for: prosecutors said many of the 252 thefts charged, nine of them in the 48th Precinct, were captured on surveillance video.

A camera does not change a borough's statistics. It changes what happens after something goes wrong at your door: whether there is a face, a time and a clip to hand over.

"A camera system costs about the same for any Bronx building."

Cost guides tend to give a single range per camera or per system, and AI answers repeat it. In Belmont the building moves the price far more than the camera does.

A four-camera system in an owner-occupied house with a basement is one kind of job. Four cameras in a five-story walk-up, where the cable climbs from the cellar to the roof without a hollow wall to use, and the store downstairs needs its own private account, is another, even with the same cameras.

That is why we quote after photos or a walk, list every camera point and cable route separately, and do not quote a number that is not built from our own list for your building.

Our Installation Process

Every Belmont camera job, from two cameras on a rowhouse to a full mixed-use building, follows the same eight steps. The order matters most in walk-ups, where a cable route chosen in a hurry is the usual reason a camera ends up with a visible wire across a hall or a hole through someone's apartment.

1. Send photos and the building type

The front door, the shop entrance if there is one, the cellar and the rear yard, plus whether the building is a walk-up, an apartment over a store, an owner-occupied house, a co-op or a business.

2. Walk it from cellar to roof

Power, internet, the wall between store and building in the cellar, each landing's light, the roof bulkhead and the view of the yards.

3. Write the camera plan

Each camera point with what it must show, who may watch it and what must be masked, and a line-by-line written quote.

4. Schedule around store and tenants

Drilling timed to shop hours and street closures; certificate of insurance sent to the owner, board or managing agent ahead.

5. Run cable and set the recorder

Cable pulled on the agreed route, fire-rated openings sealed, recorder, switch and battery backup locked away from the door.

6. Mount and aim the cameras

Fixed into brick, block or framing with anchors suited to each, and aimed so a face at each door fills enough of the picture.

7. Create the viewing accounts

Owner, super, store and tenant accounts with only their cameras, and privacy masks over windows across the street and neighbors' yards.

8. Test day and night, then hand over

Every camera checked in daylight and after dark, a clip export shown to the owner, and a written record of cameras, cables and accounts left behind.

Belmont Security Camera Installation FAQ

Does a Belmont shop have to post a sign saying cameras are recording?
+

We could not confirm a city rule that requires it. One vendor blog cites a "Local Law 50" requiring posted notice of cameras, but we were unable to verify that law, so we do not tell customers it applies. Many Arthur Avenue shops and Belmont landlords post a small notice anyway, because a visible sign at the door discourages trouble. If you want certainty for your business type, ask your own lawyer or the agency that licenses you.

Is a pharmacy or laundromat in Belmont required by city law to have cameras?
+

A vendor source claims a "Local Law 26" requires laundromats, pharmacies and electronics stores to install and maintain cameras. We could not verify it in city law, so we cannot tell you it applies. Check with the agency that licenses your business or with counsel. If you decide to install cameras either way, we plan the door, counter and back room coverage the same as for any other shop.

Which parts of a walk-up apartment building can a landlord legally put on camera?
+

Common areas: the lobby or vestibule, hallways, stairwells, the mailroom, the cellar and the outside of the building. Cameras may not go where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as inside apartments or bathrooms. In practice we aim stair and hall cameras along the stair and corridor rather than into any apartment's doorway, and we mask anything that looks into a private space.

As a tenant, can I get a copy of the hallway footage after my bike was taken?
+

You can ask, but the landlord is not obliged to give it to you. The footage belongs to the building owner. The quickest route is usually to file a police report and give the precinct the date and time; the police can request the clip from the owner. If you are on good terms with the owner or super, a polite request with the exact time often works, and a viewer with export rights can pull it in minutes.

Can a camera on our building cover the city parking lot between Arthur Avenue and Hoffman Street?
+

A camera on your own building can record what is plainly visible from it, including a public lot, as long as it does not look into anyone's private space. We would aim it at the part of the lot that matters to you, such as the entrance nearest your back door or where your delivery van parks, and mask any windows in view. Cameras mounted on the lot itself would be a matter for whoever controls it.

Can a stall inside a shared market hall have its own camera?
+

Usually, if the hall's management allows it. A vendor in a shared market can often mount a small camera on its own stall facing its counter and register, recording to a compact recorder kept locked in the stall. Power, mounting and any cable crossing shared space are management's decision, so get their written permission and rules first, then send them to us with your stall layout.

Does a co-op walk-up need the board's approval before cameras go up in the halls?
+

In most co-ops, yes. Building-wide work such as a new camera system usually needs board approval, and the managing agent will often ask for a certificate of insurance and set working hours. Get the resolution and the agent's requirements to us before booking. We also suggest the board decide in advance who holds the administrator account and what the super can see.

What happens to the camera system and its logins when we sell the building?
+

The cameras and recorder normally stay with the building, but the accounts should change hands. Before closing, give the buyer the administrator login, delete your own phone access and any former tenants' accounts, and hand over the written record of cameras and cable routes we left you. The buyer should then change the administrator password. If the old record is lost, we can trace and relabel the system for the new owner.

Yes. The recorder can drive a monitor that shows every camera in a grid, or one camera full-screen, such as the shop door.
