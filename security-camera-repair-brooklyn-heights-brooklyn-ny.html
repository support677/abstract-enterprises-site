The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Brooklyn Heights is small, about a third of a square mile bounded by Old Fulton Street, Cadman Plaza West, Atlantic Avenue and the East River, and almost all of it was built before anyone imagined a camera on a front door. Houses went up block by block through the nineteenth century, and Wikipedia's history of the neighborhood describes it as almost fully developed by 1890. After the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883 and the subway arrived in 1908, many of the large single-family houses were divided into apartments and rooming houses. That is the building stock every camera system here has to live in: masonry walls, plaster ceilings, floors that have been reframed more than once, and wiring paths that nobody planned. Cameras were added to these buildings later, usually in a hurry after something happened, and the repair work today is often about undoing a quick installation as much as replacing a part.

Residents have been asking for cameras for a long time, and that history explains much of what we find on the walls. In March 2017, DNAinfo reported that the Brooklyn Heights Association planned street-facing cameras on homes along Joralemon Street, roughly two per block, between Furman and Clinton streets, with more on commercial buildings toward Court Street. The money came from a $2,500 grant from the Borough President's office plus contributions from homeowners. Peter Bray, then the association's executive director, put the case simply: "Having some cameras on the street could one, be a deterrent, and two, help police apprehend perpetrators." The Brooklyn Paper added that the homeowners hosting those cameras would own the footage and give police access when officers needed it. Cameras put up under plans like that are now close to a decade old, and on private houses the owner, not the city, is the one who has to keep them running.

The case for working cameras has not gone away. The same 2017 DNAinfo report said the 84th Precinct, which covers Brooklyn Heights, had recorded 32 burglaries so far that year against 12 in the same stretch of 2016. Patch and Brooklyn Paper coverage of that winter described daytime break-ins on Willow, Joralemon and Montague streets, many through open or unlocked windows, some possibly reached from a fire escape. More recently, News 12 reported that on the morning of March 2, 2024, four people burglarized a convenience store on Atlantic Avenue, two smashing through the glass while two kept watch, before running toward Hicks Street. A few days later it reported two men ordering an employee away from a register at a Henry Street store and leaving with about $670 in merchandise. Council Member Lincoln Restler has also funded cameras at two entrances to the Brooklyn Heights Promenade at the 84th Precinct's request, describing them as "more eyes to ensure the safety of both locals and tourists."

What the news rarely says is whether the cameras nearby actually worked. When thieves smashed the glass door of the AT&T store at 160 Montague Street early one morning in July 2022 and took display phones and watches, the Brooklyn Eagle quoted the store manager saying he had no video of the break-in, though he believed police could get footage from elsewhere. That is the most common story we hear from owners after an incident: the camera was there, the recorder was there, and nothing usable was saved, because a drive filled with errors weeks earlier, a camera lost power after a renovation, or the clock was so far off that the clip could not be matched to the report. A repair is how you find that out before the night it matters.

The historic district changes how the work is done. The Brooklyn Heights Historic District was designated on November 23, 1965, the first in New York City, and the Historic Districts Council describes it as a district of brownstone and brick houses, churches and carriage houses. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook sets the rules for cameras on buildings like these: equipment should be well spaced so the facade does not look cluttered, small buildings should carry no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of frontage, mounts go into mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and visible conduit is limited to about ten inches. The same chapter says ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment needs no permit. For a repair, that means a like-for-like swap is usually straightforward, while moving a camera to a new spot on the front of the house is the moment to check the rules first.

Finally, ownership. A Brooklyn Heights camera system may belong to a single family, to a landlord with five tenants, to a co-op board, to a condominium's management office, or to the shop on the ground floor, and sometimes to two of those at once. Brick Underground's reporting on New York co-ops notes that boards can pass house rules on cameras in common areas, and its advice for tenants is that landlords are not obliged to show footage outside a legal process and that city buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. Before we change anything, we confirm whose equipment it is, who holds the password, and who approves the price.

Six Brooklyn Heights facts that shape a camera repair

  • Most buildings are landmarked. The historic district, about 140 acres according to The Cultural Landscape Foundation, covers most of the neighborhood. In-kind camera repairs are ordinary maintenance; new spots on a facade follow the Commission's spacing and mounting rules.
  • The houses are old and often divided. Nearly every lot was built by 1890, and many large houses became multiple apartments after 1908, so camera cables cross floors and units that different people control.
  • Block cameras sit on private homes. The 2017 Joralemon Street plan put street-facing cameras on houses whose owners keep the footage, which makes the homeowner responsible for repairs.
  • Storefront incidents are recent. The March 2024 Atlantic Avenue burglary and the February 2024 Henry Street theft were both reported by News 12 Brooklyn, and the 2022 Montague Street break-in left the store with no video of its own.
  • The commercial core is small and busy. The Montague Street Business Improvement District counts more than 100 businesses, many in old buildings with recorders in back rooms and cellars.

The calls that cannot wait in Brooklyn Heights usually start in one of three ways. The second is a renovation: a kitchen redone, a facade repointed, a floor opened for plumbing, and afterwards a camera or two simply never came back. The third is a lost key, not a metal one: the person who set up the system has moved, sold the house, left the co-op board or stopped managing the building, and nobody left knows the recorder password.

If you need footage from an incident, leave the recorder as it is. Do not unplug it, restart it or let anyone reset it to factory settings to "get it working," because each of those can lose the hours you are trying to keep. It goes to a USB drive in the recorder's own format and in an ordinary video file, with the recorder's clock noted against real time, so it can go to the 84th Precinct or an insurer. Only then do we start testing. If the footage was never recorded, because the drive had stopped writing or the camera had no power, we tell you straight away, and we check the recorder's own logs to see how long it had been down.

For everything else the process is the same: the $150 service call, booked within 2 hours of your request, a two-hour arrival window, and a written price before any part changes. Security Camera Repair for Brooklyn Heights' Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every camera call we take in Brooklyn Heights. What separates them for a repair is where the recorder ended up, how many floors and owners the cable crosses, and who has the right to approve the work.

The single-family brownstone or brick row house

Greek Revival, Italianate and later houses on the side streets, with cameras on the stoop, in the vestibule and over the rear garden, and a recorder in the cellar or a parlor-floor closet.

The house divided into apartments

Large houses split into floor-through units long ago, often owned by a landlord who lives elsewhere, with cameras on the shared entry and cable running past several tenants' doors.

The prewar co-op or elevator building

Apartment houses and converted hotels with lobby, elevator, package room and courtyard cameras, a monitor at the front desk, and a board and managing agent who approve the work.

The carriage house on a mews

Former stables, like those on Grace Court Alley, turned into homes, with garage-style openings, short frontages and cable that has nowhere obvious to go.

The storefront building

Shops and restaurants on Montague, Henry, Clark and Atlantic, with apartments above, cameras inside and over the door, and the recorder in a back room or cellar.

The newer and converted building

Postwar and recent apartment buildings and large conversions near the north end, with network cameras, server closets and an office that runs them.

The single-family brownstone: the cellar recorder and the parlor-floor door

A one-family house in Brooklyn Heights usually has a small system: a camera over the door at the top of the stoop, one in or above the vestibule, one at the garden-level entrance under the stoop, and one or two at the back looking over the yard. The recorder is wherever the person who bought it found an outlet and a network jack, most often in the cellar near the boiler and the internet box, or in a closet on the parlor floor. The faults we see most in these houses are not exotic. A plug-in power adapter behind a bookcase gets kicked loose. A drive in a warm cellar closet wears out. A camera on the rear wall loses its connection because the cable was stapled along the outside of the brick and has cracked in the sun. We test each of those in turn, and in a house this size the cause is usually clear within the first half hour.

The part that takes care in these houses is the route. Cable from the front of a brownstone to the cellar either drops down inside the stoop, runs through the parlor floor and down a wall cavity, or comes down the facade. When it has to be replaced, the path decides the price, which is why our Brooklyn list includes the $100 to $200 brownstone access charge for pulls through plaster or between floors. We look for the path that is already there, an old bell wire chase, a gas pipe sleeve that is no longer used, a closet stacked above a closet, before suggesting any new opening, and we tell you before we start what will be disturbed and what will not.

The house divided into apartments: one system, many doors

Many of the bigger houses in the neighborhood became multiple apartments after the subway arrived in 1908. Their camera systems typically cover the street door, the vestibule and mailboxes, the hall, and sometimes the yard or the roof door, with a recorder in the cellar or a utility closet that only the landlord or super opens. The difficulty is that the cable from the hall camera on the third floor may run through the closet of the second-floor apartment and down past the garden unit's kitchen, and nobody wrote that down. A repair that needs to follow that cable means coordinating access to apartments the owner does not occupy.

We plan that with you before the visit. If the fault can be found at the recorder and the cellar end, nobody upstairs is disturbed. If we need to reach a junction or a camera inside someone's unit, we tell you which apartment and roughly how long, so you can give the tenant the notice the lease requires. And because tenants often ask, it is worth saying that a landlord's camera system is the landlord's: we take instructions on the repair from the owner or the managing agent, and we do not hand out footage or logins to anyone the owner has not named.

The prewar co-op: the board, the desk and the elevator

Brooklyn Heights has a long list of prewar apartment buildings and converted hotels, the best known being the Hotel St. George complex on the block bounded by Pineapple, Henry, Clark and Hicks streets, which Wikipedia notes has held co-op apartments since 1984 and student housing since the late 1990s. Buildings like these have the largest camera systems in the neighborhood: lobby and entrance cameras, elevator cameras, package and bike room cameras, service entrance and courtyard cameras, all wired back to a recorder in a basement office or a closet behind the front desk, with a monitor that the doorman or concierge watches.

Repairs in co-ops follow the building's process. The managing agent will normally want a certificate of insurance and set the hours, the super will hold the keys to the basement and the roof, and the board may need to approve anything beyond a like-for-like repair. We work inside that process. Practically, it also means writing things down: the board and the managing agent usually want to know what failed, what was replaced, what is likely to fail next, and whether the recorder location still makes sense. We provide that in plain language, so it can go straight into the board packet.

The carriage house: short fronts and no obvious cable path

The mews of Brooklyn Heights are some of its most distinctive streets. City Lore describes Grace Court Alley as a one-block dead end off Hicks Street lined with mid-nineteenth-century carriage houses that served the mansions on Joralemon and Remsen streets, two or three stories high, most still keeping their hay cranes, later turned into garages and then homes. As camera sites they are awkward: the frontage is short, the wide opening that was once for carriages takes up most of the ground floor, and the walls are thick masonry with little room inside for cable. Cameras on these houses tend to be mounted high near the old loft door and wired straight through the wall, which is exactly where water finds its way in. On a repair we reseal that entry, check the camera for moisture, and look for a better path when the cable has to be replaced.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Tracing a Camera Fault Through a Brownstone That Has Been Renovated Three Times

These notes describe how our technicians work through a camera system in an old, much-altered house of the kind that fills Brooklyn Heights. They set out standard practice, not the story of any particular address.

Start at the recorder and write down the clock. Before touching anything, the technician photographs the screen: which channels show live video, which are black or frozen, what the drive status says, and what time the recorder thinks it is. If the clock is wrong, any clip already saved will be off by the same amount, and that goes in the notes for whoever needs the footage.

Follow the renovation history. The technician asks what work has been done and when: a new kitchen, a bathroom moved, a facade repointed, a roof replaced. In a house altered several times, the dead camera usually sits just past the most recent job, and the cable is usually cut or crushed where that work met the old route.

Measure at both ends. Voltage at the camera, PoE power at the switch port, and a continuity or analyzer test on the cable show in minutes whether the camera, the cable or the power has failed. Nothing is unplugged or reset until any footage you asked for has been saved.

Respect the facade. If a camera on the front of a landmarked house has to be remounted, it goes back into a mortar joint or plain masonry, never into carved stone or cast iron, and exposed cable is kept as short as the Commission's guidance allows. Old holes that the new mount does not cover are noted for your mason or painter, since we do not patch or paint.

Price first, label last. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the work is done, every cable we traced is labeled at the recorder, the login is handed to the person you name, and the notes stay on your job record for next time.

Fault by Fault

Renovations, Silent Recorders, Lost Logins and Old Facades: How Brooklyn Heights Camera Systems Fail and What Each Repair Involves

Four patterns cover most of the camera failures we find in Brooklyn Heights. Each leaves its own evidence, and reading that evidence correctly is what keeps the bill to the part that actually broke.

Renovation damage: the camera that never came back

Old houses here are renovated constantly, and camera cable is the least visible thing in the wall. A plumber opens a chase and cuts what looks like abandoned wire. An electrician moves a junction box and leaves a camera's power adapter without an outlet. A painter tapes over a camera, or a mason pointing the front of the house pulls the cable out of a joint and pushes it back loose. Afterwards one or two cameras are dark and everyone involved is sure it was not their work. We test from the recorder end out, find where the signal or power stops, and then decide whether the run can be spliced in a junction box at that point or has to be replaced. A connector reterminate runs $150 to $350 on our Brooklyn list, a single camera replaced on a working system $250 to $600, and a pull through plaster or between floors can add the $100 to $200 brownstone access charge.

The silent recorder: live view fine, playback empty

The most expensive camera fault in the neighborhood is the one nobody notices. The recorder shows live pictures on the monitor and the phone, so everyone assumes it is working, while the drive has stopped saving because it failed, filled up with errors, or was never set to overwrite. Owners discover it the morning after a burglary or a package theft, which is the situation the manager of the Montague Street phone store described in 2022 when he told the Brooklyn Eagle he had no video. On a repair we read the drive's health, check the recording schedule for every camera, confirm that overwrite is on, and look at the recorder's log for the first day it stopped writing. A drive swap runs $200 to $450 depending on capacity; if the recorder itself has failed, a DVR repair averages $300 to $700 and an NVR $400 to $900, and a full replacement with a drive runs $700 to $1,500.

The lost login: the system works, but nobody can get in

Brooklyn Heights homes change hands, co-op boards turn over and storefront tenants come and go, and the person who set up the recorder takes the password with them. Sometimes the system keeps recording for years without anyone able to view or export a clip, which is only discovered when a clip is needed. Recovering access depends on the brand and model. Many common recorders can be reset with the manufacturer's procedure and proof of the device's serial number, and some can be reset only by restoring factory settings, which keeps the recordings but loses the camera setup. We tell you which case you are in before we do anything, save any footage that can be reached first, and then set up new administrator and viewer accounts for the people you name. With no parts needed, that is usually covered within the $150 service call.

The old facade: mounts, moisture and the historic-district rules

Cameras on the fronts of Brooklyn Heights houses are attached to materials that were never meant to hold them: brownstone that flakes when drilled, soft old brick, painted wood trim, and cast iron railings and areaway gates. A camera screwed straight into brownstone often works loose as the stone around the anchor crumbles, and its cable entry becomes a path for water into both the camera and the wall. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook points to the right places, mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and away from cast iron and decorative surfaces. When we remount a camera on a landmarked front, we follow that, use the smallest suitable bracket, seal the cable entry and keep visible conduit short.

The same guidebook is the reason a straight repair is usually simple. It treats ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of safety equipment as work that does not need a Commission permit, and its in-kind replacement path asks for manufacturer cut sheets rather than drawings. A new camera in the old spot, of similar size and finish, is a repair. A new camera in a new spot on the front of the house, or a third camera on a narrow facade, is a change that deserves a check with the Commission first, and we will tell you which side of that line your job falls on.

Age: coax systems, discontinued models and tired power

Plenty of Heights systems date from the 2000s and early 2010s: analog or early HD cameras on coax cable, a DVR in the cellar, and a central power box feeding every camera. They often still work, which is a good reason not to tear them out. Their weak points are the power box, which loses capacity with age until the cameras flicker or drop at night when the infrared comes on, the drive, and the cameras whose models are no longer made. Power supply repair runs $150 to $400. Where an old camera dies and its exact model is gone, a current camera that speaks the same coax format usually works with the old recorder, and where the recorder is the problem, a hybrid recorder lets the old coax cameras and new network cameras run together, at $700 to $1,200 installed.

After a break-in: what a Heights shop needs first

Business owners in Brooklyn Heights usually call for one of two reasons. Something happened, a smashed door, a grab at the register, a delivery that never reached the stockroom, and they need to know whether the system caught it. Or the landlord, the insurer or the precinct asked whether the cameras work, and nobody is sure. In both cases we start with recording and time: is the recorder saving, for how many days, and is its clock right. Any clip you need is exported before anything else, and only then do we start on the fault. The 2024 Atlantic Avenue burglary, where two people broke the glass while two kept lookout, is the kind of event where the camera aimed at the door and the one aimed at the sidewalk both matter, and the one aimed at the ceiling or the empty corner does not.

The second job is aiming. A camera set to show the whole sales floor turns the person at the door into a dark shape against the bright street, especially on narrow, tree-shaded blocks where the light outside changes all day. Re-aiming the entrance camera so it sees faces at the door, adjusting its exposure, or adding one camera dedicated to the entrance usually fixes it without replacing the system.

Cameras on the same network as the register

Many small businesses here have their network cameras, card terminals, phones and office computers on one router and one switch, set up years ago by whoever was around. A camera repair in that setup has to be careful not to knock the payment system offline in the middle of a lunch rush. We map what is plugged into what before we change anything, schedule anything that needs a restart for a quiet hour, and where it makes sense, suggest moving the cameras to their own switch so a camera fault can never take the register down with it. That suggestion is priced separately, and only if you want it.

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security and fire alarm systems, and the state's rules apply that to closed-circuit television used to detect intrusion, theft or movement. An owner working on their own property is exempt, but a company you hire to repair a shop's cameras should hold the license. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

Landmarked storefronts. Most of Montague Street and the side streets sit inside the historic district, and the Commission has its own guidance for storefronts. A camera replaced where the old one was is a repair. A new camera or new conduit on a storefront facade is a change worth checking first.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brooklyn Heights homeowners, landlords, co-op boards, tenants and shopkeepers describe when they call about cameras on landmarked fronts, in divided houses and in storefronts. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Landmarked fronts and old surfaces

Our cameras went up before we bought the house, and we can't find any Landmarks paperwork for them. Does repairing them mean we have to apply now?

Not for a repair on its own. The Commission's guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment as work that does not need a permit, so fixing or replacing a camera in the spot where it already hangs, with something of similar size and finish, does not trigger an application. What a repair should not do is quietly add to the front of the house: a new spot, extra cameras beyond the guidebook's two per 25 feet on a small building, or a long run of visible conduit. If the existing setup already goes past that, we repair what you have without making it worse and point out what a future change would need. Questions about a specific building's history are for the Commission, not for us, and we will say so.

The camera by our areaway gate is clamped to the cast-iron railing. Can the replacement go back in the same place?

Often yes, as long as nothing has to be drilled into the iron. The guidebook says equipment must not be attached through cast iron or decorative surfaces, so the old clamp position can be reused if the new camera fits the same bracket or a clamp that grips without new holes. If the old installer drilled the railing, we do not add to that; we look for a nearby mortar joint or a plain masonry surface instead, or a spot inside the areaway that sees the gate just as well. Rust on the clamp is worth dealing with at the same time, since iron that is left to corrode around a fastener is harder to fix later than a camera.

The old vestibule camera left a cluster of holes in our original plaster. Can the new one cover them?

Usually. Many replacement cameras can sit on a mounting base or a small junction box wider than the camera itself, and choosing one that covers the old holes is part of how we pick the replacement. Where the old holes are spread too far apart, we place the new base over the worst of them and note the rest. We do not patch or paint walls, so any holes left uncovered are for your plasterer or painter, and in a vestibule with original moldings that is the right person anyway. We tell you before the work which holes will be covered and which will not, so nobody is surprised when the old camera comes down.

Divided houses, co-ops and tenants

Our house was split into apartments decades ago, and the camera cables pass through units we don't live in. How do you reach them without disrupting the tenants?

We start where we can reach without anyone's door: the recorder, the cellar, the hallway and the outside of the house. A tone generator and probe at the recorder end, plus a test at the camera, often show where a run has failed without opening anything inside an apartment. If the fault really is inside a unit, behind a closet wall or at a junction box in a ceiling, we tell you which apartment, what we need to reach and roughly how long it will take, so you can give the tenant notice and arrange a time. Where a run keeps causing trouble, the long-term fix is often a new route through the common hall or a stacked closet, priced separately, so future repairs never need to go through anyone's home.

Our co-op board is drafting a rule about video doorbells and hallway cameras. Should we hold off on repairing the building system until it passes?

Usually not. Rules like that are about what individual shareholders mount at their own doors, and Brick Underground's lawyer column notes that a board can adopt such a ban as a house rule, especially for cameras in common areas the board controls. The building's own lobby, entrance and elevator cameras are a separate matter: they are the board's equipment, and leaving them broken while a rule is debated only leaves the building without coverage. The one thing worth waiting for is any change in where the building's cameras point, for example away from apartment doors, so we re-aim once instead of twice. Tell us what the draft says and we will repair around it.

I rent in a Brooklyn Heights building and the entrance cameras have been dead for months. Does the landlord have to fix them?

Not necessarily. Brick Underground's reporting, quoting a tenant lawyer, is that city buildings are not legally required to have working security cameras, and that landlords are not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process. That does not make a dead camera system harmless, and many landlords will fix one once they know it is down, especially after a package theft or a break-in. The practical step is to tell the landlord or managing agent in writing which cameras are dark and since when. We take repair instructions from the building's owner or agent, so the booking has to come from them, and we are glad to give them a price.

Block cameras, break-ins and the precinct

Our Joralemon Street house hosts one of the street-facing block cameras from the association's plan, and it has gone dark. Whose job is it to fix it?

Under the arrangement the Brooklyn Paper described in 2017, the homeowners hosting those cameras owned the footage and agreed to give police access when needed, which in practice makes the camera part of your system and its upkeep yours. Check any paperwork you signed with the association for anything it says about maintenance, but in most cases the repair is booked like any other. It is usually a power or connection fault on a camera that has been outside for years, and the fix is the same as for your own stoop camera. While we are there we check that the recorder keeps the street camera's footage for as long as you expected and that its clock is correct, since a clip for the precinct is only useful if the time on it is right.

After a break-in we discovered our recorder hadn't saved anything for weeks. How do we find out when it stopped?

The recorder usually tells us. Most recorders keep a system log that records drive errors, recording stops, power losses and restarts, and the playback calendar shows the last day each camera has saved video. Between the two we can normally put a date on when recording stopped and why, whether the drive failed, filled without overwriting, lost power, or had its recording schedule switched off. That date matters for an insurance claim, a lease dispute or a conversation with a former installer. We write it into the repair notes, fix the cause, and turn on the warning that should have told you, so a silent failure becomes a phone alert next time.

Vestibules, carriage houses and the shop floor

The camera between our outer and inner doors fogs up on winter mornings whenever the street door opens. Is moisture getting inside it?

Usually the fog is on the outside of the window, not inside the camera. A vestibule between two doors sits at a different temperature from both the street and the hall, and when cold, damp air rushes in, the cool face of the camera collects condensation for a few minutes. If it clears within the hour, the camera is fine, and the fix is positioning: moving it slightly away from the outer door, adding a small hood, or using a camera with a heated or treated window. If the fog sits behind the glass and lingers, or there are drops or spots inside, the seal has failed and the camera needs replacing, at $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list.

Our carriage house has one camera high on the front by the old loft door, and every heavy rain it drops out for a day. What is going on?

Almost certainly water at the cable entry. On many carriage houses the camera was mounted near the top of the facade and its cable pushed straight through the masonry beside it, and rain running down the wall follows the cable into the joint, into the connector or into the camera itself. When it dries out, the camera returns. We open the connection, look for corrosion and water marks, cut back and remake the joint if needed, reseal the wall entry with a drip loop so water falls off the cable instead of following it in, and check the camera for moisture inside. If the camera has been wet many times, replacing it is often cheaper than repeated visits.

Our medical office's cameras cover the waiting room and the front desk. What should we think about when they're repaired?

Mostly where the cameras look and who can see the recordings. A waiting room camera that also shows the screens at the front desk, or a corridor camera that looks into a treatment room when a door opens, is worth re-aiming while we are there. We keep cameras out of private spaces, check that the recorder and app logins belong only to the people you name, and remove accounts for former staff. Any rules specific to your practice, from your professional body or your own compliance adviser, are theirs to set, and we will follow what they tell you. Repairs in an open office are scheduled for before or after patient hours where possible.

Our Montague Street shop's cameras run on the same network as the card readers. Can you fix them without taking the payment system down?

Yes, with some planning. Before touching the network, we map which ports and devices belong to the cameras and which to the terminals, phones and computers, and anything that needs a restart of the switch or router is scheduled for an hour when you can spare it, or before you open. Most camera repairs, a camera, a cable, a drive or a recorder, never touch the payment side at all. Where cameras and terminals share one small switch, we may suggest giving the cameras their own, which keeps a camera fault from ever reaching the register. That is optional and priced on its own line.

Switches in closets, cellars and telecom rooms, shared with internet, phones and card readers in shops and offices.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units, tested under load and kept clear of the hot corners of boiler rooms.

PTZ cameras

Pan-tilt-zoom cameras on larger buildings and commercial fronts. PTZ repair in Brooklyn typically runs $450 to $1,200 because of the parts involved.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Brooklyn Heights

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each one is paraphrased, then checked against what the work actually looks like in a landmarked neighborhood of old, divided houses.

"Expect to pay around $1,400 to fix security cameras in Brooklyn"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page for installing or repairing surveillance cameras gives an average of $1,416, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and a low-to-high spread of $160 to $3,500. AI summaries often repeat the average as if it were the cost of a repair.

That number blends new systems with repairs, which drags it up. A Brooklyn Heights repair is usually a single fault: a cut cable behind a renovated kitchen, a failed drive, a worn power box, one camera at the door. On our Brooklyn list most single faults cost $150 to $700, and only a recorder replacement, a partial brownstone rewire or a whole-building overhaul goes higher.

For one problem, the realistic planning figure is the $150 service call plus the one failed part, with any brownstone access charge of $100 to $200 stated in writing before the work. Comparing that with an average built partly from other people's new installations only makes a small repair look larger than it is.

"In a historic district you need Landmarks approval to touch a camera"

Some AI answers say any camera work on a landmarked building needs a permit, which leaves owners in Brooklyn Heights afraid to fix a dead stoop camera.

The Commission's own guidebook says otherwise for repairs. Ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of safety equipment does not need a permit, and the guidebook sets out where cameras may go and how they should be fixed, rather than forbidding them. What does need care is change: a new location on the primary facade, more cameras than the frontage allows, or visible conduit beyond the short length staff can approve.

So the right question is not whether you may repair your camera, but whether the repair changes the front of the house. A like-for-like swap in the same spot generally does not. Moving it, adding to it or running new cable down the facade might, and that is the point to check with the Commission.

"If live view works, the system is recording"

Generic troubleshooting advice often treats a working live picture as proof that everything is fine.

Live view and recording are separate jobs inside the recorder. A camera can stream perfectly to a phone while the drive behind it has failed, filled up without overwriting, or had its schedule switched off. That is how a business ends up with cameras on the wall and no video of its own break-in, as the manager of the Montague Street phone store told the Brooklyn Eagle in 2022.

The only real test is playback: pick last night on each camera and see whether video is there, with the right time on it. Every repair visit we make includes that check, and turning on the recorder's drive and camera-loss alerts so a silent failure becomes a message.

"Wireless cameras avoid all the trouble of running cable in an old house"

AI answers frequently recommend replacing a wired system with Wi-Fi cameras to avoid opening walls in a brownstone.

In Brooklyn Heights, the walls that make cabling hard also make Wi-Fi hard. Thick brick and brownstone party walls, plaster on wire lath and a router in the cellar or the top-floor office leave the front and back of a deep row house with weak signal. Wireless cameras still need power, so many end up on extension cords or battery packs that someone has to remember to charge.

Wireless has its place, on a garden fence near a strong access point, for example. But for the door camera that matters most, the better repair is usually to fix the cable that is already in the wall, or to find a short new path for it, rather than to trade one problem for another.

"A forgotten recorder password means buying a new recorder"

Some answers suggest a locked recorder is a lost cause and should simply be replaced.

Most recorders from common brands have a recovery route, through the manufacturer's reset procedure, a security question or email set up at installation, or a reset that keeps the recordings even if it clears the settings. In a neighborhood where houses change hands and co-op boards turn over, we see locked recorders often, and most of them go back into service with new accounts and no new hardware.

The catch is footage. Some resets clear settings and keep video, others wipe everything, and which applies depends on the model. Before anything is reset, we save whatever footage can be reached and tell you exactly what the reset will do.

"Any handyman can swap a security camera"

Many answers treat camera repair as a quick job for anyone with a ladder and a drill.

On your own house you are free to try. But New York's Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and state rules cover closed-circuit television used to detect intrusion or theft. In a co-op, a condominium or a store, the board, the managing agent and the insurer usually want to know who did the work and whether they were licensed and insured. On a landmarked front, a drill in the wrong place, into carved brownstone or cast iron, is damage that no camera is worth.

If you hire someone, ask for the license number and a certificate of insurance. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, printed on this page, and a certificate for your board, landlord or managing agent can be arranged ahead of the visit.

"Cameras are required in New York apartment buildings, so the landlord must fix them"

AI answers sometimes tell tenants that building cameras are a legal requirement and that a landlord has to repair them on demand.

Brick Underground's reporting, quoting a tenant attorney, is that city buildings are not required to have working security cameras, and that landlords need not show footage outside a legal process. Intercoms and entrance doors have their own rules, but cameras, in most residential buildings, are something an owner chooses to provide and maintain.

That makes the repair a decision for the owner, the co-op board or the managing agent, and it is why we take instructions from them. Tenants who want the cameras fixed get the furthest by putting the request in writing, naming which cameras are down and since when.

Why can a repair in a brownstone cost more than the same fault in a newer building?
+

Because of the route the cable takes. In a newer building, camera cable usually runs through a ceiling or a riser that is easy to reach. Can I check our cameras from a computer at the office instead of a phone?
+

Usually yes. Do you work with the IT company that manages our office network?

Yes.
