Why Long Island Properties Need First Alert Camera Installation

Long Island's camera problem starts with how the island was built: one house at a time, on its own lot, with its own back door. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey five-year estimates put three in four Nassau housing units and four in five Suffolk units in detached one-family buildings. Nassau's median home was built in 1955, Suffolk's in 1970. In practical terms, a camera job here is rarely about a lobby, a riser or a building manager. It is about one family's front walk, side yard, driveway, garage, back patio and basement windows, all of which face the street or a neighbor rather than a hallway, and all of which need a camera that can be powered, reached by Wi-Fi or cable, and seen in an app. First Alert's VX cameras were designed for exactly that kind of job. Resideo, which owns First Alert, describes the VX Series as AI video built to work with its smart home products, with a video doorbell and indoor and outdoor cameras that installers can power by AC, Ethernet, Wi-Fi or PoE and tie into ProSeries and VISTA alarm panels.

The slab houses of Levittown and the towns around it

The Levitt houses, and the many copies of them built nearby in the same years, are the single most common reason a First Alert camera job on Long Island takes a different route than the homeowner expected. With no basement, there is no place to stand under the house and drill up into a wall. The original radiant heat pipes were in the slab, and while a heating contractor's account suggests only a small share of those systems are still in use, we never assume a slab is empty of pipes. So for a wired camera, the cable goes up into the attic, across the ceiling joists, and down to the soffit or the top of the wall where the camera mounts. The 1949 ranches in Levittown, as Kerri Murray Architecture notes, came with an expandable attic, which usually means there is room to move. The earlier Capes often had their attics finished into bedrooms decades ago, which leaves only the low knee-wall spaces at the eaves. That is often the place we work from, carefully, with a flashlight and a short drill.

Masonry Capes in Uniondale and other Hempstead hamlets

A Cape like the 1948 house Redfin lists at 1121 Front Street, with masonry exterior walls, changes the question from where to run the cable to whether to run it through the wall at all. Alarm Grid's install guide for the VX3 recommends a cable hole of 7/8 inch or larger. Through a frame wall that is a small hole. Through masonry it is a core of masonry removed with a hammer drill, which then has to be sealed so water does not follow the cable inside. Alarm Grid describes Resideo's optional back box as being for installations where in-wall wiring is not possible, able to accept up to three half-inch conduits, and rated IP66. On a masonry Cape we usually look at both: a sealed hole where the inside of the wall is easy to reach, and the back box fed by conduit along the outside where it is not. Either way, you see the plan before we drill.

Split-levels and high ranches, and the far-end Wi-Fi problem

The split-levels common along Uniondale Avenue and in many Nassau and Suffolk subdivisions of the 1950s and 1960s put short floors at different heights, with the router often in a den on one level and the bedrooms half a flight up at the other end. A camera over the garage or the back patio can end up two floors and several walls from the router. Alarm Grid's VX3 guide says to verify a minimum Wi-Fi signal of -65 dBm at the install location and a continuous upload speed of at least 2 Mbps. In a split-level, those are the numbers that fail first. When they do, we have three honest choices: run Ethernet and use PoE, add a wired access point closer to that end of the house, or move the camera to a spot the Wi-Fi already reaches. We measure before we suggest any of them.

Two-family houses and garden apartments

Movoto describes 176 Uniondale Avenue as a 1952 legal two-family, two bedrooms and a bath over two bedrooms and a bath. In a house like that, two households share one front door, one driveway and one back yard, but usually only one owns the house and pays for the First Alert account. Cameras on the outside of a two-family are the owner's decision, and the footage they capture of the shared yard and entrances needs to be agreed between the two households before the install, not argued about afterward. Redfin classes 1140 Front Street, built in 1959, as an apartment property of more than six families. In buildings like that, an individual tenant or shareholder can usually put a First Alert VX5 indoor camera inside the unit, and its automatic privacy shutter makes it easier to live with; anything outside the unit needs the landlord or board.

Suffolk colonials, North Shore estates and South Shore shingle houses

Farther east, the 1970 median build year shows in the colonials with attached garages and larger lots. Our own residential installer guidance for Long Island suggests one to three cameras for a condo or townhouse, four to eight for a ranch or split-level, and six to twelve for a colonial with a driveway, a pool and a garage. At that count, wired PoE from a switch in the garage or basement is usually more reliable than a dozen cameras on one home router. On the North Shore, where Wikipedia records more than 500 Gold Coast mansions built from the early 1890s and many estates later subdivided, distance and outbuildings rule out Wi-Fi at the edges almost every time. On the South Shore, cedar shingle and clapboard houses near the water need every camera mounted on solid wood behind the shingles and every hole sealed, because wind-driven rain off the bays will find any gap.

Run the speed test where the camera will hang

A speed test taken on the sofa tells you very little about a camera over the garage. Stand as close as you can to where the outdoor camera will mount, outside if the weather allows, and run the test there. Look at the upload figure, not the download. Many home internet plans offer plenty of download and much less upload, and cameras push video up, not down. If the upload at that spot is below what the VX3 guide asks for, or the test stalls, tell us. It does not mean the job cannot happen; it means that camera probably needs a cable, a wired access point, or a different spot. We measure signal strength ourselves on the day, but your screenshot lets us bring the right parts the first time.

Find the panel's model and the dealer's name

If your house already has an alarm keypad or touchscreen, look for a model label on the side or under a flap, and look for a dealer sticker on the panel or on the alarm box in a closet or basement. First Alert Professional works through authorized dealers who hold the accounts, and a review site notes that prices and offers vary among the dealers in an area. You do not need to know anything technical about the account. You only need to know which company it is with, so we can tell you whether new cameras can be enrolled to it, whether the dealer needs to do that part, or whether a separate camera account makes more sense.

Look at the doorbell before you buy the VX doorbell

On many older Long Island houses, the doorbell button has been replaced or painted over several times, and the chime in the hall may be decades old. Take a photo of the button, the chime cover and the area where the doorbell transformer might be, often near the boiler, the electrical panel or in a closet. If there is no chime at all, or the button is wireless, that tells us the doorbell will need a new power plan. We would rather know that before the visit than discover it with the new doorbell in hand.

On hot attics and PoE cable. On a slab ranch or a colonial, the attic is usually the only path from the inside of the house to the camera, and Long Island attics get hot in July. Fluke Networks explains that as temperature rises, a cable link's insertion loss rises too, and quotes the ISO/IEC standard reducing allowable length by 0.4 percent per degree Celsius between 20 and 40 degrees for unscreened cable, and by 0.6 percent per degree above that. A short run to a camera over the patio is nowhere near the limit. A long run along the ridge of a big colonial to a camera at the far corner can get closer to it than people think. So we keep attic runs as short as the house allows, lay them low near the ceiling joists rather than up under the roof deck where it is hottest, and test every run before the camera goes on.

On drilling masonry and sealing it. The VX3 guide's recommended cable hole is 7/8 inch or larger, which is easy in a wood-frame wall and serious work in masonry. We mark the hole where the inside is reachable, drill with the hammer action off until we are through the surface so the face does not spall, then switch it on to finish. The hole is angled slightly down toward the outside so water runs out, not in. Once the cable is through, the gap is sealed with an exterior-grade sealant. Field Notes: The Signal Walk Around a Long Island Ranch

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the walk our technicians do before mounting a First Alert camera on the kind of house Long Island is full of: a one-story ranch on a slab, the router in the living room, a detached or attached garage, and a back door onto a patio.

Wiring, Wi-Fi and power

Our 1950s ranch sits on a slab with no basement. How does cable reach a First Alert camera on the back wall?

Through the attic and down to the soffit, in almost every case. The Levitt-era slab houses, and many built near them, have no space below the floor, and the original ones carried radiant heating pipes in the slab, so we never drill into the floor. From inside, the cable starts at a PoE switch or injector near the router, goes up into the attic through a closet ceiling or a wall plate, runs along the joists, and comes out through the soffit right behind the camera. Whichever passes the numbers at that spot. Alarm Grid's VX3 install guide calls for a minimum signal of -65 dBm at the install location and a continuous upload of at least 2 Mbps. Our Installation Process

Every First Alert job on Long Island follows the same eight steps, whether it is a single VX doorbell on a Levittown Cape or a dozen cameras on a Suffolk colonial and its outbuildings. The order matters, because the problems that make cameras fail later, a weak signal, a power cord that does not reach, an account nobody can log into, are cheapest to solve before anything is mounted.

1. Send the address, house type and account details

Town and street, the kind of house or business, how many cameras you want, and whether a First Alert or Honeywell panel and a monitoring dealer are already in place. Requests are booked within 2 hours.

2. Confirm the account holder and the panel

We confirm who holds the Total Connect smart video account and, if there is a touchscreen, its model and software version, so enrollment can happen on the day.

3. Walk the property and choose the views

Front, sides and back, including back doors, basement windows, the driveway and the garage. We agree what each camera should see and what it should not.

4. Measure signal and upload at each spot

Wi-Fi strength against the -65 dBm minimum and a 2 Mbps upload test at every mounting point, outside and at height.

5. Choose the power and cable path

PoE over a cable through the attic or garage, an adapter within reach of its 10-foot cord, or a back box with conduit where the wall stays closed.

6. Mount, drill and seal

Holes at 7/8 inch or larger as the guide recommends, mounts on solid wood or masonry, and every penetration sealed against rain and salt air.

7. Enroll and configure

Cameras enrolled to the account and panel, detection zones and privacy masking set, alerts routed to the right phones, audio left off unless you decide otherwise.

8. Test day and night, then hand over

Each camera triggered and checked in daylight and after dark, the app and panel shown to you, cables labelled, and camera registry options explained.

Do you install First Alert cameras out east, far from the city line, or only in western Nassau?

What does a professionally installed First Alert system end up costing on Long Island?

How does a professional First Alert camera system actually work day to day?
+

The cameras and doorbell connect to your network by Wi-Fi or a PoE cable and send video to a Total Connect smart video account, which you see in the app and, with a compatible touchscreen, on your panel. If the system is monitored, a sensor tripping on a door or window can be checked against the camera covering it before anyone is dispatched. Alerts go to whichever phones you choose.

If our internet goes down, do the VX cameras keep recording?
+

Remote viewing, app alerts and cloud clips all depend on the internet connection, so while the connection is down you will not see the cameras from your phone. What a particular VX model keeps locally during an outage depends on that model, and we confirm it from the manufacturer's documentation for the cameras on your quote rather than guessing.

Can I mount the First Alert cameras myself and just have you set up the account?
+

You can mount cameras on your own home, and some owners do. The parts that usually go wrong are the signal at the mounting point, the 10-foot limit on the VX3's power cord, sealing holes in masonry or siding, and enrolling to the right account. If you have already mounted them, we can check signal and power and handle the enrollment and settings on a shorter visit.

How can I confirm a camera installer on Long Island is properly licensed?
+

Ask for the New York State security or fire alarm installer license number, which the Department of State issues, and check it before you sign. In Nassau, ask about the county consumer affairs license for home improvement work, which requires fingerprinting; in Suffolk, ask about the county license under Chapter 563. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

Should the microphone on our First Alert doorbell be switched on?
+

Two-way talk, when you are speaking with someone at the door, is fine because you are part of the conversation. Continuous audio recording is different, because New York makes it a felony to record a conversation you are not part of without at least one party's consent. We leave audio recording off by default and explain the setting so you can decide.

Our alarm and cameras were just installed in Nassau. Do false alarms in the first weeks count against our permit?
+

A Long Island law firm's summary of the Nassau ordinance says a newly installed alarm system is not subject to false alarm notifications for the 90 days after installation, up to a maximum of three. Use that time to fix any sensor or camera that keeps triggering. After it ends, the county counts false alarms toward the two-per-year limit.

What happens to the cameras when a storm knocks out our power?
+

Without power to the router and any PoE switch, the cameras go offline. Tropical Storm Isaias left almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers without power at its peak in August 2020, according to ABC7. A battery backup on the router and switch keeps short outages from taking the cameras down, though no backup lasts through a multi-day outage.

We rent our house in the Town of Hempstead. Can we put up First Alert cameras?
+

Indoor cameras inside your home are usually your choice, and the VX5's privacy shutter makes them easier to live with. Anything drilled into the outside of the house needs the owner's written agreement first. Owners should also know that the Town of Hempstead requires a rental occupancy permit for residential rentals, which is a separate matter from the cameras.

Our First Alert panel came from a dealer we no longer use. Can new cameras still be added?
+

Often, but it depends on who controls the account the panel reports to. If the old dealer still holds it, they may need to release it or enroll the cameras. If you have moved to a new dealer, they hold it now. We find out which before booking, so the cameras can actually be enrolled on the day rather than left waiting.

Will the hole for the camera cable let water into our shingle-sided South Shore house?
+

Not if it is done right. We drill at a slight downward angle toward the outside so water runs out, mount on solid wood behind the shingles rather than the shingles alone, and seal the opening with exterior-grade sealant once the cable is through. Where the run is exposed, it goes in conduit into the camera's back box.
