The Neighborhood

Why Ridgewood Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Ridgewood is a neighborhood of small buildings with many people coming and going through one door. Most of its housing went up between about 1905 and the First World War, much of it as rows of three-story six-family flats with two apartments on each floor, a stoop up to a pair of glazed doors and a cellar underneath. The neighborhood sits in Queens, in the 104th Precinct and the 11385 ZIP code, but its western blocks run straight into Bushwick and East Williamsburg in Brooklyn, the name itself began on the Brooklyn side, and the same kinds of buildings continue across the line. We serve Ridgewood from our Brooklyn office and price it from our Brooklyn list for that reason.

What that housing means for cameras is simple to say and easy to get wrong. A six-family has six households, their visitors, couriers, the mail carrier, the super and the owner all using the same street door every day. Somebody is always being buzzed in. A camera that only watches the stoop from outside records a crowd of people who all had a reason to be there. The camera that settles a theft is the one that shows a face as a person comes through the inner door and walks toward the mailboxes, because by then the only people left in the picture are the ones who got inside. Ridgewood's reported lobby and mail-room thefts were solved, or at least identified, exactly that way.

The avenues have a different pattern. Myrtle Avenue, Fresh Pond Road, Forest Avenue and Wyckoff Avenue are lined with stores in the ground floors of older buildings, many with a cellar reached through a sidewalk gate and a back door onto a yard behind the row. The break-ins QNS has reported on those streets came through the places a front camera does not see: a cut lock on a roll-down gate, a pried back door, a lock removed from a basement gate before opening time. In one case the burglars took the recording with them. A store system here is planned around the back and the cellar as much as the front window, and around keeping the recorder out of reach.

None of this means Ridgewood is getting more dangerous. The 104th Precinct's CompStat count showed 81 burglaries through July 5, 2026, against 85 at the same point in 2025, a 4.7 percent drop, as QNS reported. Cameras here are less about fear and more about the everyday facts of shared doors, deliveries left in halls, cars parked on the curb and stores that open early and close late. A good system answers the question people actually ask the morning after something goes missing: who came in, when, and which way did they go.

Ridgewood facts that shape a camera plan

  • Inside the door: in April 2019 a woman reached the mail room of 803 Wyckoff Avenue "through an unsecured entry" and took 15 mail items; "Security personnel at the apartment building discovered the theft upon reviewing surveillance camera footage," QNS reported.
  • Buzzed in: in December 2020 a man rang multiple apartments at 1680 Madison Street, was let in, took a package from the mail room, and the 104th Precinct "recovered photos of the suspect from inside the building."
  • Back doors and gates: the 2016 pattern QNS described included a cut lock on a roll-down gate on Woodward Avenue, a pried back door on Cypress Avenue where the crew "damaged the surveillance camera and removed its tape," and entry through basement access on 60th Place.
  • Early hours: the July 2022 burglar at Fabco Shoes on Myrtle Avenue got past the basement gate by removing one of its locks shortly after 7:30 a.m.
  • The curb: at an April 2023 community council meeting, a 104th Precinct officer told residents to lock their cars and to park where there may be surveillance so police can see what happens.
  • The trend: 81 burglaries in the precinct through July 5, 2026, down 4.7 percent from 85 a year earlier, by the CompStat figures QNS cited.

The Hall and the Mail Area

Inside the Street Door: Where Ridgewood's Useful Footage Has Come From

Read the Ridgewood police reports that mention cameras and a pattern shows up. At 803 Wyckoff Avenue in April 2019, the thief came in through the lobby at about 9 p.m., reached the mail room through an entry that was not secured and left with 15 mail items, and the building's own security staff only learned of it by reviewing the footage. At 1680 Madison Street in December 2020, a man rang several apartments at about 9:30 p.m. until someone let him in, went to the mail room and took a package, and the precinct's photos came from inside the building. At 67-12 Fresh Pond Road in June 2019, a burglar forced the door of a second-floor apartment at about 8 a.m. while nobody was home, and the camera footage police released showed his clothes and the backpack he carried. In each case, the picture that mattered was taken after the street door, not in front of it.

That is the reason a Ridgewood building plan starts indoors. The outside of the stoop still matters, for parcels left at the top of the steps and for seeing who is ringing. But a six-family's front door is opened dozens of times a day by people who live there, and the person who means trouble often walks in behind one of them or gets buzzed through by a tenant expecting a delivery. Once they are inside, there are only a few places they can go, and each of those places can be given one camera with one clear job.

The inner door: a face, not the top of a head

Many Ridgewood flats have a small vestibule between the glazed street doors and the hall, or a hall that opens straight off the doors. The best spot for the main building camera is usually on the hall side, facing back toward the door at about head height or a little above, so that everyone coming in walks straight toward it. Mounted high in a corner and aimed down, a camera records hair and shoulders. Mounted facing the door from the far end of the hall, at a moderate height, it records faces in the two or three steps after the door closes, when people look up to find the stairs. We set the picture to favor the doorway itself, where light from the glass can otherwise turn a face into a silhouette, and check it at dusk, when the hall lights are on and the street is still bright.

The mail area: hands, boxes and the shelf where parcels wait

In a six-family the mailboxes are usually a bank on the hall wall near the door, and parcels end up on the floor below them or on a shelf. A second camera covers that wall from the side so it shows hands at the boxes and anyone picking up a parcel, framed tight enough that the label on a box is readable on a good day. Mail theft is not only a building problem: CBS New York reported in 2024 that the U.S. Postal Service Inspector General found Queens post offices had not properly put mail-theft safeguards in place and had failed to keep track of keys to the green relay boxes. A building owner cannot fix that, but a camera on the mailboxes at least shows whether a theft happened inside the hall or before the mail ever arrived.

Landings, the cellar stair and the back door

The Fresh Pond Road burglary happened at an apartment door, upstairs. In a three-story flat, a camera at the first landing can see the stair coming up and two apartment doors, and in many buildings that one view covers more ground than people expect. We aim landing cameras along the stair, not into any apartment, so they show who went up and when without watching a tenant's doorway all day. The cellar stair and the back door to the yard get their own view, because those are the doors that are used least and checked least, and in a building where the street door is busy they are the quiet way out.

Alerts instead of a morning review

The Wyckoff Avenue theft was found by reviewing footage after the fact. With a modern recorder, the mail-area camera can be set to send a phone alert when a person is detected at the boxes during hours when nobody normally collects mail, or to flag any person in the hall after midnight, so the owner or super can glance at the clip that evening instead of searching a week of video later. Alerts are set per camera and per hour, so a busy daytime hall does not buzz anyone's phone every few minutes.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Installation for Ridgewood's Architecture

Ridgewood's buildings were put up quickly and in rows, by a few builders working from a few plans, and most of them are masonry. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's reports describe more than 300 Mathews-style six-family flats sold in the neighborhood between 1909 and 1912 alone, and its 2014 Central Ridgewood district covers 990 buildings. That sameness helps a camera job: once we have worked out the route in one flat, the building next door usually has the same cellar, the same stair and the same hall.

Six-Family Flats

Three stories over a cellar, two apartments a floor, a stoop up to paired glazed doors and a hall that every household shares, with the recorder best placed in the cellar.

Two-Family Brick Rowhouses

Attached houses with a stoop, two households and a yard behind, where the only way to the back is through the house or the cellar.

Corner Buildings With a Store

A store on the ground floor and apartments above, with two sets of cameras, two owners or tenants and often two recorders sharing one facade.

Landmark-District Rows

Buildings inside Ridgewood's city historic districts, where front cameras follow the Commission's count, size and concealed-wiring rules.

Frame Houses and Later Homes

Older wood-frame houses from before the 1905 masonry rule and later one-family homes with garages, where siding and a driveway change the plan.

Factory and Loft Buildings

Former industrial buildings toward the north, now shops, studios and storage, with wide loading doors, high ceilings and several tenants on one floor.

Six-family flats: one cellar, three floors, every cable starting downstairs

The six-family is the building we wire most in Ridgewood. The recorder goes in the cellar, in a locked spot away from the cellar door and the stair, with its own battery backup and the internet brought down to it. From there, cable runs up to each camera: the inner door and the mail area on the first floor, the landings above, the stoop outside, and the yard and back door at the rear. In buildings with a light shaft between neighbors, we do not use the shaft as a cable route unless the owner wants it and it can be reached safely; the usual route is up the back of a closet that stacks on each floor, or alongside existing pipes, with fire-stopping where cable passes through floors. Hall cameras mount on the wall or ceiling in a small dome or turret that tenants will not bump with furniture on moving day.

Old cable is common in these cellars. Buzzer pairs, alarm wire, telephone cable and coax from a long-gone system all run the same way to the same places. Some of it can be reused, and some of it should never be trusted, so we trace and test what is there before planning around it. Where an older analog system already has good coax to the hall and stoop, an HD-over-coax recorder can sometimes reuse it; where the wire fails a test, new network cable goes in and each run is certified before the camera is mounted.

Two-family rowhouses: the back is reached from below

An attached rowhouse has no side alley, so cable for a yard camera usually comes up from the cellar through the rear wall, or out of a second-floor closet onto the back wall under the cornice line. The rear camera's job is the yard and the back windows; the front camera's job is the stoop and the door below it if the lower unit has its own entrance under the stoop. With two households in the house, we set up separate accounts so the upstairs and downstairs tenants each see the cameras for their own entrance, and the owner sees everything.

Corner buildings: the store's cameras and the building's cameras kept apart

A corner building on Fresh Pond Road or Forest Avenue often has a store with its own cameras and its own recorder, run by a tenant, and a residential door around the corner that belongs to the landlord. We plan each system on its own recorder with its own accounts, so the store owner cannot browse the apartment hall and the landlord cannot watch the register. Where the two sets of cameras share a facade, we place them so they do not duplicate each other or crowd the front, and we agree with both parties which camera watches the sidewalk outside the residential door.

Landmark-district rows: two cameras for every 25 feet of frontage

Ridgewood has four city historic districts: Stockholm Street (2000), Ridgewood North (2009), the Seneca–Onderdonk–Woodward district once called Ridgewood South (2010), and Central Ridgewood (2014). For buildings inside them, the Commission's permit guidebook lets its staff approve security cameras that meet set conditions. On small buildings that means "no more than two security cameras can be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage," they "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," equipment must be "as small as possible and painted to blend," and conduit must be concealed, with "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit." A typical six-family lot is about 27 and a half feet wide, so the front gets one or two small cameras, finished to match the brick or the stone enframement, with the cable taken through the wall behind them. We check whether a building is in a district before quoting, prepare the cut sheet the Commission asks for, and never mount a front camera ahead of the permit.

Frame houses, garages and the north side

The older wood-frame houses that survive from before the masonry rule, and the one-family houses with garages built later on the eastern blocks, wire more like a suburban house: cable through an attic or basement, mounts on blocks so siding is not cracked, and a camera for the driveway or garage door. In the former factory and loft buildings near the northern edge, the cameras are higher, the cable runs are longer and often go in conduit across ceilings, and a single floor may hold several tenants who each need their own view of their own door.

Business Security Camera Installation in Ridgewood

Ridgewood's stores are mostly independent shops in the ground floors of older buildings, on a handful of long commercial streets. The Myrtle Avenue Business Improvement District, founded in 1988, runs from Wyckoff Avenue to Fresh Pond Road and counts roughly three hundred businesses. Here is how the camera work changes from one corridor to the next.

Myrtle Avenue

The neighborhood's main shopping street, from the Myrtle–Wyckoff hub to Fresh Pond Road, with clothing, shoe and discount stores, banks and restaurants, many with a basement reached by a sidewalk gate.

Fresh Pond Road

A long commercial street of shops, restaurants and services in the ground floors of apartment buildings, where the store's cameras and the building's often share one front.

Forest Avenue

Shops, restaurants and services near the M station, including businesses run by the neighborhood's Serbian community, with curbside parking right outside the door.

Wyckoff Avenue

The line with Bushwick, busy around the Myrtle–Wyckoff station, where corner delis and stores keep long hours and some keep an ATM inside.

Side-Street Corners

Corner delis and groceries on streets such as Woodward Avenue, Cypress Avenue and Grove Street, the kind of store the 2016 ATM crew targeted.

The North Side

Workshops, studios, storage and small industrial spaces in older factory buildings, busy by day and empty at night, with loading doors on the street.

Myrtle Avenue: the front, the counter and the cellar gate

A Myrtle Avenue store is usually a deep, narrow room with the counter near the door, stock along both walls and a cellar below for storage, reached from inside and from a gate in the sidewalk. The Fabco Shoes burglary in July 2022 shows why the cellar matters: shortly after 7:30 in the morning, the burglar removed one of the locks on the basement gate, got into the store and filled a shopping bag with bags and wallets from the shelves before taking the register, and the video police released showed him filling the bag. The usual layout for a store like that is a door camera aimed in from just behind the entrance, a counter camera over the register, one or two cameras down the length of the shelves, and a camera at the top of the cellar stair facing down, so anyone who comes up from below is seen face on. The sidewalk gate gets an outside view from the storefront if the facade allows it.

Fresh Pond Road: shops under apartments

Many Fresh Pond Road stores sit under apartments, with a landlord upstairs or a residential door beside them, and where the store is a restaurant or food business, the kitchen is in the back or in the cellar. The front camera and the counter camera are the store's business; the residential door next to them is the landlord's. We keep the two separate, and in food businesses we keep cameras out of grease and steam, using housings rated for the kitchen where a camera is wanted at the prep area or the back door used for deliveries and garbage.

Forest Avenue: the curb outside the door

Forest Avenue is busy with traffic and parked cars. In October 2023 a car left parked and running in front of 70-08 Forest Avenue was taken during the evening rush and found abandoned several blocks away in Glendale with its plates gone, and the 104th Precinct's public screenshot of the suspect came from surveillance video. For stores and buildings on streets like this, a camera aimed along the sidewalk and the curb, set to a narrow field so it records the cars and people right outside without trying to cover the whole avenue, gives detectives something to start from.

Wyckoff Avenue and the corner delis

The stores along Wyckoff Avenue and the corner delis on the side streets keep long hours, handle cash, and in some cases host an ATM. Those were the targets in the 2016 pattern, which reached from Woodward Avenue to Cypress Avenue, Grove Street, 60th Place and Wyckoff Avenue itself. A corner store's plan covers the front door, the counter, the shelf behind it, the ATM, the back or basement door, and the roll-down gate from inside, with the recorder kept well away from all of them.

Workshops and studios on the north side

The burglary at One Eyed Studios on Centre Street, reported by QNS in April 2020, happened at around 3:40 in the afternoon, when two people forced their way through the secured front door and left with about $13,000 worth of wigs. A workshop or studio is often busy in a back room while the front sits empty, so its cameras are set to alert on anyone at the front door during working hours as well as at night, and the recorder sits in a back office or locked closet rather than beside the entrance.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Ridgewood

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431, and every camera, recorder and cable run on a commercial job is installed by our own licensed crew. Landmarks. Stores inside one of the four historic districts follow the same camera count, size and wiring rules as the residential buildings above them, and a store tenant who wants a camera on the facade needs the building owner's agreement for the Landmarks application. At a 2023 Myrtle Avenue BID meeting, the precinct's commanding officer urged owners to call 911 rather than confront anyone: "So rather than engage, we want you to be safe." A camera is there to record, so nobody has to chase.

Store Break-Ins

Gates, Back Doors and ATMs: What the 2016 Ridgewood Pattern Teaches a Store Owner

In June 2016, QNS published the NYPD's description of a burglary pattern in Ridgewood. Between February and April, the same group or groups broke into five businesses and went for the ATMs. On February 27 at about 5:30 a.m. they cut the lock on a roll-down metal gate at 466 Woodward Avenue, broke into the ATM and took 40 cartons of cigarettes and $600 from the register. On March 23 at 3 a.m. they pried open the back door at 803 Cypress Avenue, took $17,380 from the ATM, "damaged the surveillance camera and removed its tape." On April 10 a lock on a metal door at 2042 Grove Street was cut and $17,840 taken. On April 18 someone came in through basement access at 62-86 60th Place. On April 26 or 27 the ATM at 853 Wyckoff Avenue was broken into for $15,760. Every one of those entries came through a gate, a back door, a metal side door or a basement, not through the front glass.

The way in is rarely the front window

A store owner thinking about cameras pictures the front door and the counter, because that is where customers and staff are. Burglars who come at 3 or 5 in the morning think about the opposite: the door at the back of the yard, the side door with an old lock, the sidewalk gate to the cellar, the roll-down gate with a padlock a pair of bolt cutters can open. A camera plan for a corner store or deli in Ridgewood names each of those entry points and gives each one a view, from inside if the outside wall is not the owner's to drill. Inside the roll-down gate, a camera aimed at the door from behind the counter sees the gate go up and whoever ducks under it.

The ATM and the cigarette shelf

The Woodward Avenue crew left with cigarettes as well as cash. In a deli the counter camera usually looks at the customer and the register, which leaves the shelf behind the clerk and the ATM by the door to other cameras. We give the ATM its own view, close enough to show someone working on its front, and set a counter camera from behind and above the clerk so the stock shelf, the register drawer and the customer's face are all in one picture. Overnight, both can be set to alert the owner's phone the moment a person is detected in the closed store.

Keeping the recording when the camera is attacked

The Cypress Avenue crew broke the camera and took the tape. Recorders no longer use tape, but the lesson holds: if the recorder sits on a shelf behind the counter, it can be pulled out and carried off with the night's video. We put the recorder somewhere a burglar walking in will not see, in a locked cabinet, a ceiling space, a back office or a cellar room, with no camera cable leading obviously to it. For stores that want more, the system can be set to send motion clips or alerts off site as they happen, so the first seconds of a break-in leave the building before anyone reaches the box. A battery backup keeps it recording if someone cuts the power at the panel.

Cameras placed out of reach

The same crew damaged the camera itself. Security Camera Installation in Ridgewood: Questions Answered

At 1680 Madison Street a man pressed several apartment buttons until someone let him in, and police later took his picture from cameras inside. If our six-family can afford only one new camera this year, should it face the stoop or the hall behind the door?

The hall. A camera inside, facing back toward the street door, records every person who actually enters, at close range and in steady indoor light, including anyone a tenant buzzed in without looking. A stoop camera records everyone who walks up the steps, which in a six-family is mostly residents and couriers, and it has to fight the sky behind them and rain on the lens. When the budget allows a second camera, put it over the mailboxes; a third can go outside for parcels at the top of the stoop.

If you already have a video intercom at the door, its panel camera sees callers from outside, which makes the hall camera even more useful, since it shows who came in after the buzz.

At 803 Wyckoff Avenue, staff learned about the mail-room theft only after going back through the footage. Can our system tell us the same evening that someone has been at the mailboxes?

Yes. The mail-area camera can be set to detect a person within a box drawn around the mailbox bank and the parcel shelf, and to send an alert only during the hours you choose, such as after the mail has come and late at night. The alert carries a short clip or snapshot, so the owner or super can see in a few seconds whether it was a tenant collecting their mail or someone going through boxes that are not theirs.

We set the hours with you, because a building where tenants come home at all hours needs a different schedule from one where the hall is quiet after 10 p.m. The point is to look at one clip that evening, not to search a week of video after a neighbor complains.

The 2016 crew went after the ATMs in Ridgewood stores. Should the ATM in our deli have a camera of its own, apart from the register camera?

Yes. A register camera is aimed at the counter and the customer, and an ATM by the door or along the wall is usually at the edge of its picture or out of it. A camera of its own, mounted close and high, shows anyone working on the machine's front or cabinet, and at night it can be set to alert you the moment a person stands in front of it while the store is closed. If the ATM belongs to an outside operator, check whether they already have a camera on it, so the two are not duplicated.

The Cypress Avenue break-in came through a back door. Our store's back door opens onto a yard shared with the buildings behind. What does a camera there need to see?

The door itself and the approach to it. From inside, a camera facing the back door shows it opening and whoever comes through, which works even when you are not allowed to mount anything on the yard wall. From outside, where the wall is yours, a camera above the door looks out across the yard to the route someone would use to reach it, high enough not to be knocked off. Because the yard is shared, we frame the outside view to your door and the path to it rather than the neighbors' windows, and keep it well away from their yards.

The June 2026 burglar near 72nd Street and Central Avenue came in through a gate and later broke a rear basement window. Should our camera watch the gate or the basement window?

Both, if you can, but if only one, the gate. QNS reported that a surveillance camera recorded him in the yard, smoking and taking a phone call, before he broke the window. A camera at the gate or the side path catches anyone coming in, face on, while they are still walking and not yet crouched at a window, and gives a phone alert before anything is broken. A second camera on the rear wall, aimed down at the basement windows, records what happens at the house itself. Where the yard is too dark for faces, a camera with its own light or a small motion light helps both.

A car left parked and running on Forest Avenue in 2023 was gone when its owner came back, and the precinct has told drivers to park where there may be surveillance. Can a camera on our building cover the curb where we park without recording the whole block?

Yes. A camera with a narrow, zoomed view mounted on the front of the building, aimed along the curb rather than across the street, can cover the few car lengths in front of the house and the sidewalk beside them. It will not read every plate passing at speed, and it should not try to; its job is to show who walked up to your car, from which direction and when. We check the view at night, when headlights and streetlights make plates and faces harder to read, and adjust the angle before we leave.

Our workshop on a north Ridgewood street is busy by day with everyone in the back room, like the Centre Street studio that was broken into at 3:40 in the afternoon. What should the front camera do while we are working?

Announce visitors. Set the front-door camera to detect a person at the entrance and send an alert to the phones of whoever is working, during business hours as well as overnight, so nobody walks in unnoticed while the team is in the back. A small monitor in the back room showing the front door helps too. At night the same camera records as usual, and the recorder stays in a back office or locked closet, never beside the door that was forced.

Our windows face the elevated M line near the Forest Avenue and Fresh Pond Road stations. Will motion alerts go off every time a train passes?

Not if the camera is set up properly. Modern cameras can be told to alert only on people, not on every change in the picture, which filters out trains, passing headlights and shadows from the structure. We also draw the detection zone so it covers your door, yard or curb and leaves the tracks out. Where a camera has to look past the line, we test it while trains go by before we leave, and adjust the zone and the sensitivity until the alerts come only from people.

We own a six-family and live on the first floor, and two tenants have asked to see the stoop camera on their phones. Should they get access, and to which cameras?

Many owners say yes to the stoop camera and no to the rest. A tenant who can see the outside of the door can check a delivery or a caller, which helps everyone. Hall, mailbox and landing cameras show neighbors coming and going, so most owners keep those to themselves and the super. The recorder lets you give each tenant their own login limited to the cameras you choose, with live view only and no playback if you prefer. When a tenant moves out, their login is deleted from the owner's account, without changing anyone else's.

Our Fresh Pond Road restaurant has a long, narrow dining room and the prep kitchen is in the cellar. How many cameras does a storefront like this really take?

Usually five to seven. One at the front door looking in, one over the register or host stand, one down the length of the dining room, one at the top of the cellar stair, one or two in the cellar covering the prep area, walk-in or storage and the delivery door, and one at the back door if there is one. A narrow room is covered best from the ends, looking along its length, not from the middle. Kitchen cameras go in housings that tolerate heat and grease, away from the steam over the stoves.

Couriers leave our parcels at the top of a tall stoop behind an iron railing. Can a camera at the door see the face of someone climbing up, not just the top of their head?

Yes, if it is placed for it. A camera mounted high over the door looks straight down on people as they climb, which records hats and hair. A small camera set lower, at the side of the entrance enframement or under the hood over the door, looking down the steps at a shallow angle, sees faces as people come up and the parcels sitting by the door. On a building in a historic district, we choose a small camera finished to match the stone or brick and keep its cable inside the wall.

We lease a storefront on Myrtle Avenue and the landlord already has cameras on the facade. Can we add our own inside without connecting to his recorder?

Yes, and in most cases you should. Your cameras inside the store go on your own recorder, with your own logins, so the landlord cannot see your register and you are not depending on his system working when you need a clip. Can a camera be clamped to the iron railing or fence in front of our building instead of drilled into the wall?
+

We advise against it. Railings and fences move, rust and get hit by bins and bikes, so a camera on them drifts off aim, and the cable has to cross the areaway or the sidewalk to reach it. A small camera on the building, at the entrance or under a hood or cornice, does the same job more reliably, and on a landmarked row a railing is part of what the Commission protects.
