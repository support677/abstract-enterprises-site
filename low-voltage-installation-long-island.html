THE NEIGHBORHOOD

Low Voltage Work on Long Island Is a Two-County Problem

Low voltage is the limited-energy wiring a building runs on — the data and voice cabling behind the network, the home runs feeding cameras and access points, the reader-to-controller wiring behind a controlled door, the entry and intercom runs at the vestibule, the alarm circuits, the speaker and paging pulls, the fiber that ties buildings together, and the racks and closets where all of it lands. Generally it operates at or under 50 volts, which is what separates it from the line-voltage work an electrician does.

What makes Long Island different from the city across the water is not the buildings. It is the rulebook, and there are two of them. Suffolk County issues its own Restricted Electrical Licence through the Department of Labor, Licensing and Consumer Affairs, administered under Suffolk County Code Chapter 924, and communications cabling falls inside that framework — the county’s own approved continuing-education catalogue lists low voltage and Chapter 8 communications circuits among its course categories. Nassau County is structured differently, generally handled through contractor licensing rather than a county electrical licence, with town and village requirements layered on top. In both counties those local layers are real: practitioners who work the whole Island describe carrying a stack of town and village licences on top of whatever the county requires.

That matters to you before it matters to us. A contractor who is properly credentialled in Suffolk is not automatically clear to work in a Nassau village, and the reverse holds too. If your portfolio spans both counties — and plenty of Long Island property owners have a building in Hauppauge and another in Garden City — it is worth asking any bidder which counties and which towns they are actually cleared for, rather than assuming a Long Island address covers it.

The buildings themselves are their own kind of work. Long Island runs on low-rise office parks, single-storey industrial, school districts, medical parks and strip retail. That means longer horizontal runs measured against a hard hundred-metre limit, more pathway crossing between separate buildings on a single parcel, more slab-on-grade and open ceiling, and far more parking-lot and campus trenching than you will ever do in a tower. It is a different set of problems from the ones a Manhattan fit-out presents, and the design decisions follow the buildings rather than the borough playbook.

EQUIPMENT

Cable, Racks and Hardware We Standardize On

Solid copper only. Copper-clad aluminium sold as Cat6 is the most common reason a system that worked on day one starts dropping powered devices in year two, and it is why a plant fails certification when a landlord or insurer finally asks for a report. We do not install it at any price on any job.

Cat6 Horizontal Cable

The baseline for a Long Island office drop. Dependable for gigabit and for shorter 10G runs, and the right choice on a suite where nothing past the closet will ever need more.

Cat6A Horizontal Cable

Carries 10 Gigabit to the full hundred-metre channel and handles higher PoE classes with less heat rise in a dense bundle. On a wide single-storey floorplate the extra headroom matters more than it does in a compact tower suite.

Plenum, Riser and Shielded Jackets

Plenum-rated CMP in air-handling ceilings, riser-rated CMR in vertical shafts, shielded where a run parallels heavy electrical or lives in a machine room. Code and lease requirement, not a quality tier.

Multimode and Single-Mode Fiber

Backbone between separate buildings on a campus, or any run past copper distance. Fiber carries no metal, so it sidesteps the surge and ground-potential problems that plague long copper between structures fed from different services.

Outdoor and Direct-Burial Rated Cable

For the runs that leave the building. On a Long Island parcel that crosses a lawn, a lot or a service yard, the jacket rating and the conduit are what decide whether the run is still good in five years.

Patch Panels, Racks and Management

Wall-mount racks suit the single-closet suites that make up most Long Island office work; two-post frames go in where a building carries a real MDF. Panels, keystones, plates, cable management, UPS protection and rack grounding are specified per closet rather than assumed.

Support Hardware and Pathway

Support hardware is chosen for the run length rather than the room. Long horizontal pulls across an open floorplate want tray or closely spaced hooks; short drops take bridle rings. Anything crossing a rated assembly gets a sleeve and firestop. Cable lying on tile is not an installation, it is a future service call.

Managed PoE Switches

Switch capacity is calculated from what the devices actually draw together, not from how many ports are populated. Exterior cameras with heaters and high-draw access points push a budget quickly, and a switch picked on port count is the one that starts rebooting devices when the weather turns.

Certification Testing

Wiremap and length testing on every drop as standard. Full TIA-568 certification with a Fluke DSX-class analyser, delivered as printed and digital pass or fail per drop, wherever documentation is specified.

TERMINOLOGY

Long Island Low Voltage Terms, Translated

Most quote disputes in this trade come down to two parties using the same words differently. This is the vocabulary you need to read any cabling proposal in Nassau or Suffolk.

Drop. One cable run from the closet to one outlet position, terminated at both ends. Almost all cabling is priced per drop, not per foot.

Home run. One cable from one device back to the closet with nothing shared along the way. Every camera, door and access point gets its own.

The hundred-metre channel. The distance limit that governs a copper run end to end, patch cords included. On a wide Long Island floorplate this is the constraint that decides where closets go, and it is the reason a second IDF is sometimes cheaper than a heroic long run.

MDF and IDF. Main distribution frame — the primary closet where service enters. Intermediate distribution frame — the satellite closets serving other wings or buildings. On a campus the MDF sits in one building and every other structure gets its own IDF off the backbone.

Backbone. The fiber or copper trunk between the MDF and each IDF. On a multi-building parcel this is the spine of the whole design.

Patch panel. Where the permanent cable ends and short patch cords take over, so a switch can be swapped without anybody re-terminating a run.

Punch-down. Seating each conductor into its contact at the panel or jack. Do it carelessly and the drop still works, it just never certifies clean.

Plenum (CMP) versus riser (CMR). Plenum jacket belongs in air-handling spaces; riser belongs in vertical shafts. Substituting down is one of the few cabling mistakes that creates genuine exposure.

PoE class and power budget. Every switch has a total power budget as well as a per-port class. Exceeding the total makes devices brown out and reboot in rotation, which looks exactly like a cable fault.

Wiremap versus certification. A wiremap confirms the pairs are on the right pins with no shorts or splits. Certification measures length, crosstalk, return loss and delay against TIA-568 and produces a printed pass or fail per drop.

As-built. The closeout drawing and port map showing what was installed where, so the next person does not trace it by hand.

CCA. Copper-clad aluminium. Cheap cable marked as Cat6 that is not solid copper, runs hot under power and fails certification.

Put simply, low voltage installation in Long Island: the design, installation, testing and documentation of limited-energy systems — also called extra-low-voltage or ELV systems, and sometimes electronic security systems or building technology systems. What a low voltage installer does is run and terminate the communications infrastructure a building needs, which is a different trade from line-voltage electrical wiring. The difference between Cat6 and Cat6A is bandwidth headroom and heat under PoE: Cat6A carries 10 Gigabit to the full channel where Cat6 does not.

The recurring low-voltage problem in Nassau is inherited plant. A suite that has turned over four times carries four generations of cable above the tile, none of it labelled and none of it documented, and the pathway you need is already occupied by runs nobody can identify. That is a scoping question before it is an installation question, which is why the survey looks above the ceiling rather than at a floor plan.

On credentials, Nassau is generally handled through contractor licensing rather than a county electrical licence, with town and village requirements layered on top. Suffolk County

Suffolk covers far more ground and a wider spread of building types — the Hauppauge Industrial Park along Motor Parkway, Marcus Boulevard and Oser Avenue, which is among the largest industrial parks in the Northeast; the Route 110 corridor through Melville and Farmingdale; the Veterans Memorial Highway spine; Ronkonkoma and Islandia around Johnson Avenue; Bohemia, Smithtown along Route 111, Huntington on New York Avenue, and the East End out past Riverhead on Route 58.

Suffolk is also where the licensing question has a concrete answer. The county issues Restricted Electrical Licences through the Department of Labor, Licensing and Consumer Affairs, administered under Suffolk County Code Chapter 924, and communications cabling sits inside that framework — Suffolk’s own approved continuing-education catalogue lists low voltage and Chapter 8 communications circuits among its course categories, alongside a communications cable installer course based on the NEC. Under those rules only a Suffolk Master Electrician makes direct line-voltage connections to a service panel, safety switch, unit disconnect or junction box, and a restricted licence holder working outside the purpose their licence was issued for is in violation of the code.

The county takes it seriously enough to attach real consequences. Licences run in two-year terms with continuing education as a precondition of renewal, and working under a revoked licence carries a penalty starting at $750 for a first offence and $1,500 thereafter, with unlicensed operators listed publicly by the county. If you are hiring in Suffolk, the licence is not a formality to wave at — it is a thing you can and should verify with Consumer Affairs directly.

The building stock drives the technical side. Hauppauge and the industrial belt mean high-bay work from a lift, long horizontal runs across wide floorplates, and fiber backbone between structures on a single parcel. The office parks along Route 110 and Veterans Memorial Highway mean distance-limited copper and closet placement that has to be decided on site.

Working across both counties

Plenty of Long Island owners and property managers hold buildings in both. The practical consequence is that a single vendor has to be cleared for both regimes, plus whatever the individual towns and villages require, and that is a fair question to put to anyone bidding your work. HOW IT WORKS

How a Long Island Low Voltage Project Runs

  1. Free on-site survey. Ceilings, closets, distances and existing pathway inspected, county and municipality confirmed, and access rules and insurance requirements settled before anything is quoted.
  2. Distance and pathway plan. Runs measured against the hundred-metre limit, closet positions fixed, backbone route decided where more than one building is involved, and firestopping identified.
  3. Written scope and proposal. Drop count and locations, cable category and jacket rating per space, pathway method, closet and rack scope, testing level, labelling standard, documentation deliverable and a written exclusions list.
  4. Rough-in. Cable pulled on supported pathway with the correct jacket for each space, ideally before drywall closes.
  5. Termination. Keystones at the outlet, patch panel punch-down at the closet, rack dressed as it is built rather than afterwards.
  6. Testing. Wiremap and length on one hundred percent of drops, with full TIA-568 certification where it is specified.
  7. Labelling. Every cable labelled at both ends to a scheme that matches the port map.
  8. As-built and closeout. Drawing, port map, configuration notes and digital closeout package handed over, site left clean.

Start and completion timing turns on building conditions, equipment availability, access to the property, permitting where it applies, and how large the project is.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Low Voltage Installation on Long Island: Your Questions Answered

Do I need a licence for low voltage work on Long Island?

It depends which county you are standing in, and that surprises people. The low voltage license question on Long Island has a county-level answer: Suffolk County issues Restricted Electrical Licences through its Department of Labor, Licensing and Consumer Affairs under County Code Chapter 924, and communications cabling falls inside that framework. Nassau County is generally handled through contractor licensing rather than a county electrical licence, with town and village requirements layered on top. On top of either, security and fire alarm work is licensed separately by New York State. So the honest answer is that there is no single Long Island licence — there is a county layer, a municipal layer and a state layer, and which ones apply depends on where the building is and what system you are installing.

What licence do low voltage contractors need in Suffolk County?

Suffolk administers Restricted Electrical Licences under County Code Chapter 924 through Consumer Affairs, and the county’s own approved continuing-education catalogue lists low voltage and Chapter 8 communications circuits among its course categories, alongside a communications cable installer course based on the NEC. Under those rules, only a holder of a Suffolk County Master Electrician’s licence may make direct line-voltage connections to a service panel, safety switch, unit disconnect or junction box, and a restricted licence holder performing work outside the purpose the licence was issued for is in violation of the code. Verify any contractor’s standing with Suffolk County Consumer Affairs directly rather than taking a licence number on a truck at face value.

Is a low voltage licence required in Nassau County?

Nassau does not run the same county restricted-electrical structure Suffolk does. In practice the requirement is generally handled through contractor licensing, and the layer that catches people out is municipal — individual towns and villages set their own requirements, and what applies in one does not automatically apply in the next. Because that varies address by address, the reliable move is to check with the town or village your building sits in, and ask any bidder to show what they hold for that specific municipality.

What is the difference between Nassau and Suffolk low voltage licensing?

Suffolk centralises more of it at county level through a named licence and a code chapter, with published penalties and a continuing-education requirement attached. Nassau devolves more of it to towns and villages on top of contractor licensing. The practical consequence for an owner with buildings in both counties is that one vendor may be properly credentialled for a Hauppauge property and not automatically clear for a job in a Nassau village. Practitioners who work the whole Island describe carrying a stack of town and village licences alongside whatever the county asks for, and that is worth confirming before a crew mobilises rather than after.

Does New York require a special licence for structured cabling?

New York State does not run a single statewide structured cabling licence covering pure data and voice work. What the state does license is security and fire alarm installation, which is separate and applies regardless of county. Below the state level, the counties and municipalities set their own rules — which is exactly why Long Island splits the way it does between Suffolk, Nassau and the towns inside each.

Do low voltage installers need an electrical licence on Long Island?

Not the same licence an electrician carries for line-voltage work, but in Suffolk the credential lives inside the county’s restricted electrical licensing structure rather than outside it. That distinction matters when you are comparing bidders: a Suffolk restricted licence for low voltage is not the same thing as a Suffolk Master Electrician licence, and neither is a substitute for the state credential on security and alarm work. Ask which specific licences a contractor holds rather than accepting “licensed and insured” as an answer.

How much does a low voltage installer cost on Long Island?

Our published starting points are $175 for a Cat6 data or voice drop and $250 for a Cat6A drop, with a $500 minimum job. Independent industry guides put commercial Cat6 in a national band of roughly $125 to $400 per drop fully installed, terminated and tested, and consistently note that labour rather than cable drives the number — the wire in a single drop is often around twenty-five dollars in materials. On Long Island the variables that actually move a number are distance, whether ceilings are open or hard, how many separate buildings the job crosses, and whether anything has to go underground between them.

How much does structured cabling cost per drop on Long Island?

Same starting points — $175 Cat6, $250 Cat6A, $500 minimum job — with the caveat that a per-drop figure only means something once someone has looked at the pathway. Twenty drops in an open-ceiling office park suite and twenty drops across two buildings on a campus are not the same job at any price, and the second one carries a backbone and a trench the first one does not. A survey converts the starting point into a number for your building.

Is there a minimum job size?

Ours is $500. Getting a van, a technician, tools and materials to a site is a fixed cost that does not scale down with the work, and on Long Island the drive between a Nassau job and an East End job is a real part of the day. One drop and eight drops carry nearly identical mobilisation, which is also why bundling systems into a single visit lowers your effective per-drop cost.

Cat6 or Cat6A for a Long Island office?

Cat6A in most commercial cases, and the case is slightly stronger here than in a tower. Cat6 carries 10 Gigabit only to around fifty-five metres while Cat6A carries it to the full hundred-metre channel, and a wide single-storey floorplate is exactly where long runs live. Cat6A also handles higher PoE classes with less heat accumulation in a dense bundle. For a small suite where nothing will ever exceed gigabit, Cat6 is genuinely fine.

Do I need a permit for data cabling on Long Island?

Building permits for pure low-voltage cabling are not the usual requirement, but this is one to confirm locally rather than take from a webpage, because Long Island jurisdictions differ and the town matters as much as the county. What is more commonly in play is licensing rather than permitting, plus any requirement attached to trenching, exterior work or a rated penetration. We identify what applies to your specific scope during the survey and put it in writing.

Should I hire an electrician or a low voltage contractor?

People search for a low voltage electrician on Long Island and get a mix of both trades back. An electrician is the right trade for line voltage, and many will pull Cat6 competently. The question is whether they carry a certification analyser, work to TIA-568 rather than to power-wiring habit, and hand over a labelled port map and as-built. The failure we are called in to fix most often is cable pulled well and terminated without a tester, where nobody finds the problem until powered devices start dropping under load.

Our campus has four buildings — how does that change the design?

It moves the centre of gravity from horizontal cabling to backbone. One building holds the MDF, every other structure gets an IDF, and the fiber between them becomes the spine of the whole plant. The decisions that matter are which building carries the head end, whether the runs go underground or aerial, and what spare capacity goes in the conduit while it is open. Fiber is also the right answer between structures because it carries no metal, which sidesteps the surge and ground-potential problems long copper suffers between buildings fed from different electrical services.

Can the cable already in our Long Island building be reused?

Frequently it can, and the cable is the part worth keeping because pathway is the expensive half of the plant. The correct sequence is to certify what is there first. Reusing an untested legacy run is how a new system inherits an old fault and then spends six months being blamed on the new equipment. AI OVERVIEW REALITY CHECK

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Long Island Low Voltage

Six AI answers, one price range — what that agreement actually tells you

Ask six different AI answer engines what a low voltage installer on Long Island does and you get six confident summaries that agree almost word for word. Systems under fifty volts. Suffolk requires a restricted licence. Nassau varies by municipality. Security work needs the state credential. And, in every single one, the same price range.

That agreement feels like verification. It is not. Six engines drawing on the same body of ranking content will produce the same answer six times, and the repetition tells you what the internet says about Long Island low voltage rather than whether any of it is accurate or complete for your building.

The useful test is not whether they agree with each other. It is whether any of them can tell you the code chapter, the penalty for working under a revoked licence, or how far your closet can sit from your furthest workstation. None of them can, and the rest of this section is what they leave out.

They all name the Suffolk licence. None of them names the code, the fees or the penalties

Every one of those answers will tell you Suffolk requires a low voltage restricted licence. That is correct, and it is a genuinely useful thing to know. It is also where all of them stop.

What none of them mentions: the licence sits under Suffolk County Code Chapter 924, administered by the Department of Labor, Licensing and Consumer Affairs. Licences run in two-year terms with continuing education required before renewal. Only a Suffolk Master Electrician may make direct line-voltage connections to a service panel, safety switch, unit disconnect or junction box, and a restricted holder working outside their licence’s purpose is in violation of the code.

Nor do they mention that the county attaches real consequences — a penalty starting at $750 for a first offence of working under a revoked licence and $1,500 thereafter, with unlicensed operators listed publicly. If you are vetting a contractor, that is the difference between knowing a licence exists and being able to check whether the person in front of you actually holds one.

"Nassau varies by municipality" is true and almost useless

Every answer says it. None explains what to do with it. The statement is accurate — Nassau devolves more of this to towns and villages on top of contractor licensing — but as advice it leaves you exactly where you started.

What it means in practice is that the requirement attached to a building in one village is not automatically the requirement one village over, and a contractor cleared for one municipality is not necessarily cleared for the next. For an owner with a portfolio, that is a per-property question rather than a per-county one.

The actionable version: identify the town or village your building sits in, check what that municipality requires, and ask each bidder to show what they hold for that specific jurisdiction. That takes one phone call and it is the single most useful thing an owner can do before signing.

Article 6-D is a state credential, and the specimens never say so clearly

All six mention that security and alarm work needs the New York State credential. What gets lost in the summarising is that this is a fundamentally different layer from everything else on the page — it is issued by the State, not by Suffolk or Nassau or any town, and it applies across New York regardless of which county the building sits in.

That distinction matters when you are reading a proposal. County licensing, municipal licensing and state licensing are three separate things, and a contractor can hold one without holding the others. A Suffolk restricted licence says nothing about state security credentials, and the state credential says nothing about whether someone is cleared to work in a particular Nassau village.

Ask which specific credentials a bidder holds, at which level, for the work you are actually buying. “Licensed and insured” on a truck door is not an answer to that question.

One national price range, quoted for two very different counties

The same range appears in every AI answer. It is not a made-up number — it sits inside the band independently published industry guides use, and our own starting points of $175 for Cat6 and $250 for Cat6A fall inside it too. So the problem is not that the figure is wrong.

The problem is what a single national range cannot account for. Two counties with different licensing regimes, building stock running from Hauppauge industrial to Garden City medical suites, runs measured against a hard distance limit on wide floorplates, and campuses where the job includes getting fiber from one structure to another. None of that is visible in a per-drop average assembled across the whole country.

A number quoted without anyone looking at your pathway is not a quote. It is a placeholder, and treating it as a budget is how projects end up in change orders after the ceilings come down.

What every answer omits: pathway, testing tier, and the hundred-metre limit

Three things decide what a Long Island cabling job actually costs and none of the six mentions any of them.

Pathway. Whether there is a route from the closet to the outlet, and what it takes to make one. Open ceiling with existing support hardware is one job; hard ceiling, a rated wall to cross, or a trench between two buildings is a different one on identical drop counts.

Testing tier. There are three, and they cost different amounts: a cable that lights up, a cable with a wiremap and length test, and a cable certified against TIA-568 with a printed pass or fail. A split pair passes the first tier perfectly and degrades under load. Every published price range folds these into one figure as though there were one standard.

The hundred-metre limit. On a wide single-storey Long Island floorplate this is a live constraint rather than a technicality, and it is often what determines whether a job needs one closet or two. That decision is worth more to your budget than the per-drop rate is.

What an honest Long Island low voltage proposal contains

Nine lines. Drop count and locations. Cable category and jacket rating for each space. Pathway method, including anything going underground or between buildings, and who is responsible for firestopping. Closet and rack scope, including whether panels and switches are in. Testing level, stated explicitly as wiremap or full certification. Labelling standard. Documentation deliverable, meaning as-built and port map, or nothing. Access assumptions, including hours and any municipal requirement. And a written exclusions list.

A proposal missing several of those cannot be compared like for like against one that includes them. What usually looks like one contractor being expensive is two contractors quoting different jobs, and the cheaper one having left out the trench, the testing and the drawings.

Ask for all nine from every bidder, ourselves included. A contractor who will not put those in writing before starting is telling you something useful about how the closeout is going to go.

Get a real scope in writing, not a national average.

LOCAL PROBLEMS

Low Voltage Problems Specific to Long Island Buildings

Distance nobody measured. The single most common design fault on a wide floorplate. A closet placed for convenience puts the far corner past the hundred-metre channel, and the run works at idle then fails under load. Measured on survey, it costs nothing to fix. Discovered after termination, it costs a second closet.

Inherited plant above the tile. A suite that has turned over four times carries four generations of cable, none of it labelled. The pathway you need is occupied by runs nobody can identify, and every future service call gets more expensive.

Backbone that was never planned. A campus that grew one building at a time often ends up with a network stitched together rather than designed, with no clear MDF and no spare capacity in whatever conduit exists.

Underground runs with no spare conduit. The trench is the expensive part. A run put in without spare capacity means the next addition needs the ground opened again.

Devices added past what the switch can power. A camera count grows by four, the combined draw crosses the budget, and equipment starts cycling. It presents as a cabling fault and gets misdiagnosed for a season.

Bundles cut by another trade. Someone opens a ceiling for unrelated work, catches a bundle, and says nothing. Without an as-built there is no way to trace what was lost until something stops working.

School work squeezed into a recess window. A schedule with no give in it. Scope that was not surveyed properly turns into a district starting the term with an unfinished network.

The wiring map that lives in one person’s head. An enormous amount of Long Island building infrastructure was never documented. When that facilities manager retires, the building loses its map.

FIELD NOTES

Field Notes from Long Island Ceilings

Measure before you promise. On a wide single-storey floorplate the tape measure is the design tool, not the floor plan. We have walked into suites where the drawing showed a tidy closet position and the actual distance to the far office put the run out of spec before a single cable moved. Ten minutes with a measuring wheel on survey day settles it.

The trench is the job. Anywhere a run leaves one building for another, the cost lives in the ground rather than the cable. Whatever conduit goes in should go in with spare capacity, because the second trench costs the same as the first and nobody budgets for it twice.

Label everything, every time. Any technician who has traced an unlabelled bundle at eleven at night in a cold closet becomes permanently religious about labelling. It costs a few minutes per drop and saves hours a year for as long as the building stands.

Ask which town, not just which county. The first question on a Long Island job is not what you want installed — it is where the building sits, because the municipality decides part of the answer before anything technical does. That is a five-second question that saves a mobilisation.

What counts as low voltage wiring?

Limited-energy circuits generally operating at or under 50 volts: structured data and voice cabling, camera wiring, access control wiring, intercom and entry runs, intrusion alarm circuits, audio-visual and paging, digital signage and building control wiring. It is a separate trade from the line-voltage work an electrician performs.

How do I verify a Suffolk County licence?

Contact Suffolk County Consumer Affairs directly. A legitimate contractor will give you their licence number and encourage you to check it independently. Given that the county publishes penalties for working under a revoked licence and lists unlicensed operators, verification takes one call and is worth making.

Do you provide a certificate of insurance?

Yes. We issue certificates of insurance naming the owner, the managing agent and any additional insureds your property requires, before we mobilize.

Can you work around school calendars and trading hours?

Yes, and on Long Island it is routine. District work is scheduled into recess and summer windows, and retail and restaurant work is sequenced around trading hours so the space stays usable. Restricted windows extend the calendar without changing the labor hours.

Do you work as the low voltage sub for Long Island general contractors?

Yes. We work as the low-voltage trade on fit-outs, renovations and new construction, with certificate of insurance, schedule commitments, rough-in coordination before drywall, and a closeout package including as-built drawings and a port map.

How long does a Long Island office park cabling job take?

A small suite with accessible pathway is typically a single day. A full building with a rack build and certification is usually several days. Start and completion timing turns on building conditions, equipment availability, access to the property, permitting where it applies, and how large the project is.

Can I supply my own Cat6 and have you install it?

Wiremap and length testing is performed on one hundred percent of drops as standard.
