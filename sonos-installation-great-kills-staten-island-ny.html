The Neighborhood

Why Great Kills Homes Need Sonos Installation

In Great Kills, a Sonos project very often starts with a contractor's dumpster in the driveway. A March 2026 market report by Prodigy Real Estate put it plainly: there are "no tower cranes on the skyline" here, yet "block by block, older homes are being expanded, repositioned, and replaced." The same report walks through the three kinds of Buildings Department filing behind that change — ALT-1 major alterations that add rooms or rework the layout, ALT-2 interior renovations of kitchens, bathrooms and systems, and demolition permits where the old house comes down — and points to active filings on Hillside Terrace, Tennyson Drive and Armstrong Avenue. For a homeowner who wants music in the ceilings, that churn is good news. Speaker wire is cheap; getting it into a closed, finished wall is not.

The houses being reworked are mostly from one era. Brick Underground's guide to the neighborhood describes the detached and semi-detached houses as dating largely "to the 1960s and '70s," in styles that include ranches and center-hall colonials, and notes that they "typically have backyards, driveways, and garages." That is the generation that arrived after the Verrazzano–Narrows Bridge opened; Wikipedia's history of the area describes Great Kills and the rest of the East Shore as "mostly rural and dotted with shoreline resorts until the 1950s." One Reddit user remembered their parents buying in Great Kills about a year after the bridge opened, in a house that backed onto Kingfisher Pond. ABC7 New York summed up the result in December 2025: trees, lawns and single-family homes, "a corner of the city that looks more like Nassau County."

Those post-bridge houses were wired for the way families listened and watched sixty years ago. Their owners now want something else entirely: no visible wires, music in every main room, a television without a pile of boxes under it, and a network that reaches the bedroom over the garage. Robert DeFalco Realty's August 2026 buyer's guide lists what separates two similar Great Kills houses on price — "a renovated kitchen, newer mechanical systems, updated bathrooms, central air, modern flooring" — and every item on that list opens a wall or a ceiling. When the ceiling is down for new ductwork anyway, adding a speaker opening and a run of in-wall cable is a small line on a big job. When the ceiling was finished last month, the same speaker means cutting, fishing and patching.

That is why our Great Kills visits are usually two or three trips rather than one. The first is a walkthrough with the plans or with the framing bare, where we mark every speaker, every network drop and the closet or shelf where the Sonos Amp and router will live. The second is the rough-in: cable pulled, labelled, tested and photographed before insulation and drywall. The last comes after the painters, when the speakers go in, each room is tuned and the system is handed over. Houses that are already finished still get Sonos — on shelves, on brackets and through cable fished behind closed walls — but the cleanest results in this neighborhood come from catching the house while it is open.

Two more things shape the work here. The first is the neighbors. Lots are generous by city standards, but backyards still share fences, and music carrying over them has come up in the local Eltingville and Great Kills Facebook group, where one resident asked how others dealt with a neighbor "blasting music in their backyard." We set volume limits in the Sonos app and aim speakers into your own space as a matter of course. The second is the harbor. Blocks closest to Great Kills Harbor carry real flood exposure, and on those streets we plan where the Amp, the router and the switch sit before we plan anything else. Both points have their own sections further down.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Great Kills's Architecture

DeFalco's guide lists the styles a buyer meets here: traditional colonials, high ranches, broad ranches, Cape-style homes, split-levels, contemporary houses, semi-attached houses, townhomes and condos. Each opens up differently during a renovation, and each puts the Sonos equipment in a different place.

Broad Ranch

One long main floor over a basement or crawlspace, often with the bedrooms down a hallway at one end. An open basement ceiling is the easy highway for cable; the hallway is where a single ceiling pair can carry music between the living room and the bedrooms.

High Ranch

The split entry with the garage and a finished lower level below and the kitchen and bedrooms above. DeFalco notes these houses often have "flexible lower-level areas." The lower level is usually the den, which makes it the natural home for the Arc and Sub, with the Amp in a utility room beside it.

Center-Hall Colonial

Two full storeys, a central stair and formal rooms either side. Renovations here tend to open the back of the house into one kitchen-family room. The central stair wall is the shortest route from basement to bedrooms, and we plan the cable around it.

Cape

A storey and a half, with bedrooms tucked under the roof slope. Reporting on City of Yes approvals described a Great Kills block "with many Cape-style homes." Knee-wall spaces upstairs are the usual cable route, and dormer additions are the moment to wire the upstairs ceilings.

Semi-Detached

Pairs of houses from the 1970s sharing one wall, with a deck and yard behind each half. We keep speakers off the shared wall and point them into your own rooms, so a renovation on your side does not become noise on theirs.

Teardown Replacements

Where a demolition permit has cleared the lot, the new house goes up from a bare foundation. That is the one project where every speaker, every network drop and the equipment closet can be drawn on the plans before a single stud is set.

Harbor-Side Houses

Homes on the streets nearest Great Kills Harbor, some rebuilt after Sandy damaged the shorefront. Here the first decision is height: the Amp, router and switch go on the main floor or above, never on the lowest level, and the speaker plan follows from there.

Townhomes & Condos

Attached homes and condominium units where the exterior and shared walls belong to an association. Interior ceilings are usually yours to open; anything that touches a party wall or the outside of the building needs the association's say first.

The Renovation Calendar

Sonos on Your Contractor's Schedule

A Sonos pre-wire is not one appointment; it is a set of decisions made at fixed points in a renovation. Miss one and the choice is made for you. Here is how the stages of a typical Great Kills job line up with the audio work, from the first set of drawings to the week you move back in.

Stage 1 — Drawings and permits

Before demolition, the architect or contractor has a plan. This is when we mark where speakers go, where the television will hang, where the Sonos Amp and router will sit, and which rooms need a network jack. The marks matter to other trades: the framer needs to know which joist bays to leave clear of blocking, the HVAC installer needs to keep ducts away from the speaker positions, and the electrician needs to put a dedicated outlet where the Amp and switch will live. Low-voltage cable is not the electrician's job unless you hire them for it, so it helps to have the audio plan on paper before anyone prices the electrical work.

Stage 2 — Demolition and open framing

Once the old drywall and plaster are out, the house shows its bones. We walk it again with the plan in hand, because what is behind a 1960s ceiling never quite matches the drawings: a beam where the architect expected a joist bay, a duct that took the space a speaker needed, a pipe running across the route to the basement. This is also the moment to confirm the furniture layout as far as anyone can, since speaker positions follow the seating more than the walls. Small changes now cost nothing; the same changes after drywall cost a patch.

Stage 3 — Rough-in

The rough-in is the core visit. Every speaker position gets its own run of in-wall-rated cable back to the equipment location, and every network drop gets a run back to the router or switch. Each end is labelled with the room and position, coiled and secured where the drywall crew will find it, and the openings are marked so nobody screws a sheet of board straight over them. Where the cable passes through framing, it is kept away from electrical wiring and away from where trim nails will go. Outside zones, if you want them, get their cable now too, ending in a weatherproof box at the wall rather than a hole that will be argued about later.

Stage 4 — Test, photograph, then insulate

Before insulation and drywall go up, we test every cable: speaker runs for continuity and shorts, network runs end to end with a tester. Then we photograph each open wall and ceiling with the cable visible and label the photos by room. The test catches the run that was nicked by a staple or a screw while the wall is still open; the photographs mean that when someone hangs a picture or adds a shelf in five years, they know where not to drill. Other speaker makers make the same point — Sonance's manual for its invisible speakers asks for a listening check before any taping starts.

Stage 5 — Drywall, tape and paint

The drywall crew cuts the speaker openings where our marks are, or leaves the area clear for us to cut, whichever you and your contractor prefer. Through taping and sanding the wire ends stay coiled in their boxes or brackets, clear of dust. If the ceiling is painted after the speakers go in, Sonos's own installation manual says the speaker flange and baffle must be masked first; in practice we prefer to fit the speakers after the final coat and paint the grilles separately if you want them to match.

Stage 6 — Trim-out and tuning

With paint dry and floors down, we come back for the finish visit. Speakers go into their openings and lock in place; the Amp goes on its shelf with air around it; the soundbar goes under the television; everything joins one Sonos system. With a Sonos Amp, the app can detect Sonos architectural speakers and unlock their tuning, and we run Trueplay in each room with the furniture in place. Volume limits go on any room that faces a neighbor, and the rooms are named the way your family talks about the house.

Stage 7 — After you move back in

Two things commonly happen in the first month: the internet provider swaps the router, and someone moves furniture. The first can make Sonos disappear from the app; the second changes how a room sounds. We show you how to re-run Trueplay and leave a one-page note of the network settings the system relies on, so a new router can be set up to match. If the system still drops out, the setup-only rate applies, and the photographs from Stage 4 make any repair quicker.

Business Sonos Installation in Great Kills

Sonos suits the smaller rooms on all of them — a shop floor, a waiting room, a bar — where a full commercial sound system would be more than the space needs.

The neighborhood's main shopping street, anchored by supermarkets such as the Stop & Shop at 4343 Amboy Road and Top Tomato at 4045. DeFalco's guide lists restaurants, pizzerias, cafes, bars, delis and neighborhood businesses along it, and rooms like these often sit under suspended tile ceilings.

Hylan Boulevard

Staten Island's longest street runs along the south of the neighborhood, past the harbor and the park. Dental and medical practices, restaurants and service businesses along it often want quiet music in a waiting area and nothing in the treatment or back rooms.

Giffords Lane

The street of the Great Kills station and the public library, running up to Amboy Road. Short storefronts here usually need one or two speakers and a volume limit, not a zone plan.

Mansion Avenue & the Harbor

Home to the Richmond County Yacht Club at 142 Mansion Avenue and the marinas Brick Underground describes along the western shore. Brick Underground also lists waterfront restaurants here, among them Cole's Dockside, a seafood house, and the Marina Café, open for more than four decades.

Nelson Avenue

Where the Great Kills post office (1 Nelson Avenue) and the FDNY firehouse for Engine 162 and Ladder 82 (256 Nelson Avenue) stand, a short civic stretch; wherever a shop sits under apartments, here or on Amboy Road, the music has to stay downstairs.

Arthur Kill Road

Partly residential, partly commercial, and home to the Atrium Stadium Cinemas. Offices and showrooms here range from older storefronts to newer buildings, and each ceiling gets checked before we choose speakers or cable.

Compliance notes for business music in Great Kills

  • The cable above a drop ceiling may need a plenum rating. Where the space above the tiles is used as a return-air path for the heating and cooling, the cable that runs through it must be listed for that use. New York City's Electrical Code is built on the 2020 National Electrical Code with city amendments, and under the national code Class 2 and Class 3 plenum cable (types CL2P and CL3P) is what is permitted in those "other spaces used for environmental air." We check the ceiling before quoting and price the right cable from the start.
  • Speakers stay inside the premises. The city's Administrative Code, section 24-244, bars using a sound device to attract attention outside a business that faces a street, park or public place. A speaker over the door aimed at the sidewalk is not a Sonos question we can say yes to.
  • Apartments above set the ceiling. The Department of Environmental Protection's noise code fact sheet says commercial music must not exceed 42 dBA measured inside a dwelling unit that receives it. On Nelson Avenue or Amboy Road, where shops sit under homes, we set volume limits on every zone and keep any Sub off the floor joists that carry sound upstairs.
  • Licensing the music is the business owner's job. Sonos plays streaming services and business music services equally well; whether a personal streaming account may be used in a shop is between the business and the service, and we leave that choice with you.
  • Insurance paperwork. If the landlord or the plaza's manager wants our certificate of insurance on file before the first visit, we send it free.

How much sound a Great Kills shop actually needs

Most storefronts on Amboy Road and Giffords Lane are small enough that two ceiling speakers on a Sonos Amp, or a pair of Era 100 speakers on shelves, fill the room at a comfortable level. Bigger floors split into zones — front of house and back room, or dining room and bar — each with its own volume. Waiting rooms want even, low-level sound across the seats rather than one loud speaker by the desk. We size the system to the room and to the people working in it all day, not to the loudest moment of a Saturday.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Great Kills: Questions Answered

These are the questions Great Kills homeowners and business owners ask us, grouped by where they come up: during an open renovation, at the framing and code stage, and in commercial rooms. Each answer is the short version of what we would tell you on a walkthrough.

While the walls are open

How do we place ceiling speakers before we've decided where the sofa and TV will go?

Decide the one thing that rarely changes, and plan around it. In most living rooms that is the television wall: the doorways, windows and fireplace usually leave only one sensible place for the screen. Once that is fixed, the main seating faces it, and the speakers can be placed relative to the seating even if the exact sofa is not bought yet. Sonos's in-wall manual suggests a stereo pair 6 to 10 feet apart with the main listening position 4 to 10 feet away, which gives a generous window rather than a single point.

Where the room really could go either way, we hedge. A pair positioned for general music — spread across the middle third of the ceiling — sounds good from almost anywhere, and a second run of cable can be left coiled in the ceiling for a later surround position at very little cost while the framing is open. What we avoid is putting speakers tight to a wall or in a corner just because a joist bay happened to be clear there; sound from a corner speaker is boomy, and it is usually the one thing owners ask to move.

For music in the new open living area, are in-ceiling or in-wall speakers the better choice?

For background music across a kitchen, dining and living space, ceiling speakers are usually better. They spread sound evenly over a wide area, they stay out of the way of cabinets and art, and in an open plan they do not care which way people are facing. Most of the open-plan rooms we wire in Great Kills renovations get two or three ceiling pairs on one or two Sonos Amps.

In-wall speakers earn their place when the room has a clear front, usually around the television. Placed either side of the screen at ear height, they give a more focused stereo image than ceiling speakers, and Sonos In-Wall speakers have a pivoting tweeter to aim the sound. The manual's guidance for surrounds is side walls 2 to 6 feet behind the seats and 5 to 7 feet off the floor. Many renovated rooms end up with both: in-wall at the TV end for films, ceiling speakers over the kitchen and dining area for everyday listening.

Will the 8-inch Sonos ceiling speaker fit between the joists of a 1960s ranch?

Usually, yes. Sonos lists the cut-out for its 8-inch In-Ceiling speaker at 10.125 inches and the 6-inch model at 8.25 inches. Ceiling joists in houses of that age are most often spaced 16 inches on center, and because that measurement runs from the middle of one joist to the middle of the next, the clear gap between standard joists is roughly 14½ inches. Either cut-out fits with room to spare, provided the opening is centered in the bay.

What usually decides it is what else is in the bay: a duct, a pipe, blocking added for a light fixture, or a doubled joist under a wall above. During a renovation we can see all of that, and if the perfect position is blocked we shift the speaker along the bay rather than across the room. Depth matters in shallow spaces too — Sonos gives mounting depths of about 4.73 inches for the 6-inch model and 5.32 inches for the 8-inch — so a ceiling directly under a low roof deck gets checked before anything is cut.

Should every pair of ceiling speakers get its own cable back to the equipment spot?

Every speaker should. We run a separate cable from each speaker position back to the place where the Amp will live — a "home run" — rather than chaining one speaker to the next. It costs a little more cable during a renovation and saves a lot of trouble later. If a speaker fails, it can be tested on its own. If you later decide the dining room should play separately from the kitchen, the change happens at the Amp shelf instead of inside the ceiling.

Home runs also matter because one Sonos Amp drives a limited number of speakers: Sonos says it reliably powers up to three pairs of its architectural speakers wired in parallel. With every cable arriving separately, splitting the house across two or three Amps is a matter of how the ends are connected, not where the wires were pulled. The cable itself is in-wall rated speaker wire, the CL2 or CL3 type the National Electrical Code expects inside walls.

Framing, insulation and the code

Can in-wall speakers go in an outside wall that the contractor is re-insulating?

They can, but it is rarely the best wall. An exterior wall is packed with insulation and, in many houses, a vapor barrier on the warm side. Installers on home-theater forums point out that fitting an in-wall speaker usually means cutting into that barrier, and the speaker's open back then sits in the insulated cavity. Done carelessly, the result is a cold spot and a draft around the grille in winter, and a speaker that sounds dull because the insulation is pressed against it.

Our first suggestion is almost always an interior wall or the ceiling, which gives better sound and leaves the building envelope alone. If the exterior wall is the only place that works for the room — a front window wall where the TV must go, for instance — we raise it with your contractor before insulation, so the speaker positions are framed out and the insulation and vapor retarder are restored neatly around them rather than patched afterwards.

Once the tapers and painters arrive, how do the speaker openings and wire ends survive?

Each wire end is coiled and tucked inside a low-voltage bracket or ring at the speaker position, with the room and position written on it, so the drywall crew can find it and the tapers do not bury it. The openings are either cut by the drywall crew from our marks as the board goes up, or left for us to cut with the speaker template afterwards — you choose with your contractor. Either way, the openings stay empty through sanding, because fine drywall dust and new speakers do not mix.

Painting is the other hazard. Sonos's installation manual says that if a wall or ceiling is painted after the speakers are in, the speaker flange and baffle must be masked first. We prefer to avoid that by fitting the speakers after the final coat. If you want the grilles to match a colored ceiling, they are painted separately following Sonos's instructions and clipped on at the end.

Can the workshop in our built-in garage get speakers without breaking the fire separation to the house?

Yes, if the speakers do not go into the separation. Many Great Kills houses from the 1960s have a garage built into the house, sometimes with a bedroom or den above. The New York City Building Code requires a private garage to be separated from the dwelling and its attic by at least 1-hour fire-rated construction, and garages under habitable rooms to be separated from those rooms by the same. A round hole cut for an in-ceiling speaker in that ceiling, or in the wall shared with the house, is an opening in the barrier the code requires.

So in a garage we keep to surface-mounted options: an Era 100 or Era 300 on a shelf or bracket, or a pair of passive speakers on wall brackets driven by an Amp that sits inside the house, with the speaker cable brought through in a way your contractor or electrician is happy meets the rating. If the garage is being converted into living space as part of the renovation, the rules for that room change with the plans, and the speakers go in once the new ceiling is built.

Shops, offices and the harbor clubs

Above our Amboy Road shop's drop ceiling is a return-air plenum — does that change the speaker cable?

Yes. When the space above the ceiling tiles carries air back to the heating and cooling system, it counts as a plenum, and ordinary cable is not allowed there because of how it burns and the smoke it produces. The cable must be plenum-rated: CL2P or CL3P for speaker runs and plenum-rated network cable for any wired Sonos product. New York City enforces the 2020 National Electrical Code with local amendments, and plenum ratings are part of it.

We look above a few tiles during the walkthrough to see how the ceiling is used, and the quote is written with the right cable from the start. If we find cable left behind by earlier tenants while we are up there, we will point it out, and you can decide with your landlord whether it comes out.

Can music in our Hylan Boulevard dental office help keep treatment-room conversations private?

It can help, but it is one comfort measure, not a privacy system. The federal health-privacy rules accept that some conversation may be overheard, as long as the practice has "reasonable safeguards" in place; the Department of Health and Human Services gives speaking quietly in a waiting room as an example. Low, steady music in the waiting area makes it harder for people there to pick out words from the front desk or a nearby room, and patients tend to find it calming.

What music cannot do is replace doors that close, walls that reach the deck above, or a layout that keeps the waiting room away from treatment rooms. We set the waiting room as its own zone at a fixed level, keep speakers out of treatment rooms unless you want them there, and place them so they mask the reception desk rather than shouting over it. For anything beyond that, an acoustic consultant or a dedicated sound-masking system is the right call.

Can a club hall on the harbor use the same Sonos speakers for a microphone and announcements?

Not well, and we would not set it up that way. Sonos adds a delay to anything coming in through a line input so that grouped rooms stay in sync, and its app offers line-in delay settings from 75 milliseconds upward, with no zero option. For recorded music that delay is invisible. For a person speaking into a microphone in the same room, it means hearing their own voice a fraction of a second late, which is distracting at best and unusable at worst.

The better arrangement for a clubhouse or function room is a simple microphone amplifier and speakers of its own for announcements, with Sonos handling the background music. During an announcement the Sonos zone is paused or turned down from a phone, or automatically if the venue's sound equipment allows it. The two systems can share a space without sharing a signal, and each does what it is good at.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Great Kills

We could not view Google's AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so we have not quoted it. What follows are the claims that cost guides, product pages and forum answers make about Sonos and home audio — the same material answer engines draw from — set against what we find in Great Kills houses.

Claim one: "Sonos is wireless, so there's nothing to wire"

Sonos speakers stream over Wi-Fi, and many products need nothing but a power outlet. That is the message on the box, and it is true as far as it goes. It leaves out two things. The first is Sonos's own architectural range: ceiling and wall speakers are passive and need speaker cable to an Amp, and Sonos's guide to in-wall speakers says plainly that you will likely need a professional to cut the openings and run that cable.

The second is the network. Sonos's system requirements list wireless range extenders and powerline adapters among the things it does not support, and offer a wired connection from at least one product to the router as an option for a steadier system. In a long ranch or a three-level high ranch, that one cable is often the difference between a system that holds together and one that drops the far rooms.

A renovation is the cheapest moment to provide both. "Wireless" describes how the music travels between Sonos products; it does not mean a well-built system has no cable in the walls.

Claim two: "Pre-wiring can wait until the end of the project"

Homeowners are often told that audio is a finishing touch, to be sorted once the kitchen and bathrooms are done. For free-standing speakers that is fine. For anything in a ceiling or wall, the end of the project is the most expensive moment to start, because the walls that were open for months have just been closed, taped and painted.

Prodigy Real Estate's description of Great Kills ALT-2 renovations — "kitchens, bathrooms, systems" — is a list of exactly the trades that open ceilings. The speaker cable needs to go in during the same window, after framing changes and before insulation.

If the audio decision really cannot be made in time, the cheap insurance is to run cable to the likely positions anyway and leave it coiled behind the drywall at marked spots. It is far easier to ignore a cable you did not need than to fish one you did.

Claim three: "Whole-home audio costs $500 to $1,000 per room"

Angi's guide to whole-home stereo systems quotes $500 to $1,000 per room for multi-room additions, with labor at $50 to $100 an hour and wall or ceiling modifications at $100 to $500. Answer engines repeat ranges like these as if they were a price list. They are national averages for a generic system, and they say nothing about whether the walls are open.

Our published numbers work differently. Fixed jobs have fixed prices — Sonos setup at $125, a soundbar on the wall at $129, a cable run of up to 25 feet at $99 — and wired ceiling or outdoor zones fall within the $800 to $2,500 per zone range we publish for whole-home audio, with the exact figure written after a walkthrough. The biggest single factor in where a zone lands in that range is access: open framing at one end, a finished ceiling under a finished floor at the other.

That is why the quote for a Great Kills renovation is written against your contractor's schedule, not a per-room average.

Claim four: "Add mesh Wi-Fi and the far rooms stop dropping out"

Mesh systems are sold as the cure for dead spots, and they often help phones and laptops. Sonos behaves differently. Its requirements say every Sonos product and the app must be on the same network, and that if a wired setup is used with a mesh system, the wired Sonos product must connect to the primary mesh unit. Extenders that create a separate network are not supported at all.

In practice, the Great Kills houses with the steadiest systems have a wired backbone: a cable from the router to the TV wall, another to the Amp, and an access point or wired mesh node where the house turns a corner. A renovation is when those cables are easy to run.

Mesh is not the enemy — it simply works best for Sonos when the units are linked by cable rather than by air, which again comes back to having the walls open at the right time.

Claim five: "Ceiling speakers go anywhere there's a gap between joists"

Online guides tend to treat ceiling speakers like recessed lights: find a joist bay, cut a hole. Angi notes that ceiling joists are typically 16 or 24 inches on center, and Sonos's cut-outs fit comfortably between either. Fitting is not the same as sounding right.

A speaker placed tight to a wall or in a corner sounds boomy; a pair placed too close together sounds like one speaker; a pair placed for the dining table may miss the sofa entirely. Sonos's manuals give spacing guidance for a reason — stereo pairs 6 to 10 feet apart, at least 2 feet from side walls.

We plan speaker positions from the seating first and then look for the joist bay that allows it, moving along the bay if a duct or pipe is in the way rather than giving up the position.

Claim six: "A garage is the easy place for speakers"

Garages look like the simplest room to wire: unfinished, uninsulated, no one minds a hole. In an attached Great Kills garage the opposite can be true. The New York City Building Code requires 1-hour fire-rated construction between a private garage and the dwelling, and between a garage and any habitable room above it.

That ceiling and that shared wall are not places for speaker openings or casual cable holes. Surface-mounted speakers and Era products on shelves give the garage music without touching the separation.

Advice that treats the garage as a blank space misses a rule that matters more in this neighborhood's built-in-garage houses than almost anywhere else in the city.

Claim seven: "Where the equipment sits doesn't matter"

Setup guides put the Sonos Amp and router wherever the cable is shortest, often the basement. Near the harbor that is a gamble. First Street estimates that 1,230 Great Kills properties — 14.4 percent — have some risk of flooding over the next 30 years, and DeFalco's checklist for harbor-area buyers includes "location of mechanical systems."

On those blocks we put the Amp, router and switch on the main floor or higher, on a shelf with air around it, and run cable down to the speakers rather than up to the gear.

Even away from the water, Sonos asks for at least 18 inches between its products and other electronics to limit interference, so the "anywhere" answer rarely holds up once the cabinet is full.

Where the Sonos Gear Lives Near the Water

On the streets closest to Great Kills Harbor, the speaker plan starts from a different question: where will the electronics be safe? First Street estimates that 14.4 percent of Great Kills properties — 1,230 of them — have some risk of flooding over the next 30 years. DeFalco's checklist for buyers near the harbor includes FEMA flood-zone status, elevation and the "location of mechanical systems." A Sonos Amp, a router and a network switch are mechanical systems in miniature, and they deserve the same thought.

Gear up, cable down

The usual habit is to put equipment in the basement and run cable up. On harbor blocks we reverse it. The Amp, router and switch go on the main floor or higher — a hall closet, a cabinet in the family room, a shelf in an upstairs linen closet — and the speaker cable runs down to ceilings and out to the yard. The cable itself does not mind water nearly as much as the electronics do, and a low-voltage cable that gets wet in a storm is far cheaper to replace than an Amp.

Space and air around the shelf

A closet that holds the Amp, router and switch needs a dedicated outlet, room to breathe and a little space between products; Sonos asks for at least 18 inches between its products and other electronics to limit wireless interference. During a renovation, the electrician can put that outlet exactly where the plan shows, and the cabinet maker can leave a ventilated shelf instead of a closed box.

When the grid is strained

In July 2026, SILive reported that Con Edison asked about 41,200 customers in West Brighton, Great Kills and Richmond Valley to conserve power during repairs. A Sonos system draws little, but it does depend on the router, and both come back on their own when power returns as long as they are not sitting in water. Keeping the network gear on a higher floor is the simplest protection there is.

FAQ

Great Kills Sonos Installation FAQ

Is it cheaper to wire speakers during a renovation than after the walls are closed?
+

Almost always. With the framing open, a speaker run is a matter of pulling cable through bare studs and joists and marking the opening; once the drywall is taped and painted, the same run means locating framing, cutting, fishing cable through closed bays and leaving a patch for your painter. We do not publish a per-speaker price for either, because both depend on the route, but every wired zone falls inside our published $800 to $2,500 per zone range and open framing sits toward the bottom of it. The written quote shows the difference for your house.

Can you plan the speaker layout from our architect's drawings before demolition starts?
+

Yes, and it is the best place to start. Send the floor plans and reflected ceiling plans if you have them, along with where the television and main seating are likely to go. We mark every speaker, network drop and the equipment location, note which joist bays need to stay clear and where the Amp needs an outlet, and send the marked plan back for your architect, contractor and electrician. The marks are then checked against the real framing once demolition opens the house, because 1960s ceilings rarely match the drawings exactly.

Who cuts the speaker openings in brand-new drywall — your crew or the drywall contractor?
+

Either works, and the choice is yours and your contractor's. Some drywall crews prefer to cut the openings as each sheet goes up, using the brackets and marks we leave; others would rather hang full sheets and let us cut afterwards with the speaker template. What matters is that the openings are agreed before the board goes up, the wire ends are left where they can be found, and nobody sands or paints over a bracket. We confirm the method with your contractor at the rough-in visit.

Can you test the speaker and network cables before the walls are closed?
+

Yes, and we do it on every pre-wire. Before insulation and drywall, each speaker run is checked for continuity and for shorts between conductors, and each network run is tested end to end. Any run that fails is replaced while the wall is still open. We then photograph every open wall and ceiling with the cables visible, labelled by room, and send you the set, so the route of every cable is on record when the walls are finished.

If a renovation trade damages a wire after you've run it, how is the repair handled?
+

It happens: a drywall screw through a cable, a trim nail in the wrong place, a cable cut when a plumber reroutes a pipe. Because every run was tested and photographed before close-up, we can show where the cable was and that it worked, which usually settles who is responsible. The repair itself is billed at our Staten Island setup-only rate of $225 for the first hour and 30-minute blocks after that, plus any cable or patching needed, and we tell you the likely scope before starting.
