The Neighborhood

Why Castle Hill Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Castle Hill grew in layers, and every layer changes what a camera installer is allowed to touch. The name is old. Local histories say it comes from a fortified Siwanoy village that the Dutch explorer Adriaen Block saw in the seventeenth century and thought looked like a castle. The modern neighborhood is much younger. Accounts of the area say the 1895 annexation of this part of Westchester County into New York City, and the city services that followed, opened the land to building, and that early growth clustered near the IRT Pelham Line on Westchester Avenue and along Castle Hill Avenue, which once had a streetcar. By the 1930s and 1940s it had settled into a middle-income neighborhood. The housing that came out of that period is described in one local history as a mix of attached and detached two-story buildings with one or two units, closely set on small lots. For a camera plan, that sentence matters more than any crime map: houses close together, narrow side passages, shared walls on attached rows, and a neighbor's windows a few feet from almost any spot you might mount a camera.

The southern half of the neighborhood is dominated by two large developments. Wikipedia says the New York City Housing Authority built Castle Hill Houses in 1959, adding more than 2,000 housing units, and that the Houses take up significant land in the southern part of the community together with the Mitchell-Lama Jamie Towers complex. Other sources give different building and apartment counts for Castle Hill Houses, so we do not rely on one number. NYCHA's own development map lists building addresses along Castle Hill Avenue, including 575, 580 and the run from 615 to 655, and marks a community and senior center, a management office and laundry room, a maintenance office and a glaziers' and plumbers' shop, with Lacombe, Olmstead, Havemeyer, Seward, Randall, Turnbull and Cincinnatus Avenues around the grounds. A 2025 profile in The Neighborhoods newsletter reported that the buildings had recently held the most open work orders of any city housing project, at more than 11,000. Repairs that slow are part of why residents think hard about what they can secure on their own.

The city has treated Castle Hill Houses as a security priority for a decade, and it did the job in an order that every camera owner should copy. The NYCHA Journal reported that in 2016 the city spent $8.2 million on 749 new lights at Castle Hill Houses, and said the next phase of security work at those sites would be CCTV cameras and layered access security. The Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice reported that since 2014 NYCHA has spent more than $64.6 million installing and upgrading CCTV cameras, bringing its citywide total past 13,000. Light first, then cameras: a camera only records what light reaches. We could not confirm from public sources exactly which cameras now cover which Castle Hill Houses building, floor or entrance, and we will not guess. Those cameras belong to NYCHA and change only through NYCHA's own programs.

What a resident can control is the apartment door. NYCHA's public guidance allows residents to attach a wireless camera or video doorbell to their own apartment door for security purposes, either over the peephole or with adhesive, with no holes and no physical alterations. The rule is worth taking seriously. AOL reported in 2012 that a resident of Parkside Houses, another Bronx NYCHA development, received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing cameras there. We do not know the details of that resident's equipment, and the guidance today may differ from what applied then, but the lesson holds: in public housing, stay inside the written rule, keep the camera on your own door, and keep anything wired or mounted on walls and frames out of the plan.

Jamie Towers is a different kind of building with a different decision-maker. The Bronx Times describes it as a four-building Mitchell-Lama co-op at Olmstead, Seward and Pugsley Avenues, built in 1966 on about 12 acres; its articles give the apartment count variously as 620, 624 and 720, and one says about 60 percent of residents are seniors. In 2016 the paper reported residents' complaints that security hours had been cut and that lighting around the grounds was inadequate. More recently it reported that the board fired its management company after the shareholder board president said she had found evidence of misspent funds and mismanagement. All of that points to one practical fact. Cameras in Jamie Towers hallways, lobbies, garages and grounds are a board and managing-agent decision, and guidance on NYC co-ops says hallway cameras almost always need board approval, because hallways are common areas under board control. A shareholder who wants a camera on their own door should ask management first, and a board that wants better coverage should start with a written proposal it can review.

Castle Hill's business life is spread along three named corridors and one industrial edge. Wikipedia lists White Plains Road, Castle Hill Avenue and Westchester Avenue as the main commercial streets, and says the Castle Hill Avenue Business Improvement District started in June 2012 with help from Council Members Annabel Palma and James Vacca. The city's 2021 filing for that district describes mainly local businesses with a few national eateries and retailers. The Shops at Bruckner Commons, which Wikipedia says expanded greatly through the 1990s and was mostly renovated in 2018, separates Castle Hill from Soundview. The eastern edge along Westchester Creek is mostly storage, warehousing and municipal use, and Zerega is one of the Bronx's five Industrial Business Zones. The Bronx Times reported Kamco Supply leasing about 57,544 square feet of warehouse space at 1200 Zerega Avenue; LoopNet lists 22,000 square feet of industrial space at 450 Zerega; and the Bronx Chamber lists Castle Hill Recycling at 1000 Zerega. Warehouse and yard cameras are a different trade from doorbells, and Castle Hill has plenty of both.

Castle Hill facts that shape a camera plan

  • Lights first, then cameras: $8.2 million for 749 new lights at Castle Hill Houses in 2016, with CCTV and layered access named as the next phase, per the NYCHA Journal.
  • A citywide camera program: more than 13,000 NYCHA CCTV cameras and more than $64.6 million spent since 2014, per the Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice.
  • A firm resident rule: NYCHA allows only a wireless door camera or video doorbell on your own door, over the peephole or with adhesive, with no holes, and a 2012 Parkside Houses case shows the lease risk of getting it wrong.
  • A co-op with history: Jamie Towers residents reported cut security hours and dim grounds in 2016, and the board later replaced its managing company.
  • Two kinds of houses: older two-story one- and two-family homes close together on small lots, and post-2000 tract houses and condo townhouses near Castle Hill Point.
  • A working industrial edge: the Zerega Industrial Business Zone, with large leased warehouses on Zerega Avenue and storage and municipal uses along Westchester Creek.

What Castle Hill customers often add once the cameras are planned. When a two-family owner or a co-op board looks at a camera over the front entrance, the next question is usually about the entrance itself: who can get in, and how a lost key or a departing tenant is handled. Card or fob entry, a video intercom and cameras all meet at the same doorway and can often share one cable route, so if the entry hardware is on your list too, tell us when you book and we will plan the camera's position around it rather than on top of it.

Inside Castle Hill Houses: the door is yours, the hallway is not

For NYCHA residents the boundary is clear. The camera can live on your door and nowhere else, it cannot need a hole, and it should be there for security. That rules out the standard wired doorbell, any bracket screwed into the frame, and any cable run to an outlet. It leaves battery-powered peephole cameras and light, adhesive-mounted doorbells. The practical problems are the ones nobody mentions in the box: whether your WiFi reaches the door through masonry walls, whether a metal or painted door will hold an adhesive pad, and whether the camera's motion area can be trimmed to your doorway so it does not alert every time a neighbor walks past. We solve those, and we stay off NYCHA's walls, frames and hallways entirely.

At Jamie Towers: paperwork before brackets

In a co-op, the camera question goes to the board and the managing agent, and management at a large complex often has its own steps for any outside contractor, such as proof of insurance and a scheduled work window. For a shareholder that usually means a short written description of the device, how it attaches and where it points, which we prepare for you to submit. For a board it means a full proposal: each camera's view, where cable will travel and how wall and floor openings will be sealed, where the recorder will sit, who will have access to footage and who will be able to change settings. Because Jamie Towers has changed managing agents, it is worth deciding now that the board, not any one company, holds the master login.

On a private house: your walls, your neighbor's windows

An owner of a two-family house on Seward, Lafayette or Olmstead Avenue decides what goes on the building, which makes the job simpler on paper and harder in practice, because houses here sit close together. A camera over the side door can look straight into the next house. A backyard camera can see three yards at once. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a, often called the backyard camera law, lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that intrudes on their property. So every house plan starts with a list of what you have a right to watch, and we draw privacy masks over everything else before you approve the job.

The older houses: close quarters and masonry fronts

The two-story houses that went up near the Pelham Line and Castle Hill Avenue in the decades after annexation sit on small lots, often with only a narrow passage between one house and the next. That narrow gap is usually the way into the back yard, which makes it the most important view on the property and the hardest to aim without looking into a neighbor's window. We mount the passage camera high on your own wall and point it along the passage rather than across it, then mask the strip where the neighbor's windows appear. On masonry fronts we set anchors in the face of solid brick rather than the softer mortar joints, drill any wall pass-through on a slight downward slope toward the outside so water runs away, sleeve and seal the hole, and fit a junction box behind the camera to hide the connection. Cable in these houses usually drops into the basement, which keeps it inside the owner's space and away from a tenant's floor.

The millennium houses near the point: siding and hidden cable

A house built on the old beach club land around 2000 is a different job. Siding over wood framing cracks if a camera is screwed straight into it, and water follows the screws, so we fit a mounting block made for siding and find the framing behind it so the screws bite into wood. Soffits under the roof edge are often the best camera spots on these houses, because they shelter the camera from rain and put it high enough to be out of reach. Houses of this age sometimes have low-voltage cable already run to the garage, the utility room or the exterior corners. We never assume it is usable; we trace and test every run before we rely on it, and if it is sound and goes where a camera should be, we use it and save you a new pull.

Condo townhouses: small mounts and a tidy facade

In a gated condo development, the outside of your unit is often subject to rules about appearance. Before we quote, we ask you to check your association's bylaws on exterior fixtures and, if needed, to get approval in writing. Then we plan for the smallest camera that will do the job, a color that matches the trim where the manufacturer offers one, and cable that disappears through the wall rather than running down the face of the building. On attached townhouses, the cameras over your door and garage should cover your own entry and stop at the line between your unit and the next one.

Warehouses: height, racking and distance

Industrial buildings in the Zerega area are big, and the camera work is mostly a problem of height and distance. Cameras on outside walls need to be high enough that nobody can reach them from the ground or a truck bed, which usually means a lift rather than a ladder. Inside, racking blocks long views, so aisle cameras go at the ends of aisles looking down them, not across them. Cable runs are long, so we carry them on J-hooks or tray clear of lights and heaters, core-drill block walls once in a planned spot, and put any cable on the outside of the building in metal conduit. Where a run passes through a fire-rated wall or floor, we seal it with a rated fire-stopping material, which is both a code requirement and, as industry guidance points out, a real part of the cost.

Corridor storefronts: faces at the door, hands at the counter

A shop on Castle Hill Avenue needs different views from a house. The door camera has to show faces as people come in, which means mounting it inside, facing the door, at a height and angle that catches people before they turn away, and checking it against the glare of a bright street behind them. The counter camera needs to look down at the register and the counter top so cash and goods are clear. A stockroom camera should cover the rear door, because back doors on commercial blocks are where deliveries come in and where losses tend to go out. The recorder should not sit under the counter; it belongs in a locked cabinet or back office, on battery backup, with the owner able to view it from home. If you rent the space, the inside is yours to cover, but the outside of the building and any shared entrance to the apartments above belong to the building owner's decision.

Shopping-center stores: your space, their lot

Stores in a shopping center sit inside someone else's security system. The center's management controls the parking lot, the walkways and the outside of the building, and it often has its own cameras there. That is useful, but it does not show what happens inside your store, and you may not be able to get its footage quickly. So the plan for a tenant concentrates on the inside: the entrance from the inside looking out, the register, high-value displays, the stockroom and the receiving door. Before we mount anything on a wall, ceiling or storefront, we ask you to check your lease and the center's tenant rules on contractors, working hours and ceiling access, and to get management's answer in writing.

Warehouses and yards: planned from the gate inward

An industrial site in the Zerega area is busy during the shift and empty afterward, and the cameras have to be useful in both states. We start at the gate: one camera close enough that a person or vehicle coming through fills the picture, and a second looking down the yard. Then the dock doors, each covered from inside and outside, because that is where goods leave. Then the aisles, covered from the ends because racking blocks every view across. Then the office and wherever the recorder lives, so the recorder itself is on camera. Outside cameras go high, on the building or on poles you own, with cable in metal conduit. And because the person checking the cameras is often an owner at home or a manager on the road, we set up remote access with separate users, so staff can watch live views without being able to delete recordings or change settings.

Rules and practical limits for Castle Hill business cameras

  • No blanket camera mandate. As one NYC security guide puts it, there is no blanket law in the city's Administrative Code requiring every building to install cameras. A business installs cameras because it chooses to, so the owner sets the scope.
  • Landlords control shared space. A tenant business, like a tenant household, needs the owner's permission for equipment in common areas and on the outside of the building. Brick Underground makes the same point for residential hallways.
  • Fire-rated walls need rated seals. Wherever camera cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor, the opening has to be sealed with rated fire-stopping. We seal what we open, and the quote shows it.
  • Light comes before lenses. NYCHA paired its Castle Hill Houses camera plans with new lights for a reason. We check the lighting at every camera position and tell you where a light is needed before a camera can do its job.
  • New circuits are electrical work. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable powered from the recorder or a switch. If a site needs a new outlet or circuit, that is work for a licensed electrician, and the quote says so plainly.
  • Some places stay off camera. We do not put cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere people expect privacy, and we will tell you if a requested view crosses that line. A lawyer's answer on Justia notes that common-area cameras are generally lawful when voice is not recorded, so we set up video-only systems unless you have taken your own legal advice.

My door camera keeps dropping off the WiFi inside our Castle Hill Houses apartment. What is going on?

Almost always, the router is too far from the door or there is too much masonry between them. Large public housing buildings of that era are built with heavy interior walls, and a battery camera at the front door may be several rooms away from a router sitting by the TV. Battery cameras also save power by connecting only when they detect motion, so a weak signal shows up as missed alerts and late clips rather than a dead camera. We check the signal strength at the door with a phone before choosing a device, and where it is weak we move the router to a more central spot or add a small plug-in WiFi extender inside the apartment, which needs no drilling. Because NYCHA's guidance allows only a wireless device on the door, solving the signal inside your own apartment is the right fix.

The city said CCTV was coming to Castle Hill Houses after the new lights went in. How do I find out what actually covers my building?

Ask NYCHA directly, through the development's management office or your resident association. A resident at another Bronx development got a lease violation over cameras. How do I make sure mine does not cause trouble?

AOL reported in 2012 that a Parkside Houses resident received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing cameras, and we do not know the details of her setup. Keep the view on your doorway, not down the corridor into other homes. Do not add anything to walls, ceilings, stairwells or the building entrance. If you are unsure, show the device to the management office before it goes up. We will not install anything for a NYCHA resident that steps outside the guidance.

Jamie Towers and other co-ops

I own shares at Jamie Towers. What should I take to management before putting a camera on my apartment door?

A short written description is usually enough to start: the make and model of the device, how it attaches, whether it needs any holes or wiring, where it points and how far it sees, and whether it records video only. Include a photo of the device and a note of who will install it. Large co-ops often ask any outside contractor for proof of insurance and a scheduled time to work, so ask management what it needs. We prepare the description for you, keep the device to the smallest and least invasive option that does the job, and install only what management approves.

Our co-op board is weighing cameras for the grounds and parking areas. What should a proposal cover?

A good proposal answers six questions before the board votes. Which areas each camera will see, shown on a site plan or photos. How each area is lit after dark, since residents at Jamie Towers raised inadequate lighting in 2016 and a camera only records what light reaches. Where cable will travel and how every wall, floor and ceiling opening will be sealed. Where the recorder will sit and how it is protected. How long footage is kept before it is overwritten. And who can view footage, who can export it and who can change settings, with the board itself holding the master account so that a change of managing agent does not take the system with it. We write proposals in that order.

Can cameras make up for the security-guard hours a building has cut?

Not on their own. A guard can stop someone, call for help and notice a door propped open; a camera records what happens so it can be reviewed later, and only if the area is lit and the camera is aimed well. What cameras can do after a cut in staffing is cover the places guards used to watch most often, such as entrances, garages and package areas, and give management a record of incidents. If a board is deciding between the two, we will give an honest description of what a camera plan can and cannot cover, and leave the staffing decision where it belongs.

Houses near the point and on the older blocks

Our house near Castle Hill Point was built around 2000. Could there already be camera wiring in the walls?

A new building is going up on the empty lot next to our two-family. Should we install cameras now or wait?

Install the views you need now, and plan to adjust one or two later. Construction sites attract their own traffic and noise, and the side of your house facing the lot may be the one you most want covered while work goes on. But a new building will change light, shadows and sight lines once it is up, and may put new windows facing your yard. So we aim the cameras on that side for today's conditions, mount them so they can be re-aimed without new holes, and suggest you look at the views again when the neighbor's building is finished, masking any new windows that come into frame.

The upstairs apartment in our two-family is empty between tenants. Can a camera keep an eye on it?

Yes, for the time it is empty, and the plan should say clearly when that ends. A small indoor camera aimed at the apartment's entry door, connected to the owner's recorder or WiFi, shows anyone going in while the unit is vacant. The exterior cameras on the stair entrance and yard cover the approaches. Before a new tenant moves in, the indoor camera inside the apartment comes out completely, not just switched off, because that space becomes the tenant's home. We plan it as a temporary camera from the start, so removing it takes minutes and leaves nothing behind but a small screw hole you can fill.

Our back yard faces the water near Castle Hill Point. Will glare off the water spoil the picture?

It can at certain times of day, mostly when the sun is low and reflecting off the water toward the camera. Cameras with a wide dynamic range setting handle bright and dark areas in one scene better than basic models, and the setting needs to be tuned on site, not left at default. Placement matters more than settings: a camera angled slightly down at the yard and patio, with as little sky and water in the frame as possible, avoids most of the problem. A soffit or overhang above the camera helps shade the lens. We check the view at the times of day you care about and adjust before we leave.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Castle Hill

"Castle Hill is a high-crime area, so you need cameras on every side of the house."

The data do not agree with each other, let alone with that claim. DwellCheck, using NYPD figures, counts 2,630 crimes in Castle Hill in 2026, down 10 percent, and calls it safer than 76 percent of the Bronx. DoorProfit puts it at safer than 52 percent of Bronx neighborhoods.

"Public housing is already covered by city cameras, so residents don't need their own."

NYCHA does run a large camera program, with more than 13,000 cameras citywide and more than $64.6 million spent since 2014, according to the Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice. Castle Hill Houses got 749 new lights in 2016, with CCTV named as the next phase.

But a citywide total does not tell you whether a camera covers the hallway outside your door. We could not confirm the current camera layout at Castle Hill Houses, and NYCHA's footage is NYCHA's to review and release, not yours.

That is why many residents also want a small camera on their own door, inside NYCHA's guidance, that records to an app they control. The two do different jobs, and one does not replace the other.

"A state law lets every tenant put up a Wi-Fi camera without asking."

We searched for the statute behind those claims and did not find it. When two sources give two different years for the same law and neither cites the text, we do not repeat it as a rule.

What is clear is narrower: NYCHA publishes its own guidance for door cameras, co-op boards control common areas, and Brick Underground notes that tenants need landlord permission for equipment in hallways. Check your lease, ask in writing, and keep the camera on your own door.

"Co-op shareholders own their apartment, so they can put up whatever camera they like."

In a co-op you own shares and hold a proprietary lease, and the building's common areas, including hallways, garages and grounds, are under the board's control. Guidance on NYC co-ops says hallway cameras almost always require board approval.

At a complex the size of Jamie Towers, management may also have rules about contractors, insurance and working hours, and the board has its own reasons for wanting a say in where cameras point and who sees the footage.

So the order is simple: ask management, describe the device in writing, and install what is approved. A camera that goes up without approval can be ordered down, which wastes the money spent on it.

"Better lighting makes cameras unnecessary."

The city's own approach at Castle Hill Houses says otherwise. It installed new lights first and described cameras and access controls as the next phase, treating lighting as the groundwork for cameras rather than a replacement.

It works the other way too: a camera in the dark records very little. Our plans pair each camera with the light on its target, and we tell you plainly where a light should be added before a camera is worth mounting.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for any house."

Wireless cameras are easy to buy and quick to put up, which is why quick answers recommend them. On a Castle Hill two-family, the trouble starts later: batteries to recharge on every wall, WiFi that has to reach through masonry, and a picture that drops when the signal does.

A house with four to six views is usually better served by wired cameras running to one recorder indoors, with the recorder on battery backup. The cameras are powered constantly, the footage stays in the house, and nothing needs to be climbed to for a charge.

Wireless still has its place. It is the right answer for a NYCHA door, a temporary indoor camera in a vacant apartment, or a spot where a wire truly cannot go. We use each where it fits, not one everywhere.

"A warehouse just needs more of the same cameras you'd put on a house."

A warehouse in the Zerega area is not a big house. Distances are longer, walls are higher, racking blocks views, and the building may have fire-rated walls that every cable has to pass through with a rated seal, which industry guidance notes is a code requirement and a cost factor.

The cameras themselves differ: longer lenses for aisles and yards, cameras close to the gate so faces and vehicles are recognizable, and outside cameras high enough to be out of reach, often requiring a lift.

The back end differs too: a PoE switch, a recorder in a locked room on battery backup, and separate users so staff can watch without deleting. That is why we quote industrial jobs from a walk-through, never from a per-camera average.

Castle Hill Security Camera Installation FAQ

We own a condo townhouse behind a gatehouse. Can we put cameras on the outside of our own unit?
+

Usually yes, but check your association's bylaws first, because many condo communities have rules about fixtures on exteriors. Ask the board or managing agent in writing and keep their answer. Once approved, we use small cameras in colors that suit the trim where available, run cable through the wall rather than down the facade, and keep each view on your own door, garage and drive, stopping at the line with the next unit. A manned gate watches who enters the community; your cameras watch your own doorstep.

Several neighbors on our block of two-family houses want cameras. Can we share one system for the whole street?
+

We recommend separate systems, one per house, each owned and controlled by that household. A shared recorder raises hard questions about who can see what, who keeps the footage and what happens when a house is sold. Separate systems can still be planned together so the cameras complement each other, with each house covering its own entrances and yard. If neighbors want to share particular clips after an incident, each owner can export and send their own footage.

I have a house in Castle Hill and a store on Castle Hill Avenue. Can I watch both in one app?
+

Often yes, if both systems use the same recorder brand or a brand whose app can add more than one recorder. Each site keeps its own recorder and footage, and the app shows them as two locations you switch between. Staff at the store can have their own login that sees only the store. If you already have a system at one site, tell us the make so we can plan the second one to match.

I already bought a camera kit online. Will you install it on my house?
+

Yes, as a labor-only job quoted in writing. Before we mount anything, we check that the cameras are rated for outdoor use where they will be outside, that the kit's recorder has enough channels and storage for what you want, and that the included cable lengths will actually reach. If a piece of the kit will not do the job, we tell you before we start. We then plan the views and the cable route the same way we would for equipment we supply.

How would I know if one of my cameras stopped recording?
+

Most recorders we install can send a phone notification when a camera loses its video signal or when the hard drive has a fault. We switch those alerts on at handover and send a test so you know what they look like. It is also worth opening the app once a week and checking that each camera shows a live picture and that last night's recordings are there. If a camera goes dark, call us with the camera's label from the written map.

Can someone hack into my cameras through the internet?
+

Any device on the internet can be a target, and the most common weakness is a default password that was never changed. At handover we replace every default password on the recorder and cameras, set up separate user accounts instead of sharing one login, and update the recorder firmware where an update is available. We also keep the cameras on the recorder's own network where the system allows it. Keep your passwords to yourself and remove users who no longer need access.

The time stamp on our old recorder is wrong. Does that matter?
+

Yes. If the clock is off, finding a clip takes longer, and footage handed to an insurer or the police has to be explained before it is useful. Recorders can drift or reset after a power cut, and some never switched for daylight saving time. When we install or take over a system, we set the correct time zone, turn on automatic time updates from the internet where possible, and check the time against a phone before we leave.

When we move out of our NYCHA apartment, how does the door camera come off without damage?
+

Because the camera was mounted over the peephole or with adhesive and no holes, it comes off cleanly. Most peephole cameras simply unclamp. Adhesive mounts are released by warming the pad gently with a hair dryer and working it loose slowly, then removing any residue with a product meant for painted surfaces. Take the device with you, since it is yours, and remove it from your WiFi and app account. If you would like help, we can do the removal as a short visit.

A home attendant works in my parent's apartment. Is it all right to have an indoor camera there?
+

Be open about it. Your parent should agree to the camera and choose where it points, and the attendant should be told that a camera is in use and where it is. Keep it out of bathrooms and bedrooms used for dressing, and use video only. If an agency employs the attendant, ask whether it has its own policy. For a firm answer on the legal side, speak to an attorney. We set the camera up on the apartment's WiFi and show your parent how to switch it off.

The narrow walkway between our house and the next one is the only way into our back yard. How do you cover it?
+

From one end, high on your own wall, looking along the walkway rather than across it. That way anyone entering the passage walks toward the camera and fills more of the picture as they come. We add a privacy mask over any of the neighbor's windows that appear in the frame and check the light, since these passages are often the darkest part of the property; a camera with its own light or a small fixture on your wall usually solves that.
