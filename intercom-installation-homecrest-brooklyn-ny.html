The Neighborhood

Why Homecrest Properties Need Intercom Installation

Intercom planning in Homecrest has to start from a number most residents never think about: how many households stand behind a single front door. In the city's PLUTO tax-lot file, read for the blocks inside Wikipedia's boundaries, the neighborhood holds about 13,610 homes on roughly 3,097 residential lots, and 95 elevator buildings account for about 6,351 of those homes, close to half. Fifteen lots hold a hundred or more homes each, 1,706 homes in all, and another 51 lots hold between fifty and ninety-nine, 3,594 in all. In buildings like these, the lobby panel is not a doorbell. It is the only way a visiting nurse, a grandchild, a plumber or a food delivery reaches sixty or more apartments, and when it fails, it fails for every one of them at the same moment. The work therefore looks less like hanging a station by a door and more like replacing a small telephone exchange: a panel with a directory at the entrance, a controller and power supply in the cellar, a riser that climbs through the building beside each line of apartments, and a station in every unit, each of which has to be reached, tested and labeled.

Much of that equipment comes from a single burst of building. The same tax-lot file records 179 lots and 2,322 homes built in the 1960s, most of them six-story brick elevator buildings on the numbered East streets. Brick Underground's 2022 guide to Homecrest describes two of them: a six-story, 66-unit co-op at 2299 East 13th Street from 1960, with an elevator, an upgraded lobby, a security system and a live-in super, and a six-story, 64-unit building at 1750 East 14th Street from 1961. Twenty-four of the neighborhood's elevator buildings are co-ops, holding about 1,893 apartments between them. A co-op cannot replace its entry system the way a homeowner swaps a doorbell. The board budgets for it, sometimes across more than one year; shareholders have to open their apartments so stations can be changed; and the managing agent wants insurance certificates, a schedule and a notice for the lobby. The wiring in these buildings is now around sixty years old, and in many of them it has been patched by several different companies along the way. Before a board spends anything, it needs to know which parts of that wiring still test well, because that one fact decides whether the job is a panel-and-stations change or a full rewire.

The second generation of Homecrest front doors arrived after 2005, when zoning restrictions on the north side of the neighborhood and along Ocean Avenue were relaxed and condominium development followed. The tax-lot file shows 57 elevator and condominium lots built since 2000, with about 1,007 homes, the largest of them on Ocean Avenue. Brick Underground's examples include the eight-story, 22-unit building at 2381 Ocean Avenue from 2006 and a 16-unit condo of eight floors at 2131 Ocean Avenue that opened in 2019. Buildings of this age usually came with networked entry panels chosen by the developer, sometimes tied to a phone app, a cloud account or a monthly plan. That is convenient on the day the sales office closes and awkward a few years later, when a small owners' board finds that nobody holds the administrator password, that the account is registered to a contractor who has moved on, or that calls stopped reaching phones the week the building changed internet providers. Intercom work in these buildings is often less about new hardware than about taking back control of the system that is already there, and replacing only what cannot be recovered.

The third thing is who stands at the door and who answers it. NeighborhoodScout puts the share of residents born in another country at 65 percent for the Homecrest Avenue and Avenue U area. Nextdoor's neighborhood page notes a large Russian-speaking population, and Avenue U between Coney Island Avenue and Ocean Avenue has carried southern Brooklyn's second Chinatown since the late 1990s, when Chinese residents moved there from Sunset Park. Brick Underground's reporter heard Russian, Hebrew, Italian, Spanish, Chinese and Greek on a single block. A visitor at a Homecrest lobby may not read the language the directory is written in, and the resident upstairs may not read the language the indoor monitor or the app speaks; both sides have to be planned for. So does the lobby as a place where things go missing. A Nextdoor post from the area near East 13th Street and Kings Highway warned neighbors about a man going into buildings around Midwood and Homecrest and carrying off bags of packages. No intercom stops every person who follows a resident inside, but the layout of the entrance decides how often the inner door is left open for strangers, and whether a delivery driver ever needs to be let past the vestibule at all.

Same-Day Repair in Homecrest

Need Repair in Homecrest? One Line of Apartments Gone Quiet, a Panel Calling the Wrong Floor, or an App That Stopped Opening the Lobby

Repair calls from Homecrest buildings follow the shape of their wiring. In a six-story elevator building the apartments stack in lines, and each line's stations usually hang off a cable that climbs the building in one riser. When a whole line goes quiet, every apartment ending in A, for example, while the rest of the building still rings, the fault is nearly always in that line's riser or in a splice box on one floor, not in the handsets themselves, and replacing handsets one by one only spends money. When calls arrive at the wrong apartment, the usual cause is a renovation that combined, split or renumbered units without anyone updating the panel, or a pair moved on the cellar terminal board by someone who did not mark it. When the release clicks but the door will not open, the strike or its power supply is the suspect before the panel is. In condos built since 2005, the typical complaint is an app that rings but cannot open the door, or a panel that stopped calling phones after a router was replaced or a cloud account lapsed. In walk-ups and two-families it is the old pattern of a faint call on the top floor and a release that works one week and not the next.

Partial failures matter more than owners sometimes assume. Connextivity, a building-technology firm, points out that a system in which some apartments work and others do not still counts as a violation, and Parker Custom Security's summary of the city rules says HPD treats a non-working intercom in a building that must have one as a Class B hazardous condition, enforced on tenant complaint. If the street door is not latching, lock it by key and deal with that first, because an open door is the real danger. Then call (347) 934-8335. Many single faults, such as one dead riser pair, a failed power supply or a strike that has drifted out of line, are found and fixed in one visit. Service calls and fault tracing are billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, which does not apply to an initial installation, and we tell you before starting how long the tracing is likely to take. If a repair would cost a large share of a replacement, or parts for an old system can no longer be bought, the owner or board gets both options in writing before anyone commits to either.

Intercom Installation Services in Homecrest

Homecrest jobs range from a two-button house system to a lobby that calls more than a hundred apartments. Each one starts with the same three counts, households, floors and entrances, and with one test: whether the wire already in the building can be trusted.

Lobby panels for elevator buildings

Entrance panels sized for sixty to more than a hundred listings, with buttons, a scrolling directory, a keypad or a mix, mounted at the outer or inner lobby door and sealed against weather.

Whole-building station changes

Every apartment's handset or monitor replaced on a planned schedule, floor by floor, so shareholders and tenants know exactly when someone needs to be home.

Retrofits on tested risers

Audio or video systems designed to run over existing two-wire or multi-wire cable, used only on lines that pass continuity and leakage tests during the survey.

New risers where the old ones fail

Condo board takeovers

Directories for many languages

Panels set up so a visitor who reads none of the building's languages can still reach the right apartment by number, with monitors and apps set to each household's language where supported.

Two-family and house systems

Door stations and indoor stations for limestone and brick two-families, rebuilt one-family houses and one-story rows, with a release on each floor that needs one.

House intercoms between rooms

Systems that answer the street door and also let family members call between the kitchen, the basement, an office and the bedrooms in a large rebuilt house.

Store and office doors

Residential doors beside shops on Avenue U, Kings Highway and Coney Island Avenue, and door stations for medical offices, schools and houses of worship.

If a board is also weighing fobs or codes for residents, raise it on the first call. An intercom and an access control reader on the same lobby door can share the strike, the power supply and much of the cable when they are planned together, and the result is one tidy entrance instead of two systems fighting over the same door frame. The same goes for cameras: if the building already records its lobby, we design the panel so its picture and its cable path do not duplicate what is there.

The elevator building of 1959 to 1964: lines, risers and a sixty-apartment directory

This is the building that defines intercom work in Homecrest. A brick front with a canopy or a recessed entry, an outer street door and an inner lobby door, a lobby with mailboxes and the elevator, and five or six floors of apartments arranged in lines along the corridors. The original intercom in a building like this usually put the call buttons and a speaker at the entrance, an amplifier or power unit in the cellar, and a handset beside the door of every apartment, all joined by multi-conductor cable climbing in a riser next to each line. Over sixty years, handsets were swapped one by one as they failed, a few buttons were replaced, cable was spliced in hallway boxes, and the cellar terminal board was relabeled by hand, if anyone labeled it at all. The first job on a survey is to turn that history into a map: which pairs serve which line, where every splice sits, how many spare conductors remain, and which lines test clean from the cellar to the top floor. Without that map, every quote for the building is a guess.

The plan then turns on the riser. If each line's cable tests well for continuity and leakage and has enough conductors, systems built to run over existing two-wire or multi-wire cable can bring audio, and often video, to every apartment without opening a single corridor wall. If the cable is brittle, short of conductors, or spliced in too many floor boxes, new cable goes up the building, normally through the existing riser path, a closet stack or a painted raceway in the stairwell, and sometimes through a core in a cellar block wall into the room where the controller will sit. The panel has to hold sixty or more listings, so the choice is between a long bank of buttons and a directory with a keypad; in a building where visitors read several alphabets, apartment numbers on a keypad tend to work better than names alone. Because one controller now calls every apartment, it belongs in a dry, locked cellar space with battery backup, on a circuit labeled at the panel, and every line is labeled at both ends before the crew leaves.

The larger co-ops at the edges: more than a hundred apartments behind one panel

A handful of buildings near Homecrest's edges are far bigger than the six-story norm. Listings for the co-op at 2665 Homecrest Avenue, which Compass files under Sheepshead Bay, describe 157 units on six stories, built in 1962, and rental listings there mention security cameras throughout. At that size, the directory becomes the main design problem. A list of 150 names is too long for a column of buttons and too slow to scroll on a small screen, so the panel needs a search, a keypad for apartment numbers, or both, plus a short printed instruction for visitors who have never seen it before. Calls have to reach the right apartment within a few seconds, because a delivery driver who waits longer than that tends to press something else, and every wrong call is a resident buzzing in a stranger out of politeness.

Big buildings also have more people with a stake in the lobby. The super, the managing agent, the board, a camera contractor and sometimes a separate access control vendor all work around the same entrance. Before we draw anything, we ask who already records the lobby, where the building's network equipment lives, who answers after-hours calls, which doors besides the main one residents actually use, and how the building prefers to schedule access to apartments. The answers go into the written design so the panel, the cable and the programming fit what is already there, and the building does not end up with three systems that each do part of the job and none of them well.

Ocean Avenue condominiums since 2006: networked panels and the missing login

The condos that followed the 2005 zoning change look nothing like the 1960s lobbies inside. Their entry panels are usually networked devices on the building's own data cabling, with apartment monitors or apps connected over the same network, a switch and a router in a closet or a small rack, and often a cloud account that lets residents answer from a phone. Built well, this is a better system than any of its predecessors: listings change from a laptop, calls forward to phones, and a dead monitor in one unit does not affect the others. The trouble in small condos is rarely the hardware. It is that the developer's installer set everything up under accounts the board never received, that the network gear is not on battery backup, or that a monthly plan renews on a card nobody recognizes.

When a board asks us to take over a system like this, we start with an inventory rather than a sales pitch: the make and model of the panel and stations, whether the manufacturer still supports them, who the cloud account is registered to, what the network equipment is and who knows its passwords, and whether the panel can still be reset and reclaimed by the building. Where the system can be recovered, the board receives the administrator login, the owners get fresh app invitations, and the directory is rebuilt from the board's own list. Where it cannot, because the hardware is locked to an account that will not release it, we price the replacement of only the parts that are stuck and keep the cabling, which in buildings of this age is usually sound.

The prewar walk-up: twenty-four apartments and no elevator shaft

Homecrest's prewar walk-ups, like the three-story, 24-unit building at 1717 East 14th Street from 1932, spread their apartments sideways rather than up. Eight apartments to a floor means long horizontal runs along each corridor, and the original wiring often travels along hallway ceilings and around door frames rather than in a tidy vertical riser. There is no elevator shaft or machine room to share, so the route for any new cable has to be found in closets, stair walls and the cellar. The old system in a building like this is frequently a basic audio intercom or a buzzer with a speaker at the door, and its stations have been replaced piecemeal by different hands. On the survey we follow the wiring floor by floor, open every junction box we can reach, and test each apartment's run on its own, because a single tired splice in a corridor ceiling can silence three apartments at once.

A building of this size also carries clear legal weight. With twenty-four apartments it is well above the eight-apartment line in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, which the rules section below explains, so a working intercom from each apartment to the main entrance is part of keeping the building in order, along with entrance doors that close and lock by themselves. The law asks for a working intercom, not a video one. Whether to add video is a choice about tenants and visitors, and in a walk-up the deciding factor is usually the wire: where the existing runs test well, a retrofit can add video without opening plaster; where they fail, the job becomes a rewire, and the cost of video over audio becomes a small part of a larger number.

Limestone and brick two-families: two households, one decision

Accord Real Estate Group describes many Homecrest homes as limestone row two-families, alongside brick and brick-and-stucco two-families, and the tax-lot file counts about 1,312 two-family lots. In some of these houses both households come through one front door and a shared hall; in others the second apartment has its own entrance, and a visitor has to know which door to try. The intercom question is the same either way: each household needs its own call, its own release for the door it actually uses, and nothing that lets the tenant's visitor into the owner's apartment. Where the house still has its original two-button bell plate and transformer, we test the old bell wire before planning around it; where it has a battery video doorbell and nothing connected to the door, the work is mostly a strike, a power supply and two indoor stations.

Two-families are also where owners most often ask for less, and that is sometimes right. An owner who lives on the first floor and is home most of the day may need only an audio handset by the kitchen, while the tenant upstairs gains the most from a screen. Both stations sit on one system with one supply, each releasing only the street door. A limestone front is hard, dense stone, and we would rather mount a station on a trim plate over an existing opening, or on the door surround, than drill new holes in a carved face. If the opening left by an old plate does not fit, the quote says so, along with how the stone or brick will look afterward.

Rebuilt one-family houses and the one-story rows south of Avenue V

Wikipedia notes that many private two-story homes in Homecrest have been renovated into much larger houses, and that south of Avenue V one-story bungalow homes are common, as are rows of one-story brick homes with community backyards and driveways. The rebuilt houses raise a different question from every other building on this page: how many places inside the house need to hear the door, and should the same system let family members call one another. A large house with a finished basement, a home office and bedrooms two floors up can have stations in each of those places, plus a door station at the front and, if visitors really arrive there, at the side. Where the walls are open during a renovation, cable to each future station costs very little; once the drywall is painted, the route has to be planned around it.

The one-story rows need care for a different reason. When the backyard and the driveway belong to several houses together, nothing of ours goes onto that shared ground without the neighbors' written agreement. A door station on a row house belongs on the house's own wall or door frame, with its cable entering the owner's own space, and its camera aimed at the owner's own step rather than across a neighbor's window. These houses are small, the runs are short, and most of the work is careful mounting on brick and a clean path to an indoor station by the door the family uses most.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Homecrest Lobby Tells You Before the Panel Comes Off the Wall

Compare the directory with the mailboxes. In the lobby, the technician counts the listings on the panel and the boxes on the mailbox bank. When they disagree, apartments have been combined, split or renumbered at some point, and the riser almost certainly carries a pair that no longer goes where its label says.

Ride to the top and test downward. The top floor is the far end of every line. If a call from the lobby sounds clean there, the trunk of that line is probably sound; if it is faint or crackling, the technician works down floor by floor to the first box where it gets better.

Read the cellar terminal board as a history. Hand-written labels in three different inks, jumper wires crossing between blocks, and pairs tied off with tape are the record of every repair since the 1960s. Each one is photographed before anything is moved.

Open one handset in a stack. The back of one apartment station shows how many conductors reach each unit and what condition they are in, which says more about the riser than any lobby test.

Let the lobby door go from full open. If it does not close and latch by itself, the closer and strike come before any panel.

Changeover Planning

Replacing a Sixty-Year-Old Lobby System in Homecrest Without Leaving Anyone Unreachable

The hardest part of a Homecrest lobby replacement is not the panel. It is changing a system that sixty or more households use every day without a stretch in which some of them cannot be reached, and doing it inside a co-op or rental where access to every apartment has to be arranged. This section explains how we plan that changeover, so a board, a managing agent or an owner knows what the survey is checking and what will happen on each day of the work.

Map every line first. Before anything is ordered, each line of apartments is traced from its station to the cellar terminal board and tested for continuity and leakage between conductors. The result is a simple table that goes into the quote: which lines will be reused, which will be replaced, and why. That table is also what lets a board compare our quote with anyone else's on the same footing, instead of comparing two panel brochures.

Choose a directory visitors can actually use. In a building where residents speak Russian, Chinese, Hebrew, Spanish and English, no single language on the panel serves every visitor. What works for everyone is a number. We usually recommend that the panel call apartments by their apartment number on a keypad, with a name directory as a second way in for visitors who know only a family name, and a short instruction on the panel that relies on digits and simple symbols rather than sentences. Names can be shown in more than one script on many screens, and each household's indoor monitor or app can often be set to its own language, but the visitor's path should never depend on reading. Whether names appear at all, and in what form, is the building's decision; some boards list apartment numbers only.

Keep the old system alive until the new one answers. Where the existing riser is being replaced, new cable is pulled alongside the old, and the old system keeps calling apartments until the new panel and stations are ready line by line. Where the riser is being reused, the cutover happens one line at a time, usually in a single working day for the lobby and a set of scheduled days for the apartments, and the building posts notices in advance so residents know when to expect a technician at their door. Nobody should come home to a lobby where neither system works.

Plan access to apartments like a schedule, not a hope. Changing stations means entering every unit. We work with the super or the managing agent to group apartments by line and floor, publish a timetable, and keep a list of units that could not be reached so they are finished on a return visit rather than forgotten. In a co-op, the board decides how shareholders are notified; in a rental, the owner handles notice to tenants. We never enter an apartment without the building's arrangement and the resident's knowledge.

Put the controller somewhere the super can find it in the dark. The controller and power supply go in a dry, locked cellar location away from the boiler's heat and any spot where water collects, on a labeled circuit, with battery backup sized for the panel and the strike where the design calls for it. If a new or relocated electrical circuit is needed, a licensed electrician does that part, and we coordinate the timing.

Leave a record the next person can read. At handover the building receives the line table, the label map of the cellar terminal board and every floor box, the directory list, the administrator login and the model numbers of everything installed. That paperwork is what turns the next repair from a hunt into a phone call.

The Rules at the Lobby Door

Four-Unit Houses to 157-Unit Co-ops: What New York Rules Ask of a Homecrest Intercom

Homecrest has every size of residential building the city's rules distinguish between, often on the same block, so the same intercom question can have different answers a few doors apart. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources and the vendor guides we read say, including where they disagree, so you can put the right questions to your attorney, your managing agent or the city.

Sources disagree on where the intercom requirement starts. Seneca Security's compliance guide says the requirement applies to most residential buildings with three or more units. Parker Custom Security says it attaches at eight apartments, and Connextivity ties it to buildings of eight or more units built after January 1, 1968. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 speaks of buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968 containing eight or more apartments being equipped with automatic self-closing and self-locking doors and an intercommunication system, and Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a sets out when Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments need an intercom at the self-locking main entrance. Our reading is that eight apartments is the line for the intercom itself. A two-story, four-unit building like 2026 East 12th Street, built in 1926, sits below it; a 24-unit walk-up or a 64-unit elevator building sits well above it.

Below eight apartments, the doors still matter. Parker notes that buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement but still have to meet the self-closing and self-locking door rules. HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors in all multiple dwellings must be self-closing, and that the owner, manager or superintendent should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms. HPD's FY24 report on its proactive self-closing door inspections counts 3,151 violations issued citywide that year.

What a "working" intercom means. 1 RCNY 42-01 describes the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device. Impera Security's guide puts it plainly: the law requires a working intercom, not a modern video intercom. Connextivity adds that a system where some apartments work and others do not is still a violation, and Parker describes a dead intercom as a Class B hazardous violation, enforced on tenant complaint.

Heights at the panel. 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. We measure from where a visitor actually stands, which on a stepped Homecrest entrance is not always the lobby floor. For offices and stores open to the public, ADA reach rules for operable parts apply too.

Getting out is never controlled. Connextivity notes that any access control configuration that impedes interior egress violates the city's fire code. Every door we fit with a strike opens from inside without a key, code or button. Parker also points out that roof doors need to be self-closing but not self-locking, with a bolt or lock operable from inside, which matters if anyone suggests wiring a roof door to the intercom release.

Permits and licenses. The UpCodes text of the city's electrical code says no electrical permit is needed for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, provided the work is done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person, and that life-safety system wiring is reserved for licensed electricians. Some sources, including Low Voltage Nation, describe low-voltage work in the city as needing DOB permits, so on larger jobs we confirm the filing position before work starts. Any new line-voltage circuit for a power supply is done by a licensed electrician. AESS works under New York State license #12000287431.

Landmarks. We found no historic district inside Homecrest. Ocean Parkway, which some definitions use as the western edge, was designated a city scenic landmark on January 28, 1975; we found nothing saying that designation reaches the facades of buildings facing it, but if your entrance is on the parkway and you are replacing a panel on the front of the building, a check of the Landmarks Preservation Commission's map before drilling costs nothing.

When a violation is open. If HPD has already issued an intercom violation, repairing the system is the first step and documenting the repair is the second. We read open violations for the building before quoting and leave a written completion record the owner can use.

Business Intercom Installation in Homecrest

CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes Avenue U as a mix of Chinese, Uzbek, Russian and Caribbean shops and eateries. The cards below describe the kinds of premises that ask for door stations here; we do not name any business as a customer.

Practices that see patients by appointment and need a front desk to see and speak with each arrival before releasing the door, with calls that stop when the office closes.

The residents' door beside a shop window on Avenue U, Kings Highway or Coney Island Avenue, with its own station, its own power and a release no shop employee controls.

Tax, travel, insurance and legal offices on the second floor over a store, where clients ring at a street door shared with other tenants.

Entrances where staff need to see who is outside, speak with them and decide, and where the station must work even when a front desk is briefly empty.

Offices of groups like Homecrest Community Services, founded in 1997, where clients arrive throughout the day and a single staff member answers the door.

Most commercial work in Homecrest happens in rented space, which shapes everything that follows. The store or practice leases its floor, the building owner controls the facade and the residential stair, and the residents upstairs have their own door that must keep working whatever happens in the shop. Before any commercial job we confirm in writing who approved the work, which doors belong to the business and which to the building, and what hours we can be there without being in customers' way. For a medical office, the useful setup is a door station with a clear picture at the front desk, a release that opens the street door for a few seconds and then relocks it, and a schedule that sends after-hours calls to a phone or nowhere at all. For a store with apartments above, it is two separate systems sharing nothing but the facade. For a school or a house of worship, it is a station that calls an office during set hours, with a clear fallback when the office is unstaffed, and door hardware that never stands in the way of people leaving.

Compliance notes for Homecrest offices, stores and mixed-use buildings

  • Free exit at every controlled door. A strike controls the way in only; the door opens from inside without a key, code or button, in line with the fire code limits on egress.
  • Self-closing residential doors. Where apartments share the building, the residents' entrance must still close and lock on its own after each release, under HPD's self-closing door rules.
  • Reachable controls. 1 RCNY 42-01 caps push buttons at six feet and puts the speaking device between four and five feet; public-facing offices and shops also meet ADA reach rules, which usually means lower buttons.
  • Residents kept apart from the business. The apartments' system gets its own power and cable path so store hours, store renovations and changes of tenant do not touch the residential door.
  • Life-safety devices left clear. Stations and cable never block exit signs, fire alarm devices, sprinkler heads or the swing of an exit door, and low-voltage cable does not share a pathway with power wiring.
  • Owner approval in writing. In leased space, the building owner's written approval comes before any hole in the facade or the common hallway.

Packages, Many Languages and Daily Traffic: Planning a Homecrest Intercom Around How the Front Door Is Used

Most of what goes wrong at a Homecrest entrance is ordinary, not dramatic. The 61st Precinct, which patrols Homecrest, ranked 5th safest of 69 patrol areas for per-capita crime in 2010. A third-party crime index puts burglary in the neighborhood at 29% below average. So the case for a good intercom here is less about fear and more about the daily traffic through the lobby door: deliveries, visitors, shoppers coming off Kings Highway, riders walking from the B and Q stations at Kings Highway, Avenue U and Neck Road, and residents who speak many different first languages. A system planned around that traffic works for everyone. A system planned only around the minimum legal requirement usually doesn't.

Homecrest is also more than one kind of neighborhood. Different sources draw its edges differently. Wikipedia uses Kings Highway to the north, Avenue X to the south, Coney Island Avenue to the west and Ocean Avenue to the east. Others end it at Avenue U or start it at Ocean Parkway. Within any of those boundaries, the traffic at the door varies a great deal from one block to the next, and the intercom plan has to match the building it serves.

Package theft starts at the entry, not at the mailbox

A post on Nextdoor from near East 13th Street and Kings Highway warned neighbors about a thief who was going into buildings and stealing bags of packages around Midwood and Homecrest. The key detail is that the thief went into the buildings. Packages left in a vestibule or lobby are only as safe as the door in front of them. In practice, that door gets opened in two ways. A resident buzzes in someone they didn't really identify, or someone follows a resident or courier in before the door closes.

An intercom can't fix the second problem alone, because that depends on the door closing and latching properly. That is covered in the next section. It can do a lot about the first problem. When we lay out a Homecrest system, we consider three things that affect whether the wrong person gets buzzed in:

Can the resident tell who is asking? New York law requires a working intercom, not a video intercom. An audio system that works in every apartment meets the requirement. Still, a resident who hears "delivery" through a crackling speaker has very little to go on. A resident who can see the caller, and see whether a second person is standing just out of frame, can make a better decision. For buildings where packages have gone missing, that difference is often the reason to choose video, even though the code doesn't require it.

Is the door release tied to a real call? In some older lobby systems, the release button in the apartment will open the door at any time, whether or not anyone called. Residents then get used to pressing it whenever the buzzer sounds. When we replace a system, we ask the board or owner how they want releases to work and set it up that way. We also make sure residents know what the new panel and handsets do before the old ones come out.

Where do couriers actually go? Deliveries need a predictable path. If the building has a package room, a locked vestibule or a super who accepts deliveries, the intercom directory and any app-based access should match that arrangement. Otherwise couriers end up buzzing apartment after apartment until someone lets them in.

Mixed-use frontage on Kings Highway, Avenue U and Coney Island Avenue

Kings Highway is a major shopping street, and Coney Island Avenue and Avenue U both have commercial stretches. Near Avenue U, low-rise condominiums and mixed-use buildings have replaced older garages and bungalows. In these buildings, shop customers, deliveries for the stores and residents going upstairs all arrive on the same sidewalk. The apartment entrance needs to be clearly separate from the storefront so a visitor who wants an upstairs unit doesn't wander through the shop, and a shop delivery doesn't get buzzed into the residential hallway.

Small mixed-use buildings do get proper entry stations. A Coney Island Avenue business, Amerikey at 2713 Coney Island Ave, has a photo on its Yelp listing captioned "New 3 unit apartment intercom station for mixed use building." Three apartments over a storefront is a common layout along these avenues. Even at that size, a clearly marked panel at the residential door is what keeps the two kinds of traffic apart.

Along Ocean Avenue, the traffic is different again. Zoning restrictions on the north side of Homecrest and along Ocean Avenue were relaxed in 2005, which led to significant condo development. 2381 Ocean Ave, an eight-story building from 2006, has 22 units. Newer buildings like this usually have wiring and lobby layouts that can support video and app-based entry without much rework. Their owners also tend to expect those features from the start.

Directories and apps for a multilingual building

According to NeighborhoodScout, 65.0% of residents in the Homecrest Avenue and Avenue U area were born in another country. Nextdoor notes a large Russian-speaking population. The Avenue U Chinatown formed in the late 1990s, when Chinese residents moved in from Sunset Park and established a second Chinatown on Avenue U. In many Homecrest buildings, the person reading the lobby panel, the visitor trying to find an apartment, and the resident answering the call won't all share a first language.

This affects how we choose and set up equipment. The research behind this page doesn't confirm which panels or apps support which languages. That varies by manufacturer and model, so we check it during equipment selection rather than assume it. What we can control on every job is the directory. It should be easy to scan, list names the way residents want them listed, and be simple for the super or managing agent to update when someone moves. Where the hardware and resident app offer language settings, we set them to match the people who will actually use them. A visitor who can't work out how to reach an apartment, or a resident who can't follow the app's prompts, makes a building less secure, because both end up depending on whoever happens to be holding the door.

Matching the system to the size of the building

Daily traffic grows with the number of apartments, and so does the cost of getting the design wrong. At one end are houses like 2026 East 12th St, which has four units on two floors and was built in 1926. Many homes on the side streets are two-family limestone rowhouses, or two-family houses in brick or brick and stucco. For a four-unit house, the main goals are reliability and a door station people can use without help. In a building with a live-in super and an existing camera system, the intercom also has to fit how the super handles visitors and deliveries, and it should work alongside the cameras already watching the lobby instead of duplicating them. In a 157-unit co-op, the directory alone becomes a management task. A system that makes updates easy for the managing agent will stay accurate for years. A system that requires a service call for every change will not.

The actual cost of a Homecrest job depends on how many doors there are, whether the existing wiring can be reused, and whether the building chooses audio or video.

Fire, Egress and Roof Doors: Wiring Homecrest Door Releases So the Way Out Always Works

An intercom controls how people get in. A building also has to let people get out, and in an emergency that matters more. Homecrest saw this in 2026. A fire at 1780 East 13th St, a six-story building, injured at least two people, one critically. News 12 Brooklyn reported that the fire was being investigated as arson and that a partial vacate order was issued for the building. Nothing in those reports links the fire to the building's intercom or doors. We mention it because it shows what every door release in a mid-rise apartment building must allow for: residents leaving quickly, sometimes in smoke, while the building's systems are being damaged.

Locking the entrance without trapping anyone inside

The rule is simple. Any access control setup that blocks people from leaving the building violates the New York City fire code. A lobby door can be locked to anyone outside without a key, fob, code or buzz from upstairs. From the inside, it must open for anyone, without a credential, without power and without needing to know how it works.

This affects the hardware choices on every install. When we add an electric strike or magnetic lock as part of a new intercom, we plan how the door opens from inside before we plan how it releases from upstairs. Free exit from inside must not depend on the intercom working. If a fire damages the low-voltage wiring, or the building loses power, the door still has to open from the inside. A release system that is convenient for visitors but blocks the exit is a code violation, regardless of how well the rest of it works.

Roof doors follow a different rule

Roof doors are where well-meaning upgrades most often go wrong. The requirement for roof doors is not the same as for entrance doors. Roof doors must be self-closing but not self-locking, with a bolt or lock that can be operated from the inside. Many of Homecrest's six- and eight-story apartment buildings along the avenues have roof access, so this comes up often when owners ask us to secure "all the doors" during a lobby upgrade.

In practice, we don't put a roof door on the same locking scheme as the street entrance. A roof door that locks automatically behind someone could cut off a way out, and that conflicts with the rule. If an owner is concerned about people reaching the roof from inside, that is a separate discussion from the intercom. We would rather say so at the survey than install something an inspector, or an emergency, shows to be wrong.

A door that doesn't close makes the intercom pointless

The other half of entry security is mechanical. A Homecrest entrance can have a new video panel and an app that works perfectly, and the building will still be open if the door doesn't swing shut and latch. Separate from intercom requirements, sources agree that the self-closing, self-locking entrance door rule applies to buildings of any size. HPD enforces it actively. In fiscal year 2024, HPD's proactive inspections resulted in 3,151 self-closing door violations.

Homecrest's housing is old. The median year built is 1930, and about 41% of homes went up before the 1940s, with another 14.1% by 1949. Doors and frames that old have usually been adjusted, rehung or patched many times. Before we mount a strike or a mag lock, we check whether the closer brings the door all the way shut and whether the latch catches every time. If it doesn't, we tell the owner. Installing electronic access on a door that sticks open defeats the purpose of the upgrade, and it brings back the problem described in the previous section: packages disappearing because anyone could walk in behind a resident.

Who is allowed to do the wiring

The licensing and permit rules for this work are less clear than they should be, so we state what we found and where sources disagree. Under the New York City electrical code provision we reviewed, no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits, as long as the work is done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person. The same provision says life-safety system wiring may only be done by a licensed master electrician or special electrician. Some other sources say low-voltage work in New York City does require DOB permits. At the state level, one licensing guide lists access control, along with burglar alarm, fire alarm and CCTV work, as requiring an Article 6-D license.

Because sources disagree, we don't treat permits as a box to tick based on general reading. We confirm what applies to each building's specific scope before work starts. This matters most when an intercom upgrade connects to anything that could be considered life safety. A door release that interacts with a fire alarm, for example, is not a job to give to whoever offers the lowest price for the panel.

Partial outages after a fire, a flood or a failed repair

Damage to a building rarely takes out the whole intercom. More often, one riser or one line of apartments goes silent while the rest still work. That still counts as a violation. A system where some apartments work and others don't is treated as a violation, not as mostly compliant. A dead intercom is a Class B hazardous violation, and HPD enforces it when tenants complain. One vendor puts fines at $250 to $500 per day until the problem is corrected.

Waiting for enforcement isn't a plan either. In one older case reported by Brick Underground, a renter waited three months for a city inspector to respond to her 311 complaint about a broken intercom. For three months, she couldn't buzz in visitors or deliveries. In a building recovering from a fire or other damage, where some apartments may be vacated while others remain occupied, the people still living there need the intercom more, not less. When we restore a damaged system, we start with the occupied apartments so nobody living in the building is left without a working connection to the front door. We then bring the remaining lines back as their apartments are repaired.

Some buildings will be able to reuse existing wiring and some won't. Abstract Enterprises, a company that also does intercom work in Brooklyn, says Comelit Ultra, Aiphone GT and Akuvox retrofit kits can reuse legacy 2-wire and 6-wire cable. That is a vendor's claim, not independent testing. In a building where heat or water has damaged the risers, the cable may not be worth reusing regardless of what the equipment supports. We test the existing runs before promising that a retrofit will avoid opening walls.

Facades on the parkway side

One more limit applies to some buildings on the western edge. Ocean Parkway was designated a New York City scenic landmark on January 28, 1975. It is also the street where, on June 15, 1894, the first designated bike lane in the United States opened. We found no historic district inside Homecrest itself. We could not confirm whether the parkway's scenic landmark status affects facade work on buildings facing it. For a building on the parkway side where a new entry panel, camera or exterior conduit would change the facade, we recommend checking with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before anything goes on the wall. Moving a panel after it is installed costs far more than asking first.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Homecrest: Questions Answered

These are the situations Homecrest boards, owners, tenants and shareholders raise about new systems: budgets that will not stretch to a whole building in one year, elevators, the aftermath of a fire, tenants who want video, a mail carrier's lock in the old panel, and houses big enough to need an intercom of their own. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Co-op boards and six-story lobbies

Our co-op wants to replace a 1960s lobby panel and every apartment handset, but this year's budget covers only half. Can the job be split across two years without leaving residents on two systems that cannot talk to each other?

Yes, if the split is planned around the riser rather than the calendar. The cleanest first phase is usually the lobby panel, the controller and the power supply, together with any riser lines that failed testing, because those are the parts every apartment depends on. The panel and controller are chosen from a system that can work with the existing handsets during the gap, or with simple adapters on each line, so residents keep getting calls throughout. The second phase replaces the apartment stations line by line, often starting with the lines whose handsets are most worn. What a board should avoid is buying a new panel from one family of equipment and stations from another a year later, which is how buildings end up with calls that work on half the floors. The written quote shows both phases at today's scope, so the board can vote on the whole design once and fund it in two parts.

In our six-story elevator co-op, can a visitor who has been buzzed in also have the elevator sent down to the lobby for them?

That is elevator work, not intercom work, and it is decided by the elevator contractor and the rules that govern elevator controls, not by us. Some elevator controllers can accept a call signal from an access or intercom system; many older controllers in 1960s buildings cannot without changes inside the elevator equipment, which only the elevator company may make. What we can do is provide a clean, documented signal from the intercom controller at the point the elevator contractor specifies, if they confirm it is allowed and their equipment accepts it. In practice, most Homecrest buildings find the lobby call button is fast enough, and the money is better spent on the riser. If your building is planning elevator modernization anyway, raise it with both contractors at the same meeting, because that is the one time the connection is simple.

After a fire, and when the alarm sounds

After a fire, part of our building is under a vacate order. Can the intercom keep serving the floors people still live on while the closed floors are rebuilt?

Usually, yes, and it should. News 12 Brooklyn reported in March 2026 that a fire at 1780 East 13th Street, a six-story Homecrest building, left at least two people injured, one critically, was being investigated as arson, and led to a partial vacate order. In a building in that position, the riser lines that serve occupied floors are separated from those that run through the damaged area, tested, and kept live, while lines on the closed floors are disconnected at the cellar terminal board so a soaked or burned section cannot short the whole system. The directory is changed so visitors cannot ring vacated apartments, and the street door is checked so it still closes and locks for the people living there. When reconstruction starts, new cable can be pulled to the rebuilt floors while the walls are open, which is far cheaper than fishing it in afterward. We coordinate with the owner's contractor and do nothing on floors the city has closed without the owner's arrangement.

Our building has a fire alarm panel in the lobby. Should the intercom unlock the street door by itself when the alarm goes off?

Not on our say-so. Whether any door unlocks automatically on a fire alarm is a decision for the fire alarm system's designer under the rules the FDNY enforces, and it depends on what kind of lock the door has. A fail-secure strike on a street door, the usual intercom arrangement, does not need to release for people to get out, because the door already opens freely from inside. Magnetic locks are a different matter and are treated differently under the code. We do not wire an intercom so that it unlocks a street door on its own initiative, because a door that opens itself in an alarm also opens itself for anyone outside. If the fire alarm designer specifies an interface, we provide it exactly as drawn and leave it documented for the next inspection.

Tenants, landlords and the city

Tenants in our 24-unit walk-up signed a letter asking for a video intercom. Does the owner have to replace a working audio system with video?

Based on what we read, no. Impera Security's guide says the law requires a working intercom, not a modern video one, and the city rule describes the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. If every apartment can call and release the door, the owner has met that part of the rules. A letter like that is still worth taking seriously, because it usually means something is not working well: calls too faint to hear, a release that fails on some floors, or residents walking downstairs to check who is there. A survey tells the owner whether the existing runs could carry video with a retrofit, which can be a modest step on wire that tests well, or whether the building's wiring is the real problem. Either way, the owner gets a written comparison of keeping audio, repairing it, and moving to video.

HPD has an open intercom violation on our building. Once the new system works, is fixing it enough, or does the owner have to tell HPD?

Fixing it is not the end of the matter. HPD expects owners to certify that a violation has been corrected, and a violation that has been repaired but never certified can stay open on the building's record, which shows up when the building is sold, refinanced or inspected again. The certification itself is the owner's responsibility, and the exact procedure and deadlines are set by HPD, so check them on HPD's site or with your managing agent. What we provide is the evidence: a dated written record of the work, the apartments tested, and the result of each call and release, which the owner can keep with the certification. We read the building's open violations before quoting, so the quote says which violation the work addresses.

The mail carrier opens our lobby door with a postal lock built into the old intercom panel. What happens to that lock when the panel is replaced?

Many apartment-building panels include a space for a postal lock, a key switch the mail carrier uses to open the door for deliveries. The lock cylinder in that switch is the Postal Service's, not the building's, so we never make keys for it and never throw it away. When a panel is replaced, we choose one with a matching postal lock cutout where the building needs one, and the building arranges with the local post office for the cylinder to be moved to the new panel or replaced. Tell us on the first call if your panel has one, so the new panel is ordered with the right cutout and the mail is not interrupted. If the building would rather the carrier be let in another way, that is a conversation with the post office, not something we decide.

Every apartment in our building also has its own doorbell beside the apartment door. Can that bell and the lobby call ring the same new indoor station with different sounds?

On many current systems, yes. Most apartment stations have an input for a separate call from a button at the apartment door, and they can play a different tone for it than for a lobby call, so the resident knows at once whether the visitor is downstairs or already outside the apartment. Neighbors and the super are the usual callers at the apartment door. Where the old apartment doorbell has its own wiring back to a chime inside, that wiring can often be reused to feed the station's input, which removes one more device from the wall. If your building's apartment bells run on a separate transformer, we test and label that circuit too, so the super knows which supply feeds what.

Rebuilt houses

We rebuilt our two-story house into a much larger one-family. Can the door intercom also work as a house intercom between the kitchen, the basement and the bedrooms?

Yes. Many door intercom systems allow indoor stations to call one another as well as answer the door, so a parent in the kitchen can call a teenager in the basement, or someone in a home office can be reached without a shout up the stairs. Some systems also let one station broadcast to all the others. The things to decide up front are how many stations the house needs, which ones should ring for the front door and which only for room-to-room calls, and whether any station needs a screen. In a house still under renovation, cable to every future station location costs little while the walls are open; in a finished house, we look for routes through closets, basements and baseboards before touching plaster. If you would like the door to ring your phone when you are away, that is added on top, not instead of the wired stations, so the house still works if the internet is down.
