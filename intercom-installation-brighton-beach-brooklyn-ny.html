Brighton Beach is a narrow strip of Brooklyn squeezed between the Belt Parkway and the Atlantic, from Ocean Parkway on the west to Corbin Place on the east, and almost everyone in it lives behind a shared front door. Developers put up more than thirty six-story apartment buildings here in the 1920s and 1930s, many with Art Deco lobbies. The Neighborhood

Why Brighton Beach Properties Need Intercom Installation

The people behind those doors shape the job as much as the buildings do. Brighton Beach became "Little Odessa" when the large Russian-speaking population arrived from the 1970s onward. In the 2010s, Central Asian immigrants followed. Wikipedia's figures put the share of foreign-born residents at 72.9 percent, and the share who do not speak or understand English at 36.1 percent. It gives a median age of 47.9 for Brighton Beach and Coney Island together, with more than a quarter of residents over 65. Hester Street counted 30.3 percent of residents aged 60 and up, and about 71 percent of households renting. On Brighton Beach Avenue, The Guardian noted in 2022, Cyrillic signs still outnumber almost everything else. For an intercom, that means three things. The directory on the lobby panel has to be readable by the people who use it, and sometimes that means names in Cyrillic letters. The apartment stations have to work for residents who do not hear well, see well or move quickly to the door. And residents need a reliable way to check who is downstairs before they open up. Neighbors still remember the 2011 Brooklyn Paper report of a man who talked his way into apartments, including one on Brighton Seventh Street, by claiming the super had hired him as an exterminator. A face on a screen is a better check than a voice through a grille.

Then there is the water. When Hurricane Sandy came ashore in October 2012, City Limits reported that the surge from the ocean and from the Sheepshead Bay side met on Brighton Beach Avenue in a wave about five feet high. It submerged streets and cars and flooded every basement apartment in the neighborhood. Bklyner later described floodwater on Brighton 7th Street rising to just below the second-story windows. Scientific American noted that some buildings stayed without heat because their basements had flooded. City Limits described a high-rise whose elevator was out because the salt-soaked machinery in its basement needed repair. Those basements are exactly where most of Brighton Beach's intercom transformers, amplifiers and door-release relays were installed. First Street's flood model now rates 99 percent of the neighborhood's properties at risk of flooding over the next thirty years. Salt does not need a storm to do damage, either. A panel facing the boardwalk wind takes a fine salt mist every day, and in summer the sand that piles into breezeways and carports finds its way into button gaps. Put an indoor-grade panel and a cellar power supply in a building like that, and the intercom will look fine on the day it is installed and fail when the building needs it most.

Six Brighton Beach facts that shape an intercom before we pick a single part

  • Most homes share a front door. More than thirty six-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s still stand between Coney Island Avenue and Ocean Parkway, and Hester Street found about 85 percent of the housing was built between 1939 and 1969. Large multi-apartment entrance systems are the normal job here, not the exception.
  • Some directories are very long. Brighton Towers alone has 448 apartments, Oceana has more than 900 units across sixteen buildings, and several prewar buildings have 150 or more. A directory that a visitor can search in seconds matters more than any extra feature.
  • Language and age change the setup. With 36.1 percent of residents not speaking or understanding English and more than a quarter over 65, we plan the directory spelling, the screen prompts and the apartment stations around the people who will use them.

  • The air itself is corrosive. Brightwater Court and the blocks nearest the boardwalk face onshore wind, and the summer brings sand into every vestibule. Outdoor stations have to be built and sealed for that.
  • Underground power is not perfect. On September 27, 2026, News12 reported a manhole fire near 2912 Brighton 7th Street that briefly cut power to ten residents, with Con Ed pointing to underground equipment failure. We plan what the intercom and the door do when the lights go out.

Six kinds of building account for almost every intercom we plan in Brighton Beach. They differ in how many names the directory holds, where the old wires run, how close the front door is to the ocean and who signs off on the work. Those things decide the system long before anyone talks about models.

The prewar six-story co-op: one entrance, a hundred names and a riser older than anyone in the building

Most of the intercoms we plan in Brighton Beach are in buildings like these. They are six stories of brick around a central stair and an elevator, built between the late 1920s and the mid-1930s, with an entrance that was meant to impress. The association that documented the neighborhood's buildings noted that many of these elevator buildings were styled in the Art Deco of the 1930s in their lobbies or entrances, and that owners have been restoring them. That changes how we work at the door. Before we drill anything, we look at what the entrance is made of. It may be brick, a cast-stone surround, terrazzo or decorative metal. We also check where the old panel's holes and conduit already are, because reusing an existing opening is far better than cutting a new one in a facade the board has just paid to clean.

Behind the walls, the intercom wiring in these buildings has usually been through several generations. The first system may have been a bell-and-buzzer setup. A later voice system was often run up the stair or the dumbwaiter shaft. After that came decades of handsets swapped one apartment at a time and splices made wherever a wire broke. We tone every pair from the cellar to each floor before deciding anything. Some risers are worth reusing for a new audio or two-wire video system. Some are so mixed up that new cable up a single shaft costs less in the end than chasing faults for years. Inside each apartment, the old handset usually hangs on plaster beside the kitchen or entry door. The replacement goes in the same spot wherever it can, so we are not opening new holes in old plaster. Where the building is a co-op, the board, and often its managing agent, approves the plan. So the survey ends with a written scope: the entrance station, the apartment stations, where the head-end will live and what happens to the old equipment. That way there is one clear document to vote on.

The postwar mid-rise: plainer lobbies, longer halls and wiring in pipes

The six-story buildings from the 1950s, such as 2727 Ocean Parkway with its 174 apartments, look less dramatic than the prewar ones, but they are usually easier to wire. Many were built with conduit or a dedicated chase for low-voltage cable. Where that pipe is clear, new cable can often be pulled through it without opening any walls. Where it is full of dead wire from the original system, we pull the old cable out first, sometimes using it to drag the new cable through behind it. The lobby is often a simple rectangle with a mailroom off one side. That gives good choices for where the entrance station goes: in the vestibule, protected from weather, or on the outer wall, where it is easier for a visitor to find.

These buildings tend to have longer corridors and more apartments per floor than the prewar ones. That makes the directory longer and the voltage drop on old wiring worse at the far end of each floor. A system that sounds fine in apartment 1A can be faint in 6L. When we test, we test the farthest apartments first. If the riser is reused, we size the power supply for the whole run rather than the average. In buildings of this age the cellar is still the usual home for the transformer, and the same flood logic applies. So the plan normally includes moving it upstairs.

The 1960s tower: a front desk, hundreds of apartments and a board that decides

Brighton Towers on Brighton 1st Road is the clearest example. Compass lists it as a co-op built in 1963 with 448 units on sixteen stories, an elevator and a voice intercom. A second listing describes an intercom to the front desk, a secure lobby, management by Neptune Associates and board approval required for purchases. A tower like that does not need one door panel. It needs a whole system. That means an entrance station a visitor can search quickly, a desk station that sees every call and can step in, and apartment stations on every floor. It also needs riser cabling that can be serviced floor by floor. The desk changes the design. Some buildings want every call to ring the desk first. Others want calls to go straight to the apartment, with the desk only seeing that a call is happening. Some want different rules at different hours. Those are decisions for the board and the managing agent, and we put them in writing before anything is ordered.

Work in a tower this size is scheduled around the people who live there. We do not take the lobby panel out until the new one is ready to go in. Apartment stations are changed floor by floor, with notices posted in advance. The old system keeps working on floors we have not reached yet wherever the wiring allows. Riser work happens in closets and shafts during agreed hours. Because many residents here are older and some are home all day, we plan apartment visits in short, announced windows rather than leaving people waiting at home all day for a technician.

Oceana and the newer condos: guard booths, network wiring and equipment rooms built after the storm

The Oceana condominium was built in phases from 2001 to 2014, and Commercial Observer's 2014 report gave it sixteen buildings and 927 units. It is a gated complex by the ocean. One building, 65 Oceana Drive East, is a twelve-story building from 2004 with 88 units, according to PLUTO figures published by a broker. The same Commercial Observer report described how the newer building at 50 Oceana Drive East was designed after Sandy: its electrical switchgear sat on a raised platform above the flood plain and its elevator controls went on the roof. That is the right instinct, and an intercom should follow it. In a complex like this, the intercom usually has two layers. A gate or guard booth handles cars and walkers coming in from the street. Each building's lobby has its own entrance station for residents and their guests. The two layers have to agree on who has been let in, or the guard ends up calling apartments by phone while the lobby panel rings separately.

Newer buildings are more likely to have IP intercoms, which run over network cable with power over Ethernet rather than a dedicated intercom riser. New construction such as 1014 Banner Avenue lists a door intercom among its features, and an Akuvox distributor's case study describes eight buildings on Brighton Beach Avenue, each with 60 or more apartments, that moved from analog systems to IP intercoms with a 10-inch indoor monitor in every living room. IP systems bring real advantages: better video, easier directory changes and remote management. They also bring network equipment that has to live somewhere dry, cool and secure. In a building that already has a raised equipment room, it goes there. In a building that does not, finding that space is part of the job.

Bungalows on the lanes: small houses, separate doors and the cellar question

Between Brighton Beach Avenue, Neptune Avenue, Ocean Parkway and Coney Island Avenue, Forgotten NY mapped the lanes that grew out of the old bungalow colonies. They include Brighton 1st Walk, Terrace, Lane, Place and Path; Brighton 3rd Court, Walk and Lane; and the wedge of Brighton 10th Court, Path, Terrace and Lane. The bungalows were winterized for year-round living long ago, and many are being replaced by taller buildings. The ones that remain often hold more than one household. City Limits reported in 2012 that these bungalows sit on cellars or basements, many rented to recent immigrants, and that some of those basement apartments are legal and others are not. Those basements were the first to flood in Sandy.

For a small house, the intercom is simple in hardware and careful in planning. A two- or three-button video door station at the gate or the front door, with an indoor station for each legal unit, covers most of them. A narrow lane can mean the door station faces another house a few feet away, so we pick a camera angle that sees the visitor and not the neighbor's window. If a lower unit's door is down a few steps, it may need its own station rather than sharing the one at the street. We wire indoor stations for legal units only. If an owner asks us to wire a cellar room, we ask whether it is a legal apartment. Our job is the intercom, not settling that question. On these small jobs the owner is usually the person who decides, and the scope is often a single page.

Rooming houses and the shops under the El

An undated page from a Brighton Beach neighborhood association describes several hundred prewar boarding houses spread through the community. The city calls them single-room-occupancy buildings. They are wood-framed and, in the page's words, honeycombed with rooms from the basement to the top floor. An intercom in a house like that is less about a grand entrance and more about many small doors. Each room may need its own call button, the hallways are narrow and the wiring is often surface-run. Wood framing makes cable runs easier than masonry, but it also means every penetration has to be sealed properly. We discuss with the owner what each room needs before deciding on the panel.

Brighton Beach Avenue is a different kind of building. Under the elevated B and Q, brick buildings put stores at street level and apartments above, with a narrow residential door squeezed between storefronts. Trains passing overhead make it hard to hear, and the avenue is busy almost all day. That residential door needs an entrance station that a visitor can find among the shop signs, that is loud enough to use when a train goes by, and that is kept separate from the store's own deliveries. The shops below often use their basements for storage and food preparation, as the Forward reported after Sandy. So we keep the apartments' intercom equipment out of the store's cellar wherever there is any other choice.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Hour in a Brighton Beach Lobby

These notes describe how our technicians survey buildings like the ones in Brighton Beach. They are our standing practice, not an account of any one address.

Start outside, facing the wind. The technician stands where a visitor would stand and looks at the old station: which way it faces, whether the sun hits the screen in the afternoon and whether there is a white crust around the screws and grille. On the blocks nearest the boardwalk, the side of the station facing the ocean usually shows corrosion first. That tells us which housing and sealing the new one needs.

Find the water line. In the cellar, before touching the intercom box, the technician looks at the walls. A tide mark, rust on the lower half of a steel cabinet or a newer section of drywall at knee height tells us how high water has come before. Anything we install stays well above it, and usually on another floor.

Tone before you trust. Every pair in the riser is toned from the cellar to a sample apartment on each floor. Labels on old intercom wiring are often wrong. The tone generator is not.

Ask who answers the door. The technician asks the super, and anyone else in the lobby, who actually uses the intercom. Is it home attendants, grandchildren, couriers, the desk? Which language do they read? The answers shape the directory and the apartment stations more than any spec sheet.

Photograph the entrance surround. Before any old panel comes off, the entrance is photographed from three angles, so the board can see exactly what the new station will cover and what will show.

Shore Conditions

Salt, Sand and the Flood Line: Building an Intercom That Lasts on the Shore

Every intercom has two halves. The entrance station faces the street, and the head-end, meaning the power supply, amplifier or controller and any network gear, sits somewhere inside. In most of Brooklyn, both halves live fairly comfortable lives. In Brighton Beach, the entrance station faces salt air and the head-end has historically been put in the one part of the building most likely to fill with seawater. Plan both halves for that, and a system lasts. Ignore it, and the building pays twice.

The entrance station. Salt corrodes ordinary steel screws, the solder on unprotected circuit boards and the springs behind cheap buttons. Our own Brooklyn repair page puts the life of a standard panel within about a thousand feet of salt water at five to eight years. That is our field estimate, not independent data, but it matches what the old panels on Brightwater Court look like. For stations facing the boardwalk or open to the ocean wind, we choose housings made for coastal or harsh outdoor use, with stainless fasteners. We look for a high ingress rating against water and dust, and electronics that the maker coats or seals. The back box is sealed to the masonry so wind-driven rain cannot get behind the panel. The cable entry is sealed and dripped, so water runs off the cable instead of following it inside. Where the building has a vestibule, putting the station inside the outer door keeps it out of the worst weather. The trade-off is that visitors must be able to reach it, so we check that the outer door is meant to stay open.

Sand. City Limits described sand piled into breezeways and carports after Sandy, and on a breezy summer weekend a smaller version of that happens on its own. Mechanical buttons with gaps around them collect grit. Over months they stick, double-press or stop returning. Flush buttons, sealed keypads and touch directories handle sand better. So does a station with a slight overhang above it. We also tell supers how to clean a station without forcing water or grit into it, which matters as much as the hardware.

The head-end and the flood line. Hester Street's 2016 report recommended that Brighton Beach buildings move mechanical rooms above the flood zone. Commercial Observer described Oceana's newer building doing just that with its switchgear and elevator controls. An intercom head-end is small enough that this is usually easy. A ventilated enclosure in a ground-floor closet, a corner of the mailroom, the back of the super's office or an upper-floor electrical closet will normally hold it. The cost is the cable run from the riser to the new location, which may mean a core through a block wall and a length of conduit. We compare that cost with the cost of replacing the whole head-end after the next flood, and the board decides with both numbers in front of it.

When the power goes. After Sandy, parts of Brighton Beach were without power for days, and the September 2026 manhole fire on Brighton 7th Street showed how a single underground fault can cut off a block. An intercom with no power cannot call anyone, and an electric door release with no power does whatever it was built to do: some lock, some unlock. We go through this with the owner or board before choosing parts. Should the head-end have battery backup, and for how long? Should the door release fail locked or unlocked, and does the building code allow that choice for this particular door? Can residents still get in with a key? Who has that key? Most of these answers already exist in the building's routine. We make sure the new intercom fits them rather than quietly changing them.

Cable. Riser cable that runs through a cellar that has flooded before should be inspected, not trusted. Water wicks along old cable jackets, and corrosion can creep a long way from where the water stood. Where we reuse a riser, we test it under load. Where we replace it, the new cable leaves the cellar as soon as the route allows, usually in conduit, and climbs to the head-end above.

Owners, Boards and Tenants

What the Rules Ask of a Brighton Beach Front Door

Most Brighton Beach residents live in buildings with eight or more apartments, and New York has rules about the intercoms in buildings like that. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. Here is what the public sources say, including where they disagree, so an owner, board or tenant knows what to ask.

The basic duty. The New York Attorney General's Residential Tenants' Rights Guide, citing Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, says that multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors. If such a building has eight or more apartments, it must also have a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door, and tenants must be able to buzz visitors in. For older buildings, the guide says self-locking doors and a two-way intercom are required if a majority of the apartments ask for them. It adds that landlords may recover the cost of that equipment from tenants. That matters here, because most of Brighton Beach was built before 1968.

Where the texts differ. The Housing Maintenance Code text published on up.codes reads differently. It says that from January 1, 1969, every entrance to a class A multiple dwelling built or converted before January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercommunication system, with no mention of a majority request. At least one installer's site says the duty covers buildings with three or more units, which most other sources do not support. We point this out so that nobody quotes one version as the whole story. If the question affects your building, ask a housing lawyer or HPD.

Enforcement. An installer's guide to NYC intercom requirements says a non-working intercom or buzzer is a Class B hazardous violation under the Housing Maintenance Code. Another industry source notes that HPD acts on routine inspections and on tenant complaints made through 311. Habitat Magazine's February 2024 article, titled "A Working Intercom Isn't an Amenity. It's Required by Law," points out that co-ops are covered too. It says a shareholder whose board does not respond can complain to 311 or bring an HP proceeding in housing court. For boards in Brighton Beach's many co-ops, a working intercom is part of keeping the building legal.

Doors and mounting heights. The intercom is only half of the front door. HPD's FY25 self-closing door report counted 2,937 self-closing door violations citywide, which is a reminder that a slow closer or a sticking latch can undo a perfect intercom. The city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 also sets heights: no push button more than six feet above the floor, and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet. The same rule says the minimum acceptable system is a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device. We mount entrance stations to those heights, and we check that the door closes and latches before we call the job finished.

Apps, fobs and faces. Many new intercoms can do more than ring a bell. They can open the door from a phone app, a key fob or face recognition, which the Akuvox systems on Brighton Beach Avenue were set up to offer. Once an intercom opens doors with electronic credentials, New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law can apply. HPD's guidance says owners of multiple dwellings using smart access systems, including fobs, cards, phone apps and biometrics, must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy. They must collect only limited reference data, get express consent before collecting it, and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. The law also says owners may not require smart access to enter a tenant's own apartment. If your board wants those features, we flag the paperwork early so it is ready before the system is switched on.

New owners, same front door. Buildings change hands here like anywhere else. Crain's New York reported in October 2026 that the apartment building at 3052 Brighton First Street sold for $11.25 million. A new owner takes on the intercom along with the boiler and the roof. An honest survey of a newly bought building's entrance system is a sensible first step, and we are glad to do it.

Commercial jobs here rarely happen in a building the business owns outright. A shop on Brighton Beach Avenue usually rents from a landlord who also owns the apartments above. A station set into a recessed doorway, with a screen that shows the call visually, works through it.

Compliance notes for Brighton Beach businesses and buildings

  • Mounting heights. The city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 puts push buttons no higher than six feet and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet above the floor. We mount to those heights, so a resident in a wheelchair and a tall courier can both use the station.
  • People must always be able to get out. An intercom that releases an electric lock must never trap people inside. The NYC Building Code requires electromagnetic locks on egress doors to use listed hardware, with a release that is obvious and works with one hand, and that unlocks when power is lost. We match the release to the door and its code requirements, and we will not wire a lock that blocks an exit.
  • Self-closing doors. Entrance doors in multiple dwellings must close and latch on their own. A new intercom is useless if the door does not latch, so we check the closer and latch as part of every job and tell you if they need work.
  • Tenant data. If the system opens doors with an app, fob or face, the Tenant Data Privacy Law can apply to residential buildings. We flag that before the system goes live.
  • Fire alarm interfaces. Where a door release is tied into a fire alarm system, that connection belongs to the fire alarm contractor and the building's approved plans. We coordinate with them and do not change it on our own.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Brighton Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brighton Beach boards, owners, supers and residents raise when they call about an intercom: ocean wind at the front door, equipment in a cellar that has flooded, lobbies with hundreds of names, residents who read Cyrillic or do not hear well, small houses with several households, and doors under the El. Each one is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Wind, salt and the entrance

Our front door on Brightwater Court takes the wind straight off the beach. What should the outdoor station be built from, and how is it sealed to the wall?

For an entrance that faces the ocean, the station should come in a housing made for coastal or harsh outdoor use, with stainless steel fasteners, sealed or coated electronics and a high rating against water and dust. A good station is only half of it. How it meets the wall matters just as much. We set the back box into the masonry or onto a sealed surface plate, gasket the faceplate and seal the cable entry so water cannot follow the wire inside. Above the station, a small hood or the existing door canopy keeps rain and spray off the screen. On Brightwater Court and the blocks nearest the boardwalk, corrosion usually starts on the side of the old panel facing the water, so we also look at how the old one failed. If the building has a vestibule and the outer door stays open, putting the station just inside it is the simplest protection of all.

Our 1935 lobby still has its Art Deco metalwork around the old panel. Can a new station sit inside that surround instead of being drilled in beside it?

Often it can, and it is usually the best result for the building. Many of the 1930s buildings here have a decorative surround or a recess built for the original directory or bell plate. If the opening is large enough, the new station can go in with a custom trim plate that fills the gap and leaves the metalwork untouched. If the new station is larger than the opening, we look for a model with a smaller face, or a split design where the camera and buttons sit in the old opening and the directory screen goes inside the vestibule. What we avoid is cutting new holes in original metal or stone. Before any work, we photograph the surround and show the board a mock-up of exactly what will be covered and what will stay visible.

Cellars, flood lines and power

The intercom transformer and amplifier sit in the cellar next to the boiler. Should they stay there, and if not, where do they go?

In Brighton Beach, we almost always recommend moving them. Sandy put seawater into basements across the neighborhood, and First Street rates nearly every property here at risk of flooding. A head-end below grade will be lost in the next flood, and heat and humidity near a boiler shorten its life even in a dry year. The usual new home is a ventilated enclosure on the ground floor: a closet off the lobby, a corner of the mailroom, the super's office, or an electrical closet on an upper floor. The riser cable is extended to reach it, often through a single core in a block wall and a length of conduit. If the building has no space above grade at all, the equipment goes as high on the cellar wall as possible, well above any known water mark. The board then knows the risk it is accepting.

When the power on our block dropped during the manhole fire on Brighton 7th Street, the lobby panel went dark. What should a new system do in an outage?

That depends on two decisions the building should make on purpose, not by accident. The first is whether the intercom keeps running. A head-end with battery backup can carry the entrance station and the apartment stations through a short outage like the one News12 reported in September 2026, which cut power to ten residents for a short time. How long it lasts depends on the battery and the system. The second is what the door release does with no power at all. Some locks stay locked, so residents need a key. Others unlock, which is required on certain egress doors but leaves the building open. We go through both with the owner or board, check what the building code allows for that door, and make sure the super knows how to get in and out if the outage runs past the battery.

Towers, desks and long directories

Our tower's lobby calls go to a front desk. Will a new system keep the desk in the loop, or does it ring apartments directly?

Either, and many systems can switch between the two on a schedule. In desk-first mode, a visitor's call goes to the desk station. The attendant sees and hears the visitor, then rings the apartment or lets them in. In direct mode, the call goes straight to the apartment, and the desk can see that a call is in progress and step in if needed. Many towers use direct mode overnight when the desk is thinly staffed and desk-first during the day. Brighton Towers is listed with an intercom to the front desk, which is a common setup in buildings of that size. We write the call rules into the scope so the board decides them, and we set up the desk station so a new attendant can learn it in minutes.

With more than 400 apartments, how does a visitor find one name on the panel quickly?

With a directory that is searched, not scrolled. A good entrance station for a large building lets a visitor type the first few letters of a surname, or an apartment number, and see a short list immediately. Large buttons and a clear screen matter, and so does how the names are entered. We enter them the way residents ask, with the surname first. Where two families share a name, we add the apartment number so nobody calls the wrong door. For buildings that prefer not to list names at all, visitors can dial an apartment number or a code the resident gives them. Whatever method you choose, a short printed instruction beside the station, in English and Russian, saves visitors and residents a lot of frustration.

Many of our visitors know their relatives' names only in Cyrillic. Can the directory show and search names in Russian letters?

Many current entrance stations can display Cyrillic names, and some can switch their on-screen prompts between languages. This varies by model, so we confirm it with the maker before recommending one to your board. Where it works, each resident chooses how their name appears: in Latin letters, in Cyrillic, or both. Visitors can then search in either alphabet if the station supports it. Where a model can only show Latin letters, we enter the spelling the resident gives us and, with the board's approval, post a printed bilingual directory inside the vestibule. With more than a third of Brighton Beach residents not speaking or understanding English, by Wikipedia's figures, this is not a detail. It decides whether grandparents and home attendants can actually use the system.

Inside the apartments

When the old handsets come off the plaster beside our kitchen doors, what is left on the wall?

Usually two or four screw holes, a hole where the wire comes through, and sometimes a faded outline where the old handset kept the paint from yellowing. Wherever possible, the new station goes in the same place on the same wire, and its back plate covers most of that. Video monitors are often larger than old handsets, so they hide the old marks completely. If the new station is smaller, or a resident wants it moved to a better spot, we fill the old holes. The outline may still need paint, which is the apartment owner's or the building's to arrange. In old plaster, we drill slowly and use anchors made for plaster, so a station that hangs beside a door that is slammed a hundred times a week stays put.

My father barely hears the buzzer anymore. What kind of apartment station works for someone who is hard of hearing?

A video station with a large screen, an adjustable ringer and a visible call light is usually the answer. When someone rings from the lobby, the screen lights up and shows the visitor, so he sees the call even if he does not hear it. Many stations let the ring volume go well above a standard handset. Some can connect to an extra bell or flashing light in another room, such as the bedroom. A hands-free station with one large button to talk and one to open the door is easier than a handset for many older residents. With more than a quarter of residents over 65, by Wikipedia's figures, we offer this choice in every Brighton Beach building. We set the station up with the resident, not just for them.

Small houses and rooming houses

Our converted bungalow off Brighton 1st Place has a basement unit and two upstairs. Does each household need its own station?

Each legal unit should have its own button at the door and its own station inside, so a visitor for one household does not ring the other two. The door station itself can be shared: a three-button video station at the gate or front door usually works well on the lanes. If the basement unit has its own entrance down a few steps, it is often better to give that door its own small station. A visitor then waits at the right door and does not wander through the yard. We wire stations only for units that are legal apartments. If you are not sure about the basement, check before booking. We will plan around whatever the answer is.

We manage a wood-frame rooming house with many rooms. Can each room get its own button and handset?

Yes, in most cases. A multi-button entrance station or a small directory panel can call each room. A simple audio or video station goes inside each room, usually by the door. In wood-framed houses, cable runs are easier than in masonry because wire can be fished through stud bays and floor joists. Every hole through a floor or between rooms is then sealed properly afterward. Narrow halls and many doors mean we plan the route carefully, often along one hallway wall and up one chase rather than a separate run to every room. The owner decides how rooms are labeled on the panel. We suggest room numbers rather than names, since residents in these houses change more often.

Doors under the El

Our apartment door is beside a shop under the elevated tracks. When a B or Q train passes, nobody hears the intercom. What helps?

Three things help, and most buildings use all three. First, placement. A station set into a recessed doorway, or just inside the outer door, is shielded from much of the street noise. A station mounted flat on the open facade under the structure picks up every train. Second, the station itself. Current stations handle background noise much better than the old ones, and a video station lets the resident see the visitor and open the door even if neither can hear the other for a few seconds. Third, the apartment station. A screen and a call light mean a resident upstairs knows someone is calling even while a train rolls past the window. Some buildings also post a short note by the station asking visitors to wait for the screen to light.

The upstairs apartments share a hallway with the store's back door. Can the intercom keep residents' calls apart from the business?

Yes. The residential entrance station rings only the apartments, and the store gets its own station, or none at all, on its own door. Where the hallway is shared, the two doors should be on separate systems or clearly separated groups on one system. That way a courier for the shop cannot buzz into the residents' stair, and a visitor for an apartment does not end up at the shop's back door. The release wiring matters too. The apartments' door release should be controlled only from apartment stations. We also keep the residential equipment out of the shop's basement, both because of flooding and because the residents should not depend on access to a store's cellar to fix their intercom.

Gated complexes

Our gated complex has a guard booth at the drive and a lobby door in each building. How do the two layers work together?

The best setups make the guard booth part of the same intercom system as the lobbies. A visitor at the gate calls the guard. The guard can see and talk to them, then ring the apartment from the booth station. If the resident agrees, the guard opens the gate, and the lobby station knows to expect them, or the resident lets them in at the lobby when they arrive. Where the gate and the lobbies are on separate systems, they can still work well, but the guard needs a reliable way to call apartments other than a phone list. In a large complex like Oceana, with sixteen buildings, we look at the cable or network path between the booth and each building first, because that link decides what is practical.

FAQ

Brighton Beach Intercom Installation FAQ

What should a Brighton Beach co-op tell residents the week before the lobby panel is changed over?
+

Tell them the day and the hours, that the old panel will keep working until the new one is ready, how to let visitors in during the short gap, and when someone will visit their apartment to change the station. We supply a short notice in English and Russian that the building can post in the lobby and elevator.

Our entrance faces south toward the beach. Will the camera still show faces in bright afternoon sun?
+

It can if the station is chosen and placed for it. We look for cameras that handle strong backlight, angle the station so the sun is not directly behind the visitor, and use the door canopy or a small hood to shade the lens. We check the picture on a sunny afternoon before the board signs off.

How high will the call buttons sit for a resident who uses a wheelchair?
+

The city rule for intercoms puts push buttons no higher than six feet and the speaking device between four and five feet above the floor. Within that range we mount as low as the entrance allows, so residents in wheelchairs and children can reach the buttons, and we check the reach from where a person actually sits or stands.

Sand blows into our vestibule every summer. How should the super clean the new station?
+

Brush the sand off dry with a soft brush, then wipe the face with a damp cloth. Never spray water or cleaner into the buttons, speaker or camera. We leave written cleaning steps with the super and choose stations with flush or sealed buttons where sand is a known problem.

How do names on the directory get changed when an apartment is sold or a tenant moves?
+

It depends on the system. On cloud-managed systems the managing agent updates the directory from an office computer. On others the super changes it at the panel with a code. We train whoever the building chooses and leave written steps, including how to enter names in Cyrillic where the station supports it.

What paperwork does the managing agent get when the job is finished?
+

A riser map showing which cable serves which floor and apartment, matching the labels in the equipment closet, the model numbers and manuals for every station, the directory export where the system allows it, and a short guide for the super. That paperwork is what lets the next technician work quickly.

Does the light over the entrance matter for a video intercom?
+

Yes. The Attorney General's tenants' guide notes that building entrances must be lit from sunset to sunrise, and a camera at night is only as good as that light. We check the entrance light during the survey and tell the building if it is aimed badly or too dim for the camera to show a face.

If residents can open the lobby door from a phone app, does the building have privacy duties?
+

Very likely. HPD's guidance on the Tenant Data Privacy Law covers smart access systems in multiple dwellings, including phone apps, key fobs and biometrics. Owners must give tenants a written privacy policy, get consent and limit the data kept. We flag this early so the building can prepare the paperwork before switching on those features.

What happens to the old handsets and the old lobby panel?
+

We remove them as each new station goes in and take them away unless the building wants to keep a few as spares for a system it is keeping elsewhere. If the old panel sits in a decorative surround, it comes out carefully so the surround is not damaged.

Will we need an electrician as well as an intercom installer?
+

Sometimes. The intercom itself runs on low voltage from its power supply. If the new head-end location has no outlet nearby, a licensed electrician may need to add one. We spot that during the survey and put it in the scope, so the building can arrange it before installation day.

How do you protect the terrazzo floor and Art Deco finishes in the lobby while you work?
+

Floors are covered with drop cloths and runners from the door to the work area, ladders get padded feet, and decorative metal and stone around the entrance are taped and covered before any tool comes near them. Drilling dust is collected at the bit, and the lobby is cleaned before we leave each day.

Will the intercom still ring apartments if the building's internet goes down?
+

For wired systems, and for IP systems running on the building's own local network, calls from the lobby to apartment stations usually keep working without the internet.
