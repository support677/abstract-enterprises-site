The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need TV Installation

East Williamsburg is a name people argue about. Wikipedia notes that much of the area is still called Bushwick, Williamsburg or Greenpoint by the people who live there, and that the label has been falling out of use since the 1990s. CityNeighborhoods draws it between Flushing Avenue, the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, Bushwick Avenue and Woodpoint Road, and the Queens line along Newtown Creek. Corcoran's guide makes the most useful point for anyone hanging a TV: Morgan Avenue roughly splits the neighborhood in two. East of it, toward the creek, the land is zoned for industry and belongs to what the city calls the East Williamsburg Industrial Park. West of it, the blocks mix apartments, stores and old factory buildings that have become homes. We cover all of it at the same Brooklyn rate, so the name on your lease does not change your price. What changes the job is the building, and few Brooklyn neighborhoods put so many kinds of wall within a few blocks of each other.

NYC Planning's North Brooklyn Industry and Innovation Plan, which studied this area together with parts of Greenpoint and Bushwick, called it the third largest industrial hub in the city by employment, with about 19,500 jobs, 77 percent of them industrial. The same study found that between 2010 and 2016 non-industrial businesses grew faster than industrial ones, and that more than a third of new jobs were in offices, retail, arts and entertainment. That is the shift a TV installer sees on the ground. A building that made paper boxes or metal parts eighty years ago now holds a recording studio on one floor, a design firm on the next and, in some cases, apartments above. Planners also described the historic loft buildings here as generally three to five stories, which matters because buildings of that height and age were built with masonry walls thick enough to carry floors loaded with machinery. Those walls are some of the best anchors in Brooklyn for a heavy screen, as long as the drill goes into the brick and not into the tired mortar between bricks.

Many of those buildings became homes before the law caught up. The NYC Loft Board was created under the 1982 Loft Law to oversee the conversion of former commercial and manufacturing buildings into what the Board calls safe, rent-stabilized residential units, and buildings still going through that process are known as interim multiple dwellings, which must meet minimum standards of safety and fire protection while they are being legalized. PincusCo reported in December 2024 that plans had been filed to legalize 43 residential units in an East Williamsburg interim multiple dwelling, with a photo captioned 57 Thames Street. For a TV, a Loft Law home brings two practical questions that a normal rental does not: the walls inside the unit were often built by an earlier tenant rather than the owner, and the owner may want to know about any work in the unit while legalization plans are being drawn. We ask about both when you book, because a partition that a tenant framed out of two-by-threes in 1995 holds a screen very differently from the original brick around it.

The newer parts of the neighborhood bring their own walls. The Paper Mill at 456 Johnson Avenue, a paper mill built in 1931, was planned as 35 commercial office units after a gut renovation, and Bushwick Daily listed it alongside other creative office conversions at 455 Jefferson Street, 95 Evergreen Avenue and 215 Moore Street. Brooklyn Paper reported that the Netflix facility at 333 Johnson Avenue covers 170,000 square feet with six sound stages and editing and meeting rooms. Buildings like these get new interior walls of drywall on steel studs inside an old masonry shell, so the same office can have a meeting-room wall that needs a toggle rated for thin steel and an outside wall that needs a masonry sleeve anchor. On the residential side, Lindsay Park, the Mitchell-Lama housing cooperative of seven buildings and 2,702 apartments completed in 1965, sits on the western edge near Broadway, and its tower apartments bring the questions every co-op raises: what the walls are made of, and what the management office wants before anyone drills.

Then there is the evening side of the neighborhood, which is where a lot of commercial TV work happens. Brooklyn Steel at 319 Frost Street, a former steel fabrication plant, opened in 2017 with an 1,800-person capacity and, according to Relentless Beats, three full-service bars and an elevated mezzanine. Its co-owner John Moore told amNY, "It's a steel fabrication plant and we wanted to embrace that." Brooklyn Pacha opened on November 6 and 7, 2026 at 140 Stewart Avenue, with an indoor room for about 2,500 people. Along Morgan Avenue, Capricorn's listing names Roberta's, Otis, Sobre Masa, Sey Coffee, Swallow Cafe and Syndicated Bar Theater Kitchen among the food and coffee spots near the L. Every one of those kinds of business needs screens somewhere, whether it is a menu board over a counter, a game on behind a bar or a display at a door, and in a building that embraces its steel and brick, the wall behind that screen is rarely the drywall that a big-box installer expects.

Six East Williamsburg facts that change a TV job before anyone picks a bracket

  • The loft buildings are low, old and heavy. City planners describe them as generally three to five stories. Their outside and bearing walls are usually solid brick, which holds a screen well but needs a hammer drill, a masonry anchor and the $65 brick add-on on our list.
  • Morgan Avenue divides homes from industry. East of it toward Newtown Creek, buildings sit in an industrial zone, and the TV work there is mostly offices, studios, break rooms and venues. West of it, it is mostly apartments, lofts and storefronts.
  • Some homes are still being legalized. Interim multiple dwellings under the Loft Law are owed minimum safety and fire protection standards while they work toward a residential certificate. A filing in 2024 covered 43 units at an East Williamsburg building on Thames Street.
  • Broadband has been thin in the industrial blocks. NYCEDC called the North Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg industrial business zones "digital deserts" when it launched Connect IBZ, which used rooftop-mounted fixed-wireless equipment. A smart TV in those buildings often streams better on a wired drop than on Wi-Fi.
  • Power can drop without warning. Greenpointers reported that a manhole fire at 101 Montrose Avenue in February 2026 left hundreds of Williamsburg residents without power. We recommend a surge protector on every TV and soundbar we install, and we never hide a power strip inside a wall.
  • The L train ties it all together. The Grand Street, Montrose Avenue and Morgan Avenue stations serve most of the neighborhood. We drive, so the stations matter mostly as landmarks, but they also mark the blocks where parking a van with a 75-inch box is hardest.

Same-Day Repair in East Williamsburg

Need a TV Fixed in East Williamsburg? A Screen Sagging Off a Loft Wall, a Mount Spinning in Old Brick, or Wires Left Hanging After a Move-Out

The urgent TV calls from buildings like East Williamsburg's tend to fall into three groups. The first is a bracket on exposed brick that has started to work loose. Almost always, the screws went into the mortar joints because mortar is easier to drill, and in a building that is a hundred years old the mortar is often soft and sandy. A mason quoted by StreetEasy put it simply: "It's usually easier to drill in mortar than brick, but with older buildings, the mortar may be crumbling." This Old House is just as direct: "Mortar is much softer than brick, and the anchor's grip will eventually deteriorate." A screen that hung straight for a year and now tips forward or sits lower on one side is usually telling you exactly that.

The second group is a tenant-built partition that was never meant to carry a screen. In many converted lofts, the walls between bedrooms and living space were put up by earlier occupants, sometimes with studs far apart, sometimes with a single layer of thin board, sometimes with no studs where you would expect them. A bracket fixed with hollow-wall anchors into a wall like that can hold a small TV for years and then fail when someone pulls a full-motion arm out to watch from the kitchen. The third group is the mess an earlier tenant leaves behind when they move out: an empty bracket, a hole with a cable poking out of it, or a power cord pushed through the wall to an outlet below, which is not allowed under the electrical code and is one of the first things we take out.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back up. If two people can safely lift it, take the weight off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going back up in the same visit on a properly anchored bracket, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, with the take-down added. We tell you before we start what the wall needs, and if the trouble turns out to be in the wall itself, a loose brick, a crack running through the plaster or a partition that flexes when you lean on it, we say so plainly so you can bring in the landlord, the owner or a mason before anything else is hung there.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for East Williamsburg's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every East Williamsburg TV call. They differ in what the walls are made of, how the screen gets up to the unit, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled. Those three things settle the plan long before anyone talks about brands or sizes.

The converted factory loft

Three to five stories of brick around open floors, with high ceilings, big steel or wood windows, and living spaces carved out with partitions. Freight elevators in some, long stairs in others.

The Loft Law unit

A home inside a building still being legalized, or recently legalized, where an earlier tenant may have built the walls and the owner has its own reasons to follow any work in the unit.

The walk-up over a store

Three- and four-story brick buildings on the avenues, with a shop on the ground floor, railroad or side-hall apartments above, plaster partitions inside and brick party walls.

The co-op tower apartment

Lindsay Park's seven buildings near Broadway and other postwar towers: concrete frames, block or plaster partitions, elevators, and a management office with rules about contractors.

The studio and office building

Old industrial buildings fitted out for artists, makers, production companies and offices, with steel-stud walls built inside masonry shells and shared telecom closets.

The bar, restaurant or venue

Storefronts and big industrial rooms where screens go behind bars, over counters and near doors, often on brick, steel or block, and always around opening hours.

The converted factory loft: a wall built to carry machines, and a room built for light

The classic East Williamsburg loft gives you two gifts and one problem. The first gift is the brick. An outside wall or a bearing wall in a three- to five-story factory building is usually several wythes of solid brick, and once a sleeve anchor is set in sound brick it will hold far more than any television weighs. The second gift is space: a long wall, a high ceiling and room to sit well back from a big screen. The problem is light. Factory buildings were built with large windows so workers could see what they were doing, and a TV on the wall opposite a row of tall windows can be close to unwatchable on a bright afternoon. We tape the outline of the screen on the wall and ask you to sit where you actually watch at the time of day you watch, because moving tape costs nothing and moving a bracket on brick costs a patch.

The brick itself needs care. Many loft walls were cleaned or sandblasted when the building was converted, which leaves a rough face and, sometimes, soft or recessed mortar joints. We plan the bracket so that every fixing lands in the body of a brick, at least half an inch from its edge, which is the margin This Old House gives to avoid cracking it. If the bolt pattern of the bracket forces a fixing onto a joint, we shift the bracket slightly within the TV's mounting range or use a bracket with longer slots. On our list, mounting on brick adds $65 to the $179.99 base, so a 55-inch TV on a loft brick wall is $244.99 before the bracket. Running power and signal across a brick wall cannot be done inside it, so the honest options are a paintable cord cover, routing down to a console, or a run through an adjacent partition, which we explain later on this page.

The Loft Law unit: walls an earlier tenant built, and an owner with paperwork in progress

Loft Law homes are a special case, and we treat them that way. The Loft Board says the law exists to bring former commercial and manufacturing buildings to safe, rent-stabilized residential use, and interim multiple dwellings must meet minimum standards of safety and fire protection while that happens. In practice, the residents of many of these buildings did the original fit-out themselves years ago: the partitions, the kitchens, sometimes the electrical outlets. That means the wall you want for the TV may have been built by someone who is long gone, out of materials nobody wrote down. Before we drill a partition in a Loft Law unit we scan it, knock along it, and drill one pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, so we know whether there is wood framing, steel framing or nothing but board behind the surface.

The second question is the owner. When a building is moving through legalization, the owner's architect may be drawing plans that touch the same walls, and some owners ask tenants to give notice before any work in the unit. A TV bracket is a small thing, but a cut for an in-wall power and cable kit is an opening in a wall, and it is better to ask first than to find out later that the wall is coming down for a new fire-rated partition next year. We usually suggest a short note to the owner or managing agent saying which wall, what size TV, how many holes and whether anything will be opened. If an opening is not welcome, a paintable cord cover at $59 or a TV on its stand at $119.99 avoids the question entirely.

The walk-up over a store: plaster, a party wall and a shop downstairs

Along Graham Avenue, Grand Street, Bushwick Avenue and the side streets between them, the typical residential building is a three- or four-story brick walk-up with a store or restaurant on the ground floor. Inside, partitions are usually plaster, sometimes on wood lath and sometimes on metal lath, and the long wall in the living room is often the party wall shared with the next building, which is usually solid masonry under its plaster skin. A scanner can be fooled by both: metal lath reads as metal everywhere, and plaster on brick reads as nothing at all. Our list adds $45 for plaster on framing and $65 where the fixings go into brick. Which one applies is settled by the pilot hole, and if it is different from what your photos suggested, you hear it before any more holes go in.

Walk-ups also mean stairs, and the stairs in an old tenement are often narrow with tight turns. A 65-inch box is manageable for one technician; anything much bigger is a two-person carry, and we ask about the stairs when you book so the right crew arrives. The store downstairs matters too. If the TV wall is above a restaurant kitchen or a bar, there may be ducts, pipes or old flue openings inside it, and we check for heat and for any opening in the wall before we choose a position. Finally, many of these buildings were built well before 1960. HPD's lead-safe rules apply under Local Law 1 when a child under six lives in an older apartment and work disturbs two square feet or more of paint in a room. A bracket's few small holes are far below that, but an in-wall kit cut in an old painted wall is something we discuss with you and, where a child lives there, with the owner first.

The co-op tower apartment: concrete, block and a management office

Lindsay Park, completed in 1965 with seven buildings and 2,702 apartments, is the largest residential complex on the western edge of the neighborhood, and like other postwar towers it is built differently from the lofts and walk-ups nearby. Walls in buildings of that era are often concrete around the structure and block or plaster between apartments, and a forum thread about New York public housing from the same period sums up what many owners find: "You will need hammer drill for most areas of the apartment." That is a job for masonry anchors, and since poured concrete is not on our short list, we price it in writing from your photos before you book. Drilling concrete raises silica dust, so our technicians drill masonry with a dust shroud and vacuum attached, which is what OSHA's fact sheet for handheld drills calls for, and they clean the holes before the anchors go in.

The bigger question in a co-op is often permission rather than the wall. Many co-ops ask shareholders to tell the management office about contractor work, to send a certificate of insurance, and to keep work within set hours. NYC's noise code lists approved construction hours as weekdays between 7:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m., and buildings may set their own narrower windows on top of that. We send the certificate of insurance ahead of the visit, book inside the building's hours, and keep the noisy part of the job, the drilling, to a few short bursts. If the office has a form for contractor access, send it to us with your booking and we fill in our part before the day.

The studio and office building: steel studs inside an old shell

The creative offices that have filled old industrial buildings near Morgan Avenue and along Johnson Avenue are usually fitted out with new walls of drywall over light steel studs. A screw driven into a light steel stud can spin out under load, so we fix brackets on those walls with hollow-wall anchors rated for thin steel, or add a plywood backer between studs when the layout calls for it, and we price steel-stud walls in writing from your photos. The outside walls of the same building are usually the original masonry, so it is common for one office to have both kinds of wall in the same room. A conference-room screen on a steel-stud wall and a reception display on the brick wall beside the entrance are two different jobs, and we treat them that way.

These buildings also bring building rules. Studio buildings often have shared freight elevators, a loading door, an on-site super, and a landlord who wants a certificate of insurance on file before any contractor comes in. Signal is the other question. A conference-room display that streams presentations needs a good network connection, and the industrial blocks of North Brooklyn have historically had weak broadband, which is why NYCEDC's Connect IBZ program called them digital deserts. We find out which network jack serves the wall and test it before the screen goes up. If there is no jack, we tell you so; running a new data line is a cabling job and we price it separately.

The bar, restaurant or venue: big rooms, hard walls and fixed hours

Bars and restaurants on Grand Street, Graham Avenue and Morgan Avenue, and the larger venues near Frost Street and Stewart Avenue, have the hardest walls in the neighborhood and the least flexible schedules. Brooklyn Steel's owners chose to keep the look of a steel fabrication plant, and many smaller bars keep their exposed brick and painted block for the same reason. Screens behind a bar are usually mounted high, on brick or block, where the staff can see them and the customers can watch from stools, and they have to clear bottle shelves, taps and back-bar lighting. We plan the height from the seats, not from the floor, and we keep every fixing in solid masonry.

The schedule matters as much as the wall. A bar cannot have a technician on a ladder during service, and a restaurant does not want drilling dust near the line. We book commercial installs before opening or on the day the business is closed, we bring drop cloths and a vacuum for masonry dust, and we leave the room ready to open. Where a screen will be on for many hours a day, we talk through ventilation behind it and where the power will come from, because a TV that runs all evening above a bar needs a proper outlet, not a long extension cord taped along the ceiling.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Ten Minutes in an East Williamsburg Loft, Before Anything Is Drilled

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like East Williamsburg's. They are our standard practice, not a story about any single address.

Find out who built the wall. In a converted building, the first question is whether the wall is original or added. Original walls are usually brick, sometimes concrete or block. Added walls might be drywall on wood, drywall on steel, or something an earlier tenant improvised. The technician looks at the corners, the base and the top of the wall: an added partition often stops short of a high ceiling or meets it with a gap, while an original wall runs up into the structure.

Knock, then scan, then trust neither. A knock along the wall at screen height says a lot: a hollow drum means a framed partition, a dead, hard knock means masonry or plaster on masonry. A deep-scan wall scanner then looks for framing and metal. On plaster over metal lath, or on brick, the scanner can say very little, which is why the next step matters.

One pilot hole, where the bracket will hide it. The technician drills a single small hole inside the outline of the bracket plate and reads the dust. Red grit means brick, grey grit means block or concrete, white powder followed by wood means plaster on lath, and a sudden drop into nothing means a hollow partition. That one hole decides the anchor and the add-on, and if it changes the price from what you were quoted, you hear it then.

Check the windows and the light. With painter's tape on the wall marking the screen, the technician asks you to sit where you watch, and checks the glare from the factory windows, the radiators and any pipes running along the wall. In a loft with exposed sprinkler or heating pipes, the screen and its cables are planned around them, never on them.

Plan the dust and the noise. In masonry, the drill runs with a dust shroud and vacuum attached, the floor is covered, and the drilling happens in a few short bursts after warning anyone in the next room or the next unit. Everything that can be done quietly, unboxing, assembling the bracket, programming the remote, is done quietly.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Concrete, Timber, Steel or a Tenant's Partition: What Holds a TV in an East Williamsburg Wall

The single most useful thing you can tell us before a visit is what the wall is. In most of Brooklyn that means choosing between plaster, brick and drywall. In East Williamsburg the list is longer, because the buildings were put up for industry and then fitted out for living and working in several different decades. This section walks through each kind of wall we meet here, what holds a bracket in it, how wires can and cannot be hidden, and where it sits on the price list.

Original exposed brick

The exposed brick wall is the signature of a converted factory loft, and it is also one of the strongest walls in the borough for a screen. The rule is simple and often ignored: drill the brick, not the mortar. Brick is hard and holds a sleeve anchor or a masonry screw firmly; mortar in a building of this age may be soft, sandy or partly washed out by cleaning, and an anchor set in it loosens over time. This Old House puts the margin at half an inch from a brick's edge, and its advice to use proper masonry bits is not optional on old, hard brick. We use a hammer drill with a dust shroud and vacuum, set the anchors, and torque the bracket so it sits flat against an uneven face, sometimes with spacers where the brick is especially rough.

What you cannot do on a brick wall is hide wires inside it. There is no cavity to fish a cable through, and chasing a channel into a loft's brick is a masonry job with a landlord's permission, not part of a TV install. The clean options are a paintable cord cover, which can be painted to blend with brick reasonably well, a short drop to a console directly below, or bringing the wires through a neighboring partition. On our list, brick adds $65 to the base and a paintable cord cover is $59.

Poured concrete and columns

Concrete appears in the co-op towers near Broadway, in some warehouse buildings and in many newer buildings, often as columns or as walls around elevators and stairs. Concrete holds anchors very well, but it can hide reinforcing steel, and hitting a bar with a masonry bit stops the hole. When that happens, we move the hole within the bracket's slots rather than forcing through. Because concrete is not on our short price list, we quote it in writing from your photos. Hidden wiring in solid concrete has the same limits as brick: a cord cover, a drop to a console, or a route through an adjacent framed wall. Bob Vila's cost summary notes that concealing wiring in masonry costs more than in drywall, which matches what we see.

Painted concrete block

Block is common in warehouse offices, break rooms and storefronts east of Morgan Avenue. Hollow block has thin faces around large voids, so an anchor that works in solid brick can crush a hollow block face. We use anchors made for hollow masonry, or set fixings into the filled cores where the block was grouted, and we price block in writing from photos. Like brick and concrete, block cannot be fished, so wires go in a cover or down to a console.

Heavy timber posts and wood plank

Some older industrial buildings in this part of Brooklyn were built with heavy timber posts and beams and wood plank floors rather than steel and concrete. A big old timber post is sound wood and can take lag bolts well, but posts are usually narrower than a standard bracket, and a screen on a post is centered on the post, not on the room. We check the post for checks and splits, avoid old bolt holes, and use a bracket narrow enough to sit on the post's face. Timber is priced in writing. For many people a post is not actually the best spot; it is just the only one without a window. We say so if a nearby partition or a TV on a stand would serve the room better.

Plaster in walk-ups and older lofts

Plaster is hard on the surface and crumbly underneath, and it may sit on wood lath, metal lath or directly on brick. Anchors made for drywall grip the plaster skin but not what is behind it, and that is how most plaster mounts eventually fail. We fix into the framing behind the plaster, use toggles rated for hollow plaster, or, where the plaster is on brick, drill through into the brick and use masonry anchors. On our list, plaster adds $45. On plaster over framing, an in-wall power and cable kit is usually possible, $169 on our list, and we explain below why that kit is the only legal way to hide power.

Drywall on steel studs and tenant-built partitions

New office fit-outs and many loft partitions are drywall on light steel studs, or on wood studs set at odd spacing. Steel studs look identical to wood until a screw spins in them. We use anchors rated for thin steel or add a plywood backer between studs, and we price steel in writing. Tenant-built partitions vary the most: we have to find out what is inside before deciding whether the wall can hold the screen at all, and if it cannot, we tell you and suggest a better wall or a stand. Framed partitions are the one kind of wall in a loft where an in-wall kit can usually go in, which is why we sometimes route a brick-wall TV's wires through the partition next to it.

Hiding power: what the code allows

The question we hear most about wires is whether a TV's power cord can simply be pushed through the wall and plugged in below. It cannot. A power cord is not rated to run inside a wall, and a StreetEasy discussion of TV mounting in New York makes the city's position clear, with one commenter warning that a Romex-based kit "is not NYC code compliant" because city walls need armored cable. The legal way to put power behind a screen is a proper in-wall power and cable kit, which carries power inside the wall in its own rated cable to a recessed outlet behind the TV, plugged into your existing outlet below, or a new outlet installed by a licensed electrician. We install the kit at $169 on our list and do not do electrical work beyond it. Low-voltage cables are different: under Local Law 128 of 2024, wiring that runs below 50 volts for communication and data does not need an electrical permit, which is why HDMI, network and speaker cables can be run without one when done properly.

Renters, Owners and Boards

Leases, Loft Law Owners and Co-op Offices: Mounting a TV When Someone Else Owns the Wall

Most homes in East Williamsburg are rented, and the walls belong to somebody else. That does not mean you cannot mount a TV. It means one short conversation should happen before the drill comes out, and the right person to have it with depends on the kind of building you live in.

Ordinary rentals: what the lease says decides it

Brick Underground asked New York lawyers and managers this exact question. Attorney Steven Kirkpatrick said it is not common for a landlord to ban hanging items, and that a restriction which is not in the lease would not be enforceable; broker Bill Kowalczuk said a landlord is within their rights to ban hanging items if the lease says so, and advised asking permission before installing TV brackets. Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group gave the line most landlords use: "If the hole is larger than the size of a nail, then it's considered damage." The same article explains that a landlord who keeps part of a deposit for holes has to itemize the charges and return the rest within two weeks of move-out.

So the practical steps are short. Read the alterations clause in your lease. If it says nothing, a polite text to the landlord or managing agent saying which wall, what size TV and roughly how many holes is still a good idea. If it forbids holes, a TV on its stand at $119.99, with a soundbar placed in front at $79, is a proper job on our list, and it leaves nothing to patch. Posts, beams, dividers and loft surfaces

The only solid spot in our loft's living area that is not a window is a big old wooden post. Is a timber post a sound place for a TV?

Often it is, with a few conditions. A heavy timber post in an old industrial building is usually dense, seasoned wood that takes lag bolts very well, and a TV weighs far less than the floors that post was built to carry. The limits are width and condition. Most posts are narrower than a standard bracket's wall plate, so we choose a bracket whose plate fits the face of the post, center the screen on the post, and check the wood for deep drying cracks, rot near the base and old bolt holes before choosing where the lags go. We never notch or cut a structural post. Two other questions decide whether the post is the right answer: whether the screen centered on the post sits where you actually want to look, and where the power and signal will come from, since cables on a post can only run on its surface. Timber is not on our short list, so we price it in writing from photos of the post and the room.

A steel beam runs across our loft ceiling. Could the TV hang from the beam instead of going on a wall?

It is possible, but it is rarely the best choice in a home. A ceiling mount hung from a steel beam uses a clamp or a bolted plate on the beam's flange, and it suits places where no wall faces the seating, such as a long loft where the TV has to float in the middle of the room, or a bar where screens need to be seen from both sides. The trade-offs are height, movement and wires. A screen on a long drop pole can sway, it usually ends up higher than is comfortable for long viewing, and the power and signal have to come down the pole from the ceiling, which in a loft with a concrete or plank ceiling means a surface run to the nearest outlet. Before anyone clamps anything to structure, the building's owner should agree, and in a Loft Law building that is worth getting in writing. Ceiling mounts are priced in writing from photos of the beam and the room.

Our living area and bedroom are separated by a tall freestanding bookcase, not a wall. Can the TV be fixed to the bookcase?

We advise against it. A bookcase is furniture, built to carry books sitting on shelves, not a cantilevered load pulling outward from its face, and most freestanding units are not anchored to the floor or ceiling well enough to resist a screen being pushed, pulled or bumped. The Consumer Product Safety Commission counted 217 tip-over deaths from 2013 through July 2023, and 47 percent of them involved a television, so a tall divider with a screen on it is the kind of setup we would rather not create. The better options are a TV on its own stand or console in front of the divider, which is $119.99 on our list, or a real partition that can carry a bracket. If you want the room divided and the screen on that line, a properly framed partition built by a carpenter and anchored to floor and ceiling can do both jobs, and we can mount on it once it is up.

The exposed brick in our loft was coated with a clear glossy sealer during the conversion. Does that change how a bracket goes on?

A little. A clear sealer or masonry coating does not weaken the brick, but it can chip around a drill bit as the hole starts, leaving small pale rings that show if the bracket plate does not cover them. We start each hole slowly, keep every hole inside the area the wall plate will hide, and use a dust shroud so the coating's surface is not dusted over around the TV. The bigger effect is on cord covers: adhesive-backed covers stick poorly to some glossy coatings, so on a sealed wall we fix a cover with small screws into the mortar or brick rather than trusting tape alone. The coating also makes the brick harder to read by eye, so the pilot hole matters even more for telling solid brick from a filled or repointed patch. None of this changes the price; brick is the same $65 add-on whether it is bare or sealed.

Loft Law buildings and owners

Our building is an interim multiple dwelling, and the owner's architect is drawing the legalization plans now. Should we wait before cutting an in-wall power kit into our living room partition?

For an in-wall kit, waiting or asking first is sensible; for a simple bracket, it usually is not necessary. Legalizing a loft can mean new fire-rated partitions, new wiring and changes to walls that tenants built years ago, and the partition you want to open could be one of the walls on the architect's drawings. A bracket on that wall is a handful of small holes that can be filled if the wall is rebuilt, but an in-wall kit means two openings and a rated cable inside the wall, which you would not want to pay for twice. Our suggestion is to ask the owner or the managing agent, in writing, whether that wall is staying, and to keep a copy. If the answer is unclear, a paintable cord cover at $59 does the same visual job without opening the wall, and it can be replaced by an in-wall kit once the legalization work is finished.

We own a converted building with several loft units and re-rent them every few years. Should we choose one TV wall and bracket for every unit before tenants move in?

Many owners in buildings like yours find it saves trouble. When each tenant mounts their own TV, the walls collect a different set of holes every few years, sometimes in the mortar, sometimes with cords pushed through partitions, and every move-out turns into a patching job. Picking one wall per unit, ideally a framed partition that can take an in-wall power and cable kit or a brick wall where the fixings will land in sound brick, and installing a quality bracket there, gives every future tenant a safe place to hang a screen. We would price each unit from our list: the base mount, the wall add-on and the kit, and we can do several units in one visit if they are ready together. Owners in Loft Law buildings should check that the walls chosen are not affected by legalization plans first.

Our loft faces west, and the only long wall is directly across from a full row of factory windows. What can be done about afternoon glare?

Several things, and none of them require a different wall. First, the position: moving the screen a little lower and angling the seating slightly off the window line reduces the reflection more than people expect, and we test this with painter's tape before drilling. Second, the bracket: a tilting bracket lets you angle the screen down a few degrees, which pushes the reflection of high windows below your eye line, and a full-motion arm lets you swing it away from the light on bright afternoons. Third, the room: shades on the upper part of tall factory windows cut most of the glare while keeping the light that makes a loft worth living in. Matte-finish screens help too, though that is a choice to make when buying the TV. Bracket types other than a flat or tilting mount, and screens over 55 inches, are priced in writing on your quote.

The break room in our warehouse near the creek is painted hollow block. Will anchors hold a 65-inch TV on it?

Yes, with the right anchors in the right places. Hollow concrete block has thin faces around large voids, so a standard masonry anchor can crush the face and lose its grip. On a break-room wall we also think about who uses the room: a fixed or tilting bracket is sturdier than a full-motion arm that people will pull and push all day. Wires on block cannot go inside the wall, so they run in a cord cover to the nearest outlet. FAQ

East Williamsburg TV Installation FAQ

Our loft building's freight elevator is reserved for the commercial tenants during the day. How does a big TV get up to our floor?
+

We ask about the freight elevator when you book and plan the visit for a time it can be used, or for the stairs if it cannot. A screen up to about 65 inches in its box is a one-person carry; bigger boxes need two people on the stairs, and we send the right crew once we know the building. If the building wants the freight booked through the super or the managing agent, ask them for the slot and send it to us so the visit is set around it.

We are moving into a loft next month and the furniture arrives later. Can the TV go up before we have a couch to sit on?
+

Yes, as long as we can agree on where you will sit. An empty loft is actually the easiest room to work in. We tape the screen outline on the wall and mark where the sofa will go, often with a chair placed at the same distance and height, so the TV lands at the right height for the furniture that is coming. If the console is arriving later, we leave the cables neatly coiled at the right height for it.

Can a TV go on the wall of a sleeping loft or mezzanine where the ceiling is low?
+

Often, with a smaller screen and a low-profile bracket. Mezzanines and sleeping lofts built inside tall industrial rooms usually have framed walls or railings rather than masonry, and limited headroom, so we check what the wall is made of, how the screen will be seen from the bed, and how a technician can work safely up there. A railing is not a mounting surface. If the only solid surface is a framed wall, it is priced from our list once we know what is inside it.

Does a TV that stays on its stand in a loft need to be strapped to something?
+

We recommend it, especially where children or pets live. The Consumer Product Safety Commission found that nearly half of tip-over deaths involved a television. In a loft where the stand faces open space, an anti-tip strap can fix the TV to the console and the console to a partition, a post or the floor, depending on what the owner allows, and we talk through the options when we set the TV up on its stand.

Can a paintable cord cover be made to blend with exposed brick?
+

Reasonably well, though never invisibly. A cord cover is a smooth channel and brick is a textured surface, so the best results come from painting the cover to match the brick's main color and running it straight down along a mortar line or the edge of a column, where the eye expects a line. On a sealed or glossy brick wall we screw the cover on rather than relying on adhesive. The cover is $59 on our list; paint matching is up to you or your painter.

We live on Frost Street near the venues. Does a show night change when you can come?
+

It can affect parking and traffic, so we plan around it. With Brooklyn Steel on Frost Street and Brooklyn Pacha on Stewart Avenue drawing large crowds in the evening, we book installs on those blocks during the day, well before doors open, and ask whether the street has any event restrictions that week. Working within the city's weekday construction hours of 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. usually keeps us clear of the evening crowds anyway.

Does my roommate need to be home, or can someone else let the technician in?
+

Anyone over eighteen who knows where the TV should go and can approve the wall can let us in. Before the visit, tell us who will be there and make sure they know the screen position, how the wires should run and any building rules. At the start, the technician confirms the wall and the price with that person before drilling, and at the end tests every input with them.

Our studio is full of artwork and equipment. What happens to the brick dust when you drill?
+

Most of it never leaves the drill. In masonry, our technicians use a dust shroud with a vacuum attached, which captures the dust at the hole, the method OSHA describes for handheld drills in concrete. We also cover the floor and anything directly below the wall and clean up before leaving. If there is delicate artwork or open equipment within a few feet, move or cover it before the visit or tell us so we can plan around it.

Can you move our TV from one wall of the loft to another?
+

Yes. Taking the TV down is $85 on our list, and putting it back up on the new wall is priced as a new mount on that wall type, for example $179.99 plus $65 if the new wall is brick. If your existing bracket is rated for the TV and in good condition, we can reuse it. The old fixings stay in the first wall unless you want them removed; filling the holes is up to you or your painter.

The block outside our building is a loading zone all day. Where does your van park?
+

We work it out when you book. In the industrial blocks, technicians often use a legal loading spot briefly to unload the TV and tools, then park nearby; on busy shopping streets, we plan the arrival for a quieter hour. If your building has a loading dock or a yard that contractors may use, tell us, and if the landlord has to approve it, ask before the visit.

Does the TV price change if our address is east of Morgan Avenue, in the industrial part of the neighborhood?
+

No. Every East Williamsburg address is billed at the same Brooklyn rate, whether it is a loft on the residential side or a break room in a warehouse near the creek. What changes the total is the wall, the screen and the wiring, not which side of Morgan Avenue the building is on.

Do I need to clear the artwork and shelves off the brick wall before you arrive?
+

It helps a lot. Take down anything hanging on the TV wall and clear the floor within about six feet of it, including shelves and plants. That gives the technician room to set the ladder, lay down the floor cover and see the whole wall when reading where the bricks and joints are. If something is fixed to the wall and cannot come down, tell us beforehand so the screen position can work around it.
