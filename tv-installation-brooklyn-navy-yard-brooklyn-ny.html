The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need TV Installation

The Brooklyn Navy Yard is not a neighborhood in the way Clinton Hill or Fort Greene are. It sits inside a boundary of Navy Street, Flushing Avenue and Kent Avenue on the land side and the East River and Wallabout Bay on the other, behind a wall and a set of gates, and every road inside it is private. The Navy built ships here from 1801 until 1966. The city bought the site, and since 1981 BNYDC has run it as a not-for-profit developer and property manager on the city's behalf. What that means for anyone hanging a TV is simple: you are almost always a tenant or a licensee, BNYDC is almost always the landlord, and the wall behind the screen belongs to someone who has written rules about it. That is the first difference between a Navy Yard job and a TV job in a Brooklyn apartment, and it shapes everything else on this page.

The second difference is who the screen is for. BNYDC describes more than 550 businesses on the campus, across food and beverage, fashion and design, manufacturing, media, robotics, medical technology and clean energy, with over 13,000 people working there. Those businesses want screens for the same handful of reasons. A meeting room needs a display that a laptop can reach and a camera can sit on. A reception desk or showroom wants a screen that shows the work to a visitor. A counter needs a menu or a queue display. A production team wants a monitor on the wall of an office that overlooks the floor. And a staff room wants an ordinary TV for lunch breaks and for the playoffs. The equipment is usually a commercial display or a consumer TV bought for an office; the hard part is the wall, the lease and the route from the loading dock to your suite.

The walls are hard because the buildings were built for war, for ships and for storage, and then reused. Building 77 went up in 1941 as a 16-story concrete storehouse, and The Bridge's account of its renovation puts it plainly: "The first 11 floors had no windows at all." Wikipedia's history of the Yard gives the lower walls as about 25 inches thick. Building 3 is an eleven-story reinforced-concrete supply storehouse from 1917. Dock 72, by contrast, is a new 16-story office building finished at the end of the 2010s, with 14-foot ceilings and eight-foot windows, according to The Bridge. Between those extremes sit low brick warehouses and shops, steel-framed halls such as the former Building 128 that now holds New Lab, and fit-out partitions that tenants have added inside all of them. Two suites on the same corridor can have completely different walls behind the screen, and the scanner that works in one will mislead you in the next.

The third difference is logistics. The Yard runs on its own calendar. BNYDC's Tenant Manual says the freight elevators shut down between 8 p.m. and 5 a.m. on weekdays and give no ordinary service on Saturday, Sunday or federal holidays. After-hours freight use is by request at least two business days ahead, at $50 an hour per car from 8 to 11 p.m., and with a four-hour minimum of $200 overnight and at weekends. Visitors, including installers, are registered by the tenant through BNYDC's visitor system and receive a QR code for the gate. Packages may not be left in shared areas, contractor waste has to be removed under the Tenant Alteration Guidelines, and the manual is blunt that "If waste is left behind, the tenant or licensee will incur penalties." None of that is a reason not to put a TV on the wall. It is the reason a Navy Yard screen job is planned a little differently from one in a brownstone.

Prices around the borough show why planning matters. Angi's cost data for TV mounting in Brooklyn gives an average of $280 and a typical range of $172 to $376, with jobs reported from $100 to $670. Bob Vila's national guide, drawing on Angi and HomeAdvisor, puts drywall jobs at $100 to $300 and brick, concrete or stone at $200 to $800, and says running wires inside a wall can cost as much as $1,000. Those spreads come mostly from the wall and the wiring. In the Navy Yard, the wall is more often concrete or brick than anywhere else in Brooklyn, and the wiring question is tangled up with a lease rule, so a fixed list with each add-on named matters more here than a national average does.

Six Brooklyn Navy Yard facts that decide a screen job before we pick a bracket

  • The landlord is BNYDC, and alterations need its approval. The Tenant Manual says plans of proposed work "must be submitted to and approved by BNYDC," licensees may not make alterations without submitting plans, and unapproved construction is a default under a lease. Minor work should be confirmed with Property Management first.
  • Electrical work has its own rule. The manual says electrical work cannot be performed until approved by BNYDC and that "Any and all electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician." That decides how power reaches a screen mounted high or in the middle of a wall.
  • Freight cars keep fixed hours. Weekdays 5 a.m. to 8 p.m. as standard, with paid after-hours and weekend use booked two business days ahead. A large display in its box usually rides the freight car.
  • Some of the walls are very old concrete. Building 77's lower floors were built without windows and with walls roughly two feet thick, and Building 3 is reinforced concrete from 1917. Those are masonry jobs, not drywall jobs.
  • Some are new and full of glass. Dock 72 has 14-foot ceilings and eight-foot windows, and because it is only 90 feet wide, The Bridge says every desk is within 45 feet of a window. Glare is the first thing we check there.
  • Telecom closets are controlled. Four providers already have infrastructure in the Yard, Brooklyn Fiber, Crown Castle, Spectrum and Verizon, and tenants coordinate closet access with their Property Manager. A display that streams or plays signage needs that settled.

Same-Day Repair in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Need Repair in the Brooklyn Navy Yard? A Display Sagging Off Its Bracket, a Screen Left Behind by the Last Tenant, or Cables Hanging From a Ceiling

The urgent screen calls we expect from a place like the Yard come in three kinds. The first is a display that has started to droop or lean. In a fit-out partition, that usually means the bracket was screwed into drywall between metal studs, or into the thin steel of the stud itself with a wood screw, and the weight has slowly pulled it loose. On old brick it usually means the anchors were set in the soft mortar joints rather than the brick. On concrete it can mean a plastic plug was used in a hole drilled too wide. The second is the screen, bracket or tangle of cables the previous tenant left behind when the suite changed hands. Leases at the Yard turn over, companies grow from one suite to a bigger one, and the wall you inherit may carry someone else's guesswork. The third is a meeting-room display that works but cannot be used: the laptop cable does not reach, the camera has nowhere to sit, or the sound comes from a laptop speaker at the far end of the table.

If a display is tipping, do not push it back up. If two people can safely lift it off, unplug it, take it down and call (347) 934-8335. If it cannot be lifted safely, keep people out from under it and call. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same screen goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type with the take-down added, and if we find that the wall itself is the problem, a cracked block or a crumbling patch of brick, we tell you so you can bring in BNYDC's Property Management before anything else is fixed to it.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for the Brooklyn Navy Yard's Architecture

The Yard is a collection of buildings from three centuries, and a screen job inside one of them depends on three things: what the wall is made of, how the screen gets from the street to the floor, and how the space is used around the person doing the work. Six kinds of space cover nearly every request.

Wartime concrete floors

Building 77's former storehouse floors, built in 1941 with no windows on the lowest eleven stories and very thick walls, now offices, studios and light manufacturing.

Early concrete storehouses

Building 3, the eleven-story reinforced-concrete supply storehouse of 1917, with a loading platform around its base and Pratt's Research Yard among its tenants.

New glass-lined offices

Dock 72's sixteen floors of offices raised on V-shaped columns, with 14-foot ceilings, eight-foot windows and a coworking operator on several floors.

Big steel-framed halls

Shipbuilding-era halls such as the one New Lab occupies, where 6sqft described ceilings of about 70 feet and a mix of studios, labs and event space.

Low brick warehouses and shops

One- to four-story brick buildings around the dry docks and the old perimeter, now workshops, distilleries, studios and makers, often with roll-up doors and mezzanine offices.

Fit-out suites inside any of them

Drywall partitions on metal studs that tenants add to divide a floor into offices, meeting rooms and kitchens, which is where most screens actually end up.

Wartime concrete floors: a wall built to hold ammunition will hold a TV, but it has to be drilled right

The lower floors of Building 77 are the clearest example of how unusual Navy Yard walls can be. Wikipedia's account of the building says its lowest eleven stories were built with walls about 25 inches thick and no windows, likely for storing ammunition, and the 2017 renovation cut new windows into them. The Bridge describes the building as a "16-story concrete monolith" built in five months, with 58,000-square-foot floors and 10-foot ceilings after the renovation. For a TV, poured concrete is the best wall you can have once the bracket is in. It will not flex, it will not crush around an anchor the way old plaster does, and it will carry a large display for as long as anyone wants it there.

The work is in the drilling. Old concrete can be hard, and it can hide steel reinforcing bars a few inches from the surface. A hammer drill finds a bar by stopping dead, and the right answer is to move the hole, not to force it. So we scan first for anything metal, drill one pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it, and plan the bracket position so that if one hole meets steel the plate can shift an inch without changing where the screen sits. The anchors are concrete anchors rated for the load, set to the depth the anchor needs, which in a wall this thick is a small fraction of the wall. Concrete also means dust: OSHA's fact sheet on silica says handheld drills used on concrete should have a shroud with a vacuum of the right airflow and a filter of 99 percent efficiency or better, with a HEPA vacuum to clean the holes. In an occupied office, that is also simply good manners.

Two more things matter on those floors. Some of the exterior walls were opened up for windows, and the frame around a new opening carries loads we will not drill into, so a screen goes beside a window, not into its surround. And many interior walls on a renovated concrete floor are not concrete at all but tenant partitions built against it. Knocking tells you which you have: a partition sounds hollow and gives a little, the original wall sounds like nothing at all. If your suite is on one of these floors, a photo of the wall from a few feet back, with the window or column beside it in the frame, is usually enough for us to tell you which wall we expect and which line of the list applies.

Early concrete storehouses: Building 3 and the buildings like it

Building 3, by the background research we hold for the Yard, is an eleven-story reinforced-concrete supply storehouse built in 1917 by Turner, with a loading platform wrapping its base and a farm on its roof. Since 2023 it has also housed Pratt's Research Yard. A concrete storehouse from that era tends to have columns at regular intervals, deep beams overhead and walls that are either original concrete or later infill, and the column is often the most tempting place for a screen because it faces the desks. A display can go on a concrete column if the column is wide enough for the bracket plate and there is nothing running down its face, and the same anchor rules apply as on a wall. Where a column is too narrow, a fixed bracket on the nearest wall with the screen tilted toward the room is usually better than an arm reaching out from a pillar.

The loading platform around the base is where your screen will arrive. A display in its box is long and flat, and it is fragile when carried on its face; the box is printed with the side that should stay up. We bring the box up the freight car upright and keep it boxed until it is inside your suite, partly to protect it and partly because the Tenant Manual does not allow anything to be left in a corridor. If the screen is being delivered by a retailer before our visit, have it delivered to your suite, not to the dock, and keep the box. A display that arrives scratched is easier to return in its original packaging, and a sealed box tells us the screen has not been dropped on its way to your floor.

New glass-lined offices: Dock 72 and the light that comes with it

Dock 72 is the newest kind of Navy Yard building and the most ordinary in one way: inside a tenant suite, the walls you will want a screen on are usually drywall partitions. The difference is the light. The Bridge's tour of the building describes 14-foot ceilings, eight-foot windows around the floors and a building only 90 feet wide, so that every desk is within 45 feet of a window. That is wonderful for the people working there and hard on a screen. A display facing a window wall will show the window back to the room for much of the day, and a display on the window wall itself can be impossible to read with the sky behind it. So the first thing we check on a Dock 72 floor is where the sun comes from at the hours the room is used, and which wall lets the screen face away from the glass.

Ceiling height changes the mount, too. With a 14-foot ceiling, there is a temptation to put a screen high, where it looks tidy from the door. In a meeting room that is wrong: people at the table end up looking up, and the camera that sits on or under the screen looks down on everyone. We set meeting-room displays so that the middle of the screen is close to the seated eye height of the people at the far end of the table, and we mark that with painter's tape so you can sit in the chairs before anything is drilled. Lobby and lounge screens can go higher because people are standing or passing, and a slight downward tilt helps them in a tall room. The same building also has a large shared conference room and a rooftop conference room by The Bridge's account; those belong to the building, so screens there are a matter for its operator, not a tenant.

Big steel-framed halls: when the wall is too far away

Some Yard spaces are not rooms at all. 6sqft's tour of New Lab described an 84,000-square-foot former shipbuilding building with ceilings of about 70 feet, workshops for around 60 companies and an event space used for workshops of around 200 people. In a hall like that, the walls may be steel, glass, or a long way from where people sit, and a screen is often wanted in the middle of an open floor. Fixing a display to a structural steel column or to the steel of a building we do not control is not a tenant decision; it is the landlord's. The honest options are a screen on its own stand, which is $119.99 on our list and needs no drilling at all, or a wall-mounted screen on a partition the tenant built, which counts as part of the tenant's own fit-out.

Sound matters in a big steel hall in a way it does not in a small office. A soundbar under a screen in a room with hard surfaces and a very high ceiling will echo, and in shared space it reaches people who are not in your meeting. For a presentation corner or a demo screen in an open hall, we usually suggest a soundbar placed directly in front of the screen at seated height ($79) rather than a wall bracket, set to a lower volume with dialogue enhancement turned on, so the people watching hear it and the neighbors at the next bench do not.

Low brick warehouses and shops: solid walls with a past

Much of the Yard is low brick buildings: shops, storehouses and small industrial buildings, many of them now home to makers, distillers, studios and workshops. Brick is a good wall for a TV if the anchors go into the brick and not into the mortar between bricks, and if the brick itself is sound. Old brick in an industrial building may have been painted, patched, drilled for long-gone machinery and soaked in more than one flood. Brooklyn Paper reported in 2025 that Hurricane Sandy put up to six feet of water across the campus in 2012, and BNYDC's resilience plans since then include raising equipment and workstations above the floodline. A screen on a ground floor near the water should therefore keep its connections, power strip and any player up off the floor, in a cabinet or on the wall behind the screen, not on the floor below it.

The brick or stone line on our list is $65 on top of the $179.99 base, because brick takes a hammer drill, masonry anchors and more time to place each fixing. On a wall that has been drilled many times before, we look at the old holes and work around them, and if the surface is crumbling where the bracket needs to go, we say so before drilling rather than trusting it. A mezzanine office above a workshop floor is often a light timber or steel structure added later, and its walls are usually partitions, not brick; the knock tells us which, and the price follows the wall.

Fit-out suites inside any of them: the wall the tenant built

Most screens in the Yard go on a wall that is neither concrete nor brick but a partition a tenant built to divide its space: drywall on metal studs, sometimes with a second layer of board for sound. Metal studs are thin steel channels that look exactly like wood studs on a scanner but will not hold a wood screw. A bracket on metal studs needs fasteners made for hollow walls or for sheet steel, spread across as many studs as the bracket plate allows, and for a larger screen a plywood backer behind the board is better still if the wall is open. Because the partition is part of your fit-out, it is usually the wall your lease is most relaxed about, but it is also the wall most likely to hide cables, sprinkler lines or a fire-rated corridor wall on the other side. A fire-rated wall between a suite and a corridor is not one we cut open for an in-wall kit.

If your partition is drywall on wood studs, the $179.99 base covers a screen up to 55 inches on a flat or tilting bracket. Metal studs and larger screens have their own lines on the same Brooklyn list, and we write them into the total from your photos. If you do not know what the studs are, a photo of the wall with the light switch plate taken off, or a note about what the person who built the suite told you, usually answers it before the visit.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Three Things We Check Before Carrying a Display Into a Navy Yard Building

These notes describe how our technicians plan commercial screen jobs in buildings like the Yard's. They are our practice, not a story about any one tenant or address.

Who said yes, and in writing. Before we load the van, we ask the tenant who approved the wall work: the Property Manager by email, a facilities person, or the lease clause itself. In a building where unapproved alterations can count as a default under the lease, a two-line email from the tenant to Property Management describing the screen, the wall and the cable route is worth far more than the five minutes it takes to send.

How the box reaches the floor. A 65-inch box will fit in most freight cars and in some passenger elevators; an 85-inch box needs the freight car and two people. We ask the tenant which car serves the suite and whether a slot has been booked, because the Tenant Manual's freight hours are fixed and after-hours use is booked two business days ahead with the building number, the date, the time and the car number.

What the wall is, confirmed once. On the day, the technician scans, knocks along the wall at screen height, then drills a single pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it. Grey grit and a hard stop mean concrete; red dust means brick; a few millimeters of white powder and then nothing means drywall over a cavity; a bright scratch and a spinning bit means a metal stud. If that hole changes the line on the list from what was quoted, the tenant hears it before any other hole is drilled.

Tape, then chairs. Painter's tape outlines the screen on the wall. Someone sits in the far chair, someone stands at the door, and if the room is used for video calls, someone opens a call on a laptop to see where the camera will look. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket on concrete costs a landlord's wall.

Power and Cables

Power Behind the Screen: In-Wall Kits, Cord Covers and When a Navy Yard Job Needs an Electrician

Hiding the wires is the part of a Navy Yard screen job most likely to run into a rule, so it is worth understanding the options before you choose a wall. There are four ways to get power and a picture to a mounted display, and each one fits a different kind of wall and a different answer from your Property Manager.

Leave the cables to a console or credenza. If the screen is mounted low enough above a credenza or a cabinet, the power cord and the HDMI or network cable can drop straight down behind the furniture into an outlet near the floor. Nothing is cut, nothing is hidden inside a wall, and the only holes are the bracket's. This is the most common answer on solid concrete and brick, where there is no cavity to run anything through, and it is the easiest one to undo when a lease ends.

A paintable cord cover on the surface. A cord cover is a slim channel fixed to the wall that carries the cables from the screen down to the outlet. It is $59 on our list, it can be painted the color of the wall, and it is fixed with a few small screws or a strip of adhesive, depending on the wall. On a concrete or brick wall, a cover is the only way to hide a vertical run. It does not cut the wall, which makes it the option most leases treat as minor, though you should still confirm with Property Management that it counts as non-material in your building.

A code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit. On a drywall partition, an in-wall kit puts a recessed power inlet behind the screen and a second one near the floor, joined inside the wall by the kit's own rated cable, with a pass-through for the HDMI and network cables. It plugs into an existing outlet; it does not connect to the building's wiring. It is $169 on our list. It needs a hollow wall with no fire rating, no insulation packed solid, no sprinkler line and nothing else in the stud bay, and it means two neat openings in a wall your landlord owns, so in the Yard it is exactly the kind of work to describe to Property Management before the visit.

A new outlet behind the screen. Some tenants want a real receptacle wired from a circuit, especially for a screen high on a wall, a video wall or a display in the middle of a long partition. That is electrical work. BNYDC's Tenant Manual says electrical work cannot be performed until approved by BNYDC and that "Any and all electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician." We do not do electrical work. If your screen needs a new outlet, the right order is: you get BNYDC's approval, a licensed electrician installs the outlet, and we come back to mount the screen and dress the low-voltage cables to it. We are happy to mark the exact spot for the outlet on the wall before the electrician comes, so the box ends up hidden behind the screen.

A word on what never goes inside a wall: the TV's own power cord. The cord that comes with a display is a flexible cord, and the National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be run concealed inside walls, floors or ceilings. Pushing it through two holes behind the screen is the most common shortcut in TV mounting, and in a building where BNYDC's rules already treat electrical work as something to approve, it is also the one most likely to be noticed. The in-wall kit exists precisely to avoid it.

Signal cables have rules of their own. A long HDMI run may need an active cable or an extender to carry a 4K picture reliably, and a meeting room where the laptop sits at the far end of a long table often needs one. A display that plays signage from a network player needs a working network jack near the screen, and in the Yard that jack leads back to a closet that may be yours or the building's. The Tenant Manual lists four providers with infrastructure on campus, Brooklyn Fiber, Crown Castle, Spectrum and Verizon, and says tenants should coordinate with their Property Manager about access to the building's telecom closets. If the jack behind your screen is dead, we can trace it within your suite; if the trace leads to a closet we cannot open, the next step is your Property Manager or your provider.

Leases and Approvals

Whose Wall Is It? Mounting a Display When BNYDC Is Your Landlord

Every TV installer in Brooklyn has met the renter whose lease says something vague about wall fixtures. In the Navy Yard the question is less vague, because the landlord publishes a Tenant Manual and leases refer to Tenant Alteration Guidelines. BNYDC says the manual does not replace your lease, so the lease always wins, but the manual gives a clear picture of how the landlord thinks. Part C says that, generally, plans of proposed alterations must be submitted to and approved by BNYDC; licensees may not make any alterations without submitting plans; some work, such as painting, may count as a "Non-Material Alteration" that does not need plans, but tenants should confirm that with Property Management before starting; and unapproved construction or permits left open are each a default under the lease or license. The Tenant Alteration Guidelines themselves are not published online; tenants get them from Leasing or Property Management.

Where does a TV bracket fall? We do not decide that, and neither should any installer. A bracket on a partition you built is very different from a bracket on the original concrete of a landmark-era building, and an in-wall kit that cuts two openings is different again from a screen on a stand. What we can do is make the question easy to answer. Before the visit, we give you a short description in plain words: which wall, what it is made of, how many holes of what size, what the anchors are, whether any opening will be cut, how the power reaches the screen, and how everything is removed at the end of the lease. You send that to your Property Manager, and in most cases you have an answer before you would have had a delivery date for the screen.

Insurance is the other paper question. The Tenant Manual says BNYDC must have up-to-date certificates of insurance on file for each tenancy, collected and tracked by a compliance firm, with the requirements set out in your lease or license. Many tenants also have their own landlord or coworking operator, or a facilities team, that wants a contractor's certificate before work starts. We are licensed under NYS license #12000287431 and insured; tell us the exact names and addresses to show on the certificate and we send it ahead of the visit.

The end of a lease is worth thinking about at the start. A screen on a stand leaves nothing behind. A bracket on drywall leaves four to eight small holes that a painter fills in an hour. A bracket on concrete or brick leaves anchor holes that can be filled but not hidden as easily, and an in-wall kit leaves two openings that need patching. If your space is temporary, or you expect to move within the Yard as the company grows, pick the wall and the method you will be glad to undo. If you are signing a long lease and fitting out a suite, it can be worth planning the screens with the fit-out, so the power, the network jack and a backer board go into the partition before the drywall is closed.

Two smaller rules come up on almost every visit. Building 77's lobby needs a swipe card between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., so a late job there needs an escort from the tenant. And roof use is prohibited at the Yard except in emergencies unless a lease grants the roof, which rules out the occasional request for a rooftop screen at a staff party unless your lease says otherwise. Neither is a problem if it is known in advance; both are problems if they are discovered at the gate.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn Navy Yard tenants

  • Approval comes before drilling. BNYDC's Tenant Manual says alterations generally need plans approved by BNYDC, and that non-material work should be confirmed with Property Management first. Get the answer in writing before the visit.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

These are the situations Navy Yard tenants describe when they ask about a screen: walls that turn out to be concrete, a lease that wants approval, a freight car with set hours, a meeting room that has to work for video calls, and a suite somebody else fitted out. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Concrete, columns and very old walls

Our office is on one of Building 77's lower floors, where the outside walls are original concrete. Can a 75-inch display be anchored into a wall like that without hitting the steel inside it?

Yes. A wall of old poured concrete is one of the strongest places a large display can hang, and steel inside it is a routing problem, not a stopping point. We scan the area first for metal, drill a single pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it, and plan the bracket so the plate can slide an inch or two if a hole meets reinforcing bar. The anchors are concrete anchors rated well above the display's weight, set only as deep as the anchor requires, which in a wall about two feet thick is a small fraction of its depth. Concrete is not drywall, so the job is priced from the masonry lines of our Brooklyn list rather than the $179.99 drywall base alone, and a 75-inch screen carries its size line on top. We write both into your total from a photo of the wall before you book.

The only wall facing our desks in a converted storehouse is a concrete column. Is a column a safe place for a screen?

It can be, if the column is wide enough for the bracket plate and nothing runs down its face. Concrete columns in early-twentieth-century storehouses like Building 3 are usually solid and very strong, so the anchors are not the worry; the width is. A bracket plate for a mid-size display needs a flat face somewhat wider than the plate itself, and a column that is narrower than that leaves the screen wobbling on an arm or the anchors too close to the corner of the concrete. If the column is wide enough, a fixed or tilting bracket on its face works well. If it is not, a screen on the nearest wall with a slight turn toward the desks, or on its own stand, is better, and we will show you both with tape before anything is drilled.

The brick wall in our workshop has dozens of old holes from machinery that was bolted there decades ago. Can a bracket still go on it?

Usually. Old holes are only a problem when they land where the new anchors need to be, or when they have cracked the brick around them. We look at the wall first, choose a bracket position where the anchors meet solid brick rather than an old hole or a mortar joint, and test the pilot hole. If the brick around the spot you want is crumbling or cracked, we say so and suggest a nearby position that is sound, rather than relying on filler to hold a screen. The brick or stone line on our list is $65 on top of the $179.99 base, and a heavily patched wall does not change that price; it only changes where the bracket goes.

Leases, approval and the Property Manager

Our Property Manager asked for a description of the TV work before saying yes. What exactly should we send?

Send a short paragraph that answers six questions: which wall and what it is made of, the size and weight of the screen, the type of bracket and how many anchor holes of what size, whether any opening will be cut in the wall, how power reaches the screen, and how everything is removed at the end of the lease. We write that paragraph for you from your photos once you have picked the wall. If the plan uses a cord cover or a console, say so, because those do not cut the wall. If it uses an in-wall kit, say that it plugs into an existing outlet and does not connect to building wiring. And if a new outlet is wanted, say that a licensed electrician will do that part with BNYDC's approval, because the Tenant Manual requires it.

Does a TV on its own stand need BNYDC's approval, or only a wall mount?

A screen standing on its feet or on a floor stand is furniture; nothing is fixed to the building, so it is hard to see it as an alteration. A wall bracket is different, because it puts holes in a wall your landlord owns, and the Tenant Manual says even non-material alterations should be confirmed with Property Management. If your approval is taking longer than your deadline, a stand setup at $119.99 gets the screen working now, and the same screen can go on the wall later once the answer comes back.

We sublet part of a floor from another company. Who has to approve a screen on our wall?

Usually both the company you sublet from and, through them, BNYDC. A sublet or licence sits underneath the main lease, and the main tenant normally carries the obligations to the landlord, including the rules about alterations. So the request usually goes from you to the company you sublet from, which checks its own lease and passes it to Property Management if needed. We can send the same written description to both, and if the answer is a stand for now, that is a perfectly good way to start.

Getting the screen in

Our new 85-inch display is being delivered to the Yard. Should it go to the loading dock or straight to our suite?

Straight to your suite, if the carrier will bring it up. The Tenant Manual says packages cannot be left in shared public areas and loading docks must be kept clear except during loading and unloading, so a display left at the dock is a problem for you and for the building. Ask the carrier for delivery to the floor, have someone there to receive it, and leave it in its sealed box until we arrive. An 85-inch box takes two people and usually the freight car.

Can you install on a Saturday, when our office is empty?

Sometimes, but the freight car is the deciding factor. BNYDC's Tenant Manual says there is no freight elevator service on Saturday, Sunday or federal holidays as standard, and weekend use is by request two business days ahead with a four-hour minimum of $200, paid to BNYDC, not to us. If your display is small enough to go up in a passenger elevator, or is already in your suite, a weekend visit is easier. If the screen is large and still in a box at the dock, a weekday visit with the freight car booked is usually simpler and costs you nothing extra.

Meeting rooms and video calls

Our meeting room is used mostly for video calls with investors. How high should the display be, and where does the camera go?

For a room used for calls, the screen and the camera have to be planned together. The middle of the screen should sit close to the seated eye height of the people at the far end of the table, so nobody looks up, and the camera should sit just above or below the screen, close to its center, so people on the call see faces rather than the tops of heads. In a room with very high ceilings, such as the 14-foot floors at Dock 72, the pull is always to mount higher; for a call room that is the wrong instinct. We mark the screen with tape, open a call on a laptop, and move the tape until the picture of the room looks right before we drill.

The laptop sits at the far end of a long table from the display. How does the picture get there without a cable across the floor?

There are three tidy answers. The first is a wireless presentation device, which many teams already own, so the laptop sends its picture to a receiver behind the screen. The second is a cable run under the table and along the wall in a cord cover or an existing raceway, which is fixed and reliable. The third is a run through the ceiling or wall to a connection plate near the table, where the wall and the lease allow it. A long HDMI run can need an active cable or an extender to carry a sharp picture, and we test it at the length it will be used. Whatever the route, nothing is taped across the floor where people walk.

Will a soundbar under the display help our calls, or will it echo in a concrete room?

It will help, if it is set up for the room. A laptop speaker at one end of a table is too quiet for people at the other end, and a soundbar under the screen puts the voices where the faces are. In a room with bare concrete or glass, sound bounces, so we keep the soundbar close to the screen, turn down the bass, switch on any dialogue or voice setting the bar has and test it with a real call at your normal volume. A wall-mounted soundbar is $129 on our list, or $79 placed on a credenza below. If the room still echoes after that, the fix is soft furnishing or acoustic panels, not a louder speaker.

Suites someone else built

We took over a suite with an empty TV bracket and a network jack left by the last tenant. Can we just hang our display on what is there?

Only after the bracket has been checked, and often it is better replaced. We look at what the bracket is rated for, whether it fits your display's bolt pattern, what it is fixed into, and with what. A bracket rated for a smaller screen or screwed into drywall between metal studs is not safe for a bigger display, however solid it looks. The jack is the other half: we tone it back to the panel to find out whether it is live and where it goes. If the bracket is sound and right for your display, we hang the screen on it and price the job as a mount on that wall. If it is not, it comes off and a new bracket goes on, and the old holes are filled or covered by the new plate.

The wall we want sounds hollow in some places and solid in others. What is going on behind it?

Most likely a drywall partition built in front of an older solid wall, with a gap between them, or a partition with blocking or a backer board in one part and an empty cavity in the next. Both are common in buildings that have been refitted many times. A hollow-and-solid wall is not a reason to move the screen, but it is a reason to find out exactly what is there before choosing anchors, because a hollow-wall anchor in the solid part will not set and a concrete anchor in the hollow part will not reach. We scan, knock and drill the pilot hole where the plate will cover it, then choose an anchor for each point the bracket will use.
