The Neighborhood

Why Fulton Ferry Properties Need Alarm Installation

Fulton Ferry exists because of a river crossing. Brooklyn Bridge Park says boats were taking people from this spot to Manhattan as early as 1642, the first ferry service between the two shores. Wikipedia records that the Fulton Ferry Company began regular service in 1814 and that by 1872 there were 1,200 crossings a day. Warehouses, hotels, shops and offices grew up around the landing. Then the Brooklyn Bridge opened overhead in 1883, traffic moved up onto it, and the ferry stopped in 1924. The neighborhood that was left behind is the one people walk through today: a short main street, now called Old Fulton Street, running down to the water from the foot of Cadman Plaza West, with Front Street, Water Street and Furman Street crossing it, all within sight of the bridge.

What survived was protected. The Fulton Ferry District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on June 28, 1974, and Wikipedia describes it as 15 contributing buildings built between 1830 and 1895 on about 16 acres, mostly brick commercial and commercial-residential buildings of two to four stories, cut through overhead by the bridge. The city’s Landmarks Preservation Commission held its hearing on March 8, 1977, and designated the Fulton Ferry Historic District that year. Its designation report calls Fulton Street the district’s “main street,” singles out the 1861 offices of the Brooklyn City Railroad Company at Furman and Fulton, the commercial row on the old Fulton Street, the Tobacco Inspection Warehouse and the seven Empire Stores warehouses on Water Street, and the Eagle Warehouse of 1893–94, the only building in the district designed by a noted architect.

That list is also the list of what an alarm installer meets inside. These are not modern apartment buildings with a doorman and a security desk. Wikipedia says the Eagle Warehouse at 28 Old Fulton Street, eight stories of Romanesque Revival brick by Frank Freeman, was converted to condominiums in 1980 by architect Bernard Rothzeid and now holds 85 apartments, one of them the penthouse whose window is the building’s great clock face. CityRealty says the Brooklyn City Railroad building at 8 Old Fulton Street was erected in 1861 and converted to cooperative apartments in 1975, and StreetEasy lists it as a five-story co-op of 11 units. The smaller brick buildings on the street mix shops and restaurants at ground level with apartments or offices above. A loft in a building like these has deep masonry window openings, high ceilings, heavy timber or iron inside, mezzanines and roof access, and a ground floor that sits close to the river.

The river is the second reason this page exists. On the night of October 29, 2012, Hurricane Sandy’s storm surge came over the landing. Brooklyn Heights Blog reported two days later that water reached the River Café’s doorstep at the 8 a.m. high tide and that by nightfall the evening tide and the surge left water three to four feet deep in its dining room and kitchen; HuffPost described refrigerators knocked around the kitchen and every appliance but the range destroyed. The same Brooklyn Heights Blog report spoke of major flooding in low-lying DUMBO next door. The New York Times reported the River Café reopening in December 2013. An alarm panel, battery and communicator mounted low in a Water Street cellar would have gone under with everything else. NYC’s flood map guidance describes FEMA’s high-risk flood area as one with a one percent chance of flooding in any year and a 26 percent chance over a 30-year mortgage; we ask every owner to look up their own address on the city’s map before we choose where the panel goes.

The third reason is people. Brooklyn Bridge Park’s first section, Pier 1, opened at the foot of Old Fulton Street in March 2010, and the park says the refurbished Fulton Ferry Landing pier reopened in 1997. In November 2023 the Brooklyn Eagle reported the 85-acre park was celebrating nearly five million visitors that year. NYC Ferry boats call at the slip just north of the old landing, according to Wikipedia. Many of those visitors walk down Old Fulton Street past its restaurants; Wikipedia names Grimaldi’s Pizzeria among the area’s attractions. A street that busy at noon is empty in the small hours, and that gap shows up in the police blotter: in October 2022 Brooklyn Paper reported that two Old Fulton Street restaurants between Water and Front Streets had their front doors shattered on the same night, one of them at about 4:30 a.m., with cash taken from a register and from a wall safe that was damaged in the process.

So a Fulton Ferry alarm answers three questions most Brooklyn neighborhoods never raise together: where the system’s brain can live so a surge does not drown it, how to protect a loft built for warehousing, and how to make a glass-fronted shop impossible to enter quietly at night. Our DUMBO alarm page and Brooklyn Heights alarm page cover the neighbors on either side.

Most Fulton Ferry loft owners pair the alarm with a camera at their own door, and most shops add one at the register. For those, see security camera installation in Fulton Ferry; for the shared street door of a small co-op, Fulton Ferry intercom installation.

Trouble Light After a Storm, a Keypad That Lost Its Signal, or a Shop That Will Not Arm: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Fulton Ferry

Near the river, most alarm repair calls follow the weather. After a heavy rain or a coastal storm, a panel that shows “AC loss,” “low battery” or “communication failure” usually means one of three things: the building lost power long enough to drain the backup battery, water or damp reached a cellar junction where the internet or telephone line enters, or a sensor near a door or window on the lowest floor took moisture and now reads as open. A panel that chirps every minute in a loft is most often a sensor battery or the backup battery itself. A storefront alarm that will not arm at closing usually has one door that does not quite meet its contact, a motion detector seeing a moving sign or a fan, or a rear door that someone propped open during deliveries.

Before you ring (347) 934-8335, write down three things: the maker’s name printed on the keypad, the exact message on its screen or in the app, and how high any water came. That usually lets us bring the right part on the first trip. A siren left running should cut out on its own, because New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says a building’s audible burglar alarm must be able to, and must, shut off within fifteen minutes of starting; one that runs past that is a same-day job. If a panel has been under water and is not worth saving, you hear that before anything is billed, and nothing below the flood mark is re-armed until it has been tested.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Fulton Ferry’s Architecture

Fulton Ferry has almost no buildings that were built to be lived in the way they are lived in now. Nearly every property is a commercial or industrial building of the 19th century that has been converted, and each type of conversion leaves its own set of openings and weaknesses for an alarm to deal with.

The converted storage warehouse

The Eagle Warehouse is the model: an eight-story brick storage building of 1893–94 turned into 85 condominiums in 1980. Units like these have big window openings set deep in thick walls, high ceilings, and sometimes a mezzanine or a roof door. Upper-floor windows are rarely the way in; the unit door, the roof and the lowest floors are.

The small co-op in former offices

The 1861 Brooklyn City Railroad building became a co-op in 1975, five stories and 11 units by StreetEasy’s count. A building this small shares one street door, one stair and one cellar, so the alarm question is as much about who comes and goes through common space as about each apartment’s windows.

The commercial row with apartments above

The two- to four-story brick buildings along the old Fulton Street, described in the 1977 designation report, now hold restaurants and shops at street level with apartments or offices upstairs. The ground floor has glass fronts and a cellar; the upper floors have rear windows on light wells and, in places, roof access over a neighbor.

The long waterfront warehouse

Empire Stores on Water Street is seven connected buildings from 1869 with 1885 additions, five stories, about 330,000 square feet of offices, retail and a food hall since its reopening. Tenants there install suite alarms inside a large managed building, not stand-alone systems.

The venue in a warehouse shell

The Tobacco Inspection Warehouse, which the 1977 report notes had been cut down from five stories to two, became St. Ann’s Warehouse theater in fall 2015, designed by Marvel Architects. Venues mix public evenings, empty days and back-of-house storage of valuable equipment.

Ground floors and cellars at river level

Every building near Water and Furman Streets has a lowest level that the 2012 surge showed can take water. These are where meters, internet and telephone service, sump pumps and old alarm panels tend to be, and they are the first thing we look at on a survey.

Methods by building type

  • Deep masonry window openings: contacts go on the sash or on interior shutters that close at night, not on the brick reveal; where sashes are original or the board limits drilling, adhesive-mounted wireless contacts are used.
  • Tall rooms and mezzanines: motion detectors are mounted at the height their maker specifies, which is lower than the ceiling, and a second detector covers the mezzanine instead of asking one sensor to see both levels.
  • Timber, iron and brick: anchors go into mortar joints and plaster, not into decorative ironwork or original timber; cables, where any are needed, run inside existing chases or along beams with removable fasteners.
  • Roof doors and hatches: a heavy-duty contact on the hatch or roof door, with a delay programmed so a resident going up to the roof is not mistaken for an intruder coming down.
  • Cellars: water sensors on the floor, nothing else important below the high-water line, and any wiring that has to cross a cellar run high and sealed.

Designing for the Water: Where an Alarm Belongs in a Building That Has Flooded

Most alarm advice assumes the basement is a good place for the panel: out of sight, near the electrical service, close to where the telephone line came in. At the foot of Old Fulton Street that assumption is what fails first. When the River Café took three to four feet of water in 2012, the building beside the landing lost its kitchen in a single evening tide. A panel in a cellar on Water Street would have been under water within the same hour, and with it the battery that is supposed to keep the alarm running when the power goes out, the communicator that is supposed to report the problem, and the siren driver. The alarm would have gone silent at precisely the moment the building was most exposed: power off, doors swollen or forced by water, and owners kept away by the storm.

Our rule here is simple. The parts that make decisions and send messages go high; only the parts that have to sense something low stay low. In a loft that means the panel and its battery sit inside the unit on an upper floor, which is where most loft owners want them anyway. In a restaurant or shop with a cellar, the panel goes on the ground floor, mounted high on an inside wall, often in the office or behind the bar, and the cellar gets only water sensors and, if the cellar has its own street hatch or door, a contact on that opening. Where an existing system’s panel lives in the cellar, moving it upstairs is usually the single most valuable change we can make, and in a wired system the old cable runs can often be extended rather than replaced.

The second rule is about the message path. In older buildings the internet service, the telephone lines and sometimes the cable television all enter through the cellar. If water reaches that entrance, an alarm that reports only over the internet goes deaf. We specify a cellular communicator as the main path for every Fulton Ferry system we install, mounted with the panel above the water, and we add the internet as a second path only where the owner wants faster video or app performance. Cellular signal on the lower floors of thick-walled brick buildings near the bridge is not always strong, so the communicator’s position is chosen with a signal meter, never by guesswork.

The third rule is about power. A panel’s backup battery is sized to run the system for a period after mains power fails; the actual hours depend on the panel, the number of wireless devices and whether a siren sounds. We tell you what your specific system is rated for, set the panel to report a power loss to your phone within minutes, and set a second alert for low battery so you know how much time remains. Owners who leave a loft empty for weeks often ask for a small battery-backed router or a larger battery where the panel allows it, so that cameras and app access last as long as the alarm does.

Water sensors themselves need thought. A sensor on the cellar floor tells you water has arrived; a second one a few inches up a wall, or a float-type sensor beside a sump pit, tells you it is rising. Under a kitchen sink or beside a water heater, a flat sensor catches a leak long before it becomes a ceiling stain downstairs, which matters in a co-op or condo where your leak is your neighbor’s problem. A temperature sensor in a seldom-heated back room or cellar warns of a freeze before a pipe splits. Each of these alerts can go to a different list of people: the owner always, the superintendent or managing agent for anything in common areas, and the central station only if you want a call at three in the morning.

None of this replaces flood insurance, sandbags, a sump pump or the building’s own resilience work, and we do not pretend it does. What a flood-aware alarm does is keep reporting through the event, tell you when the water arrives and when the power drops, and come back up afterwards without a technician having to dry out the brain of the system. After any flood or major leak we test every device below the high-water mark before the system is put back into service, and we replace any contact or sensor that took water rather than trust it.

Inside a Converted Loft: Mezzanines, Deep Windows and the Roof

A loft in a former warehouse is mostly one large room, sometimes with a sleeping level built over part of it, and that shape changes how detection works. A passive infrared motion detector looks for warm bodies moving across zones fanned out from its lens. Its makers specify a mounting height, usually somewhere around seven to eight feet, and a coverage pattern that assumes an ordinary ceiling. Mount it at the top of a fourteen-foot wall and it sees the floor badly; aim it up at a mezzanine and it sees the ceiling instead of the stair. So in a loft we usually put one detector at the specified height covering the main floor and the path from the door, and a second one on the mezzanine covering the stair head and the sleeping area, with that second one set to stay off when the household is home at night.

Windows in warehouse conversions are a different problem from windows in a brownstone. They tend to be large, set deep in thick brick walls, sometimes in arched openings, and sometimes fitted with interior shutters or modern secondary glazing behind the old frames. On upper floors facing the street, they are rarely an entry route, and we say so rather than sell a contact for each one. On the lowest residential floor, on light wells, beside fire escapes, and anywhere a neighboring roof comes close, they matter. There, a contact goes on whatever actually closes at night, the sash or the shutter, and a glass-break detector covers a wall of panes from inside the room. Adhesive-mounted wireless contacts avoid drilling into old sashes or frames that a condo board or the owner wants left untouched.

The roof is the opening owners forget. Top-floor units in converted warehouses and small commercial buildings often have a roof hatch, a bulkhead door or a private roof deck, and in a row of attached buildings the roofs can connect. A roof door is one of the few openings in a loft that an intruder can reach without being seen from the street. It gets a heavy-duty contact and, if it opens onto the unit directly, motion coverage at the foot of the stair. Where the roof is shared with other residents, we put the contact on your side of your own door, not on the building’s bulkhead, so your alarm does not trip every time a neighbor goes up to look at the bridge.

Unit doors in these buildings are usually solid and set in masonry, and many open onto a stair or a small landing rather than a long corridor. A contact on the door starts the entry delay; a motion detector inside the door catches anyone who opens it with a key or comes in some other way. In a small co-op where one street door serves a handful of apartments, the shared door belongs to the building and is a matter for the board and the intercom, not your alarm, but your own door, your cellar storage cage and your roof access are yours to protect.

Old Fulton Street at 4:30 in the Morning: Protecting Glass Fronts on a Visitor Street

The Brooklyn Paper blotter for October 2022 describes a pattern every shopkeeper on the street should read. On the night of September 28, the owners of two restaurants on Old Fulton Street between Water and Front Streets found their front doors shattered. Security cameras at one of them showed two men yanking the front door open at about 4:30 a.m., taking cash from the register and from a wall safe that was damaged in the process, and leaving; minutes later cameras at the second restaurant recorded thieves breaking in for cash. Cameras recorded both break-ins. Neither recording stopped them. A camera tells you afterwards what happened; an alarm is what makes the first seconds after the glass breaks loud, reported and short.

For a storefront like those, we start with the doors. Every exterior door gets a contact, including the rear or side door used for deliveries and the trash. The front glass gets an acoustic glass-break detector inside, aimed at the panes, because a thief who smashes a pane and reaches through may never open the door enough to trip a contact. A motion detector covers the dining room or sales floor, and a second covers the path to the office, the register and the safe. The safe, or the closet it stands in, gets its own sensor and can be armed separately, so that even if the main alarm is somehow bypassed, opening or moving the safe raises its own signal. Cash handling at closing, with the register emptied and the drawer left open, is the cheapest security measure of all, and we mention it on every visit.

Speed matters more than volume. A loud interior siren makes staying inside unpleasant, but on a street where few people are awake at 4:30 a.m. the important thing is that the signal reaches someone at once: the owner’s phone, a manager’s phone and, if the business chooses, a central station that can call the police. We set the entry delay on a shop’s front door as short as the staff can live with, place the keypad where it cannot be seen from the street, and use individual codes so the report says which employee armed or disarmed and when. A late-arming alert tells the owner if nobody armed the shop by a set time.

Crowd Days: Five Million Visitors and an Alarm That Ignores Them

Few residential blocks in New York see the foot traffic Fulton Ferry does. Brooklyn Bridge Park’s Pier 1 begins at the foot of Old Fulton Street, the park calls Fulton Ferry Landing one of Brooklyn’s most historic sites, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported nearly five million park visitors in 2023. A Reddit thread about the city’s fireworks ticket lottery has a lifelong New Yorker recalling walking into the park from Old Fulton and Furman at 7 p.m. in years past, before access was limited. On summer weekends and event nights, the sidewalks under your windows are full until late.

That has practical consequences for alarm design. Outdoor motion detectors, beams and floodlight sensors, which make sense in a suburban yard, are almost useless facing a public sidewalk here; they would trip every few seconds. Shock sensors on storefront glass can be set off by a visitor leaning on the window for a photograph of the bridge. Acoustic glass-break detectors, interior motion detectors and door contacts, all of which ignore what happens on the far side of the glass, are the right tools. Where an owner wants outdoor detection at a private rear yard, roof deck or alley gate, we use devices that can be masked to that space alone.

Rules and compliance points for Fulton Ferry businesses

  • Noise cut-off. A restaurant’s siren that wails until morning is not only a nuisance to the apartments upstairs; the City’s Administrative Code (§ 24-238) caps a building burglar alarm’s audible signal at fifteen minutes. We set sounders to time out sooner.
  • Who may install. Alarm installers in New York are licensed by the Department of State, which lists fingerprinting, an 81-hour qualifying course and a state exam among its requirements. A restaurant owner or condo board is entitled to see the number first; AESS works under #12000287431.
  • Landmark district. Fulton Ferry is a city historic district. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s permit guidebook says health, safety and utility equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no alternative, should be as small and discreet as possible, and allows small buildings no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage. Interior alarm devices are not exterior work; anything we would mount outside, we raise with you and the owner first.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A restaurant’s or building’s fire alarm and any sprinkler monitoring belong to the building owner and follow their own codes. Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Fulton Ferry: Questions Answered

These are the questions loft owners, shopkeepers, restaurant managers and office tenants at the landing ask once we are standing in the space. They are grouped by subject. Shorter answers about cost, scheduling and practical details are in the FAQ further down.

Water, storms and the river

Our internet and phone lines come into the building through a cellar that flooded in 2012. If the cellar floods again, does the alarm go deaf?

It does if the alarm reports only over those lines. That is why every system we install near the landing uses a cellular communicator as its main path, mounted with the panel well above the cellar. When the internet entrance goes under, the panel keeps reporting over the cellular network, as long as it has power from the mains or its backup battery.

If you also want the internet path, for faster app response or camera video, we add it as a second route rather than the only one. The panel can then report a loss of either path as a trouble condition, which is itself useful: an alert that the internet path has dropped during a storm is often the first sign that water has reached the cellar.

When a coastal flood warning goes out for the East River waterfront, can we put the alarm into a storm setting ahead of time?

Yes. Most current panels let us build a custom arming mode or scene. For a storm we usually set one that keeps the door and window protection armed, raises the priority of water, temperature and power alerts, adds the superintendent or a neighbor to the notification list, and turns off motion detectors in a cellar or ground floor where staff might need to move pumps or stock.

You switch it on from the app or the keypad when the warning goes out and switch back afterwards.

After a flood, the panel shows a long list of troubles. What should we do before we arm it again?

Do not clear the troubles and re-arm straight away. Water can leave a contact reading closed when it is corroded, a sensor reporting normally when its battery compartment is wet, or a siren that will fail the next time it is needed. Note what the panel lists, take photographs of the water line, and call us.

On the visit we test every device below the high-water mark, replace anything that took water, check the backup battery’s condition after a deep discharge, confirm the communicator reaches the monitoring station and your phone, and only then put the system back in service. Your insurer may want the written test report; we provide one.

Salt air off the river rusts everything outside. Will a contact on our steel roll-down gate last?

An ordinary indoor contact will not last long on a gate exposed to weather and salt. Roll-down gates get a heavy-duty overhead-door contact, usually mounted on the floor or the guide rail with a sealed or armored cable, and the wiring runs inside to a protected point as quickly as possible.

Lofts in converted warehouses

Our loft has a sleeping mezzanine open to the living space below. Can one motion detector cover both levels?

Rarely well. A motion detector is designed to be mounted at a specific height and to look across a room, not up into a second level. Mounted high enough to see the mezzanine, it sees the main floor poorly; aimed at the main floor, it misses the stair and the sleeping area.

The usual answer is two detectors: one covering the main floor and the path from the entry door, and one on the mezzanine covering the top of the stair. The mezzanine detector is programmed to stay off when you arm for the night, so you can sleep up there with the downstairs protected.

Our windows sit deep in thick brick walls, and we close interior wooden shutters at night. Where does the sensor go?

On whatever closes. If the shutters are what you secure at night and the windows behind them are fixed or rarely opened, a contact on each shutter pair tells the alarm when they are opened. If the sashes open and the shutters are decorative, the contact goes on the sash.

Where shutters cover several panes and a thief might break glass without moving them, an acoustic glass-break detector in the room covers all the glass at once. On upper floors facing the street we often recommend neither, because those windows are not a realistic way in, and the money is better spent on the door and the roof.

Our top-floor unit opens onto a roof that other residents use too. Should the roof door be on our alarm?

Your door to the roof should be, but not the building’s shared bulkhead or hatch. Putting a contact on a door your neighbors use every evening would make your alarm trip constantly and would mean altering common property.

We protect the opening between the roof and your unit: a heavy-duty contact on your roof door or private hatch, and a motion detector at the foot of the stair inside. If the roof deck itself is private and reachable from neighboring roofs, outdoor detection masked to the deck is possible, but most owners find the door and stair protection enough.

The loft is a second home and sits empty for weeks. How do we know the panel itself has not failed?

We program a regular check-in, often called a supervisory or test signal, that the panel sends on a schedule. If the monitoring station or the app stops receiving it, that silence becomes an alert. Combined with power-loss and low-battery alerts, it means a dead panel cannot hide.

The app also shows, at a glance, whether the system is armed, whether any door or window was left open, the temperature where a sensor reports it, and the battery state. Many second-home owners add one indoor camera that records only while the alarm is armed, so that an alert can be checked from anywhere before anyone is sent.

Shops and restaurants on Old Fulton Street

In 2022 two restaurants on our block had their glass doors smashed at about 4:30 a.m. and lost cash from a register and a wall safe in minutes. What would an alarm have changed?

It would have made those minutes loud and reported. A glass-break detector inside the front window reacts to the sound of breaking glass before anyone reaches the register; a contact on the door confirms it opened; a motion detector on the path to the register and a sensor on the safe add proof that someone is inside. The owner’s phone, and a central station if the business chooses one, would know within seconds.

An alarm does not make glass unbreakable, and a determined thief may still grab something. But thieves working a street at that hour count on silence and on nobody knowing until morning. A sounding siren, an immediate alert and a register emptied at closing take away most of what makes a quick smash-and-grab worth trying.

Our staff changes every season with the visitor calendar. How do we give out and take back alarm codes for summer hires?

Each employee gets a personal code, not a shared one, and codes can be given start and end dates. A summer hire’s code can stop working on the date their season ends without anyone remembering to delete it. Managers can add or remove codes from the app.

Because codes are personal, the event log shows who opened and who closed. That makes it simple to see whether a late closing or an unarmed night was a one-off or a habit, without accusing the whole staff.

Yes, by dividing the shop into areas. The delivery door and a small entry space or cellar landing can be a separate area with its own schedule or its own code for the supplier, while the dining room, the bar, the office and the safe stay armed until staff arrive.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find the Water Line Before You Pick the Wall

This describes how we survey ground floors near the river, not a story about any particular building. Before a technician decides where a panel goes in a shop or ground-floor unit near the water, he looks for the water line. Sometimes the owner knows it exactly, because the 2012 mark is still remembered in inches. Sometimes it shows: a change in paint on a cellar wall, a line of staining on old plaster, newer drywall up to a certain height, replaced baseboards, rust on the bottom of a steel door frame. He asks, he looks, and he writes the height down.

Then he picks the panel spot well above that height, on an inside wall, and reads the cellular signal there before committing. If the best signal is at the front of the shop and the safest height is at the back, the communicator and the panel can be split, with the radio where the signal is and the panel where it is protected. Every water sensor goes in at the lowest point of the floor, which in an old cellar is rarely where you would guess, so he pours a little water and watches where it runs. It takes perhaps half an hour. It is the difference between an alarm that reports through a storm and one that drowns in the first one.

Do wireless sensors fail sooner near the river?
+

Not because of the river itself, but sensors in damp cellars and unheated rooms can lose battery life and corrode faster than those in a dry, heated loft. We use sealed or outdoor-rated sensors in cellars, mount them off the floor where they do not need to touch water to work, and check their battery and signal reports at each service visit. The panel warns about a weak battery weeks before it fails.
