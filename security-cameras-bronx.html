The Borough

Why a Bronx Security Camera Store Has to Know Bronx Buildings

Most of the Bronx lives in apartment buildings, and that one fact shapes what people here buy. A camera kit from a national website assumes a single-family house, a router in the living room and an attic to run wire through. A Bronx buyer is more often a super with a sixty-unit walk-up, a co-op board deciding on lobby coverage, a tenant who keeps losing packages at the mailboxes, a bodega owner on a corner of the Grand Concourse, or a wholesaler with a loading dock on the Hunts Point peninsula. Each of those needs different cameras, a different recorder and a very different cable plan.

The numbers back that up. The city's 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey counted about 562,300 housing units in the Bronx, roughly 15 percent of the city's total, and found that 44 percent of them are studios or one-bedroom apartments. The median Bronx rent in that survey was $1,280 a month, the lowest of the five boroughs. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2025 Housing Supply Report, drawing on the same survey, put the Bronx rental vacancy rate at 0.82 percent, the lowest in the city, and recorded 9,842 new housing units completed in the Bronx in 2023, more than any other borough that year.

Put plainly: buildings are full, many of them are old, and a fair number are brand new. The old ones have brick and terra-cotta walls, plaster hallways, long basement corridors and, very often, coax cable left from an older system somebody set up fifteen or twenty years ago. The new ones have finished drywall, a network closet and a developer who wants every camera on the same recorder as the intercom. A store that sells to the Bronx has to stock for both.

Fewer people are breaking down doors; more are walking off with things that are left within reach. That is a camera problem more than an alarm problem, and it is why we sell for identification at doors, counters and mailboxes rather than wide, blurry coverage of everything.

Package theft, Bronx style

In the Bronx, the package problem is split in two. In apartment buildings, boxes sit on the floor by the mailboxes or in a vestibule that the building door does not fully lock. In the house neighborhoods of the northeast and east Bronx, boxes sit on a porch or a stoop in plain view of the street. News 12 Bronx reported in December 2025 that a Pelham Gardens homeowner, Roberto Torres, recorded a man taking packages from his front porch; he had lost four that holiday season. "In less than 30 seconds, he picked up the packages, put them right in his car and drove away," Torres told the station (News 12 Bronx, Dec. 10, 2025). He had even posted a sign asking drivers to use the back gate, and the boxes still landed at the front door.

Thirty seconds and a car is the detail that matters for buying. A porch camera that only sees the porch records a person. Add a second view that reaches the curb, or one camera with enough resolution to pick out the vehicle, and the clip can show the car as well. In buildings, the equivalent is a camera that sees faces coming through the inner door rather than the tops of heads at the mailboxes.

Break-ins still happen too. News 12 Bronx reported on November 21, 2026 that a man broke into a home near Cruger and Arnow avenues in Allerton around 12:30 in the afternoon by tampering with the front door lock, and took nearly $15,000 in cash and the victim's wallet; the NYPD released a photo of the suspect and asked the public for help (News 12 Bronx, Nov. 21, 2026). A daytime entry through the front door is exactly the view a front-door camera at face height is meant to capture.

The industrial Bronx

The city recognizes five Industrial Business Zones in the Bronx: Bathgate, Eastchester, Hunts Point, Port Morris and Zerega. The largest single cluster is the Hunts Point Food Distribution Center. NYCEDC's Hunts Point Peninsula project page describes it as more than 100 public and private wholesalers, distributors and manufacturers, including the Hunts Point Terminal Produce Market, the Hunts Point Meat Market and the Fulton Fish Market, moving an estimated 4.5 billion pounds of food a year and employing 8,500 people directly. The produce market alone covers more than 100 acres with roughly 2,000 employees, and the meat market has 835,000 square feet of refrigerated space.

One line on that page explains a lot about our Bronx customers: 20 percent of the distribution center's customers are bodegas, and 49 percent are independent restaurants and cafes. The same small businesses that buy their stock in Hunts Point at four in the morning buy their cameras from us, and many of them want to watch the store from their phone while they are at the market.

Outdoor security cameras for Bronx buildings and yards

Outdoor surveillance cameras in the Bronx get mounted on a building's entrance canopy, the brick beside a courtyard gate, the corner of a fire-escape side wall (never the escape itself), a store's awning or sign band, a light pole in a parking lot, the eave of a house in Throgs Neck or the garage of a house in Riverdale. Three specs decide whether an outdoor camera survives and records what you need: its weather rating, its night picture and its lens.

The house-brand outdoor cameras we sell carry IP66 or IP67 ratings depending on the model, and night vision rated between 60 and 120 feet. IP66 means dust-tight and protected against powerful water jets; IP67 adds protection against brief immersion. For a courtyard or an alley between two walk-ups where the only light is a bare bulb over a cellar door, we usually suggest a full color night vision camera with a built-in white light, so the recording shows colors after dark and the light itself tells people the space is watched.

Winter security cameras and weatherproof security cameras for winters are not a separate product line; they are outdoor cameras with a rating and an operating temperature range that suit a Bronx January. Where a manufacturer publishes it, we tell you the range from the spec sheet. Reolink, for example, lists its RLC-810A 4K PoE camera at IP67 with an operating range of 14°F to 131°F on its product page. On the waterfront edges of the borough, from Clason Point and Soundview to Throgs Neck and City Island, the wind off the water is harder on cheap brackets than on the cameras, so we pair outdoor cameras there with sealed junction boxes and stainless hardware.

Indoor security cameras for lobbies, halls and stores

Indoor surveillance cameras are where most Bronx buildings spend most of their money: the vestibule, the lobby, the mailbox wall, the elevator landing on each floor, the stair halls, the laundry room, the boiler room door, the compactor room and the roof bulkhead. In stores they cover the door, the counter, the aisles, the cooler, the stockroom and the back exit.

Lobby security cameras and hallway security cameras in Bronx buildings are mostly small turrets or vandal-resistant domes. A hallway with a low ceiling and a long run of identical apartment doors is the hardest view in the borough: a wide lens makes every face tiny by the far end, and a narrow lens misses the near doors. The usual answer is one camera at each end of the hall, each aimed at the stair or elevator rather than at apartment doors. Stairwell security cameras go at the landing so they see people coming up and going down, and elevator security cameras go in the elevator lobby on each floor unless the elevator company agrees to a camera in the cab.

In stores, cash register security cameras and point of sale surveillance cameras go above and a little behind the register, so the drawer, the cash and the customer's hands are all in frame. Bronx bodegas with bulletproof glass at the counter need the camera on the customer side of the glass as well, because the glass throws reflections at anything behind it.

How the cameras connect: PoE, coax or WiFi

PoE security cameras run on one Cat6 cable each, which carries both power and video back to a PoE NVR or a PoE switch. For new runs, that is our default: no outlet needed at the camera and no WiFi to drop out. Wired analog cameras, sold as HD coax and marketed under names such as HD-TVI, HD-CVI or AHD, run on RG59 or Siamese coax to a DVR. In the Bronx that matters more than anywhere else we work, because so many walk-ups and elevator buildings still have coax in the hallways from an older system. If that cable tests clean, a modern HD DVR and new coax cameras give a large jump in picture quality without opening a single wall.

WiFi security cameras and wireless cameras still need power from an outlet or a battery, and their signal has to cross the building to reach the router. In a pre-war building with brick partitions and plaster on metal lath, that path is often too long. We sell WiFi cameras for a single view where cable truly cannot go, and tell you plainly when we expect it to struggle. If you want to buy PoE cameras or buy wired security cameras for a whole building, we size the switch or the recorder's PoE budget for the night-time draw of infrared and white-light cameras too.

Person detection, vehicle detection and smart search

The single most useful smart feature for Bronx buyers is person and vehicle detection, because it is what keeps a phone quiet when a bus goes by, a train passes on an elevated line or headlights sweep across a lobby door. Several of our house-brand packages list smart AI human and vehicle detection on the spec sheet, and those are the ones we recommend for anyone who wants phone alerts. Smart motion detection cameras, security cameras with person detection and security cameras with vehicle detection reduce false alarms; smart search security camera systems let a super search the night for "person" at the roof door instead of watching eight hours of empty hallway.

DVR: the digital video recorder, and why the Bronx still buys them

A digital video recorder stores video from coax cameras. Elsewhere in the city a DVR can look like old technology; in the Bronx it is often the smartest purchase on the list. Many walk-ups and elevator buildings here had analog camera systems put in years ago, and the coax from those systems is still pinned along hallway ceilings and fished down stair shafts. Modern HD DVRs record 5MP and 4K analog cameras over that same cable. Swap the cameras and the recorder, keep the wire, and the building gets a far sharper picture without a week of new cable work.

The catch is the cable itself. Coax that has been painted over, stapled flat, spliced in a boiler room or chewed in a basement may not carry a 4K signal however good the DVR is. Before we sell a DVR to reuse existing coax, we ask for photos of the old connectors. We sell 4-, 8-, 16- and 32-channel DVRs, matched to the cameras on the 4K kits.

Surveillance hard drives sized in days, not terabytes

CCTV hard drives and surveillance hard drives are built to write around the clock, which an ordinary desktop drive is not designed to do for years at a time. We sell a 4TB surveillance hard drive for small kits, an 8TB surveillance hard drive for most houses, stores and walk-ups, and 16TB surveillance storage, usually as two drives, for large recorders. The question we ask is not "how many terabytes" but "how many days": security cameras with two weeks recording and security cameras with one month recording are a drive decision, and the worked example below shows how we estimate it for a Bronx building.

PoE switches, injectors and power

A tall Bronx building often needs a PoE switch partway up, because a single cable from a basement recorder to a sixth-floor landing can run past the distance Cat6 is rated to carry. A PoE switch for security cameras on a middle floor, fed by one uplink cable, then powers the cameras above and below it. We size each switch by its total power budget, not just by its port count, because cameras with infrared or white lights draw more after dark. A security camera PoE injector powers a single camera where a whole switch is overkill. For coax systems, a CCTV power supply (a fused box feeding several cameras) or a single CCTV power adapter does the same job. A small battery backup on the recorder and the switch rides through the short power dips older buildings get.

Mounts, brackets and junction boxes for Bronx surfaces

Security camera mounts and security camera brackets have to match the surface. Glazed brick around a lobby door, rough common brick in a courtyard, terra-cotta trim on a pre-war facade, plaster ceilings in a hallway, a steel canopy over an entrance, vinyl siding on a house in Edgewater Park or Silver Beach, and a drop ceiling in a store each take a different fixing. A camera junction box, also sold as a CCTV junction box, sits behind the camera, hides the connector and the cable slack, and keeps water off the joint. Corner mounts, pole mounts, wall arms and pendant mounts for drop ceilings are all available.

Cable, connectors and baluns

For IP systems we sell Cat6 cable for security cameras, and outdoor ethernet cable with a UV- and water-resistant jacket for any run that crosses a roof, a courtyard or a yard. For coax systems we sell RG59 and Siamese cable, BNC connectors and the other CCTV connectors that go with them, and a CCTV video balun where a building has spare twisted-pair telephone wire that can carry an analog camera signal instead of new coax. A security camera extension cable rescues a camera whose lead is a few feet short of the junction box. We also stock security camera replacement parts and surveillance camera accessories: sunshades, wall plates, cable glands, lens covers and spare power leads.

Motion-only recording on quiet halls and H.265 compression on cameras that support it both stretch a drive further, and a lot of Bronx buildings record the entrance and mailboxes continuously and the upper halls on motion.

Security camera laws Bronx businesses should know

Two rules come up when a Bronx store buys cameras. The first is the city's biometric identifier law, Administrative Code §22-1201 and following. Retail stores, food and drink establishments and places of entertainment that collect biometric identifier information from customers, such as face geometry, must post a clear and conspicuous sign near every customer entrance, and may not sell, trade or otherwise profit from that information. Ordinary recording falls outside the disclosure duty when the footage is not analyzed by software that identifies people and is not shared except with law enforcement, as a Troutman Pepper summary of the law explains. A normal camera system does not trigger the sign requirement; a face-matching system can.

The second is sound. New York Penal Law §250.05 makes eavesdropping, including mechanical overhearing of a conversation, a class E felony, and §250.00 defines mechanical overhearing as recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party. That is why audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras are features we set up on purpose rather than by default: two-way talk on an outdoor deterrent camera is one thing, a microphone recording conversations at a counter is another. This is general information, not legal advice; ask an attorney about your own situation.

CCTV regulations and security camera privacy laws for homes and apartment buildings mostly come down to a simple line: record your own property and shared areas, never the inside of someone's apartment, a bathroom or a changing area, and do not aim cameras through neighbors' windows. Are security cameras legal in a Bronx building or store? Video of your own property and of areas the public can see generally is; the edges are sound, face matching and private spaces.

Which recorder suits a six-story pre-war walk-up off the Grand Concourse?

Usually a 16-channel recorder, and the type depends on the cable already in the building. Either way, size the drive for at least two weeks, put the recorder in a locked room with a battery backup, and leave a few channels spare for the roof door and the rear court that owners always add later.

Can the old coax in a Bronx apartment building carry 4K cameras?

Often it can, if the cable is in decent shape. Modern HD analog cameras and DVRs send 4K over standard RG59 coax for typical building distances, which is the whole point of buying a 4K DVR kit. The problems are physical: cable crushed under a stair tread, spliced with tape in a basement, painted over with its connectors corroded, or run past a boiler. We check photos of the old connectors before selling a DVR for reuse.

How many cameras does a Bronx elevator building lobby need?

For a typical lobby, three or four: one inside the street door looking in, so every person entering is recorded face-on; one covering the mailboxes and any package shelf; one on the elevator doors; and, in larger lobbies, one looking back toward the door from the far end. Add one in the vestibule between the two doors if the inner door is the one that locks. Each elevator landing upstairs then gets its own small turret.

Should a Co-op City resident buy a camera for the apartment door?

Ask management first. In a cooperative of Co-op City's size, hallways are common property, and a camera or video doorbell that records the corridor is the kind of thing a co-op's house rules usually address. Which cameras suit a Riverdale or Fieldston house set back from the street?

Start with the approach, not the house. On a deep, wooded lot the useful camera is the one at the driveway entrance or the front gate, where a 4K or long-range camera records the vehicle and the person before they reach the door. Then turrets at the front and side doors, a camera on the garage, and one or two at the back where slopes and trees hide the approach. Color night vision with a white light helps on darker private streets. A 16-channel NVR in the basement leaves room for outbuildings and a gate.

What camera setup reads a truck at a Hunts Point loading dock?

Two cameras per dock door work better than one. A camera at the side of the door, at about trailer height, records the trailer number and the driver; a second, higher camera looking down the dock apron records the forklift traffic and pallets coming off. Long-range 4K or varifocal cameras outside, IR or white-light night vision for early-morning arrivals, and a recorder with enough drive space to keep at least a month, because claims about missing or damaged loads often come in weeks later.

How should a Fordham Road store cover its door and sidewalk rack?

Put one camera inside the entrance at face height looking at the door, so every customer is recorded coming in, and one outside under the awning or sign band aimed along the storefront to cover the sidewalk rack and the door from the street. Then the register from behind and above, and the stockroom and rear door. On a corridor with heavy foot traffic, use person detection only for after-hours alerts; during the day the camera simply records.

Which outdoor cameras hold up on a Throgs Neck or City Island house?

IP67-rated cameras with sealed junction boxes and stainless or coated mounting hardware. The camera bodies usually handle salt air better than cheap brackets and exposed connectors do, so the junction box matters as much as the camera. Turrets under the eaves stay drier than bullets on an exposed wall. Keep the recorder upstairs rather than in a ground-floor or crawl-space spot that could take on water in a storm.

Can a Bronx bodega owner watch the store from a phone at the market?

Yes. Every recorder we sell has a phone app for live view and playback, so an owner at the produce market at five in the morning can check the counter and the door. The store needs a working internet connection at the recorder for remote viewing; recording itself continues if the connection drops. Give staff their own logins with only the views they need, and keep the owner's login separate.

How much drive space keeps a month for sixteen cameras in a Bronx building?

Is 2K or 4K better for a Bronx two-family driveway?

For a short driveway that ends at the sidewalk, a 5MP or 2K camera aimed down the driveway records a clear face and the front of a car. 4K earns its cost when the same camera also has to reach the curb across a wide frontage, or when you want to read a plate on a car parked across the street. We quote both, with the storage each needs, so you can see whether the extra detail is worth it on your own lot.

For a house-brand kit: the four cameras, the recorder with its hard drive, the power supply for the recorder, the mouse and, in kits that include one, the monitor. AI Answers vs Bronx Buildings

What AI Search Gets Wrong About Buying Security Cameras in the Bronx

Search summaries and "best camera" lists are written for a generic house in a generic suburb. These are seven things Bronx callers repeat to us after reading them, and what we find when we see their buildings.

"Old analog cameras mean you have to rewire everything"

Many summaries treat coax as obsolete and recommend tearing it out for network cable. In a Bronx walk-up where coax already runs up the stair hall to every landing, that advice can add days of work and a lot of plaster patching for no gain in picture.

HD analog cameras on a modern DVR record 5MP and 4K over existing coax. If the runs test clean, reusing them is the cheaper upgrade; if they don't, they get replaced.

The right answer is building by building, not a blanket rule, which is why we ask what is already in the walls before we sell anything.

"A video doorbell is all an apartment needs"

A doorbell camera suits a house with its own front door. In a Bronx apartment building, the package goes missing at the mailboxes or in the vestibule, two doors and a lobby away from your apartment, where your doorbell cannot see.

The cameras that matter for apartment package theft are the building's: one facing the inner door and one on the mailboxes or package shelf. That is a purchase for the owner, the board or the managing agent, and we sell to them directly.

Tenants can ask management to fix or add those cameras; in a co-op the house rules decide what a resident may mount.

"WiFi cameras work fine anywhere in the building"

A WiFi camera has to reach a router, and in a pre-war Bronx building the router is in somebody's apartment behind brick partitions, plaster on metal lath and steel stair framing. A camera on the fifth-floor landing may never get a reliable signal.

Hallway, stair and lobby cameras in multi-story buildings are cabled for that reason, either on existing coax or new Cat6 with PoE switches on the way up.

WiFi has a place for a single camera where cable truly cannot go. It is not a building system.

"Motion alerts tell you when something is wrong"

Plain motion detection on a Bronx avenue triggers on buses, headlights, passing trains on an elevated line, rain and shadows. Owners switch the alerts off within a week, and then no alert is useful.

Person and vehicle detection, with a zone drawn around the door, the gate or the roof bulkhead, cuts those false alarms sharply. That is the feature we recommend first on any camera that will send phone alerts.

Recording should still be continuous on key views; detection decides what you are told about, not what is kept.

"Cloud plans are the only way to watch from your phone"

Consumer camera brands often tie playback to a monthly plan, so summaries assume all cameras work that way.

The NVR and DVR systems we sell record locally and offer live view, playback and clip export through the recorder's own app with no monthly fee. Security cameras with remote monitoring and smartphone viewing are standard on every system.

The one thing cloud does better is surviving theft of the recorder, which is why we lock the recorder away and show owners how to export important clips.

"More cameras in the hallway means better evidence"

Lists recommend blanketing every corridor. In a long Bronx hallway, cameras aimed at apartment doors mostly record tenants coming and going, raise privacy complaints and fill the drive.

Better coverage comes from cameras where people must pass: the street and inner doors, the mailboxes, each stair landing, the elevator, the roof door and the cellar door. Someone moving through the building is recorded at each choke point.

That plan also keeps the camera count, the recorder size and the storage reasonable for a building owner.

"The cheapest kit online is the cheapest system"

An online kit's price leaves out what a Bronx building usually needs: more channels, a bigger drive, a PoE switch for the upper floors, junction boxes for outdoor positions, mounts for brick and terra-cotta, longer cable and the time to program it all.

We never compare our prices to a retail listing; we compare complete systems for the same views and the same number of days of storage.

Our Bronx office is at 460 E Fordham Rd, so deliveries and survey visits to Belmont, Fordham, University Heights, Bedford Park and Norwood start a few minutes away, and the rest of the borough is a short drive. The first thing we ask a building owner is where the recorder can live. A locked closet in the super's apartment beats the boiler room every time, because heat is what kills hard drives.

The second thing we ask is what is already pinned to the hallway ceilings. In older Bronx buildings it is often coax from a previous system, and sometimes it is good enough to carry 4K. Finding that out before buying a recorder can change the whole quote.

The line we repeat to supers most often: "Cameras on the doors people have to use, not on the doors people live behind." It keeps the evidence useful and the tenants on side.

  • Cameras and channels. Going past four cameras means an 8-channel recorder; past eight, a 16-channel one.
  • Cable situation. Reusing good coax on a DVR, running new Cat6 to an NVR, or WiFi for a single view each change the parts list.
  • Days of storage. Two weeks and one month need very different drives in a sixteen-camera building.
  • Features. Full color night vision, sirens, two-way talk, long-range or varifocal lenses, and person and vehicle detection.
  • Building height. A PoE switch on a middle floor, extra uplink cable and battery backups for tall buildings.

Affordable security cameras, cheap security cameras and budget security cameras are reasonable things to ask for, and the honest answer is that the least expensive system is the one sized correctly the first time. Our page on affordable security cameras in New York covers ways to hold the cost down without buying a kit you outgrow in a year. Premium security cameras, professional grade security cameras and commercial grade security cameras are worth it where the scene is difficult: long distances, low light, docks and lots. They are not worth it in a hallway where a 2K turret does the job.

Can a super or managing agent order cameras on behalf of a Bronx building?
+

Yes. Most of our Bronx building orders come from a super, a managing agent or the owner's office rather than the owner in person. Tell us who approves the purchase, who receives the delivery and who should get phone access, and the quote, invoice and app logins are set up that way.

Will a camera inside a Bronx elevator cab be allowed by the elevator company?
+

It depends on the elevator contractor, not on us. A camera in the cab needs a way to get video out of a moving car, either through a spare pair in the traveling cable or a wireless bridge, and the elevator company usually has to agree to it. Ask them first; we sell the cameras that fit their answer.

Do the systems you sell keep recording if a Bronx building loses its internet line?
+

Yes. The recorders write to a hard drive inside the box, so cameras keep recording with the internet down. You lose phone viewing until the line comes back, then playback of the gap works as normal. A small battery backup on the recorder and switch covers short power dips as well.

Can cameras cover the roof door and bulkhead of a Bronx walk-up?
+

Yes. A small dome inside the bulkhead looking at the roof door, and in some buildings an outdoor camera on the roof aimed back at the door, cover the route people use to get in from next door. The cable usually drops down the stair hall to the recorder.

How do I stop motion alerts from an elevated train next to my Bronx store?
+

Use person and vehicle detection instead of plain motion, and draw the detection zone around your door and sidewalk only. Passing trains, their lights and their shadows then stop triggering phone alerts, while the camera still records everything in its view.

Can a Bronx church or community center buy cameras through its board?
+

Yes. We quote to the organization, list every camera position in writing so the board can approve it, and invoice the organization rather than a member. Cameras cover entrances, halls, the office and parking, never restrooms or changing areas.

Can I add cameras to a system a previous owner left in my Bronx house?
+

Often. Send us the recorder's make and model and a photo of its back panel. If it is a working DVR or NVR with free channels, we match cameras it can record; if it is too old to support current cameras, we say so and quote a recorder that will.

Can you set up a camera for the laundry room in a Bronx building basement?
+

Yes. A laundry room camera usually goes high in a corner looking at the door and the machines, with a second view on the change or card machine if there is one. It records the shared room only, and the basement cable path back to the recorder is planned when we quote.

Can a storefront camera on a Bronx avenue avoid recording the apartments upstairs?
+

Yes. We aim the camera down at the door and sidewalk and, on recorders that support it, add privacy masks that black out any part of the picture showing windows above or across the street. The recorded image then never contains those areas.

Can you mount a camera on a Bronx fire escape?
+

We do not attach cameras or cable to fire escape ladders, landings or railings, because a fire escape has to stay clear. A camera that needs to see the fire escape goes on the wall beside it or inside the window frame, with the cable kept off the escape itself.

Can cameras help a Bronx landlord with illegal dumping at the curb?
+

They can show who dropped the furniture and when. A camera on the building front, aimed at the curb and trash area with enough resolution to read a person and a vehicle, records the event; the clip and its time stamp are what you report to the city or the police.
