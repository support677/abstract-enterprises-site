The Neighborhood

Why Mott Haven Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Stands Apart From the Building’s Own System

Mott Haven sits in the southwest corner of the Bronx, roughly between East 149th Street, the Bruckner Expressway and the Harlem River, with Port Morris running east and south of it to the water. It is one of the most rented places in New York. The NYU Furman Center puts homeownership in Mott Haven/Melrose at 6.1 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide, and counts 12,946 public housing apartments in the district — almost a quarter of all rental units. Add the tax-credit buildings, the 421-a units and the voucher holders in private buildings and you arrive at a simple fact about doors here: the person who lives behind one is very rarely the person who chose its hardware.

The second fact is how much of that hardware is new. Between 2010 and 2025 the district added 11,870 apartments in buildings of four or more units, and the Department of Buildings issued new-building permits for another 509 in 2025 alone. Along the Harlem River, Brookfield’s Bankside complex is planned as seven towers with more than 1,350 apartments; Maven at 2413 Third Avenue adds 200 more, and mid-rise rentals keep filling the Bruckner Boulevard corridor. On the public-housing side, the Betances buildings came out of a two-year tenant-in-place renovation with new lobbies and key-fob entry, and NYCHA lists Mill Brook among the Bronx developments that received large-scale security improvements. A great many Mott Haven households therefore walk through a credential they did not pick — a fob, a phone app, a concierge desk — before they reach the one door that is theirs.

That changes what a smart lock should be asked to do. It should not lean on the building’s system, because that system belongs to the owner, can change with a new managing agent or a PACT conversion, and logs every resident under rules set by the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act rather than by you. It should not touch the owner’s lock: New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant add a lock of their own, separate from the owner’s, while NYCHA’s handbook says the bottom lock stays and any added lock is the resident’s to install and maintain. And it should keep working on the mornings when the lobby system, the elevator or the building’s power does not — residents of the Mitchel Houses told News 12 this October that elevators there still break down every day, a year after the boiler-room explosion that collapsed part of one tower.

The apartment door also matters more than it looks on a lobby camera. In November 2025 police said three men followed a 63-year-old man into his apartment in a NYCHA building on East 143rd Street at about six in the morning, tied him up and robbed him. No lock stops someone already pushing through behind you, but a door that relocks itself the moment it closes, a bolt that can be thrown from inside in one motion and a code nobody watched you type all narrow the gap. The 40th Precinct’s own figures put burglaries at 287 for 2026 through mid-October, down from 342 at the same point last year — falling, but still a precinct where the inside door earns its keep.

Then there are the doors nobody else controls. Alexander Avenue’s brownstones, the Queen Anne rows of the Bertine Block and the 1883 houses of East 139th and 140th Streets sit inside three historic districts, where the Landmarks Preservation Commission cares what happens at the door opening. Port Morris has lofts carved out of piano factories, live-work units off freight corridors and workshops with roll-up doors. Those owners have the opposite problem from the tower tenant: every decision is theirs, and so is every mistake. This page is organised around that split — whose door it is, what the building already runs, and what is left for your own lock to do.

What we look at on a Mott Haven door before we quote

  • Whose lock is already there. NYCHA’s bottom lock, the developer’s lever, an owner’s deadbolt or nothing at all. The existing hardware almost always stays; we work out where an added lock fits without crowding it.
  • The door’s construction and label. Hollow-metal and kalamein doors in towers and public housing, solid wood in the rowhouses, steel with heavy stiles in the lofts. Labelled fire doors in new buildings get a deadbolt only where the door’s preparation and the building allow it.
  • How the door closes. Self-closers are required on apartment doors in buildings of three or more units. If the closer is weak or the door binds, a motorised bolt will not throw cleanly, so we check closing speed and latching before anything else is fitted.
  • The way out. One motion from inside, no key needed, no double-cylinder deadbolt on an apartment door — the city and HUD’s inspection standards agree on that, and so do we.
  • The building’s entry system. Fob, app, intercom, concierge or nothing. We note it so your lock stays independent of it, and so you know what the building records about your arrivals and what your own lock records.
  • Signal at the door. Concrete towers and steel doors weaken Bluetooth and Wi-Fi; the manufacturer guidance for one popular bridge is to keep it within 10–15 feet of the lock. We test before choosing between Wi-Fi, a bridge or a keypad-first lock.
  • Paperwork. Written consent where the lease, NYCHA or a PACT manager requires it, and a short written scope you can hand to them describing exactly what goes on the door and what comes off.

Compliance callouts for Mott Haven business doors

  • Free egress first. Any electrified lock on an exit door must let people out without a key, tool or special knowledge. We pair strikes with a push-to-exit button or a lever that always opens from inside, and choose fail-safe or fail-secure hardware with you based on what the door protects.
  • Shared street doors. Where a shop and the apartments above share an entrance in a building of eight or more units built or converted after 1968, the Multiple Dwelling Law expects that door to self-close and self-lock with an intercom release from each apartment. Our keypad never replaces those functions; it is added to them.
  • Roll-down gates. City Council voted in July 2026 to repeal the deadline for replacing solid gates with see-through ones; DOB permit rules for new gates still apply. A gate is not a lock, so the door behind it still needs one.
  • Accessible hardware. Operable parts between 34 and 48 inches above the floor and no tight grasping or twisting, which in practice means a lever, large keypad buttons or a reader rather than a small thumb-turn.
  • Landmarked storefronts. Inside the historic districts, intercom panels and doorbells may be approved at staff level if they sit within or beside the door opening and are discreet; plan readers and keypads the same way.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Mott Haven: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come out of Mott Haven’s particular mix of buildings — new towers with lottery leases, NYCHA and PACT homes, voucher-assisted rentals and Port Morris workspaces. Questions every New York renter asks, such as the general right to add a lock or what the city says about double-cylinder deadbolts, are answered on our New York City smart lock page and the Bronx smart lock installation page; here we deal with what is different on these blocks.

New towers on the river and along Third Avenue

Our affordable lottery apartment sits on the same floor as market-rate units — does the lottery lease change what we may add to the door?

The door is the same, and in most cases so is the starting point in law. New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law gives every tenant of a multiple dwelling the right to install a lock of their own in the entrance door, separate from the owner’s, provided the landlord gets a duplicate key on request, and it voids any lease clause that charges extra for that right. The exception is buildings under the control of a municipal housing authority. A privately owned tower with income-restricted apartments is not one of those, so a lottery tenant and a market-rate neighbour start from the same place.

Where they can differ is the paperwork that sits on top. Affordable leases often carry the programme’s own rider, and a building’s resident handbook may say how alterations are requested. Neither can remove the statutory right to an added lock, but both can sensibly govern how the work is done: notice, hours, who keeps the duplicate key. We read whatever you have been given before quoting and write the scope in a way the management office can say yes to.

What neither tenant may do is replace or disable the developer’s lock, cut into the frame without consent, or fit anything that needs a key to get out. So the practical plan is identical on both sides of the hallway: keep the developer’s lever, add a deadbolt with its own credentials above it, hand the office a key if asked, and photograph both faces of the door on the day.

Our new apartment door has only a lever that locks itself when it shuts — is that enough, and what can be added if it isn’t?

A lever that locks on closing is convenient, which is why developers fit them, but on its own it is a latch, not a deadbolt. The city’s Housing Maintenance Code expects the owner of a class A multiple dwelling to provide a heavy-duty latch set and a heavy-duty deadbolt on each apartment entrance door, operated by key outside and a thumb-turn inside, plus a chain guard. A spring latch can be slipped or forced more easily than a bolt that projects an inch into a strike. If your door has no deadbolt at all, that is first a conversation with the owner, because it is the owner’s obligation to supply one.

If the owner fits a deadbolt, or the door is already prepared for one, a smart deadbolt can usually go in that preparation, or a retrofit unit can sit on the inside of the owner’s deadbolt so the keyed face in the corridor is unchanged. If you are adding your own lock under the Multiple Dwelling Law, it goes in addition to whatever the owner provides, typically a few inches above the lever, with a new bore and a reinforced strike.

Either way the auto-lock setting on the smart deadbolt is what makes it worth having on a self-closing tower door: the door shuts behind you on its closer, and a few seconds later the bolt throws on its own. We set that delay at the visit, long enough to carry shopping through and short enough that the door is never left on its latch alone.

If someone pushes in behind me as I open my apartment door, can a smart lock do anything — or is that down to habits?

It is mostly habits, and it is worth being honest about that. The home invasion on East 143rd Street in November 2025, in which three men followed a 63-year-old resident into his apartment at around six in the morning, is the kind of crime no lock prevents once the door is already open. What a well-chosen smart lock can do is shorten the moment you stand at the door with your back to the corridor and make sure the door is bolted as soon as it closes behind you.

Practically, that means a credential that opens on the first try — a fingerprint or a phone tap rather than fumbling two keys — and auto-lock so the bolt throws without anyone remembering to turn it. Notifications can tell a family member each time the door is unlocked, so an unexpected opening at an odd hour gets noticed. And if you ever suspect someone watched you type your code, change it from the app the same day.

The habits are the part that matter most: look down the corridor before you open, do not hold the lobby door for people you do not know, and step in and close the door before you take your hands off it. If the building’s street door is not relocking, report it, because under the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Housing Maintenance Code that door is the owner’s to keep working.

NYCHA, PACT and voucher homes

Our development converted to PACT and a private company runs it now — does NYCHA’s bottom-lock rule still apply to our door?

Not automatically, and not automatically gone either. NYCHA’s own description of PACT is that after conversion it continues to own the land and buildings, administers the Section 8 subsidy and monitors conditions, while a private partner renovates and manages the development and enforces house rules. Residents sign a new PACT Section 8 lease, which NYCHA describes as similar to the public housing lease. The bottom-lock rule comes from NYCHA’s public housing handbook; after conversion, the PACT lease and the partner’s house rules are what you need to read.

There is a genuine legal grey area here that we will not paper over. The Multiple Dwelling Law’s right to add your own lock excludes buildings under the supervision and control of a municipal housing authority, and NYCHA still owns PACT buildings. Whether that exclusion still applies after conversion is a question for the management office and, if it matters to you, your tenant association or a tenant lawyer — not for an installer. What we can do is ask the manager in writing, with a one-page scope, and go ahead only on a written yes.

Ask, too, what the renovation scope includes. NYCHA says PACT partners renovate every apartment and carry out security upgrades; if a new apartment door or new lock is coming to yours, waiting until it is hung is usually wiser than boring the old one.

We pay rent with a housing choice voucher — could an added smart lock cause a problem at the unit inspection?

Not if it is fitted properly, and it can help. HUD’s NSPIRE inspection standards redefine the older Housing Quality Standards used for voucher units. NSPIRE treats an entry door that cannot be secured by at least one installed lock as a deficiency, and its egress standard says a deadbolt that needs a key from the inside is not acceptable on a door that serves as a way out.

So two things matter. The lock must open from inside with a thumb-turn or lever, no key and no code — every smart deadbolt we fit does. And the owner’s lock must still work, because the inspection looks at the unit as a whole and NSPIRE expects property management to put right a door that cannot be secured.

Tell your landlord about the added lock and give them a duplicate key if they ask, as the Multiple Dwelling Law requires, so nobody arrives for an inspection to find a door they cannot open. If the inspector needs entry when you are out, a temporary code that expires the same evening is easier than leaving a key with a neighbour.

Port Morris lofts, studios and workshops

Our live-work loft opens off a freight corridor that trucks and workshop staff use all day — what kind of lock suits that unit door?

Something built for abuse rather than for a hallway carpet. Converted factory corridors in Port Morris see pallet jacks, carts and doors swinging into each other, and a residential keypad deadbolt with a plastic interior housing is not designed for that. Commercial-grade keypad deadbolts and mortise-style electronic locks with a steel chassis, or a reader on the frame driving an electric strike, stand up far better.

The frame decides a lot. Steel frames in older industrial buildings are often out of square after decades of building movement; a strike that does not line up will make any electronic lock jam. We check the reveal, shim or re-cut the strike, and adjust the closer so the door latches every time before the lock goes on.

Because these buildings mix homes and businesses under the 1997 and 2005 mixed-use rezonings, the corridor itself usually belongs to the landlord, and so does the fire-safety plan for it. Anything mounted on the corridor face or the frame should be cleared with the building first, and the unit door must still open from inside in one motion.

Our Port Morris studio’s main entrance is a roll-up door — can it be locked electronically, or should the smart lock go somewhere else?

Usually somewhere else. Coiling roll-up doors are locked with slide bolts, padlocks or the operator’s own brake, and the electronic options are built into the door operator rather than added as a lock. Turning a roll-up into a daily keyless entrance means motor, safety edges, photo-eyes and the manufacturer’s control board — a door-operator job, not a smart lock job.

The cleaner plan is to keep the roll-up locked from inside and put the smart lock or reader on the pedestrian door beside it, or on an interior door if the roll-up is the only opening. Staff and visitors then use one controlled entrance with a log, and the roll-up opens only for deliveries.

If there is no pedestrian door, a man door cut into the wall or the roll-up itself is a building alteration that needs the landlord and, depending on the work, a DOB filing. We will tell you that up front rather than sell a lock that cannot do the job.

What the local numbers and rules tell us

Burglary in the 40th Precinct is falling — and where it still happens

NYPD CompStat for the week ending October 11, 2026 shows 287 burglaries in the 40th Precinct so far this year, against 342 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 16.1 percent. The precinct’s full-year 2025 total was 442, a long way below the 1,374 recorded in 1990. Crimes in public housing, which CompStat counts separately, stand at 360 for the year against 406 last year.

Falling numbers do not change the logic of an apartment door. A burglary needs a door that is unlocked, a lock that can be slipped or a frame that gives. A deadbolt that throws itself every time, a strike set into solid framing with long screws and a door that closes properly on its closer address all three.

A state smart-access bill keeps coming back — and it is not law yet

Senate Bill S955, sponsored by Senator Brian Kavanagh, would require building smart-access systems in New York to offer a fob, card, digital key or passcode rather than an app alone, give residents keys at no cost, and keep backup power on entry doors. It passed the Senate in May 2025, died in the Assembly, and was advanced again in the Senate in May 2026; as of this page’s review it remains in committee.

Until something like it passes, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act is the rule that applies to building systems: owners using smart access in multiple dwellings must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, get express consent and collect only the minimum data. That law governs the building’s system. Your own lock on your own door is yours, and its records stay in your account.

The Landmarks rules at a rowhouse door

In the Mott Haven, Mott Haven East and Bertine Block districts, the Landmarks Preservation Commission expects historic doors, transoms and surrounds to be kept. Its guidebook says ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of safety equipment needs no permit, and that staff can approve doorbells and intercom panels within or beside the door opening, one intercom panel per opening, if they are neutral and discreet.

Who Lives Behind the Door

Planning a Lock for Mott Haven Households of One, Older Residents and Big Families

The Furman Center counts 39.2 percent of Mott Haven/Melrose households as one person living alone, and 14.4 percent of residents as aged 65 or over, up from 7.5 percent in 2000. Those two numbers change how we set up a lock more than any feature list does.

Living alone. The single biggest risk with a smart lock is a household with one administrator and one phone. If that phone is lost on the train, or the person is taken ill, nobody can change a code or let help in. We set a second administrator — a relative, a close friend, a neighbour you trust — and make sure the mechanical key is somewhere outside the apartment, not in a kitchen drawer behind the locked door.

Older residents. Large, backlit keypad buttons, a lever rather than a small knob, and no dependence on an app are the usual choices. Big and changing households. Where several generations or visiting relatives share one apartment, each person gets their own code so the history shows who used the door, and codes for anyone staying a short while are given an end date the day they are created rather than forgotten later.

Children. A child’s own code, rather than a key on a string, means a lost key does not force a cylinder change, and a parent can see when the door opened after school. We show children how the inside thumb-turn works so they can always get out, whatever the lock is doing.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Mott Haven

Search assistants and cost guides answer smart lock questions as if every reader owns a detached house. We could not capture a live AI Overview for Mott Haven searches from our build environment, so each heading below is a claim we regularly see repeated in published cost guides, retailer pages and generic advice, followed by what actually happens on these blocks.

“Just swap your old deadbolt for a smart one”

That advice assumes the deadbolt is yours. In most Mott Haven apartments it is not. NYCHA’s handbook says plainly that residents may not replace the bottom lock but may buy an additional one. In a private rental the owner must provide the apartment’s key lock and deadbolt under the Housing Maintenance Code, and a tenant’s statutory right under the Multiple Dwelling Law is to add a lock, not to remove the owner’s.

Swapping the owner’s lock can leave you in breach of the lease and the owner unable to meet their own legal duties. It also means the owner’s key no longer opens your door in an emergency, which is exactly what the duplicate-key rules are designed to avoid.

The version that works here is additive: the owner’s lock stays, your smart deadbolt goes above it, and a duplicate key goes to the landlord if they ask. Owners of rowhouses and lofts can swap freely, and even then we keep the old lock in case the door ever needs to be put back.

“Your building’s smart entry already covers your apartment”

New towers market themselves as access-controlled, and the phrase is accurate as far as it goes: Third at Bankside describes an “access-controlled community” and a 24/7 concierge who handles visitor coordination and packages. That controls lobbies, elevators and amenity doors. It does not, by itself, tell you anything about the lock on your apartment door.

The same is true in public housing. NYCHA’s layered access control is a building-entry system that lets staff change fob permissions remotely; the apartment door behind it still has a mechanical bottom lock. In a PACT building the fob system belongs to the private manager running the development for NYCHA.

Ask the leasing or management office one simple question: what opens my apartment door, and who else can open it? The answer usually shows that your own lock is the only part you control.

“Tenants in New York can always add their own lock”

Mostly true, with one exception that matters enormously in Mott Haven. The Multiple Dwelling Law right to add a lock does not apply to buildings under the supervision and control of a municipal housing authority. With seventeen NYCHA developments in the neighbourhood, a large share of local households live where that statute does not reach.

NYCHA residents can still add a lock, because NYCHA’s own rules allow a second one; the difference is that the permission comes from NYCHA and its alteration rules, including prior written consent from the property management office, rather than from the statute.

PACT conversions add a further layer, because the buildings stay NYCHA-owned while a private partner manages them under a new lease. That is why we ask for consent in writing in every NYCHA and PACT building, however small the job looks.

“A smart lock will make your apartment much safer”

It helps against particular failures: a door left on its latch, keys in the wrong hands, a code that needs cancelling quickly. It does nothing about a person who follows you in, a lobby door that has been propped, or a frame that splits under a kick. The November 2025 home invasion on East 143rd Street began with three men following a resident into his apartment.

The 40th Precinct’s burglary count is falling — 287 so far in 2026 against 342 at the same point in 2025 — and residents of the Mitchel Houses told News 12 this fall that people who do not live there are still getting into the buildings. Both facts point at the same priorities: a door that relocks, a strike that holds and a lobby door the owner keeps working.

So we fit the lock and then spend time on what surrounds it: long strike screws into solid framing, a closer that latches the door every time, auto-lock timed so the bolt is never forgotten, and a clear conversation about the habits no hardware replaces.

“Pick a Wi-Fi lock so you can open it from anywhere”

Remote unlocking is useful when it is reliable, and in a concrete tower or a steel public-housing door it often is not. Manufacturer guidance for one widely sold Wi-Fi bridge is to keep it within 10 to 15 feet of the lock with a strong signal at that spot. Behind a metal door in a corridor of masonry, many apartments cannot meet that without moving the router.

Remote access is also the feature most people use least. For most households, a keypad or fingerprint deadbolt that works perfectly offline, with alerts when the router is in range, gives more dependable everyday service than a lock that sulks whenever the Wi-Fi drops.

We test signal at the door before recommending anything. If it is weak, we recommend a lock that does not need it rather than promising a feature that will not hold.

“A keypad on the outside is fine on any old house”

On a house in a Mott Haven historic district the outside of the door is not just a matter of taste. The Landmarks Preservation Commission expects original doors, transoms and surrounds to be retained, and its guidebook allows staff approval for an intercom panel or doorbell only when it sits within or beside the door opening and looks neutral and discreet.

A large glossy keypad screwed into brownstone beside a 19th-century door is exactly what those rules are written to prevent. An interior retrofit, or a slim keypad deadbolt sized and finished to suit the door, avoids the problem altogether.

The Mott Haven Historic District was the first in the Bronx, designated in 1969, and its rows include some of the earliest in the borough. They are worth the extra care.

“Smart lock installation costs about $330”

LawnStarter’s national guide does put the average at $330, with most jobs between $200 and $460 and labour of $50 to $110, and Fixr’s national figure is $250 to $400. Those numbers describe swapping a deadbolt on a house door with a strong Wi-Fi signal.

Our published Bronx figures for that last job are $1,500 to $2,500 installed for a single door.
