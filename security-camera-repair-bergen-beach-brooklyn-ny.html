The Neighborhood

Why Bergen Beach Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Bergen Beach is a house neighborhood with a car in nearly every driveway, and that is what most of its cameras are pointed at. Listings here read the same way again and again: a brick two-family on East 68th Street with an owner's triplex, a separate rental with its own entrance, an interior garage and a private driveway for two more cars; a custom two-family on Bergen Court with a three-car driveway, a garage, a porch and a terrace; a single-family with a full walk-out basement. Cameras on those houses watch the driveway, the garage door, the side path to the yard and the front steps where deliveries land. When one goes dark, the owner usually notices because of the car, not the camera, and the call often comes the morning after something happened at the curb.

The local police say the same thing about where the risk sits. The neighborhood falls in the NYPD 63rd Precinct, whose station house on Brooklyn Avenue serves Community Board 18, and in the precinct's Sector A, which covers Mill Island, Mill Basin and Bergen Beach. At a Build the Block meeting at Temple Sholom on East 68th Street in March 2023, about 25 residents came, and the Canarsie Courier reported the officers' summary bluntly: "Car thefts were one of the top crimes in the sector and precinct this past month." The officers tied many of them to cars left running or with a key fob close by, and the sector's Neighborhood Coordination Officer, David Belkin, said thefts tend to climb in May and June, with BMWs a favorite target. A year earlier, at a meeting held on Zoom, the same officer told residents to park in well-lit areas "in view of surveillance cameras if possible." That advice only helps if the camera you are parking in view of is recording, has its clock right, and shows a face at night instead of a white glare.

Residents' own footage has already done real work here. At that 2022 meeting, the Courier reported, a package thief in Bergen Beach was identified with help from neighbors who posted their camera clips to social media. That is the best case for a home camera: an ordinary system on an ordinary house that happened to be working on the day it mattered. The repair calls we take from this part of Brooklyn are mostly about the other case, a system that looked fine on the wall and in the app until someone went back to find a clip and found a gap, a frozen frame or a recorder that had quietly stopped writing to its drive weeks before.

The water is the other half of the story. Bergen Beach is low, open to the bay on one side and bracketed by Paerdegat Basin and East Mill Basin, and the National Weather Service's coastal flood impacts sheet for Rockaway Inlet lists Bergen Beach among "the hardest hit areas" when Jamaica Bay and the inlet flooded during the storm of October 29, 2012, when the gauge at Floyd Bennett Field reached 11.6 feet above mean lower low water. Ordinary rain causes trouble too. News 12 Brooklyn covered flooding in Bergen Beach after wet weather in December 2018 and again in April 2021, when residents told the station they were fed up with water that, by their account, had become worse after a street repaving. For a camera system that matters in two places: the recorder and its power, which in a house like this often sit on or near a basement floor, and the outdoor plugs and junction boxes at the cameras, which sit in the spray and the wind.

Salt air is slow, and that is why it catches people out. A camera with an outdoor weather rating keeps water out of its body, but the screws, the bracket, the cable gland and the plug at the end of a pigtail are another matter. The camera maker ACTi puts it plainly in its notes on corrosion-resistant design: "Sodium chloride and other halides are aggressive to metals, accelerating oxidation, pitting, and galvanic corrosion." On a house that faces the bay or one of the basins, that shows up first as orange streaks under a bracket, then as a camera that drops out on damp mornings and comes back by noon, and finally as a camera that stays dark. In our experience the camera body is often fine. The fault is in the few inches of connector and cable where it meets the house.

Power is the third pressure. On July 21, 2019, Con Edison reported "approximately 33,000" customer outages across "Canarsie, Flatlands, Mill Basin, Old Mill Basin, Bergen Beach, and Georgetown," which it put down to "continued heat and high usage." The utility said the next day that it had restored more than 99 percent of those customers while new thunderstorms were causing about 11,000 fresh outages elsewhere in its system. Camera systems do not like that kind of week. A recorder that loses power hard can come back with a corrupted setting, a clock reset to the factory date, or cameras that do not rejoin it, and a camera system on network cables can return with two cameras fighting over the same address. Those are not reasons to buy a new system. They are the ordinary aftermath of a blackout, and most of them are fixed in one visit.

Security Camera Repair for Bergen Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of property account for almost every Bergen Beach camera call. What separates them is not the camera on the wall, which is often the same model on each, but where the recorder ended up, how far the cables run outdoors, and how exposed the whole thing is to water and wind. Those three things decide where a technician looks first.

The detached house on a wide lot

Cameras on all four sides, often with one on a rear garage or shed. The long outdoor runs and the exposed corners are where most faults start.

The brick two-family with a rental entrance

One recorder for two households and two doors. The recorder may sit in the owner's space or in a shared basement, and the tenant may live with the cameras but not hold the login.

The house with an interior or attached garage

The garage often holds the recorder, the power box and the internet router, in a room that swings from hot to cold and sometimes floods first.

The house facing the water

Homes that look toward the bay or one of the basins take the most wind and salt. Brackets, screws and plugs on the water side wear out first.

The condo or townhouse in a gated complex

Common-area cameras belong to the association and are run by its manager, while an owner's own door camera is the owner's. Knowing which is which settles who books the repair.

The church, temple or community building

Large parking lots and side doors watched by a handful of cameras, busy at set times and empty the rest, with a recorder in an office or closet that few people visit.

The detached house: long runs and the far corner of the yard

A detached house gives cameras more to cover and cables more distance to travel. A typical system here has a camera over the front door, one on each side of the driveway, one on the back of the house looking at the yard, and one on a rear garage or shed looking back toward the house. The last one is the camera that fails most. Its cable crosses the yard, either buried, stapled along a fence or strung overhead, and every joint along that path is a place where rain gets in. When we are called to a house like this with one camera out, we start at the far end: the junction box behind the garage camera, the plug at its pigtail and the place where the cable enters the house. In our experience that is where the fault is more often than the camera itself.

The two-family: one system, two households and a shared basement

Bergen Beach has many two-family houses like the brick one on East 68th Street with an owner's triplex upstairs and a one-bedroom with its own private entrance below. Camera systems in these houses are almost always one recorder covering both doors, the driveway and the yard. The recorder is wherever the person who bought it put it, often the basement, which may now be part of the rental. That is not a technical problem, but it changes the visit. The owner holds the system and authorizes the repair; the tenant may have to be home to let us reach the recorder; and the cameras over the rental door may be the ones the tenant relies on most. We set the visit with the owner, confirm access with whoever lives beside the recorder, and keep the explanation short enough to pass on.

The house with a garage: heat, cold and the lowest floor

A garage is a tempting home for a recorder. It is out of the way, it is near the driveway cameras, and it often has the internet connection. It is also the room that gets hottest in July, coldest in January, and in a low neighborhood, sometimes wet first. A recorder that restarts on hot afternoons, a drive that throws errors after a cold snap, or a power strip that tripped after rain are all garage faults before they are camera faults. When a garage is where the recorder has to stay, we lift it onto a shelf well off the floor, give it ventilation, and put the power supply and any battery backup up there with it. When it can move into the house, we can usually extend the cables a short distance to a closet or utility space.

The house facing the water: the hardware wears before the camera does

Houses that look out over the bay, Paerdegat Basin or East Mill Basin get the full wind and the salt that comes with it. On these houses we look at the hardware first. Zinc-plated screws and painted steel brackets that came in the camera box tend to rust, and the rust runs down the wall and into the camera's cable entry. Plugs on the end of a camera's pigtail, which are often left hanging outside or stuffed into a shallow box, corrode at the pins. The fix is usually unglamorous: new stainless screws and brackets, the plug cut back to clean metal and rebuilt, a proper sealed junction box behind the camera, dielectric grease on the contacts and a drip loop so water runs off the cable instead of into it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Work Through a Bergen Beach Camera System After a Storm or a Blackout

These notes describe our standard approach to a camera repair on a house near the water, the kind most Bergen Beach calls come from. They are how our technicians work, not a story about any one address.

Ask for the date first. Before touching anything, the technician asks when the trouble started and what happened that day. A fault that began the night of a power cut is looked at differently from one that began after rain, and both differently from one that crept in over a winter.

Read the recorder before the ladder. Which channels show video, which show nothing, what date the recorder thinks it is, whether the drive is recording, and when the last good recording was made. A recorder clock showing a factory date is a sign it lost power hard and reset.

Check the floor and the plugs. Is the recorder, the power box or the battery backup on the floor? Is there a tide line on the wall or a power strip that looks damp? Water damage is checked before anything is plugged back in.

Group the dark cameras. If every camera on the water side is out, the shared part on that side, a power box, a junction or a switch port, is tested first. If one camera is out, its own plug and cable are checked at both ends before the camera is blamed.

Measure, then touch. Voltage at the camera, PoE power on the port and a continuity or analyzer test on the cable settle most questions in minutes. Nothing is reset or unplugged until any footage you asked for is saved.

Price before parts, and seal on the way out. Once the fault is found you get the cause and a written price. When the repair is done, every outdoor joint we opened is sealed again, every cable we traced is labeled, and the recorder is set to send a health alert to your phone if a camera or the drive fails.

Fault by Fault

Salt, Water, Power or Age: How Bergen Beach Camera Systems Fail and What Each Repair Involves

Every camera system is a chain: power, cable and connectors, the switch or power box, the recorder, and the camera. The symptom on the screen is usually the same whichever link breaks, a black tile, a frozen frame or a camera marked offline, so the repair starts with finding the link. In Bergen Beach the cause behind the broken link tends to be one of four things, and each one leaves its own clues.

Salt and wind: the slow fault at the connector

Corrosion builds over seasons, not days, which is why its first symptoms are easy to dismiss. A camera on the water side of the house drops out on a damp morning and is back by lunch. A picture develops lines or rolls on a windy day. A network camera reboots every so often for no clear reason. All three point at a connection that is partly eaten through: enough metal left to work when it is dry, not enough when moisture bridges the gap or the wind flexes the cable. Open the junction box behind a camera like that and the plug usually tells the story, with green or white crust on the pins and rust on the screw that held the box shut.

The repair is to cut back to clean copper, put on a new connector, protect it, and stop the water getting there again. On an analog camera that means a new BNC and power plug at the pigtail; on a network camera, a new RJ45 in a weatherproof coupler or a sealed box. Rusted brackets and screws are swapped for stainless ones so the rust does not return in a year. On our Brooklyn list a reterminate or PoE port swap runs $150 to $350. When a house has several cameras on the water side in the same state, it is cheaper to do them together in one visit than to come back for each one, and that kind of coastal multi-camera rebuild falls in the $700 to $1,500 major-repair range.

Water: the basement, the garage and the junction box in the yard

Water reaches camera systems in Bergen Beach from below as often as from above. A recorder on a basement or garage floor, a power box screwed low on a utility room wall, a power strip lying on the slab: all of them are in the path when water comes in. The recorder may survive, or it may come back with a failing drive or a power board that has started to corrode. The power box is the more common casualty, and its failure looks like several cameras dying at once.

From above, water gets into the cable entry of a camera whose seal has aged, into junction boxes that were never sealed, and along a cable that has no drip loop so water runs straight down it into the box. A camera with water inside usually fogs first, then shows a soft or spotty picture, then stops. A buried or fence-mounted run to a rear garage that fills with water at a splice causes a camera that fails after rain and recovers when the ground dries.

The repair depends on where the water got in. A wet recorder is dried, tested and, if it holds, moved up and given its own surge-protected battery backup; if its drive is failing, the drive is swapped for $200 to $450 depending on capacity. A failed power box is replaced, usually for $150 to $400, and mounted high. A camera with water inside is usually replaced on the same cable and mount for $250 to $600 including the camera, with the cable entry resealed. A failed outdoor run is replaced along the same route or a better one, and the price is written down before the work starts.

Power: blackouts, surges and the day after

Big outages like the one Con Edison reported across southeast Brooklyn in July 2019 leave a particular kind of mess. When the power comes back, the recorder boots before the cameras, the router or the switch are ready, and some cameras do not rejoin. A case posted on the IPCamTalk forum is typical: after a power cut drained the recorder's battery backup, "only 3 out of 7 cameras came back online," and the cause turned out to be network cameras fighting over the same addresses. The owner fixed it by re-adding the cameras one at a time and restarting the recorder. That is the kind of fault we clear on a visit, and it involves no new parts.

Surges are different. A lightning strike nearby or a hard reconnection of power can damage a camera, a power box, a switch port or the recorder's network board. On another IPCamTalk case, a sixteen-camera kit lost one camera after a suspected surge, and new connectors at both ends did not bring it back, which pointed at a damaged camera or port. When damage like that is spread across a system, the repair is scoped camera by camera; on our Brooklyn list, lightning-damaged camera systems range from $700 to $3,500 depending on how many devices were hit. Most outages do far less harm than that. A recorder that lost its date, a switch that forgot its settings or a camera that needs re-adding is usually covered within the $150 service call.

The best power repair is the one that prevents the next. A small battery backup on the recorder and, on network systems, the switch, lets them ride through a blink and shut down cleanly in a long outage instead of crashing. It goes on a shelf, not on the floor.

Age and wear: the drive, the clock and the night picture

Some faults are not about the bay at all. Recorder hard drives wear out, and they rarely announce it. A drive with bad sectors records in fits and starts, leaving gaps you find only when you go looking for a clip. A recorder whose small clock battery has died loses the date every time the power blinks. Infrared lights on a camera that has run every night for years get dimmer, and a camera that once showed the whole driveway after dark may now show only the first few feet. On our Brooklyn list a DVR that will not work averages $300 to $700 with the drive and labor, and night vision camera repair typically runs $250 to $650. A drive health check is part of every repair visit we make, because the drive is the part most likely to have failed without anyone noticing.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Bergen Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bergen Beach homeowners, two-family landlords, condo owners and building managers describe when they call about cameras near the water. Each is answered here once, and none of them is repeated in the FAQ further down.

Salt air, water and weather by the bay

The screws and bracket on our camera that faces the water have rusted orange. Is the camera itself still all right?

Often it is. The bracket and screws that come in a camera box are usually plated steel, and on a wall facing the bay or a basin they rust well before a weather-rated camera body lets water in. What matters is where the rust has spread. If it is only on the outside of the bracket, we swap the hardware for stainless steel and the camera carries on. If rust streaks run into the cable entry or the junction box behind the camera, we open the box, because that is where the plug and the cable live, and corroded pins there are what make a camera drop out on damp days. A camera whose picture is still clear and steady after the plug is rebuilt does not need replacing.

Our recorder sits on the basement floor, and our street floods in heavy rain. How high should it go, and can it move upstairs?

Get it off the floor first, whatever else you decide. A sturdy shelf well above the highest water you have seen on that wall, with the power box, the battery backup and any switch on the same shelf, takes most of the risk away for very little money. Moving the recorder to a closet or utility space on the floor above is also usually possible: the camera cables come together at one point in the basement, and they can be extended or rerouted from there through a single sealed hole. The cost depends on the distance and the route, and you get a written price before any cable is run. Either way, the recorder needs air around it and should not be closed into a cabinet with no ventilation.

Every February the camera under our gutter shows nothing but icicles. Is there a fix besides waiting for spring?

Yes. Icicles form where melting snow on the roof runs to the gutter edge and refreezes, and a camera mounted just under that edge sits right in the drip line. The fix is to move the camera out of the line, either a short way along the wall or a little lower, or to give it a small hood or a mount that sets it out from the fascia. In some cases the cable can stay where it is and only the bracket changes. Winter camera repairs on our Brooklyn list average $250 to $650 when a camera has to be replaced, but a relocation like this is often far less, and we price it on the visit.

One camera is pure white for the first hour of every sunny morning, then fine for the rest of the day. Is it failing?

Probably not. A camera that faces east over open ground or water takes the low sun straight into the lens for a while after sunrise, and on many cameras that washes the whole picture out. Two things help. Many cameras have a wide dynamic range or backlight setting that, once turned on and tuned, keeps the scene readable against the glare. If that is not enough, tilting the camera down a few degrees or moving it so the sky is out of the frame usually ends the problem. We check both before suggesting a different camera.

The camera on our detached garage at the back of the lot drops out whenever it rains and comes back a day later. Why?

That pattern almost always means water at a joint along the cable, not a bad camera. The run from the house to a rear garage usually has at least one splice or junction box, and if that box was never sealed, or the cable enters it from above with no drip loop, rain fills it and shorts the connection until it dries out. A run buried in the yard without the right cable or conduit can do the same thing at every low spot. We test the run end to end with a cable analyzer, open each joint, and either reseal it or replace that section. If the cable itself has failed, you get a written price for replacing the run before any digging or pulling starts.

Blackouts, generators and recorders that start over

After the long summer blackout, our recorder came back asking to be set up like a new one. Is our footage gone?

Not necessarily. A setup wizard after a hard power cut usually means the recorder lost its saved settings, not that the drive was erased. The recordings live on the hard drive; the settings live in the recorder's own memory. The mistake to avoid is clicking through the wizard and accepting a prompt to initialize or format the drive, because that does erase it. Leave it at the first screen and call us. We restore the settings, add the cameras back, check that the drive is still recognized with its old recordings intact, and then look at why it reset, which is often a failing clock battery or a power supply that did not survive the outage.

We have a standby generator, but the cameras still blink off and the recorder restarts when it kicks in. Why?

A standby generator takes a few seconds to start and switch over after the power drops, and those seconds are enough to reboot a recorder, a switch and every camera on them. Then they reboot again when the utility power returns and the generator hands back. Each hard restart is a chance for a setting to corrupt or a camera to come back on the wrong address. The answer is a small battery backup between the outlet and the recorder, and on network systems the switch, that carries them through the gap so the generator's switchover never reaches them. It is a modest part, and we can size and fit it on a repair visit.

Cars, driveways and what the camera actually caught

Our driveway camera recorded our car being taken, but the clip starts after the thief was already at the door. Why did it miss the first seconds?

Because the recorder was set to record only on motion, and motion detection takes a moment to decide something is happening. By the time it starts saving, the person who walked up the driveway is already at the car. Most recorders have a pre-record setting that keeps a few seconds of video from before the motion trigger, and it is often switched off or set to zero. We turn it on, set the motion area so it triggers at the end of the driveway rather than at the car, and check whether the drive has enough room to record the driveway camera continuously instead. Any of those changes would have caught the approach, and none of them needs new equipment.

The precinct told us to park in view of a camera. How do we know our camera actually covers the spot where the car sits overnight?

Look at a night recording, not the live view in daylight. Pull up a clip from about two in the morning and ask three things: can you see the whole car, can you see the doors on the side a person would open, and would you recognize someone standing beside it. If the car is at the edge of the frame or lost in the dark beyond the camera's infrared, the camera is not really covering it. We re-aim the camera so the car sits in the middle of the frame, add or adjust light if the infrared does not reach, and walk the driveway with you while you watch the phone, so you know what the camera sees before we leave.

Nuisance alerts and the things that live on cameras

Our phone buzzes with motion alerts all night, and every clip shows a spider web. How do you stop it?

Spiders build on cameras because the infrared lights draw insects, and a web an inch from the lens glows in the infrared and sways in every breeze, which the camera reads as motion. Someone on Reddit's home defense forum put it well: "I wiped this 2 days ago." Cleaning alone never lasts. What works is a mix: moving the light away from the lens by turning off the camera's built-in infrared and using a separate light a few feet away, which one reply in that same thread suggested, so insects gather there instead; switching from simple motion detection to person or vehicle detection if the camera supports it; and cleaning the camera and its housing with a treatment that discourages webs. We look at which of those your camera allows and set it up on the visit.

When nobody is home to check

We spend part of the winter away. How do we know the cameras are still recording while we are gone?

Turn on the recorder's health alerts before you leave. Most recorders can send a push notification or an email when a camera loses video, when the drive reports an error, or when the drive is full and not overwriting, and most owners never switch them on. With those on, a failure reaches your phone the same day instead of being discovered in March. It is also worth asking a neighbor or relative to look at the recorder screen once a week, and to check one recent clip in playback from the app yourself every few days. We set up the alerts and test them as part of any repair visit, and we can do it on its own before a trip.

How many days of video should our recorder hold, and why does ours keep only a few?

For a house, two to four weeks is a sensible target, long enough to find a clip after you get back from a trip or hear about something from a neighbor days later. A recorder that holds only a few days usually has a small drive, cameras set to their highest quality and frame rate around the clock, or a drive that is failing and has shrunk its usable space. We check the drive's health, look at each camera's settings, and tell you how many days the current drive should give you. Often lowering the frame rate on cameras that watch slow scenes, or recording the yard on motion while the driveway records continuously, doubles the days without any purchase. If a bigger drive is the answer, a swap runs $200 to $450 depending on capacity.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Bergen Beach

These are claims that come up in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Bergen Beach's houses, water and weather actually show on a repair visit.

"An outdoor-rated camera is built to handle any weather"

Many answers treat a weatherproof rating as the end of the story: buy a camera rated for outdoor use and it will last outdoors anywhere.

The rating describes how well the camera body keeps out dust and water. It says nothing about the steel bracket it hangs from, the screws in the wall, or the plug at the end of its cable, which are the parts that fail first on a house facing Jamaica Bay. So the useful question near the water is not whether the camera is rated, but whether the hardware and connections around it were chosen and sealed for salt air. Stainless screws, a sealed junction box, protected plugs and a drip loop do more for a camera's life here than a higher rating on the box.

"After a power outage, the cameras will come back on their own"

Generic advice says to wait for the power to return, give the system a few minutes, and everything will reconnect.

Often it does. After a large outage like the one Con Edison reported across southeast Brooklyn in July 2019, it often does not. "Spider webs on a camera are just a cleaning job"

"Put the recorder in the basement, out of sight"

A few recorders do show a warning on their own screen, which nobody sees if the recorder lives in a basement and the owner only uses the phone app. Many drives simply start recording in fits and starts, leaving gaps that only show up when someone goes looking for a clip. FAQ

Bergen Beach Security Camera Repair FAQ

Is there a best time of year to have a Bergen Beach camera system checked?
+

Two times work well. Early spring, before May and June, when the 63rd Precinct says car thefts tend to rise, so the driveway cameras are clean, aimed and recording at night when it counts. And late summer or early fall, before storm season and winter wind, so connectors on the water side are sealed, the recorder is off the floor and the battery backup still holds a charge. A clean and check visit covers both.

Should our cameras record all the time, or only when something moves?
+

For most houses, a mix works best. Record the driveway and front door continuously if the drive is large enough, because those are the views where the moments before an event matter most. Record quieter views such as the side yard on motion with a few seconds of pre-record, which saves space without losing the start of anything. We set this up to match the drive you have and tell you how many days it will hold.

Our driveway camera sends an alert for every car that passes on the street. Can it watch only our property?
+

Yes. Most cameras and recorders let you draw the area that counts for motion and mask out the rest, so the street and the sidewalk stop triggering alerts while the driveway still does. Cameras with person and vehicle detection can be told to alert only on people, or only on cars that enter the driveway. The camera keeps recording the whole view; only the alerts change.

Someone tore a camera off our side wall. Can a new one go on the same cable?
+

Usually, yes, as long as the cable was not pulled out of the wall or cut too short. We test the cable, cut back to clean wire, put on a new connector and mount a matching camera, often a little higher or with a sturdier housing so it is harder to reach. If the cable was damaged inside the wall, you get a written price for replacing that run before we start. Save any footage of the incident first if you need it.

The infrared on our side camera lights up the neighbor's bedroom window at night. Can that be changed?
+

Yes. Between closely spaced houses a side camera's infrared can shine across the gap and reflect off the next house, which also tends to wash out your own picture. We re-aim the camera along your own wall, lower its infrared strength if the camera allows it, or switch it to a separate light pointed only at your side path. Most of the time the fix improves your picture as well as your neighbor's night.

How often should camera lenses near Jamaica Bay be cleaned?
+

On cameras facing the water or one of the basins, a gentle cleaning every few months keeps salt film from softening the picture, and after a big windstorm it is worth a look. Use a soft cloth and plain water or a lens cleaner, never anything abrasive, which scratches the cover and makes night glare worse. Cameras on the sheltered side of the house usually need it once or twice a year.

We are selling our Bergen Beach house. What should we do with the camera system before closing?
+

Decide first whether the system stays with the house, and write that into the sale. If it stays, save any footage you want to keep, remove your account from the recorder and the app, change the recorder password, and leave the new password and the recorder's model and location for the buyer. If it goes, the cameras and recorder come down and the holes are sealed. We can do either on one visit.

Will our cameras still record if the internet goes out?
+

A wired system with its own recorder keeps recording to the drive when the internet is down; you just cannot watch it on your phone until the connection returns. Cameras that save only to the cloud stop recording when the internet stops. If you are not sure which kind you have, a technician can tell from the recorder label or the camera model in a minute.

A big storm is in the forecast. What should we do with the camera system before it arrives?
+

Lift the recorder, power box and any power strip off the floor if they are in a basement or garage, and make sure the recorder is plugged into a surge-protected battery backup rather than straight into the wall. Check that the system is recording and that the date is right. If water reaches the equipment, switch it off at the outlet only when you can do so safely, and do not turn it back on until it has been dried and checked.

Our cameras run through our tenant's Wi-Fi router. What happens when the tenant moves out?
+

The recorder will keep recording, but phone viewing stops when the tenant's internet is disconnected, and any Wi-Fi cameras that joined that router will drop off completely. The cleaner setup in a two-family is to give the camera system its own connection on the owner's side, wired where possible. We can move the recorder onto your network and rejoin the cameras, ideally before the tenant leaves.

Our house was power-washed and one camera has been fuzzy ever since. Is it ruined?
+

Not always. A pressure washer can force water past a camera's seals or leave residue on the cover. If the picture is fuzzy but steady, cleaning the cover often fixes it. If there is fog or droplets inside the lens, water got in, and the camera usually needs replacing on the same cable for $250 to $600 including the camera.
