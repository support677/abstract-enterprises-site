The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Fiske Terrace is a few blocks of freestanding houses on the southern edge of Flatbush, between Foster Avenue to the north, Ocean Avenue to the east, the Bay Ridge freight line's cut to the south and the Brighton line's B and Q tracks to the west. Most of it lies inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated on March 18, 2008, and which its report counts at roughly 250 houses built mostly between 1905 and 1914. Around those blocks sit a different set of buildings: six-story prewar co-ops facing Ocean Avenue, the single apartment house inside the district at 800 East 17th Street, and two- and three-story store-and-apartment buildings on Avenue H near the station.

That split matters more for a buzzer than for almost any other service. In an apartment building the question is how to replace or extend an entry system that already exists. In a Fiske Terrace house there usually is no entry system, only a push button for a bell that was wired when the house was new, or a store-bought video doorbell somebody added later. Everything works while one family lives in the house and someone is always downstairs. It stops working when the top floor is rented, when an older parent moves into a ground-floor room and cannot get to the door quickly, when the family that answered the door at the porch now comes and goes by the side door off the driveway, or when a home office means visitors arrive at hours when nobody is near the front of the house.

The question homeowners put to us is rarely "which intercom brand?" It is closer to the one a Brooklyn owner asked on the Brownstoner forum about a multi-family house: the upstairs households want to "see who is at the door," and the first wired quote they got, which the owner later put at "more like $3,000," felt like too much. Another owner on the same forum had a three-unit house whose buzzer "works only sporadically, and each unit is experiencing the same problems," and was quoted $2,000 to replace it. Both are fair questions, and both have answers that depend on the door, the wire and how many households need their own button, not on a national average.

The co-op and rental buildings on the edges have the opposite problem. They already have entry systems, many of them old. Compass lists both 800 East 17th Street, a 27-unit co-op of 1936, and 1256 Ocean Avenue, a 26-unit rental of 2014 with a full-time doorman, as having a "Voice Intercom," and neither listing mentions video. CityRealty puts 1212 Ocean Avenue, between Glenwood Road and Avenue H, at 48 apartments in a six-story co-op built in 1925, and StreetEasy lists 1280 Ocean Avenue as a six-story, 66-unit elevator co-op of 1935, a building Columbia University's digital library catalogs under the name "Fiske Terrace Apartments." Buildings of that age and size usually have an audio intercom running over wire that went in decades ago, and a board that has to decide whether to repair it, extend it or replace it.

Security worries are part of the picture, though not the whole of it. In 2012, a string of break-ins led the Fiske Terrace association to ask the 70th Precinct for a meeting; Patch reported co-president Nancy Berenbaum saying the break-ins "brought the issue to the forefront." In July 2025, News 12 reported from an unnamed Flatbush apartment building where tenants blamed intrusions on "broken locks at the building's entrances," and its crew got in through the "repaired front entrance" with keys from the news van. A buzzer does not replace a good lock. What it does is let a household answer the door without opening it, which is the point for an older resident alone in the house, for a tenant on the third floor, and for a lobby whose door should never be left on the latch for a delivery.

The front door under a porch roof: keep the station at the door, not out at the steps

Most Fiske Terrace houses are entered from a porch. The visitor climbs a few steps, crosses the porch floor and stands at a wood door that usually has a glass panel, framed by a casing that may be the original millwork. The first instinct of many owners is to put the new station at the top of the steps or on a porch post, so the visitor rings before reaching the door. Inside the historic district, that is usually the wrong place. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidance says intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," and allows one on a fence or wall "in front of the entranceway" only as the alternative where a door position does not work.

So we start at the door. On most porch doors there is a flat stretch of casing or wall on the latch side, between the door and the window beside it, where a slim station can sit at a comfortable height without touching the glass or the decorative trim. The guidance asks that equipment attach at "mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," not "on any decorative surfaces," and that intercom finishes be neutral and discreet; on painted wood casing, a dark or painted station mounted with small screws into plain wood fits both. The cable comes through the wall behind the station into the front hall, then down to the cellar, so nothing runs across the porch ceiling or down the face of a column.

The side door off the driveway: the entrance nobody designed for visitors

In a neighborhood of concrete driveways and freestanding garages, the front door is often the formal entrance and the side door is the real one. The family comes home by car and walks in through the kitchen. The grocery delivery and the plumber come up the driveway because that is where the car is parked. A grandparent who lives in a ground-floor room can hear the side door but not the front. Yet the side door almost never has a bell, because when the house was built, visitors were expected to use the porch.

A buzzer installation that ignores the side door leaves half the problem unsolved. We usually put a second, smaller station there, calling the same indoor stations as the front, with a different chime so the household knows which door to go to, and its own release if the side door has a frame that can take one. Side doors are often more exposed than front doors, without a porch roof over them, so the station there is chosen for weather, and its height is set for someone who may be carrying bags. If the side door is an older wood door that has been patched where previous locks were changed, we check the frame carefully before choosing a strike.

The house with a separate tenant entrance: two households, two buttons, two doors

Some Fiske Terrace owners rent a top floor; others have a garden or basement unit with its own door. When the tenant shares the front door, a two-button station and an answering unit on each floor is usually enough. When the tenant has their own entrance, at the side, under the porch or at the back of the house, the job becomes two small systems: each door has its own station and its own release, and neither household can open the other's door. A tenant in a basement unit should not be able to buzz a stranger into the owner's front hall, and an owner should not have a button that opens the tenant's door without them.

The cable work is usually modest, because frame houses with full cellars give a clear path from either door down to a single power supply and up through a closet or an interior wall. What takes thought is the handover. A tenant who moves out should not leave with the means to open a door, so phone-answering accounts are set up so the owner can remove a household and add the next one, and any code is changed at move-out. An owner on BiggerPockets asked exactly that about a shared smart doorbell in a duplex: how to make sure a former tenant loses access. A system set up properly from the start answers the question before it is asked.

The prewar co-op lobby: old riser wire, a vestibule and a board that has to vote

The apartment buildings around Fiske Terrace's edges were mostly built in the 1920s and 1930s: 1212 Ocean Avenue in 1925, 1280 Ocean Avenue in 1935, and 800 East 17th Street, the one apartment house inside the historic district, in 1936. They are six-story elevator buildings of between 27 and 66 apartments, entered through a vestibule and a lobby door, and those that list an intercom list an audio one. Their entry systems have usually been replaced at least once, often reusing riser wire that runs up a shaft or a line of closets from the cellar to the top floor.

The first job in a building like this is a riser test. Every apartment's pair is traced from the lobby panel or the cellar terminal to the apartment and tested before we design anything. Where the pairs are sound, a two-wire system can run over them, and Aiphone's upgrade guidance says plainly that "If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused." The Park Vanderbilt, a 148-unit Park Slope co-op, replaced its audio intercom with video over the old cable, except for two units, and its installer told Security Sales it saved $20,000 on the job. Where pairs have failed, we replace those lines, and the written price names how many we expect to reuse and how many we expect to replace.

The newer doorman rental: a desk, a panel and a door that must work when the desk is empty

1256 Ocean Avenue is the exception among the nearby apartment buildings: an eight-story, 26-unit rental built in 2014, listed on Compass with a full-time doorman, an elevator and a voice intercom. A building like this was built after 1968 with more than eight units, so the intercom duty in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies to it directly. In practice, the doorman handles most visitors, and the intercom matters when the doorman steps away, takes a package upstairs or has a break.

For a building with a desk, the entry system is designed so the desk and the panel never fight. A visitor at the outer door can call the desk first and, if nobody answers within a set time, reach the apartment directly. The doorman can release the lobby door from the desk, and residents can release it from their apartments. If the building wants video, the desk station shows the same picture residents see. The point is that the door is never left propped because the desk is empty.

The store with apartments above: a shop door, an apartment door and one narrow frontage

Avenue H near the station has a short run of small mixed buildings, such as 1608 Avenue H, a three-story building of 1931 that StreetEasy classes as "Primarily Two Family with Store or Office." The LPC designation report records that in 1916 residents opposed a store-and-flats row on Avenue H between East 16th and East 17th Streets, and that a block of stores with apartments on Foster Avenue around 1910 prompted the Midwood Park entrance posts. Store-and-apartment buildings have been part of the neighborhood's edges ever since, and they need a different entry plan from the houses behind them.

The residential door in a building like this is usually narrow and sits beside the shop's door. The apartments' station goes at that door, calls only the apartments, and releases only that door, on a power supply that does not go dark when the shop closes. If the shop wants a buzzer for its own delivery door, it gets a separate station answered at the counter. The two systems may share a cellar, but they never share a release, so no shop employee can let someone up the apartments' stair and no tenant can open the store.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ring From Where the Visitor Stands, Listen From the Farthest Bedroom, Then Find the Old Bell Wire in the Cellar

These notes describe how our technicians approach a buzzer installation at a house like the ones in Fiske Terrace. They are our standard practice, not a story about one address.

Ring from where the visitor stands. The technician starts outside, at the front walk, and walks the visitor's route: up the porch steps or along the driveway to the side door. Where does a stranger naturally stop? Where does a delivery driver leave a parcel? Which door does the household itself use most? The answers decide which doors get a station, and the porch door's station goes where the visitor's hand and face naturally are, inside the door opening, not where the old bell button happened to be drilled a century ago.

Listen from the farthest bedroom. Next, the technician goes to the place in the house farthest from the door that someone will want to answer from, usually a top-floor bedroom or a rented attic floor, and asks someone at the door to knock and call out. If it cannot be heard, the indoor station belongs there, or a second chime does. In houses with a tenant upstairs, this step also settles where the tenant's station goes: near their own door, not at the far end of their floor.

Find the old bell wire. Last, the technician goes to the cellar and looks for the old doorbell transformer and the wire from the front door. In many houses it is still there, often thin bell wire run when the house was built or rewired. It is tested, but it is rarely reused for a modern station; at best it is a pull string for new cable through a wall that would otherwise need opening. The power supply's location is chosen on an owner-controlled circuit, and if there is no outlet nearby, a licensed electrician adds one.

The Landmark Line

One Panel Per Door Opening and Ten Inches of Conduit: Installing a Buzzer on a House Inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District

Almost every house in Fiske Terrace sits inside a historic district, and a door station is one of the few pieces of security equipment that has to go on the outside of the house. That makes the Landmarks Preservation Commission part of nearly every first-time buzzer installation here. The good news is that the Commission's rules for small equipment are clear, and a well-planned station fits inside them without a hearing. The rules are set out in the Commission's Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety equipment, which covers light fixtures, intercoms, security cameras and mailboxes together.

Where the station may go. The guidance says light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening." An intercom may also be mounted "on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway." Equipment generally cannot go on or in front of a primary facade "unless no other option exists," and when it must, it should sit somewhere discreet, such as beneath a band course, under a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, or behind plantings. For a porch house, that points to the door opening under the porch roof as the natural place.

How much may go there. At staff level, the Commission allows "a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel" per door opening. That is a real constraint for a house with a tenant. A two-household house should not get two separate panels side by side at the porch door; it should get one panel with two buttons. If the tenant has a separate door, that door opening can have its own panel.

How it is fastened. Equipment must attach at "mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and attachment "through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces" is not allowed. On most porch doors that means plain painted casing or the wall beside it, not a carved bracket, a leaded glass sidelight or a decorative panel. We use the smallest fasteners that hold the station securely and place them where the holes could be filled and painted if the station were ever removed.

How the cable gets in. Conduit should be concealed by running it through the wall behind the installation. Where that is not possible, staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match the background. In practice, we bring the cable straight through the wall behind the station into the front hall, so no conduit is visible at all. On a stucco or brick wall, we drill at a mortar joint or in plain material, never through decorative work.

What the station looks like. The design, size and finish must be "compatible with the architecture of the building," with intercoms neutral and discreet. A small dark or painted station beside a painted wood door meets that; a large brushed-steel apartment panel does not. If the owner wants a camera at the door as well, the Commission's camera rules apply separately: no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage for small buildings, dome-type and finished to match. Most door stations include their own camera, which often means no separate camera is needed at the door.

What a permit application needs. Applications are filed through the Commission's Portico system, usually with color photos and comparative drawings. Manufacturer cut sheets are required for intercoms, and elevation drawings are required except for "in-kind replacement of existing light fixtures, intercoms, security cameras, and mailboxes." Ordinary maintenance and repair, such as replacing a failed station with the same kind, need no permit. A new station where there has only been a bell button, or a different station in a different spot, is new work. We prepare the cut sheet, photos and a marked-up drawing showing the station position, the fasteners and the cable route, and the owner or their expediter files it.

Installation Day

One Visit for a House, a Planned Schedule for a Lobby: How Installation Day Works From the Porch to the Top Floor

The size of the job decides how installation day runs. For a one- or two-household house, it is usually a single visit, with the household at home. For a co-op or rental building, it is a schedule built around getting into every apartment, and that schedule is the thing most likely to stretch a job if it is not planned.

A house in one visit. At a house, the technician arrives within a two-hour window agreed in advance and starts in the cellar: the power supply goes on its circuit and the cable route up through the house is opened. Then the door: the strike goes into the frame and the closer is set. Then the station outside and the answering units inside. The front door is out of service for a short stretch while the strike goes in, and we plan that for a time when someone is home. By the end of the visit, every household has been called from the door, has talked with the technician on the porch, has released the door and has watched it relock.

The Rules

Which Fiske Terrace Buildings the Intercom Law Covers, and What Every Door With a Buzzer Still Has to Do

Houses are outside the intercom mandate. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law applies in cities of 325,000 people or more, which covers all of New York City, and its §50-a sets the intercom duty at buildings with eight or more apartments. ButterflyMX's guide to New York intercom law notes that buildings with fewer than eight units are "not generally" covered. A Fiske Terrace house with a rented top floor or a basement apartment is far below that line. A buzzer there is a choice the owner makes for the household's comfort and safety, not a legal requirement.

Larger buildings need a talk-and-release system. Under §50-a, every class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk with a visitor and release the door. 1256 Ocean Avenue, built in 2014 with 26 units, falls squarely in that group. For buildings of that size built before 1968, such as the prewar co-ops on Ocean Avenue and 800 East 17th Street, the owner has to install the door and the system when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask or consent in writing; the work must start within 90 days and finish within six months.

A working intercom is treated as essential. Habitat Magazine's February 2024 article on intercom duties notes that the city's housing maintenance code requires a "functioning intercom" where one is required, and that courts have treated intercoms as an essential service under the warranty of habitability. Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group put it directly for co-ops: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." For a board weighing a repair against a replacement, that is the starting point: the system has to work.

Everyone must be able to get out. A door with a buzzer still has to let people leave without a key, code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic: Allegion's guide notes that with a strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. That is why strikes are our default on residential doors. Magnetic locks hold the whole door and fall under the building code's sensor-release provisions: a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, unlocking when power is lost and when a fire alarm or sprinkler activates, and hardware listed to UL 294. We rarely recommend them for a house or a co-op lobby.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Fiske Terrace: Questions Answered

Questions Fiske Terrace homeowners, co-op shareholders and Avenue H building owners bring to us, along with ones raised in Brownstoner and BiggerPockets threads about buzzers for multi-family houses, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Old houses and the fittings they came with

Our house is one of a series of similar houses put up by the same builder around 1906. If the neighbors' houses share our plan, can one buzzer layout simply be copied from house to house?

Partly. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report notes that the T.B. Ackerson Company built Fiske Terrace houses in series of ten to twenty, and that the builders avoided putting identical houses side by side. So two houses on the same block may share a floor plan, a stair position and a cellar layout, which helps: once we know where the cable can run in one, we have a good first guess for the other.

What cannot be copied is everything that changed in the century since. One house has a finished attic with a tenant; the next is still a one-family. One has had its porch enclosed; another has a new side entrance. One owner rewired in the 1970s and left a clear chase from the cellar; the neighbor's walls were closed up with plaster that has never been opened. Each house still gets its own walk, its own wire test and its own written price, even if the design looks familiar.

Our porch door is original, with a large glass panel set in narrow wood stiles. Is there enough solid wood in the frame for an electric release?

Usually, because the electric strike goes in the frame, not the door. The door's glass and narrow stiles matter for the lock, but the strike replaces the metal plate in the jamb that the latch bolt catches on, and the jamb of an old porch door is usually solid wood. We check its depth and condition where the strike would go, and choose a strike body that fits without cutting away so much wood that the frame is weakened.

Two things can complicate it. If the latch bolt does not line up with the plate any more, because the door has dropped on its hinges or the frame has moved, the door has to be adjusted first, or the strike will not release reliably. And if the jamb has been patched many times where older locks were changed, there may not be enough sound wood left; in that case we say so before quoting, and a carpenter can splice in new wood before the strike goes in.

Households, tenants and phones

Two roommates rent our top floor and each wants the front door to ring their own phone. Can one button reach both, and what happens when one of them moves out?

Yes. With a station that supports phone answering, one button can ring several phones at once, and whoever answers first talks to the visitor and releases the door. This solves the problem tenants raised on a Brownstoner thread about phone-dialing intercoms, where only one roommate's phone rang and the other was left out. An indoor station on the top floor can ring at the same time, for anyone who is home without a phone in hand.

When one roommate moves out, the owner removes that person's phone from the account and adds the replacement. That is the reason we set up the account in the owner's name, not a tenant's, with each household's phones as users the owner controls. An owner on BiggerPockets asked how to make sure a former tenant loses access to a shared smart doorbell; with the account in the owner's hands, the answer is a two-minute change on the day they leave.

Our tenant tutors students at home, and her students come to the side door. Can they ring her without ringing our family?

Yes. The side door gets its own station, and its button rings only the tenant's indoor station or phone. The front door's station rings the family. Each station releases only its own door, so a student at the side door can be let in by the tenant without the family's front hall being opened, and a visitor for the family never ends up at the tenant's stair.

If both households sometimes use both doors, each station can carry two buttons, one for each household, labeled clearly. What matters is that every button rings the household it says it rings, and every release opens only the door it belongs to.

The apartment buildings at the edges

We live at 800 East 17th Street, the one apartment house inside the historic district. Does a new lobby panel there need Landmarks approval like the houses around us?

It depends on where the panel goes. The Commission's designation report names 800 East 17th Street, a six-story building of 1936 at the northwest corner of Wellington Court and East 17th Street, as the only apartment house in the district, so the building is subject to the Commission's rules for exterior work. A panel mounted outside the entrance is exterior work and follows the same small-equipment rules as the houses: at the door opening, one panel per opening at staff level, fastened to plain material, with concealed cable.

A like-for-like replacement of an existing intercom panel is ordinary repair or in-kind replacement, which the guidebook treats more lightly; the Commission exempts in-kind replacement of intercoms from elevation drawings. A panel inside the vestibule, entirely indoors, generally needs no Landmarks permit unless the work also needs a Department of Buildings permit or affects the exterior. We check with the board or managing agent which of those the building's plan is before ordering, and prepare the cut sheet and photos if a filing is needed.

Our Ocean Avenue co-op has just received an HPD order about the lobby door's self-closing hardware. Should the intercom replacement wait until the door is fixed, or be done together?

Fix the door first, or at least at the same time. A self-closing door violation is about the door, its closer and its hinges, and it has its own correction deadline; the class is printed on the notice. An intercom replacement does not clear it, and a new strike on a door that does not close and latch by itself will not work reliably. 1280 Ocean Avenue's June 2026 Class C order for a self-closing condition shows that this is not a hypothetical for buildings here.

In practice, we often do both in one plan. The closer is adjusted or replaced and the hinges are fixed so the door shuts and latches every time, then the new strike goes into a frame that holds the latch properly, then the new panel goes up. If the board has already hired someone for the door, we coordinate with them so the strike and closer work together.

The intercom riser in our 1930s co-op runs through a stack of apartment closets, and some shareholders have built shelving over the junction boxes. How do you test and work on it?

With notice and patience. Riser junction boxes in closets are common in prewar buildings, and a shelf or a cabinet built over one is common too. Before the riser test, the managing agent tells the shareholders in that line which closet the technician needs to reach and asks them to clear access to the box. Where a shelf cannot be moved, we work around it if we can reach the box; if we cannot, that line's test waits for the second pass.

The test itself is done pair by pair from the cellar or lobby terminal to each apartment. Each pair is marked reusable or not, and the result is a list the board can see: which lines passed, which failed, and which could not be reached. That list decides whether a two-wire system can run on the old riser and how many new runs the job needs.

Shops and mixed buildings on Avenue H and Foster Avenue

We bought a store-and-apartment building on Avenue H where the shop's bell and the apartments' buzzer run off the same transformer in the cellar. Can they be separated?

Yes, and they should be. A single transformer feeding both means that if the shop's bell shorts, or someone switches off the circuit it is on, the apartments lose their buzzer too. It also means the shop's wiring and the apartments' wiring meet at one point, which is where cross-wiring happens: a shop button that releases the residential door is a mistake we see in mixed buildings more often than owners expect.

The fix is two power supplies on two circuits, both under the owner's control, with the apartments' system on its own. The shop's bell or buzzer stays on the shop's supply, and the two sets of wires are traced, labeled and separated at the cellar so nobody can confuse them later. If the apartments' system is old, this is often the moment to replace it with a station that calls each apartment and a strike sized for the residential door.

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost guide gives that range for installing a five-room whole-house system, within an overall average of $2,500.

A whole-house intercom, with speakers in every room, is not what most Fiske Terrace households are asking for. Mounting a station can take that long. The time at a Fiske Terrace house goes elsewhere: finding a cable path from the porch through a plaster wall to the cellar, fitting a strike into an old jamb, adjusting a closer, and, inside the district, preparing a station drawing and cut sheet for the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

So we do not price by station hours. We price the job after a walk, with the cable route, the door work and any Landmarks preparation each listed, so you can see where the time goes.

"A video doorbell does the same job as a buzzer"

Retail guides often treat a smart video doorbell as the modern replacement for a buzzer, and one Brownstoner forum reply described wiring a doorbell camera with plug-in chimes for about $40.

A video doorbell shows who is at the door and lets you talk to them. It does not release the door. In a one-family house where someone is always downstairs, that may be enough. In a house with a tenant on the top floor, or an older resident who cannot get to the door quickly, it leaves the real problem untouched: someone still has to walk down and open it.

A buzzer adds the release, a button per household and, with a closer, a door that locks again behind the visitor. That difference is the whole job.

"Smartphone intercoms need new internet cabling at the entrance"

Habitat Magazine's 2017 report on new buzzer systems noted that many smartphone systems rely on internet cabling, often requiring new wiring at the building entrance.

For a co-op on Ocean Avenue, that is not always so. Many current two-wire systems carry video over the old intercom pair and add phone answering through a gateway in the cellar, which keeps the riser in use. The Park Vanderbilt co-op in Park Slope replaced its audio system with video and reused the old cable except for two units, which its installer said saved $20,000.

"Software subscriptions add $5 to $15 per unit per month"

Swiftlane's apartment intercom cost guide gives that range for app-based systems, or $30 to $100 per door per month.

For a 48-apartment co-op like 1212 Ocean Avenue, $5 to $15 per unit works out to $240 to $720 a month, every month. Some boards decide that is worth it for phone answering and remote management; others do not.

It is not a requirement. A wired system with indoor stations has a $0 monthly fee option, and phone answering can be limited to the households that want it. Any subscription is shown on the quote, beside the hardware, before a board votes.

The words make it sound that way, and some summaries recommend fail-safe hardware because it unlocks when power is lost.

Allegion's guide explains the difference: "Fail safe products are unlocked when power is removed," while "Fail secure products are locked when power is removed," and with an electric strike "you can exit by turning the lever" either way. For a porch door or a lobby door, a fail-safe strike means the door opens to anyone during a power cut, which is the opposite of what a household wants.

So our default on residential doors is a fail-secure strike with a battery-backed power supply. People can always leave by the lever; nobody gets in during an outage without a key.

A virtual doorman can suit some buildings, but it is a service on top of an entry system, not a substitute for one; the remote attendant still needs a panel at the door and a release. FAQ

Fiske Terrace Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Does adding a second station at the side door double the price of the job?
+

No. The power supply, the indoor stations and most of the cable route are shared, so a second door adds its own station, its own strike if it needs a release, and the cable from that door to the cellar. On a house, that usually keeps the job in the same Brooklyn range or moves it from the audio range into the video range, and the written price shows the second door as its own line.

Will the electric strike show from the porch once it is installed?
+

Barely. The strike sits in the jamb where the old strike plate was, so from the porch you see a metal faceplate on the edge of the frame, only when the door is open. Nothing is added to the face of the door or the casing, and the wire runs inside the jamb and the wall.

Can a district house avoid drilling through the wall by using a wireless door station?
+

Sometimes, but a wireless station still has to be fastened to the house, still needs power, and still needs a release in the frame, which is wired. It also depends on Wi-Fi or a cellular signal reaching the porch. A small hole straight through plain material behind a wired station is usually the more reliable choice and is what the Landmarks guidance expects for concealed cable.

Can the indoor station on our top floor also call the kitchen, like a house intercom?
+

Many indoor stations can call each other as well as answer the door, so the top floor can call the kitchen and the kitchen can call back. It works best inside one household; between an owner and a tenant, most people prefer stations that only answer the door. Tell us if you want it, and we choose stations that support it.

What happens to the store-bought video doorbell already on our door when the buzzer goes in?
+

Usually it comes off, because the new door station has its own camera and the Landmarks limit is one intercom panel per door opening.
