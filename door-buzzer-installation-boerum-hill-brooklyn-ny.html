The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Boerum Hill's houses were built for one family and a front door that someone walked down to open. Between Schermerhorn Street and Warren and Wyckoff Streets, and from Smith Street east to Fourth Avenue, the blocks are lined with three-story rowhouses put up between roughly 1840 and 1870, with later brownstone, Second Empire and neo-Grec rows and a scattering of small apartment houses. The neighborhood was known as North Gowanus until the name Boerum Hill was coined in 1964. In the early twentieth century many of these houses became boarding houses and later apartments, and that is how the buzzer story here usually begins: a house divided into two, three or four homes long after it was built, with a bell or intercom added by whoever did the division, routed wherever was easiest at the time.

The city's PLUTO land-use file shows how small those buildings still are. Inside the original Boerum Hill Historic District and the 2018 extension, PLUTO counts 576 lots. Of those, 260 are two-family houses, 114 are three-family houses, 109 are one-family houses and 50 have four apartments. Only 23 lots inside the two districts hold eight or more apartments. Put another way, the typical Boerum Hill entry system calls two or three apartments, and most of the effort on an installation goes into the door, the release and the cable path, not into a large panel. That is a different picture from the Fort Greene and Clinton Hill side of the same community board, where Clinton, Greene and Washington Avenues carry the board's highest intercom violation counts, and it changes what a good system looks like here.

It also changes what the law requires. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a makes an intercom with remote release mandatory at the self-locking main door of buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and older buildings of that size must install one when tenants in a majority of apartments ask for it in writing. Almost no Boerum Hill rowhouse meets that eight-apartment line, and as one renter wrote on Reddit about a four-unit Brooklyn building, that means the landlord "isn't required to put in a buzzer system or a self-locking front door." Owners here install buzzers anyway, for reasons that are practical rather than legal: an owner on the parlor floor with a tenant on the top floor, a household whose kitchen is two floors below the front door, couriers who will not wait, and a street door that gets propped open when nobody can release it from upstairs.

That propping problem is not a theory in the 84th Precinct. In January 2013, DNAinfo reported that 48 of the 141 burglaries in the precinct the year before had happened when residents' doors were unlocked. The precinct's commanding officer at the time, Capt. Maximo Tolentino, told a community council meeting: "Even if you're home, you can't assume it's OK to leave your front door open." Howard Kolins, then president of the Boerum Hill Association, put it as a warning to his neighbors not to encourage "a crime of opportunity." A buzzer does not stop every burglary, but it removes the main reason a rowhouse door ends up unlocked in the afternoon: somebody upstairs is expecting a delivery, a guest or a dog walker and does not want to walk down three flights to open it.

Deliveries are their own pressure. Brooklyn Paper reported a parcel thief who opened the front gate of a Bond Street townhouse and took about $3,500 worth of packages, including a Ring doorbell. Boerum Hill rowhouses have a small areaway and a gate at the sidewalk, a stoop up to the parlor door and often a second door under the stoop, which gives couriers three places to leave a box and thieves three places to look. A good installation decides where packages go before it decides which screen to buy: a release that lets couriers into the vestibule during the day, a camera that shows the stoop and the gate, or simply a household that can answer from a phone while it is out.

City records show that entry systems here fail too, even if less often than in some neighborhoods. When we queried HPD's housing maintenance code violations on November 11, 2026, Brooklyn Community Board 2, which takes in Boerum Hill along with Downtown Brooklyn, Brooklyn Heights, Fort Greene, Clinton Hill and DUMBO, had 192 intercom violations written since the start of 2020. On the streets that run through Boerum Hill, from Pacific, Dean and Bergen to State, Wyckoff, Nevins, Hoyt, Bond, Smith, Atlantic and Third Avenue, we counted 38. One Nevins Street walk-up was cited six times between August 2023 and October 2026, and one Wyckoff Street building three times in under two years. Tenants are calling 311 more as well: bell, buzzer and intercom complaints from the board rose from 30 in 2020 to 81 in 2024, and 2026 had already reached 107 by November 11, the most of any year on record.

Repair or Replace in Boerum Hill

Need Repair in Boerum Hill? A Bell That Went Silent, a Release That Hums, or a Door That Won't Stay Shut

Plenty of calls that begin as "we need a new buzzer" turn out to need one part. Before we price a new system for a Boerum Hill house, we ask what actually stopped working and when. If one apartment lost its handset after a kitchen was redone, that is usually a cut pair of wires in one wall, not a dead system. If the release hums but the door stays locked, the strike may have been knocked out of line by a door that has dropped on its hinges. If the whole house went quiet at once, the old transformer in the cellar is the first suspect. Each of those is a repair. A new system makes more sense when the handsets are a discontinued model, the panel has been patched more than once, or the wiring has been cut and spliced so often that nobody can say which pair goes where.

Two situations should be treated as urgent. One is a street door that cannot be locked, because a stuck-open strike or a broken latch leaves every apartment exposed. The other is a walk-up with an open HPD violation, where the clock is already running for the owner. In either case, call (347) 934-8335 and say so. A diagnosis is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 is credited toward the repair or toward a new system if that turns out to be the better choice. You get both options priced, our recommendation and the reason for it. If a part has to be ordered, we make sure the door locks and opens with a key before we leave, so the building is never left open while you wait.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Boerum Hill's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Boerum Hill buzzer job. What separates them is not style or age so much as three practical questions: how many households the system has to call, which door it has to release, and which way a cable can travel from the stoop to the top floor without opening a finished room.

The rowhouse kept as one home

One household across three or four floors, with the kitchen often on the garden level and bedrooms two flights up. PLUTO counts 109 one-family lots in the districts. The job is reach, not apartments.

The two-family house

The single largest group, 260 lots in the districts. Usually an owner's duplex and a rental floor, or a garden-and-parlor duplex with a separate apartment above, sharing one stoop door.

The three-family house

114 lots in the districts. One apartment per floor above the garden or parlor, a long narrow stair and a three-button panel. Most multi-unit jobs here look like this.

The four-family and small walk-up

Four to fourteen apartments in a wider house or a purpose-built walk-up, often off the avenues on Nevins, Bond or Pacific Street, with a lobby door and an inner door.

The shop with apartments above

Atlantic Avenue and Smith Street buildings with a store or restaurant at street level and a narrow residential door beside it. PLUTO lists 51 such mixed-use lots in the districts.

The condo and the conversion

Small condominiums carved from rowhouses and newer buildings near Fourth Avenue and Schermerhorn Street, where a board or a developer, not one owner, decides on the entry system.

The rowhouse kept as one home: the bell rings where nobody is

A single-family Boerum Hill rowhouse has the fewest components and the hardest geography. The parlor door at the top of the stoop opens into a hall that nobody sits in. The kitchen is often on the garden floor below, the living room on the parlor floor, and the bedrooms and a home office two flights up. A mechanical bell or a simple chime in the hall is easy to miss from any of those rooms. The common answer is a single-button video door station at the parlor door, an indoor monitor on the floor where the household spends its days, and calls forwarded to phones so the top floor and anyone out of the house can answer too. If the household wants one device and nothing on the walls, a phone-only system is a reasonable choice for a house where everyone carries a smartphone.

The release decision is personal. Some owners never want the street door opened from upstairs, because they would rather see the visitor and walk down. Others want the vestibule door released for deliveries while the inner door stays on a key, so a courier can step out of the rain without entering the house. A few want the garden door under the stoop on a release because that is the door the family uses every day. We ask how the house is lived in before we fit anything. A video station with no strike at all is a perfectly good system for a family that only wants to see who is there, and it costs less than one with a release.

The two-family house: one stoop, two households and a landlord who lives downstairs

The two-family house is Boerum Hill's typical building, and its entry system usually serves two very different users. An owner who lives in the lower duplex wants to see who is at the door and often wants a release on the vestibule door. The tenant upstairs wants to let in a food delivery without coming down two flights. A two-button panel at the stoop door, a station in each apartment and a release on the door both households use is the starting design. Where the garden floor has its own entrance under the stoop, the owner's button can ring there and at the parlor level, while the tenant's button rings only upstairs, so neither household is woken by the other's visitors.

Cable is where two-family jobs vary. The old bell wire in many of these houses ran up the stair hall, but the stair hall is exactly what renovations change: walls are opened, plaster replaced with drywall, a stair enclosed or a closet added. A route that worked in 1960 may now be behind a finished wall with recessed lights in it. The better routes are often less obvious: a closet that stacks from floor to floor, a chase beside the chimney breast, the back of the stair stringer, or a neatly run line up the rear wall from the cellar. When the owner lives in the house, these decisions can be made on the walkthrough in a few minutes, because the owner can say which walls are off-limits and which closets can lose a corner.

The three-family house: a three-button panel and a long climb

Three-family houses, 114 lots in the districts by PLUTO's count, are what most people mean when they ask for a "building buzzer" in Boerum Hill. The door station is a small panel with three buttons, or a short directory on a video station. Each apartment gets a handset, a monitor or app answering, and the release goes on the street door, the vestibule door or both. None of the equipment is complicated. The time goes into getting a cable to the third floor in a house whose party walls are solid brick and whose stair hall is barely wider than the stair. Before we price a three-family, we want to know whether any old cable reaches every floor, whether a closet or shaft stacks the full height of the house, and where in the cellar a 120-volt outlet is available for the power supply.

With those three answers, most three-family installations take a day. Where nothing stacks and the old wire is unusable, there are two honest options. One is a wired system with a cable run on the rear wall or through a single riser we create, which is the most dependable choice for the long term. The other is a phone or app system that needs cable only between the door and the power supply, which saves opening walls on every floor but depends on the building's internet and on each tenant's phone. We price both, side by side, so the owner can see the trade in dollars and in reliability rather than in sales language.

The four-family and small walk-up: planned like a building, not a house

Boerum Hill has a modest number of larger houses: 50 four-family lots in the districts, a few dozen with five to fourteen apartments, and walk-ups just outside the district lines on Nevins, Bond and Pacific Street. These need a system designed for the unit count: a directory panel with a button or code for each apartment, a riser cable that serves every floor, a power supply sized for the number of stations and, often, a second door to deal with. They are also the buildings where HPD's records cluster. The Nevins Street walk-up cited six times since 2023 had violations written against the system "from building front to return buzzer" in a fourth-floor foyer and against the panel in second-floor kitchens, which is exactly what happens when a system that was never designed for the building is patched apartment by apartment.

For these buildings we plan the whole entrance, not just the panel. That means deciding which door is released, whether the inner door has a closer that actually pulls it shut, whether the lobby has light for a camera after dark, where the power supply can sit away from the boiler and the water meter, and whether the building has any rear yard or cellar door that should be on the same system. If the building has eight or more apartments and was built or converted after 1968, the Multiple Dwelling Law already requires the intercom, and HPD will treat any failure as a violation, so we document the installation thoroughly enough that the owner can show an inspector what was installed and tested.

The shop with apartments above: two doors a few feet apart

Atlantic Avenue is covered by a business improvement district that runs from Fourth Avenue to the BQE and counts more than 400 businesses, and Smith Street is Boerum Hill's restaurant row. In both, the typical building has a store or restaurant on the ground floor and two to four apartments above, entered through a narrow door beside the shopfront. The entry system serves only the apartments, but it lives next to a business that changes its signage, awnings, gates, ceiling and lighting far more often than any apartment changes anything. A bracket drilled for a new sign or a hole cut for a ceiling fixture in the shop can cut the buzzer cable for every apartment upstairs in one move, and the tenants find out when their delivery cannot get in.

When we install in a mixed-use building, we keep the residential cable out of the shop's ceiling wherever the building allows it, label it clearly where it must cross, and put the power supply in the residential cellar or a locked residential closet rather than in the shop's basement. We also make sure the shop's own deliveries have a separate way in, so couriers stop pressing apartment buttons for the restaurant. If the owner wants the shop's rear door on a release or the frontage on a camera, those are priced as separate lines on the same quote, and doing them on the same visit is cheaper than coming back.

The condo and the conversion: a board, a developer and a system someone else chose

Boerum Hill's newer housing comes in two forms: small condominiums created inside old rowhouses, and new buildings near Fourth Avenue and on the northern edge along Schermerhorn Street, where a nineteenth-century church at the corner of Third Avenue sold in 2026 with apartments planned on the site. In both, the owner of the entry system is a board or a developer, not a single person, and the system was usually chosen before any resident moved in. One Reddit poster described a 13-unit, five-story New York condo with an existing analogue intercom looking to move to video, and another a five-unit building still under construction whose owner was picking a system before completion. Those are the two moments when entry decisions are made: when a board decides to upgrade, and when a builder decides what to leave behind.

For a board, we start from what is there: the panel, the stations, the wire and who holds the admin login. We test every apartment, document what we find and either take over the existing system or price a replacement with the reasons written down, so the board can put a real comparison in front of the owners. For a developer, the best time to settle the entry system is before walls close, when a riser can be run for a few dollars of cable instead of a day of fishing. If a building is in a historic district or the extension, its front also falls under the LPC, and the panel position should be on the drawings, not chosen on the day the door is hung.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Five Things a Technician Looks at on a Boerum Hill Stoop

These notes describe the routine our technicians follow on rowhouses like Boerum Hill's. They are standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Both doors, opened and released. The technician starts at the sidewalk, not the cellar. The parlor door at the top of the stoop is opened and let go, then the vestibule door behind it, then the garden door under the stoop if there is one. Each should swing closed on its own and latch without a push. A nineteenth-century wooden door that has swollen, dropped on its hinges or been painted twenty times usually needs its closer adjusted, a hinge tightened or the keeper moved before any electric release will work every time. If a door does not latch, we say so before anything else, because a buzzer on a door that rests against its frame without catching protects nobody.

The frame and the lock. An electric strike replaces the part of the frame that the latch catches in, so the technician measures the frame depth, notes the lock and its latch type and checks whether the frame is original wood, a later metal frame or set beside glass. Old frames are often soft where decades of screws have gone in and out, and that decides whether the strike can be fitted directly or needs the frame built up first.

Where the old system ends. In the cellar, the technician looks for the old bell transformer or intercom power supply, usually near the electrical panel, and follows the cable from the door. Bell wire in these houses is often cloth-covered or brittle and spliced in several places. If any of it is going to be reused, every pair is tested from end to end; nothing is reused on the assumption that it works.

The route. From the cellar to the top floor, the technician looks for a path that avoids finished rooms: a stacked closet, a chase beside a chimney breast, the space behind the stair stringer, a dumbwaiter shaft, or the rear wall. The route decides the labor more than any piece of equipment does, and it is the part of the job that varies most between two houses on the same block.

The front. If the house is in the historic district or the extension, the technician notes where a station could sit within or beside the door opening without touching carved stone, cast iron or decorative woodwork, and photographs it, so any LPC permit application shows exactly what is proposed.

Stoop, Vestibule and Garden Door

Three Doors and One Decision: Which Boerum Hill Entrance Should the Buzzer Open?

A Boerum Hill rowhouse rarely has just one front door. There is the outer door at the top of the stoop, often a pair of doors on older houses; the inner vestibule door a few feet behind it; and, on many houses, a garden-level door tucked under the stoop behind the areaway gate. A buzzer can release any of them, or more than one, and the choice changes how safe the house feels, where packages end up and how much the job costs. It is the most important decision on a Boerum Hill installation, and the one most often made by default.

Releasing the outer door. If the outer door is released, a visitor or courier steps into the vestibule and the inner door stays locked. That keeps deliveries out of the rain and off the stoop, and it means a mistaken release lets someone into a small locked space rather than into the house. A Brownstoner forum member renovating a townhouse described wanting exactly that: a buzzer on the front doors so deliveries could be let into the vestibule without anyone going downstairs. The catch is that the vestibule then needs to be a place where packages are safe, which usually means a camera covering it and a solid inner door.

Releasing the inner door. Some households keep the outer doors open in the daytime, as rowhouses here were used for generations, and release only the inner door. Others release both. On the same Brownstoner thread, a member who installed fire alarms advised against "tying a remote release to the inner door," and another described being trapped and robbed in her own vestibule between the two doors, and urged that the interior lock not be "too difficult or time consuming to unlock." Those two comments pull in different directions, and both are right. A vestibule should never become a trap for a resident coming home, and it should never become an open door for a stranger. That is why we discuss the arrangement with the household rather than defaulting to one.

The garden door. On many two-family houses, the garden-level door is the owner's everyday entrance and the parlor door belongs to the tenant upstairs, or the reverse. In that case each door gets its own button, ringing only its own household, and each can have its own release. The LPC's Permit Guidebook allows one intercom per door opening, so a house with two entrances can have a station at each, as long as each sits within or beside its opening.

The hardware itself. Most releases here are electric strikes, a powered keeper in the frame that lets the latch pass when someone presses release. The lock is unchanged, so residents still use a key outside and turn the knob inside. Fail-secure strikes stay locked if the power fails, which suits street doors; as Allegion's guidance notes, you can always leave through a door with an electric strike by turning the lever, whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. Where the latch is worn or the door is a heavy pair that will not line up, a motorized lock or a release built into a new lockset may be better than forcing a strike into an old frame. Magnetic locks are always fail-safe and need sensor and push-to-exit releases under the building code, so we rarely recommend one for a rowhouse street door.

Pairs of doors. On a pair of doors, one leaf is held by bolts at the top and bottom and the other carries the lock. The release goes where the active leaf latches. If the fixed leaf has been left unbolted for years, or the pair no longer meets squarely, the release will bind, and that has to be corrected first, on a landmarked front without changing how the doors look from the sidewalk.

Renovations

Boerum Hill Renovations and the Buzzer: Why the Bell Disappears and How to Keep the New One

If one event explains more Boerum Hill buzzer calls than any other, it is a renovation. Rowhouses here are bought, gutted and rebuilt all the time, and the old bell wire is one of the first things to go: it runs through exactly the plaster, stair walls and ceilings that a renovation removes. A Brownstoner forum member put it plainly when asking for help with a three-family brownstone: a former contractor and crew had "knocked out the previous bell that was working," and the house now needed a new bell and intercom. The same thing happens in reverse in apartment buildings. Habitat magazine described a co-op shareholder who lost intercom service after a downstairs neighbor's renovation, and the lawyers it interviewed agreed the building had to restore it, with one saying the co-op "has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom."

The cheapest time to install a buzzer system in a rowhouse is while the walls are open. A riser from the cellar to the top floor costs a few hours and a spool of cable when the stud bays are exposed, and a day or more of careful fishing once the new plaster is up and painted. If you are planning a renovation in Boerum Hill, the useful thing to do is call us when the electrician is roughing in, not when the painter is finishing. We can run the cable for the door station, the power supply and each floor's station, label it and leave it coiled for the finish stage, then come back to mount and test once the walls and doors are done.

Renovations also move doors. A new front door, a rebuilt vestibule or a garden entrance moved to the other side of the stoop changes where the release goes and what hardware fits. If the door is being replaced, we would rather see the new door's specification before it is ordered, so the frame can be prepared for a strike and a cable can be left in the frame. On a landmarked front, the door replacement itself may need LPC approval, and the panel position can be shown on the same application.

Finally, renovations are where systems get damaged after they are installed. A plumber opening a wall to move a bathroom, an electrician adding recessed lights or a carpenter rebuilding a closet can cut the buzzer cable without knowing it is there. Every system we install comes with a written wiring map showing where the cable runs, floor by floor, and we label the cable at each end and at the cellar. Hand that map to the next contractor before work starts, and the buzzer is far more likely to survive the next renovation than the last one did.

Phones, Apps and the Rules

Answering the Stoop From a Phone in Boerum Hill: What App Entry Changes for Owners and Tenants

A growing share of Boerum Hill owners ask for a system that rings phones instead of, or as well as, a station on the wall. The reasons are good ones. An owner who lives in Manhattan or upstate can see and answer the door of a rental house. A household that is out all day can let in a dog walker or a delivery. And in a renovated rowhouse, a system that needs cable only at the door and the power supply can spare every finished room from a new wall opening. But a phone system is still the building's entry system, and in a building with tenants the city expects it to behave like one.

The first expectation is that it keeps working. The Housing Maintenance Code makes the owner keep what is installed in good repair, and HPD writes intercom violations under §27-2005 whatever the technology. An app system that loses its internet connection, depends on a lapsed subscription or has an admin login that left with the last managing agent is, to an inspector, a broken intercom. When we install one, we plan for those failures in advance: the account is in the owner's name, the login is written down where the owner keeps building documents, the panel can fall back to a cellular line where the building's internet is unreliable, and any tenant who needs it gets a wired station as a backup.

The second is privacy. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use keyless entry such as apps, fobs, cards or biometrics. In HPD's summary, the owner needs a tenant's express consent, in writing or through the app, before collecting the data the system uses; the data is limited to a short list such as name, unit, contact preference, lease information and entry times; authentication data has to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized; the data cannot be sold; and an owner cannot require a tenant to use a smart access system to get into their own apartment. Owners must give tenants a written policy. A three-family rowhouse where the owner lives on the garden floor and rents two apartments is a multiple dwelling for these purposes, so the rules apply to more Boerum Hill houses than people expect.

None of that makes a phone system a bad fit here. It means it has to be set up with the rules in mind. When we install one in a building with tenants, we configure it to collect the minimum, keep a mechanical key working on every door, and give the owner the system's data and retention settings in writing so the policy can be written from facts. The policy itself is the owner's responsibility, and for a larger building it is worth a lawyer's review. For small houses the choice is usually practical rather than legal: if every resident carries a smartphone and the owner lives elsewhere, phone answering works well; if one tenant uses only a landline or simply does not want an app, a wired station in that apartment solves it, and most systems can run both side by side.

Compliance points that apply to Boerum Hill entry installations

  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments, erected or converted after January 1, 1968, must have a self-locking main door and an intercommunication system with voice contact and remote release. Older buildings of that size must install them if tenants in a majority of apartments request or consent in writing, with work begun within 90 days and completed within six months.
  • Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005. Owners keep what is installed in good repair. Every intercom violation we found on Boerum Hill's streets since 2020 was written under this section as an order to properly repair or replace the broken or defective system.
  • Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021). Keyless entry in Class A multiple dwellings needs consent, a written policy, limited data, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, no sale of data, and no requirement to use smart access to enter one's own apartment.
  • Landmarks Preservation Commission. In the Boerum Hill Historic District and the 2018 extension, LPC staff can approve an intercom or doorbell within or beside the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, one intercom per opening, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and never through cast iron or decorative elements, with conduit concealed.
  • Electrically locked egress doors. The NYC Building Code requires sensor release on the egress side, release on loss of power, a manual push-to-exit device mounted 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and release when a fire alarm or sprinkler system activates. Fire-rated doors take fail-secure strikes.
  • Accessible reach. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, operable parts such as call buttons sit within a high forward reach of 48 inches and a low reach of 15 inches. Business entrances in particular should have the station within that range wherever the front allows it.
  • Electrical work. Our work is low-voltage entry equipment under NYS license #12000287431. Where a system needs a new 120-volt circuit or outlet, a licensed electrician provides it under the Department of Buildings' electrical rules, and we say so before the price is fixed.

New buildings and lost bells

I'm about to rent in a brand-new three-unit building in Brooklyn that has no buzzer and one shared mailbox. Is that allowed?

Usually, yes. A renter asked almost exactly this on Reddit about a newly built three-unit Brooklyn building with a first-time landlord. The Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom requirement applies to buildings with eight or more apartments, so a three-unit building can legally be built and rented without one. Mail is a separate question between the landlord and the Postal Service, so ask about it directly. What matters for you is practical. Without a buzzer, every visitor and courier either calls your phone or waits at the door, and the street door is more likely to be propped. Before signing, it is fair to ask the landlord whether an entry system is planned and to put the answer in writing. For an owner, a three-apartment audio system on our Brooklyn list sits in the $400 to $900 range and video in the $700 to $1,800 range, and in a new building with open walls it is at the easier end of the job.

Our renovation contractor cut the old bell wire. Who pays to put the buzzer back?

That depends on who did the work and what was agreed. In a house you own, it is a question between you and your contractor: if the renovation scope said existing low-voltage systems would be protected or reinstated, the contractor should cover it; if the contract is silent, it is usually a negotiation. In a co-op or condo, Habitat magazine's February 2024 article on a shareholder who lost the intercom after a downstairs renovation is a useful guide. The lawyers it spoke with agreed the co-op had to restore service, and one noted the downstairs neighbor might be responsible under the alteration agreement and could in turn have a claim against their contractor. For tenants, the owner has to keep the installed system in repair under the Housing Maintenance Code regardless of who broke it. Whoever pays, the practical step is the same: get a written diagnosis and price first, so the conversation is about a real number. We can give you both a repair and a replacement figure from one $250 diagnostic visit, which is credited to the work.

Doors, vestibules and alarms

A neighbor was cornered in her vestibule between two locked doors. How should our doors be set up so that can't happen?

A Brownstoner forum member described exactly that: being trapped and robbed between the outer and inner doors of her own house, and afterwards wanting an inner lock that was not slow or fiddly to open. The arrangement that protects residents best is one where the outer door is the one released from upstairs, the inner door opens quickly with a key or a code for someone coming home, and the vestibule is visible from inside, usually through a video station or a small camera. That way a resident who senses trouble on the stoop can get through the inner door fast, and a visitor let in by mistake is still held in a locked space. We avoid setups where the inner door needs two separate locks or a slow keypad to open from outside, and we never fit a release that would trap anyone inside the vestibule: both doors must always open freely from the inside.

Can the panel or a reader at our street door also turn off our house alarm when we come in?

Often it can, and it is worth planning for if you have an alarm or are adding one. On a Brownstoner thread about rowhouse security, a fire-alarm installer suggested a card reader at the outer door that would release both doors and disarm the alarm in one step. Many entry and alarm systems can be linked that way, using a code or credential that the alarm accepts as a disarm signal. The details depend on the alarm panel: some accept a signal from the door controller, some need an add-on module, and older panels may not support it at all. We check the make and model of your alarm on the walkthrough and tell you whether linking is possible and what it adds to the price. Visitors pressing the call button never disarm anything; only a resident's code or credential does.

The latch on our old stoop door is worn. Would a motorized lock be better than an electric strike?

Sometimes. An electric strike only works as well as the latch it releases, a point the same Brownstoner thread made when it noted that typical strikes "rely on the door handle latch." On a nineteenth-century door with a worn latch, a sagging leaf or a pair of doors that no longer meet squarely, the strike will bind or let the door pull open. The first fix is the door itself: hinges tightened, the leaf rehung, the keeper moved. If the lock is past saving, a replacement lockset with a motorized latch or bolt can be released by the buzzer and still opened with a key, and it avoids cutting a deep pocket into an old frame. It costs more than a strike, and on a landmarked front it has to keep the door's appearance. We tell you on the walkthrough which one your door can take.

Packages and visitors

Our landlord's answer to stolen packages was another lock on the front door. Would video at the door have done more?

For packages, usually yes. A Reddit poster described a New York landlord adding extra locks after package thefts while tenants asked for a video intercom so residents could see who was at the door. More locks make the door harder for couriers too, so boxes end up on the stoop, which is where thieves look. On Bond Street, Brooklyn Paper reported a thief who opened a townhouse's front gate and took about $3,500 in parcels. What helps is giving couriers a safe place inside: a release on the outer door so a tenant can let a driver into the vestibule from a phone, a camera covering the vestibule and stoop, and a household that can answer while out. A video station also cuts down on tenants buzzing in voices they do not know. It will not stop every theft, but it moves the package from the sidewalk to behind a locked door.

Our Smith Street restaurant takes produce deliveries at the side door before we open. Can a buzzer let the driver in without someone running upstairs?

Yes. The usual setup is a door station at the delivery door that calls a monitor in the kitchen or office, or a phone, with a release on that door so whoever is working can see the driver and let them in. If deliveries come at a regular time, some controllers can also hold the door unlocked for a short window, though for a door into a kitchen most owners prefer a person to see each driver. The door has to keep letting staff out freely at all times, so the release is chosen with exit rules in mind, and the station is mounted where a driver with a hand truck can reach it. If the building has apartments upstairs, the restaurant's system is kept completely separate from the tenants', so drivers never ring an apartment by mistake.

Can one smart video doorbell on our stoop work for both households in a two-family?

Not well. A consumer video doorbell has one button and one account, so a visitor for the tenant rings the owner too, and both households have to share one app login or one set of notifications. It also cannot release the door for both apartments without extra hardware. For a two-family, a two-button video station, with a separate monitor or app account for each household and a release both can trigger, does the job properly, and on our Brooklyn list a video system for a small building is in the $700 to $1,800 range. If you already own a smart doorbell you like, it can stay at the garden door for the owner's household while the shared station goes at the parlor door, which is a common split on Boerum Hill two-families.

Records, buyers and walk-ups

The same walk-up keeps getting HPD intercom violations year after year. Why would a repaired system keep failing?

Because it is usually being repaired one apartment at a time. HPD's records for one Nevins Street walk-up show six intercom violations between August 2023 and October 2026, written against different apartments: a foyer on the fourth floor, kitchens on the second. When each complaint is fixed by replacing that apartment's handset or splicing that apartment's wire, the next weak point fails a few months later. The repeat pattern usually means an aging power supply, a riser with failing joints in the cellar, or a mix of handset models that were never designed to work together. The way out is to test the whole system once: every pair from the panel to each apartment, the power supply under load, and the release. Sometimes that points to a riser and a power supply; sometimes it points to a new system. Either way the owner gets a plan that ends the cycle instead of another visit.

What should a buyer check on a Boerum Hill rowhouse's buzzer before closing?

Five things, most of which a buyer can do in ten minutes at the showing. Press each button and listen in each apartment for the call. Talk both ways and listen for hum or crackle. Press release from every apartment and watch the strike actually let the latch pass. Close each entrance door and check that it latches by itself. And ask the seller for any login, model number or installer's name, because an app system with no admin login is a system you will have to replace. If the building has eight or more apartments, also look up the address in HPD's violation records for open intercom violations, since those pass to the new owner. If anything fails, a $250 diagnostic visit, credited toward the work, gives you a written repair or replacement figure to bring to the negotiation.

Our small Boerum Hill condo board wants to replace a dated audio intercom. Who decides, and what should the board get in writing?

The board decides, within whatever the condominium's bylaws say about common elements and spending, and in many small condos a larger expense needs a vote of the owners. The entry system is almost always a common element, so it is the association's to maintain and replace. What the board should get in writing is not just a price. It should be a description of what each apartment will have, whether existing cable is reused and how it was tested, who will hold the admin account and the login, whether there is any recurring fee and who pays it, what happens if the building's internet is down, and what the warranty covers. With that in hand, owners who were not on the walkthrough can compare a wired system with a cloud system on real terms. Boerum Hill Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Do you need to get into every apartment during the installation?
+

Yes. Each apartment's station has to be mounted, connected and tested for call, talk and release, so the technician needs a few minutes inside every unit on installation day. If one apartment cannot be reached, that unit cannot be finished, so owners should arrange access with tenants in advance.

Do you remove the old transformer and the dead bell wire, or leave them in place?
+

The old transformer and any exposed dead wire in the cellar are disconnected and removed so nobody mistakes them for the new system later. Dead wire sealed inside finished walls is cut back and left, because pulling it out would mean opening plaster for no benefit.

Can the door station's finish match the black ironwork or brass on our stoop?
+

Usually. Door stations come in black, silver, bronze and brushed finishes, and some makers offer surface boxes or hoods in matching colors. On a landmarked front the LPC also expects a finish compatible with the building, so we show you the finish options at the walkthrough.

Is adjusting the street door's closer part of a buzzer installation?
+

Yes. A release only works if the door closes and latches by itself, so setting the closer, tightening hinges and moving the keeper are part of the job. If the closer is broken or missing, a replacement is listed on the quote as its own line.

If one apartment's handset gets damaged, do the other apartments keep working?
+

On a properly wired system, yes. Each station is wired and labeled so a damaged handset can be disconnected and replaced without taking down the rest of the house, and the wiring map shows exactly which pair serves which apartment.

Do tenants have to buy anything for the new system?
+

No. The door station, the apartment stations and the release are part of the owner's installation. Tenants who choose phone answering only need the free app on their own phone, and anyone who prefers not to use an app gets a wired station instead.

If we sell the house, how is an app-based entry system handed to the new owner?
+

The admin account should be transferred, not shared. Before closing, the seller hands over the login or transfers ownership in the vendor's portal, and the buyer changes the password and resets resident access. We can do the transfer on a short visit if the seller has lost the details.

Can the release be switched off at night so nobody can be buzzed in after a set hour?
+

On many systems, yes. A controller or app schedule can disable remote release during hours you choose while calls still ring through, so a resident can still talk to a late visitor and walk down to open the door with a key.

Can we see the actual door station before it is mounted on our front?
+

Yes. We send the model's photos and dimensions with the quote, and on request we hold the station against the door frame at the walkthrough so you can judge the size and position before anything is drilled.

Can a cleaner or dog walker get a temporary code instead of a key?
+

On keypad and app systems, yes. The owner can create a code or app pass that works only on certain days or hours and delete it when it is no longer needed. Audio and video systems without a keypad do not offer codes, so we mention this when you choose the system.

Is there a minimum size of job you take in Boerum Hill?
+

No. We install single-button stations for one-family rowhouses as well as systems for walk-ups and condos. Small jobs are priced from the same Brooklyn list, starting in the audio range for a small house.

Can the door station show our house number so couriers find the right stoop at night?
+

Many stations have a backlit label or number field, and some video panels can display the address on screen. On a landmarked front we keep any lit lettering small and inside the door opening, and a separate house number near the door may need its own LPC check.
