The Neighborhood

Why Melrose Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Melrose is a neighborhood where public cameras and private buildings sit side by side, sometimes on opposite sides of the same street. Wikipedia draws it as a rectangle: Saint Anns Avenue on the east, East 149th Street on the south, Park Avenue on the west and East 163rd Street on the north, with Melrose Avenue and Third Avenue as its main streets. It belongs to Bronx Community Board 1, uses the ZIP codes 10451, 10455 and 10456, and is patrolled by the 40th Precinct from its station house at 567 East 149th Street. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as dominated by large housing complexes, mostly public housing, and tenement-style apartment buildings, and Homes.com adds the attached three-story houses that line some streets. The Department of City Planning's Melrose report captures the split best on Courtlandt Avenue: one side lined with three- to five-story buildings with stores on the ground floor, the other given over to NYCHA superblocks with 15- and 16-story towers set back from the street. City lot data for the census tracts HPD tags as Melrose counts 1,216 residential lots holding 18,261 homes. For a camera plan, that mix matters more than any single number. A walk-up with a store downstairs, a new 12-story rental, a townhouse and a public housing tower can all share one block, and each has a different owner, a different set of doors and a different person who decides where a camera can go.

The local reporting shows why the camera conversation here has moved past "should we have them" to "where exactly do they need to be." News 12 reported that tenants told Council Member Salamanca in 2017 about crime and safety problems at Melrose Houses, that he secured $3 million in the next year's budget for lights, cameras, and roof and door repairs, and that the 157-camera system arrived after more than five years of waiting. Private buildings have had their own trouble. In September 2026 a building employee was shot around 11:30 a.m. inside 508 East 163rd Street while confronting a man allegedly trying to steal work supplies. CBS New York reported an 85-year-old woman assaulted in a building lobby near East 149th Street and Morris Avenue on a Sunday afternoon, caught on video, and in October 2026, surveillance video of a person in black appearing to open fire outside a strip of businesses near 726 Courtlandt Avenue. News 12 reported two men who talked their way into a building at Courtlandt Avenue and East 150th Street by claiming they had been sent to fix a leak, and left with a safe; a group robbery of JD Sports at 410 Westchester Avenue just before 6:45 p.m. on December 4 in which employees were attacked; and, in December 2025, surveillance video of the moments before a shooting in Melrose. We are not ranking any block, and these cases do not describe every building. What they show is that back-of-house rooms, lobbies in broad daylight, storefront sidewalks, visitors with a convincing story and crowded store floors are where a Melrose camera plan has to do its work.

Four sets of rules decide where cameras can go. The first is permission. Brick Underground notes that renters need the landlord's permission to put any equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside their own door, and in public housing the housing authority's lease decides; a tenant at the Bronx's Parkside Houses was once served an eviction notice that said her cameras breached her lease. In a building owned by a nonprofit sponsor and run by a managing agent, both usually need to agree. The second is landmarks. The 614 Courtlandt Avenue Building and the Bronx Borough Courthouse are designated city landmarks, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules treat security cameras as utility equipment, with proposed limits on placing that kind of equipment on a primary facade. The third is permits: Seneca Security explains that most camera installations do not need a Buildings Department permit on their own, but electrical work, conduit in common areas of a co-op or condo, or tying the cameras into fire alarm or access control systems can bring permits into the picture. The fourth is fire and privacy. Nothing we mount touches a self-closing door, its closer or its frame; every opening through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop; cameras never go inside an apartment; and outdoor views are framed so they show doors, walks and sidewalks, not other people's windows.

Melrose facts that shape a camera plan

  • Public cameras arrived slowly and cover public property: the 157 cameras at Melrose Houses came more than five years after tenants first raised safety problems, and they watch NYCHA grounds, not the private building across the street.
  • Daylight incidents: the 508 East 163rd Street shooting was late morning, the lobby assault near Morris Avenue was a Sunday afternoon and the JD Sports robbery was early evening, so cameras need a clean daytime picture as much as a night one.
  • Two kinds of street on one avenue: City Planning describes Courtlandt Avenue as three- to five-story buildings with ground-floor stores on one side and 15- to 16-story NYCHA towers on the other.
  • A large rebuilt stock: Melrose leads the borough in LEED-certified apartment buildings, which means many concrete-and-block buildings with sealed walls, tight ceilings and envelopes that must not be left leaking after a cable goes through.
  • The borough's oldest shopping hub: the Hub, where East 149th Street, Willis, Melrose and Third Avenues meet, is the oldest major shopping area in the Bronx; Bronx Times reports the Third Avenue BID counts 600-plus businesses, about 17.5 percent of storefronts vacant, and safety as a top priority.
  • Landmarks in the middle of it: 614 Courtlandt Avenue and the Bronx Borough Courthouse are individual city landmarks, and the Morris High School Historic District and the Mott Haven historic districts sit at the edges.

What Melrose owners often add next. A building that starts with cameras at the lobby and the service door often comes back asking whether the lobby view can show on the video intercom, or whether the supply room door can go on a fob so only staff open it. Store owners on Third Avenue usually ask next about an alarm on the stock room. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port at the recorder for that step, so the ceiling only has to be opened once.

Whose Camera Is Watching? Mapping the Gaps on a Melrose Block

Most neighborhoods we work in have one or two kinds of camera on a block: the owner's and the shopkeeper's. Melrose has more. Public housing developments run their own systems; the business improvement district has planned cameras for the Hub; new buildings come with whatever the developer chose; and stores, houses and older walk-ups have whatever their owners added over the years, if anything. Each of those systems answers to a different owner, records to a different place and was aimed for a different purpose. A clip that would settle what happened at your door may exist on someone else's recorder, or it may not exist at all. So the first job on a Melrose walk-through is to note who already watches the block and what their cameras were set up to see, and then put your cameras where only you can. The back of the house: supply rooms, workshops and staff areas

The shooting inside 508 East 163rd Street began, according to News 12, when a building employee confronted a man allegedly trying to steal work supplies. Nobody can say a camera would have changed what happened that morning, and we will not pretend otherwise. What a camera on a supply room, a super's workshop or a maintenance closet does change is the reason anyone has to walk up to a stranger. If the door to the room is watched by a face camera, and the room itself by an overview, staff can see from the office or a phone who went in and what they carried out, call the police with a description and a time, and stay out of arm's reach. In a rental building, these rooms are often on the cellar level or behind a door off the lobby that residents never use, and they are almost never on the developer's original camera list. We add them on the same recorder as the lobby, with a short delay alert during hours when nobody on staff should be inside, so the super's phone shows the door opening at the moment it opens, not the next morning.

The lobby at three in the afternoon, not only three in the morning

A lot of camera advice is built around night: infrared, low light, motion at two in the morning. The Melrose cases we found tilt the other way. The lobby assault near East 149th Street and Morris Avenue was on a Sunday afternoon. The JD Sports robbery was just before 6:45 p.m. The 508 East 163rd Street shooting was around 11:30 in the morning. Daytime brings its own problems. A new lobby with a glass street front fills with afternoon light, and an ordinary camera facing the door turns everyone walking in into a dark outline. A busy lobby also means constant motion, so a recorder set to save only motion clips can fill its drive in days and start writing over the week before anyone asks for it. So on a Melrose lobby we test each view in strong daylight as well as after dark, use cameras with wide dynamic range where glass faces the sun, set continuous recording rather than motion-only on the entrance cameras, and size the drive for the number of days the owner actually needs.

The sidewalk in front of a store: a few yards that belong to you

CBS New York's report from outside the strip of businesses near 726 Courtlandt Avenue rested on surveillance video. Whose camera recorded it is not ours to say. The point for any store on Courtlandt, Melrose or Third Avenue is that the sidewalk in front of a shop is where trouble on the block is most likely to cross a private lens, and that only a camera mounted on the store's own façade, with the landlord's agreement, and aimed along the frontage records it clearly. That camera is different from the door camera inside. It sits high, in a hard housing, looking along the gate and the sidewalk for a few storefronts' width in each direction, not across the avenue at buildings that are too far away to identify anyone. It records continuously. It never goes on a lamppost, a street sign or any city structure. And because the store next door may have aimed theirs the other way, we ask about the neighbors' cameras during the walk, so the two views meet rather than leave a gap between them.

The door a good story can open

The two men News 12 reported at Courtlandt Avenue and East 150th Street did not break anything to get in. They said they had been sent to fix a leak. A camera cannot judge whether a visitor is telling the truth, but it can make the story checkable, both in the moment and afterward. A face camera inside the street door, at head height, records each visitor clearly as they step in. A view of the vestibule shows who let them through and how. Where the building has a video intercom, the lobby camera's view can sit next to the intercom picture, so whoever answers sees more than one face in a small screen. And because every camera is named by door and the recorder keeps its clock accurate, a super who later wants to know who came in claiming to be from a plumbing company can find the clip by time and door in a minute, then hand it to the managing agent or the police without scrolling through a whole day.

Walk-ups with stores below: two doors, two systems, one cellar

The buildings City Planning describes on the residential side of Courtlandt Avenue put two very different doors a few feet apart: the shop entrance, busy all day, and the residential door beside it, which should only open for people who live there. The camera plan treats them as two jobs. The building's camera inside the residential vestibule is aimed through the inner door at head height, so the face it records belongs to someone stepping into the building, not a customer walking past. The store's cameras belong to the store, on its own recorder, unless the owner and the tenant agree in writing to share. Both systems usually run through the same cellar, so we label every cable with its owner as well as its door, run the building's cameras in their own path and leave a written note of which run belongs to whom. That way, when the store changes tenants, nobody cuts the cable to the building's stair camera by mistake.

Older tenement walk-ups: cellar, stair, yard

In a pre-war walk-up the cellar holds the meters, the internet provider's box and the boiler, so it is where the recorder usually goes, in a locked, dry spot away from the boiler's heat and away from any cellar door that opens to the street or yard. From there, cable for the vestibule and lobby climbs a short way, cable for the landings goes up a pipe chase or a stacked closet if there is one, and only where there is truly no hidden path do we use slim raceway painted to match the stair hall. Every floor the cable crosses is sealed with rated firestop. Before anything is cut, we trace the old wiring in the cellar, because these buildings often carry intercom, phone and abandoned camera runs from decades of different owners, and the tenants' buzzers must keep working while we are there.

Renewal-era rentals: block walls, riser closets and changed rooms

Many of the buildings that went up after the 1994 renewal plan are concrete or block structures with drywall corridors and lay-in or drywall ceilings. That usually makes cable easier to route than in a walk-up: above the corridor ceiling to a riser closet, down the riser to the recorder room. The hard part is the block. Where a camera has to reach a supply room, a stair or a cellar on the far side of a block wall, we plan one cored opening, sealed afterward, rather than a row of small holes. The other thing we look for in these buildings is change. A room drawn as storage may now be a tenant association office; a side door that was meant for emergency exit only may now be the way staff take out trash. The developer's camera plan followed the drawings. Ours follows the building as it is used today.

Hub stores: door, floor, counter, stock and frontage

A store on the Hub needs five views more than anything else. The door camera, just inside and at head height, records a clear face of each person as they walk in, which is the single most useful picture a store can keep. Floor overviews from the ceiling corners show where people go once inside, and on a long, narrow Third Avenue storefront that means at least one camera looking back from the rear wall toward the door, so the back of the store is not a blind spot. The counter camera sits above and slightly in front of the register, showing hands, the drawer and the customer. The stock room camera watches the door and the shelves where the expensive goods are kept. The frontage camera, mounted high on the façade with the landlord's written agreement, looks along the gate and sidewalk. The recorder goes into a locked wall box or above the ceiling in a back corner, away from the counter, on a battery backup, with key clips copied off site so a robbery that includes the recorder does not erase the evidence.

Busy evenings, crowded floors and staff who work alone

The JD Sports robbery News 12 reported happened just before 6:45 p.m., with several people involved and employees attacked. When a group comes in together, a single wide camera shows a crowd and identifies nobody. What helps is a tight face view at the door, where each person passes one at a time, plus a view of the display wall or the most-targeted shelf, so the clip shows who took what. Restaurants on Melrose Avenue have a different evening problem: dim dining rooms, where ordinary cameras switch to black and white. Cameras built for low light keep a color picture there. Where one clerk works a counter alone, as in many small shops, we put a monitor where the clerk can see the door and the back of the store from the register, and a camera in the stock room, so they never have to leave the counter to check who is back there.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Melrose

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, exam rooms and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we check every view so a mirror or a glass partition does not show one.
  • The landlord decides outside the store. The façade, the sign band, the gate housing and shared halls belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement before mounting anything there.
  • City structures are off limits. Nothing private goes on lampposts, street signs, bus shelters or any transit structure; sidewalk views come from your own wall.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is mounted on a self-closing door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it latching.
  • Openings stay rated. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Landmarks need their permit. On designated buildings such as 614 Courtlandt Avenue and the Bronx Borough Courthouse, exterior camera positions need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before any drilling.
  • Audio stays off. We set business cameras to record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • Tying into other systems can mean a permit. If the cameras connect to fire alarm or access control equipment, or need new electrical work or conduit in common areas, we check permit needs before work starts.
  • The installer should be licensed and insured. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords, managing agents and insurers usually ask to see first.

Buildings people live in

The building worker shot at 508 East 163rd Street was confronting someone allegedly taking work supplies. Where should cameras go in a supply room or workshop so staff never have to walk up to a stranger?

Put one camera on the door to the room, inside, at a height and angle that records the face of whoever comes through, and a second overview from a high corner that shows the shelves and anyone carrying things out. Connect both to the building's recorder and set an alert for door motion during hours nobody on staff should be in there, sent to the super's phone and the managing agent's on-call number. Then write a short instruction for staff: if the alert shows someone in the room who should not be, do not go down; call 911 with the description and the time from the camera, and save the clip. The camera does not make anyone safe on its own. It gives staff a reason to keep their distance and a record to hand over.

Our new lobby has a glass street front. Why do people coming in look like dark outlines on the recording in the afternoon?

Because the camera is facing a bright window with a dimmer room around it, and it sets its exposure for the brightness behind the person. The face falls into shadow. Cameras with true wide dynamic range handle this much better, balancing the bright street and the darker lobby in the same picture. Placement matters too: a camera mounted slightly to the side of the door, looking across the entrance rather than straight out through the glass, sees less sky and more face. On some lobbies, the best answer is a camera at the inner vestibule door, where the light is even, rather than at the street door at all. We check this in the afternoon during the walk-through, because a view that looks fine at 10 a.m. can fail at 4 p.m.

Our building's residential door is right beside the store entrance. How do you keep shoppers from setting off the building's door camera all day?

Three things together. First, aim the building's camera inside the vestibule, looking at the inner door, so the frame contains only people who have actually come into the building, not the sidewalk in front of the shop. Second, draw a detection zone on the recorder around the threshold itself, so movement elsewhere in the picture does not count as an event. Third, record continuously on that camera anyway, so nothing depends on the alert, and send alerts to the super only during the hours the door is normally quiet. The store's own cameras stay on the store's recorder. If the owner and the store tenant want to share a view of the frontage, that is written down in an agreement, with a note of who can export clips from it.

Some apartments in our building are set aside for survivors of domestic violence. Who should be able to see the camera footage, and how is it protected?

Fewer people than in an ordinary building, and every one of them named. Bronx Commons, for example, includes apartments for survivors of domestic violence, and buildings like it need the camera system to protect residents' locations as carefully as their doors. In practice, that means separate accounts for the managing agent, the super and the social-service provider, each limited to the cameras they need; no shared passwords; remote viewing turned off for anyone who does not need it; two-step sign-in on every account; a recorder in a locked room; and a written rule about who may export a clip and to whom. Cameras that face the street are framed so they do not reveal which apartment someone goes to. Ask your service provider what their own policies require, and we set the system up to match.

A nonprofit sponsor owns our building, a management firm runs it and a service provider has staff on site. Whose name should the recorder's main account be in?

The owner's. The main administrator account is the one that can add users, delete footage and change settings, so it belongs to the entity that owns the system, usually the sponsor or its housing company, with the password kept in the owner's records, not on one person's phone. The management firm gets a manager account that can view, export and manage day-to-day users. The super gets live views and limited playback. The service provider gets the views its staff need for their work, nothing more. When the management firm changes, as happens over a building's life, the owner removes its account and creates a new one, and no footage or settings are lost. We write this access list into the handover, so the next manager inherits a working system instead of a mystery.

Our building was financed through a city housing program and has a regulatory agreement. Does that agreement require cameras or set rules for them?

We cannot tell you without reading it, and nobody should guess. Many Melrose buildings were built or rehabilitated with public financing, and each agreement is its own document. Some include security or management plan requirements; others say nothing about cameras. The practical step is to ask your asset manager or the program's contact whether the agreement or the approved management plan mentions security cameras, notice to tenants, or approvals for building changes, and to get that answer in writing before equipment is ordered. Our written plan, with each camera's position, coverage and who can view it, is the kind of document that answers those questions if they come up, and we prepare it for every building job anyway.

Our building stands on land shared with a NYCHA development. Can our outside cameras cover the walkways NYCHA maintains?

Your cameras should cover your building, its doors and its own grounds. Walkways and spaces that belong to or are maintained by NYCHA are NYCHA's to decide on, and the shared arrangement on a campus is usually spelled out in an agreement between the owners. Melrose North, for example, was built on the Morrisania Air Rights campus. Before aiming any camera across a shared path, ask your owner or asset manager what the campus agreement says, and if in doubt, frame the camera to stop at your property line, using privacy masking on any part of the picture that falls beyond it. If both owners want shared coverage, that is a conversation between them, and we can show each side exactly what a proposed view would include.

Our building is still operating on a temporary certificate of occupancy. Can the owner add cameras before the final one is issued?

Usually the question is less about the certificate than about who controls the building's finishes and open items. Bronx Times reported that 450 East 158th Street has run on a temporary certificate since July 2026, and buildings in that stage often still have a contractor's warranty on walls, ceilings and doors, plus open items with the Buildings Department. Before we add anything, the owner should check with the developer's team or expediter that the work will not touch an open item, a rated assembly still under inspection, or a finish under warranty. Low-voltage cameras that do not involve new electrical work or fire alarm connections are often the easiest addition, but we do not promise that for your building until the team that built it agrees in writing.

Our building is certified Passive House. How does camera cable get through the outside wall without breaking the air seal?

Ideally it does not cross the outside wall at all. In many tight buildings, exterior cameras can be fed through existing sleeves, through the roof's equipment penetrations or from inside a sheltered entrance, without a new hole in the envelope. Where a new opening is unavoidable, it is planned with the building's engineer or the envelope consultant, kept as small as the cable allows, and sealed with materials suited to the wall system, inside and out. We also keep power-hungry equipment such as recorders and switches out of sealed closets that were not designed for the heat. Melrose has the borough's largest number of LEED-certified apartment buildings, so this comes up often, and it is worth asking before anyone drills.

Stores on the Hub and the avenues

The group that robbed the store at 410 Westchester Avenue came in together. When several people rush a store at once, how many cameras does it take to identify each of them?

Not more cameras so much as the right two or three. A wide ceiling view of a crowd shows that a robbery happened but rarely identifies anyone. The camera that does that work is a tight face view at the entrance, where people pass through one or two at a time, set at head height and recording continuously at a high enough resolution to see features. A second camera aimed at the most targeted display, such as a sneaker wall or a phone case, shows who took what. A third, at the exit if it differs from the entrance, catches faces leaving. Overviews then tie the clips together. A store our size might need four to six cameras in all, but the door camera matters most, and it is the one most often mounted too high.

Our Third Avenue storefront is long and narrow. Where do the cameras go so the back of the store is not a blind spot?

Use the length of the room. A camera at the front, inside the door, records faces coming in. A camera on the rear wall, high and looking back toward the door, shows the whole sales floor from behind and anyone who goes toward the back. If shelving runs down the middle, one more camera at the midpoint looking along each aisle covers the gap. The counter, wherever it sits, gets its own view. The stock room or back door gets a camera on the door from inside. In a narrow store, a varifocal lens on the rear camera lets us zoom so the front door area is still clear at the far end, rather than a tiny figure in the middle of a wide shot.

Our shop is one of several in a single-story strip. Where should our outside camera stop and our neighbor's start?

At your frontage, roughly, with a little overlap. Your outside camera, mounted on your own façade with the landlord's agreement, looks along the sidewalk in front of your store and a short way past each edge, so someone approaching your door from either side is recorded before they arrive. It should not try to cover the whole strip, because at that distance nobody is identifiable. If your neighbors have cameras, ask which way theirs point; where your views meet at the shared edge, the strip has no gap. Some strips agree informally to check footage for each other after an incident. That is the owners' choice, and each store keeps control of its own recorder and decides what it shares.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Melrose

Quick answers from search tools and cost sites tend to flatten Melrose into a generic Bronx neighborhood. These are claims we hear repeated, and what the local sources and the field say instead.

"Melrose already has plenty of public cameras, so private ones add little."

It is true that public cameras have grown here. NYCHA unveiled a 157-camera system at Melrose Houses, and Bronx Times reports that the Third Avenue BID's plan includes dozens of new cameras and more than 50 new light fixtures along the Hub.

But those systems were built for public housing grounds and public space. The incidents in the recent news happened inside a private building's rooms, in a private lobby and outside private storefronts, places those cameras were never aimed at, and whose footage is not yours to pull when you need it.

So a Melrose camera plan starts by noting what public cameras already show and then covering what only the owner can: the doors, rooms and frontage of your own property, recorded on a recorder you control.

"A new building comes wired with every camera it needs."

New buildings in Melrose often advertise security, and Bronx Times notes that the new buildings on East 158th Street include 24-hour security among their features. A developer's camera list usually covers the lobby, the entrances and perhaps the package area.

That list follows the drawings, not the way the building is used once people move in. Supply rooms, workshops, side doors that became staff exits and rooms that changed purpose rarely appear on it, and the 508 East 163rd Street case shows how much can happen in rooms like those.

The fix is not to replace a good system but to add to it: a walk-through with the super, a list of the rooms staff actually use, and cameras on the same recorder for each of them.

"You can put a camera anywhere on a building's front."

On most Melrose buildings, the owner or landlord decides where exterior cameras go. On designated landmarks, that is not the whole story.

The 614 Courtlandt Avenue Building and the Bronx Borough Courthouse are city landmarks, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules treat security cameras as health, safety and utility equipment, with proposed limits on placing that kind of equipment on a primary facade. The Commission's permit guidebook has its own section on cameras.

We read the current guidebook for any designated property before we suggest a position, keep cameras small and positioned where the rules point, and apply for the permit before anyone drills. Buildings at the edges of the Morris High School and Mott Haven historic districts get the same check.

"Every wired camera job needs a licensed electrician."

One New York installer writes that the city requires licensed electricians for wired camera installation. Another says the license that matters is the state Security or Fire Alarm Installer License from the Department of State. A Department of State application, meanwhile, says installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require that license at all.

The sources do not agree, and we do not paper over that. What is clear is that new electrical work, such as adding an outlet or a circuit, belongs to a licensed electrician, and low-voltage camera cable powered over the network is a different kind of work.

AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and where a job needs electrical work we say so on the quote before it starts. Landlords, boards and insurers in Melrose usually ask to see both.

"Camera installations never need a permit."

It is a common shortcut answer, and for a simple job it is often close to right. Seneca Security explains that most camera installations do not need a Buildings Department permit on their own.

The same source lists what changes that: electrical work, conduit in the common areas of a co-op or condo, and integration with fire alarm or access control systems. In Melrose's newer buildings, cameras are often expected to tie into the front desk, the access control or the fire alarm, so those exceptions come up.

We check every job for those triggers during the walk-through and put the answer in writing. If a permit is needed, it is on the plan before any work begins, not discovered halfway through.

"One per-camera price fits every job."

In Melrose, the cost is set by things a per-camera figure ignores: how far each camera is from the recorder, how many block walls and floors the cable crosses, whether a green building's envelope is involved, whether a landmark permit is needed, and how many people need their own accounts.

That is why we quote each Melrose job in writing after photos or a walk-through, with every camera, mount, cable run and task on its own line, at our Bronx rate, so you can see what each part of the job costs and why.

FAQ

Melrose Security Camera Installation FAQ

Can a renter in Melrose ask the landlord to add a camera in the hallway, and does the landlord have to say yes?
+

You can always ask, but the landlord is not generally obliged to agree. Brick Underground notes that renters need the landlord's permission to install equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside their own door, and Seneca Security says there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. A written request that explains the problem, such as a door that keeps being forced or repeated incidents on your floor, gives the owner a reason and a record. Inside your own apartment, a small indoor camera facing your door is usually your own decision.

Which police station handles a camera clip from Melrose, and what should we bring?
+

Melrose is patrolled by the 40th Precinct, whose station house is at 567 East 149th Street at Saint Anns Avenue. Bring the clip exported from the recorder to a USB stick, in the recorder's own file format with its time stamp intact, rather than a phone video of the screen. Note the camera name, the date and the exact start and end times. Keep the original on the recorder, protected from being written over, and keep a second copy yourself. If an officer asks for a different format, we can help export it.

Our camera job will connect to the building's fire alarm or door hardware. Does that change whether a permit is needed?
+

It can. Seneca Security explains that most camera installations do not need a Buildings Department permit on their own, but that electrical work, conduit in the common areas of a co-op or condo, or integration with fire alarm or access control systems can bring permit requirements into the picture. Many of Melrose's newer buildings expect cameras to work with the front desk, the door readers or the fire alarm, so we check each of those connections during the walk-through and write the answer into the plan before any work starts.

When masons repoint the brick on our facade, what happens to the cameras mounted on it?
+

Usually they come down for the work and go back up afterward. Masons need clear brick, and a camera left in place can be knocked off aim or coated with dust and mortar. Before the work starts, note which cameras are on the facade, save a picture of each one's view, and tell us the schedule. We take the cameras down, protect or coil the cable ends, and remount them on sound brick once the joints have cured, then check each view against the saved picture. Indoor cameras keep recording throughout, so the building is not blind while the scaffold is up.

Our insurer wants written proof of the camera system at renewal. What does AESS hand over?
+

A written handover that lists each camera by name and location, what it covers, the recorder model and where it is kept, how many days of footage it holds, whether it has battery backup and off-site copies, and the date of installation and last test. We include photos of the recorder and sample views from each camera if you want them. Ask your insurer exactly what they need on file, since requirements differ, and we can add a line confirming that every camera was tested by day and after dark before we left.

Our managing agent needs a certificate of insurance naming the owner before work starts. Can you send one ahead of the visit?
+

Yes. Send us the exact names the agent wants listed, usually the owner entity, the managing agent and sometimes the sponsor, plus the building address and any wording the management office requires. We request the certificate from our insurer and send it to the agent before the install date, so the crew is not held at the front desk. Larger Melrose buildings with a security desk often also want the names of the technicians coming; tell us and we include them.

Our entrance has front steps on one side and an accessible ramp on the other. Does each need a camera?
+

Usually yes, because people arriving by the ramp never pass the camera aimed at the steps. In many buildings the ramp leads to the same door, so one camera inside the door, framed for faces as people step through, covers everyone regardless of how they came up. Where the ramp ends at a separate door, that door gets its own face camera. Mounts stay clear of the ramp's handrails and landing, and no camera or cable is placed anywhere it narrows the route or creates something to bump into.

Our community room hosts tenant association meetings. Should the camera in that room be switched off during meetings?
+

Many buildings choose to, and it is a reasonable way to keep trust. The camera at the community room's door can keep recording so the building knows who came and went, while the camera inside the room is set to stop recording, or to black out the picture, on a schedule that matches meeting nights. Audio is off in any case. Write the arrangement down and share it with the tenant association, so nobody wonders whether their meeting was recorded. If the room is used for other programs, each use can have its own schedule.

People keep tagging the blank side wall of our building that faces an open lot. Can a camera catch them at night?
+

It can record them, if the wall has some light and the camera is close enough. Mount a camera high at the corner of the building, looking along the wall rather than out across the lot, so anyone standing at the wall is within a short distance of the lens. Use a camera that holds a color picture in low light, and add a motion light aimed at the wall if the lot is dark, which both improves the picture and makes tagging less comfortable. Record continuously, because a person painting moves slowly and may not trigger motion detection well.

Our complex has its own light poles in the plaza between buildings. Can cameras be mounted on them?
+

Often, if the poles belong to the property and the owner agrees. Private poles can give good overview positions across a plaza or courtyard, but each one needs power and a cable path back to a building, usually through the pole's base and an underground conduit, which may mean trenching. We check whether the pole carries spare conduit, whether its lighting circuit can be used for camera power by a licensed electrician, and whether the pole is sturdy enough not to sway in wind. City-owned poles on the sidewalk are never used.

How long does the walk-through take for a twelve-story building like the ones on Elton Avenue?
+

Plan on about one to two hours with the super or a staff member who has keys. We walk the entrances, the lobby, the service and cellar doors, the supply rooms, a sample of corridors, both stairs, the roof door, the riser closets and the recorder location, and look at the frontage from the sidewalk. If the building has a front desk, we spend a few minutes there to see what staff need on screen. A walk-up usually takes under an hour. The written quote follows once we have checked equipment for the views you need.

Can our townhouse's cameras be installed without opening finished walls inside?
+

In most townhouses, yes. The basement ceiling usually gives a path from the front of the house to the back, and cameras under the stoop canopy or at the rear wall can be fed through a single small opening near each mount, sealed afterward.
