The Neighborhood

Why Gravesend Properties Need Access Control

Gravesend is one of the oldest planned places in the city, but the doors we are asked about here are mostly new problems. Wikipedia describes the 1645 town as a sixteen-acre square inside a palisade, cut into four quarters by what are now McDonald Avenue and Gravesend Neck Road, and the plan is still visible around Village Road North, South and East. That is background. What brings an access control installer to Gravesend in 2026 is what has been built since: a dense run of congregations, schools and community buildings in the northeastern blocks, a wave of small new apartment buildings on the western streets toward the Shore Parkway, a handful of towers by the water, and storefronts on Avenue U, Kings Highway, McDonald Avenue and 86th Street that open behind roll-down gates every morning.

Each of those has the same underlying problem, which is that a metal key is a poor way to share a door. A key can be copied at a hardware counter, it cannot be switched off when someone leaves, it does not expire when an event ends, and it tells nobody who opened the door last night. In a one-family house that may not matter. In a building whose door is used by a hundred members, thirty staff, a rotation of vendors, eight households or a shift of cashiers, it matters a great deal, and it is the reason most of the Gravesend calls we get are about credentials before they are about hardware.

The northeastern part of the neighborhood shows this most clearly. 6sqft, writing in 2015, described the area as home to the largest Sephardic Jewish community in the United States, about 30,000 people by the Wall Street Journal’s count and mostly of Syrian origin, and placed its most expensive blocks around Avenues S, T and U, between synagogues on Ocean Parkway and a yeshiva on McDonald Avenue. The Brooklyn Eagle placed the cluster of large new houses within East 1st Street, Ocean Parkway, Kings Highway and Avenue U. Those blocks hold synagogues, study halls, schools, social halls and community offices, many of them with a main entrance that opens for services and a side or rear door that staff, members and caterers use every day. That second door is the one that usually ends up on an access system first.

Money for those doors has also changed. New York State’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program, run by the Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Services, lists physical access control systems among the equipment it funds, allows up to $200,000 per site, and gives religious organizations a scoring multiplier. In Brooklyn, UJA-Federation of New York’s Community Security Initiative created a fund in 2022 to put new doors, locks and cameras into at least fifty small synagogues, with access control training to follow. Grant money comes with rules about who can quote, when equipment may be bought and where it may be installed, and an installer who does not know them can cost a congregation its award. We explain the rules we have read below and leave the application itself to the organization and its grant adviser.

On the west side the change is construction. New York YIMBY reported in April 2026 that permits had been filed to turn a three-story building at 97 28th Avenue, between Cropsey and Harway Avenues, into a seven-story building with eight apartments, a cellar and six parking spaces, a short walk from the Bay 50th Street station on the D. Marketproof and YIMBY list similar filings on Harway Avenue, Bay 34th Street, East 2nd Street, Avenue T, Avenue X and Stillwell Avenue. A building that size has to have a self-locking street door and, at eight apartments, an intercom with voice and remote release under the Multiple Dwelling Law, and it usually also has a cellar, a meter room, a rear yard or parking area and a roof. Every one of those doors needs a decision about who opens it before the walls close, not after the tenants have moved in.

Larger buildings are arriving too. CityRealty lists 2230 Cropsey Avenue, also addressed as 1625 Shore Parkway, as a 31-story condominium of 248 apartments completed in 2024, with a full-time doorman, a live-in superintendent, on-site parking, a bike room, a mail room, a pool, a rooftop terrace and a community day care on the ground floor. At the other end of the scale, The Real Deal reported in July 2026 that the Opal Residences, a twelve-unit condominium at 346 Avenue U near West Street, offers a doorman, a full-time valet and an on-site superintendent, which it called rare for this part of Brooklyn. A building with a staffed desk still has a dozen doors the desk cannot see, and a building with a valet has a garage door that opens for someone who does not live there.

The city’s own figures explain why we start every job at the door rather than the reader. In September 2022 the NYC Comptroller’s office sent auditors to 262 public housing developments and found that 40.1 percent of entrance door locks were broken and that, in Brooklyn, 66 percent of entrance doors were not secured, up from 17 percent in 2018. Rear and side doors were nearly as bad as front ones. Residents told the auditors that intercoms, locks, keys and door release buttons often did not work, so doors were propped open instead. The Comptroller at the time, Brad Lander, summed it up: “Broken doors are a symbolic reminder that home safety starts at the entrance.” Those were NYCHA buildings, the same kind of public housing as Gravesend’s own Marlboro Houses, which NYCHA maintains itself; the audit release does not name Marlboro, and we do not apply its figures to private buildings. The lesson travels anyway: an entrance fails at the closer, the latch and the propped door long before it fails at the electronics.

Side Door Unlocked, Lobby Reader Offline or a Shop Door Stuck on Release: Repair in Gravesend

Not every Gravesend call is a new system. If a congregation’s side door now unlocks on schedule but never relocks, a reader in a new building’s lobby stops answering after the internet provider swapped the router, or a store on Kings Highway finds its back door releasing whenever the compressor next to it kicks on, call (347) 934-8335 and describe what the door is doing, or send a short video of it opening and closing. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. The cause on older doors is usually mechanical: a closer that has lost its oil, a latch that no longer reaches its strike after a hot summer, or a strike that someone taped open years ago and nobody remembers. On newer systems it is more often a power supply on a circuit that gets switched off, a backup battery at the end of its life, a schedule that was changed for one holiday and never changed back, or a controller that lost its network. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we say plainly when a part is not worth repairing.

What the materials mean for the install

The new mid-rise buildings on the western streets are mostly concrete and block behind a brick or panel face, with hollow metal door frames set in the masonry. That is good news for a strike, because a steel frame takes one cleanly and holds it well, and bad news for cable after the fact, because nobody wants to channel a finished block wall. In a building under construction the cheapest cable is the one that goes into the frame and the wall before they are grouted and painted; in a finished one we run it in the ceiling of the vestibule or cellar and drop into the frame from above. Aluminum-and-glass storefront doors, which many of these buildings use at the street, have narrow hollow stiles, so the lock usually goes into a panic device or the frame header rather than the door edge.

Congregation and school buildings are the opposite case: often older, often converted from a house or a commercial building, with heavy doors, mixed frames and additions that have been joined over the decades. Their exit doors usually carry panic hardware already, which can be swapped for an electrified version so the outside unlocks for a credential while the inside bar works exactly as before. The 1920s houses are brick or wood frame with wood doors, and the large rebuilt houses of the northeastern blocks tend to have heavy custom doors, often with sidelights, in stucco or stone fronts. On those, we mount readers on the wall or the frame beside the door rather than on the decorative surround, and we check the door maker’s instructions before cutting a door the owner paid a lot for.

Doors, Grants and the Order of Things: Synagogues, Yeshivas and Community Rooms

A house of worship or a school in Gravesend usually has more doors than people think and fewer people who should be opening them. The main entrance opens for services, for school arrival and for events. A side door serves the office, the staff and the members who come early to study or late to clean up. A rear door takes deliveries and caterers for the social hall. A cellar or a boiler room door is used by the custodian. Over the years keys to all of them have been handed to board members, teachers, caterers, cleaners and contractors, and very few organizations can tell us today how many are out there. That uncertainty, not the hardware, is usually what the board wants fixed.

The fix is a credential plan. Each person gets their own fob, card or code; each is placed in a group such as office staff, teachers, members, custodian, caterers or a particular contractor; and each group gets doors and hours. The custodian’s fob opens the cellar and the side door at any hour. Teachers open the side door on school days from early morning to late afternoon. Members open it before and after services. A caterer’s code works on the rear door for the hours of one booking and then stops. Nobody needs to collect a key when a teacher leaves or a caterer is replaced; their credential is switched off from the office computer or a phone, and the record shows which door each person used and when. The main entrance stays on a schedule set by the office, and it can be locked again with one command if anything happens outside.

Where the money comes from changes the order in which this happens. The New York State Nonprofit Security Grant Program request for applications we read, for the federal 2025 funding round, lists physical access control systems as eligible equipment, together with impact-resistant doors and gates and video systems, and allows up to $200,000 per site, with up to three sites per area and $600,000 per organization. Applicants must be tax-exempt under section 501(c)(3), show a heightened risk of attack, and be prequalified in the state’s grants system. Religious organizations receive a scoring multiplier of three. That round’s applications were due on January 13, 2026, and later rounds set their own dates, so check the current notice before planning around any date on this page.

Three of the program’s rules bear directly on an installer. First, the request says, “Do not make any grant-requested purchases prior to entering into a contract with DHSES.” A congregation that orders readers or pays a deposit before its contract with the state is signed risks paying for them itself. Second, procurement has to follow General Municipal Law 5A and the federal procurement rules in 2 CFR Part 200, and a contractor that helped develop the specifications or the application is generally barred from competing for the work. Third, the equipment has to go on property the organization owns, or leases and occupies, and cameras for facial recognition software are not allowed. We plan around all three: our first visit and written quote describe the doors and the problem without writing the organization’s specifications for it, we do not order equipment or take a deposit for grant-funded work until the organization tells us its contract is signed, and we flag any device the rules exclude.

Not every congregation applies for a grant, and not every grant is the state program. UJA-Federation’s 2022 Community Security Initiative fund, for example, targeted small Brooklyn synagogues with a capacity under 200 people and paid for doors, locks and cameras, followed by access control training for staff or key congregants. Its announcement named Midwood, Kensington, Williamsburg, Crown Heights, Borough Park and Flatbush, not Gravesend, and we do not know whether any Gravesend synagogue received money from it. Whatever the source, the useful part is the training: the people who run the office need to know how to issue a credential, cancel one, check the record after an incident and lock the building quickly, and we leave that written down at handover.

What a grant reviewer and a board both want to see from the door plan

The plan we write for a congregation or school lists each door by its location and use, what it does now, what it will do afterwards, the hardware on it, how it behaves in a power failure and on fire alarm, who will hold credentials for it and at what hours, and the price from the Brooklyn list. It names the problem each door solves in plain terms, such as keys that cannot be recovered or a side door that is propped during services, because that is the language a risk assessment uses. It also shows what stays the same: every exit still opens from inside with one motion, every door keeps a mechanical key override, and nothing changes about how people leave the building in an emergency.

Plan the Doors Before the Sheetrock: New Buildings on Harway Avenue, 28th Avenue and the Bay Streets

Most of the new residential building in Gravesend is happening in small packages. The 97 28th Avenue filing described by New York YIMBY is typical: a vertical expansion to seven stories, eight apartments averaging about 859 square feet, a cellar, six parking spaces, and a concrete structure. The same reporting stream lists new buildings filed for Harway Avenue, Bay 34th Street, East 2nd Street, Avenue T, Avenue X, Coney Island Avenue and Stillwell Avenue. Each of these will have a street door, a vestibule, mailboxes, a cellar and a roof, and many will have a yard or a parking area with its own gate or door. None of them will have a doorman.

The law sets the floor. Under section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, a class A multiple dwelling built after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at its entrances from the street, passageways, courts, yards and cellars, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and a building with eight or more apartments must have an intercom at the main entrance with voice communication and a remote release from each apartment. The roof door is the exception: it has to be self-closing but must not be self-locking from inside, so people can always get out onto the roof in a fire. Once fobs, apps or other smart access go on the doors of a multiple dwelling, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies too, with its rules on consent, minimal data, a written privacy policy and destroying authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.

What the law does not do is plan the building for you. In our experience the two expensive mistakes in a small new building are a street door frame that was set without a prepared strike and a cable path, and a cellar door that was left off the plan entirely. The first means cutting a finished frame and running surface cable across a new vestibule; the second means the cellar, where the meters, the boiler, the electrical service and often the tenants’ storage live, ends up on a key that the electrician, the plumber and the previous super all still have. Both are cheap to prevent while the frames are being ordered and the walls are open.

The practical list is short. Order the street, vestibule and cellar door frames prepared for an electric strike, with a door position contact, and run a cable from each to the telecom room. Put an outlet on a circuit that is not switched in the telecom room for the access power supply; that outlet is your electrician’s work, not ours, because the 120-volt side of the job is licensed electrical work. Decide whether the parking area gets a gate operator, a door or nothing. Leave a conduit from the telecom room to the roof in case a camera or a reader goes there later. And give the access system its own network connection rather than sharing the first tenant’s router. With those in place, the station, readers and controller go in during the last weeks of construction and are tested before the first fob is handed out.

Who holds the system on the day the building opens

A builder who will keep and rent the building should hold the administrator account in the owner’s name, not the general contractor’s or ours, and should get the user list, the door schedule and the cable map at handover. A builder who will sell the apartments as condominiums should plan for the board to take the account over, with the developer’s credentials and the construction codes removed on the day of transfer. Either way, the installer’s own access should be limited to service work, and the building should be able to change installers without replacing the readers.

Desk, Garage, Pool and Day Care: Towers by the Shore Parkway

The tower at 2230 Cropsey Avenue is a reminder that a building with a full-time doorman still needs an access system. CityRealty lists, besides the attended lobby, on-site parking, a fitness center, a pool, a spa, a rooftop terrace, an outdoor lounge on the 23rd floor, a lounge, a children’s playroom, a laundry room, a bike room, a mail room and a community day care on the ground floor. Every one of those has a door, and the desk can see only the first. The questions we get in buildings like this are about the other doors: who may use them, at what hours, and what happens when someone is still inside one at closing time.

Amenity doors are where schedules earn their keep. A pool or a terrace that closes at night should lock its entrance from the residential side at closing, but the door out of it must always open from the amenity side, so nobody is ever shut outside on a roof or a deck. A gym can open for residents’ fobs early and late, with a held-open alert if the door is propped for a trainer. A bike room opens for residents who have registered a bike, and only them. A children’s playroom can be set to open only for adult credentials, so a child cannot let themselves in alone. None of this needs a guard at every door; it needs readers, door contacts and a schedule that the management office can change.

Mixed buildings need partitions. A ground-floor tenant such as a day care, a medical office or a shop has its own staff, its own hours and its own reasons for keeping records private. On one controller, the building’s doors belong to the building’s management, the tenant’s doors belong to the tenant, and any shared door, such as a corridor that links the day care to the mail room or the service elevator, opens only for the people both sides agree on. The parking entrance gets its own rules again, especially where a valet or a garage operator parks residents’ cars: the valet’s credential opens the garage and nothing upstairs. The result is that each party sees and controls its own doors and nobody else’s, and the desk is told about any door that is forced or left open.

When the gate is the first lock and the door is the last

Many Gravesend stores close behind a roll-down gate, and owners often treat the gate as the security. Brooklyn Paper’s 61st Precinct report of a burglary at an Avenue U business near East 19th Street shows why that is not enough: overnight on April 20, in a year the report does not give, the lock on the gate was “clipped,” the burglar got inside, and about $3,500 in cash was taken from a safe in a kitchen cabinet. A camera was there. The gate lock is a padlock or a slide bolt on the outside of the building, and anyone with a cutter can defeat it in seconds, out of sight once the gate is lifted a few feet.

The door behind the gate, and the stockroom or office door inside, are where access control adds something. With a strike or electrified lock and a door position contact, the system knows the difference between the door opening for a valid code and the door being forced, and it can send a forced-door alert to the owner’s phone at once, at two in the morning, before anyone reaches the register. It also keeps the store’s inside doors locked after closing even if a staff member forgets, and it records who closed up. It will not stop a cutter at the gate, and it is not an alarm system, but paired with an alarm and a camera it turns a quiet entry into a noticed one.

Rules that apply to Gravesend business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path has to unlock when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs release hardware in the door that works with one hand. A shop, office or school exit opens from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above. Where a shop shares a street entrance with apartments, Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires that entrance to be self-closing and self-locking, so a business cannot put it on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. On the residential doors of a building with apartments, the Tenant Data Privacy Law governs fobs, apps and other smart access. A shop’s own staff door is not covered; a shared residential door is.
  • Grant-funded equipment. A nonprofit’s grant may limit what can be bought and when; for example, the state program’s request excludes cameras for facial recognition software and bars purchases before the state contract is signed.
  • Electrical licensing. The city’s electrical code treats signaling, alarm, communication and data wiring under 50 volts as low-voltage work outside its permit requirement; the 120-volt outlet for a power supply is work for a licensed electrician. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Gravesend: Questions Answered

Congregations, yeshivas and community rooms

Our congregation rents its rooms from a landlord. Can readers paid for by a state security grant go on a building we do not own, and what happens to them if we move?

The state program’s request for applications says equipment must be installed on real property the organization owns, or leases and occupies, so a tenant congregation is not excluded by being a tenant. In practice you will also need the landlord’s written consent before anything is fixed to the building, and a lease that runs long enough to make the investment sensible. Ask your grant adviser what the program expects you to show about the lease, and get the landlord’s agreement to the door plan in writing before the quote goes into the application.

Whether the equipment can leave with you depends on the grant terms and the lease, not on us. Technically, most of it can: readers, controllers, stations and credentials come off the wall, while strikes cut into the landlord’s frames usually stay. We design tenant installations so the controller and the readers are easy to remove and reuse, label every cable, and leave the landlord’s doors working mechanically if the system is taken out.

The grant rules bar a company that helped write the specifications from bidding on the work. How do we get a site survey without losing the installer we want?

By keeping the survey and the specifications apart. The request we read says procurement must follow General Municipal Law 5A and the federal procurement rules, and that contractors who helped develop the specifications or the application are generally barred from competing. An installer can still visit, look at the doors and give you a quote for the work as a vendor, the same as any other bidder. What an installer should not do is write the scope of work that every bidder will price against, or draft your investment justification.

So our visit produces a priced quote for the doors you show us, nothing more. If your organization needs a neutral scope, it should come from your own staff, a security consultant who will not bid, or your grant adviser, and it can name door locations, functions and standards rather than one company’s products. Ask your adviser before anyone visits, because the rule is about how your procurement looks later, and it is far easier to set up correctly at the start.

A committee member wants face-recognition readers at the front door. Would the state grant pay for them?

Probably not, and we would not price them into a grant quote without the program confirming it. The request for applications lists “cameras for facial recognition software” as unallowable, and while a face reader at a door is a credential rather than a surveillance camera, it works by matching faces, which is exactly what that line excludes. Biometric readers also bring privacy questions for members and staff that a fob or a card does not.

What the program does list, physical access control, covers card and fob readers, keypads, controllers, door contacts and the locks that go with them, and those solve the problem the committee is really describing: knowing who is allowed through the door and being able to cancel anyone quickly. If the committee still wants face readers, ask DHSES in writing before the application is filed, and keep them out of the budget unless the answer is yes.

Members who arrive after services begin knock on the locked side door. Can someone inside see who it is and let them in without leaving the room?

Yes. A video station at the side door can ring a tablet or a phone held by whoever is acting as greeter, the office’s indoor screen, or both at once. The greeter sees the person on the step, can speak to them if needed, and taps release; the door unlocks for a few seconds and relocks behind them. Nobody has to walk to the door, and the door is never propped for latecomers.

Two details make it work in practice. The station’s camera should be placed so it shows a face, not the top of a head, which on a side door with steps usually means mounting it on the wall at the latch side rather than above the door. And the greeter’s release should be logged under that person’s own login, so if something goes wrong later the office knows who opened the door. Members with their own fobs simply tap in as usual.

New buildings on the western streets

Our new building on 28th Avenue will have exactly eight apartments. Does that number change what the front door has to have?

It does. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires a class A multiple dwelling with eight or more apartments to have an intercom at the main entrance door, with two-way voice communication and a way for each apartment to release the door remotely. At seven apartments that requirement would not apply; at eight it does. Separately, because the building is new, its entrances from the street, the yard and the cellar must be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked unless an attendant is on duty.

So a fob reader alone will not satisfy the law at your street door. The usual answer is one station that does both: a video intercom that calls each apartment’s phone or indoor screen and lets the tenant release the door, plus a built-in fob reader for residents. Both go on the same electric strike. Plan it with the door frame, because the strike preparation and the cable are far cheaper before the frame is grouted than after.

Our first tenants will move in while workers are still finishing the upper floors. How do we keep construction access apart from the residents’ doors?

With separate groups and an end date. Give the general contractor and each trade their own credentials, in a construction group that opens the street door, the cellar and the floors still under work during working hours only, and set the whole group to expire on the expected completion date, so nobody has to remember to switch it off. Residents get their own group from the first day. The super or the owner can see which group opened which door, which matters when a tenant reports a door left open at seven in the evening.

Avoid the shared construction code that becomes permanent. On many small jobs a single keypad code is given to every worker and then quietly passed to the first tenants because it is easier, and nobody ever changes it. If the system goes in before the finishes, set it up the way it will run after the building opens, and treat construction access as temporary from the start. Where the stair or the roof is still a work zone, keep those doors on construction credentials only, with a door contact so the owner knows if they are left open.

Towers by the Shore Parkway

Our building’s rooftop terrace closes at eleven. If the door locks while residents are still outside, how do they get back in?

They should never need a credential to get back in. The terrace door is set so that, at closing, the corridor side locks to fobs and the terrace side stays free: anyone on the roof can always push or turn the handle and walk back into the building. What closing time does is stop new people from going out, not trap the people already there. The same arrangement applies to a pool deck or a lounge on a high floor.

Two additions make closing time easier for the desk. A contact on the door tells the desk if it is propped, which is common on a hot night. And a short announcement or a light at the door near closing, set on the same schedule, reminds residents to come in. If the building’s fire safety plan treats the terrace exit in a particular way, the building’s engineer sets that requirement first and the lock follows it.

We want the children’s playroom to open only when an adult has tapped in. Can a door tell a parent’s fob from a child’s?

Yes, if the building issues fobs by person rather than by apartment. Each resident’s credential belongs to a group, and the playroom door can be set to accept only the adult group. A child’s fob still opens the lobby and the elevator, as the household wants, but not the playroom, so a young child cannot let themselves in alone. The household’s adults are the ones whose taps appear in the playroom record.

The rest is building policy, not hardware. Management decides what age counts as an adult for the playroom and puts it in the house rules. Keep the record small, as the Tenant Data Privacy Law expects in a multiple dwelling: the system needs to know that a credential is in the adult group, not a child’s birth date. And the playroom door, like every other, must open freely from inside.

The day care on our ground floor shares a back corridor with the residents’ mail room. Who should be able to open the door between them?

Usually nobody but staff on both sides, and only as an exit. A door between a day care and a residential corridor is a security door for the children, so the day care will want it locked from the residential side at all times, and the building will want it locked from the day care side so parents at pickup do not wander into the mail room. If the fire safety plan needs it as an exit, it must open from whichever side the plan says, with a release on fire alarm.

On one controller that is straightforward: the door belongs to both partitions, and only the building’s porters and the day care’s director hold credentials for it. Each side sees its own records. Any forced or held-open alarm on that door goes to both the desk and the day care, because both are responsible for who comes through it.

Shops and older buildings

Our jewelry store keeps the door locked and buzzes customers in. A theft on East 4th Street this February happened at one in the afternoon. Does a buzz-in door help against someone we have already let in?

Only partly, and it is worth being honest about which part. News 12 reported that on February 8, 2026, around 1 p.m., a woman walked into a jewelry store at 1701 East 4th Street, took about $94,000 in jewelry from a display case and drove off; the report says nothing about the door. A buzz-in door decides who comes in, and it gives staff a moment to look at a face on camera before releasing it. Once someone is inside, the door does nothing to the display case.

What it can do is keep a clear image of every person at the door, logged against the time they were let in, which is what police ask for first. It also must never trap anyone: the inside of a buzz-in door has to open freely, so locking a thief in is not an option. The display cases, their locks and the staff routine for showing pieces do the rest. If you are planning a buzz-in door, put the release where staff can reach it from the counter without turning away from the case.

Our walk-up is a few blocks from Marlboro Houses, and the Comptroller’s audit found side and rear doors in public housing almost as insecure as the fronts. Does that pattern hold in a private building?

In our experience, yes, and often more so, because the front door gets attention and the side door does not. The Comptroller’s 2022 audit of NYCHA found broken locks on 28.5 percent of rear and side doors and 37.5 percent of them open. Those figures are for public housing, not for your building, but the reason behind them is general: a side door to the yard, the trash area or the cellar is used by fewer people, is not watched, and is the one propped open for convenience.

If you can only add one door after the street door, choose the one people prop. Fix its closer and latch first, add a door contact so the super gets an alert when it stands open, and put a reader on it so residents who need it, for the trash or the laundry, can use it without a key that everyone has copied. A door that is no longer inconvenient is a door that stops being propped.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Gravesend

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Gravesend door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim to any one of those sources unless we quote it.

“Security grants pay for whatever a nonprofit needs, so buy now and claim it later.”

This one costs organizations real money. Grants reimburse approved items under a signed agreement, and the order of steps matters as much as the equipment.

The state’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program request says, “Do not make any grant-requested purchases prior to entering into a contract with DHSES,” and it limits who may compete for the work and what may be bought, excluding cameras for facial recognition software.

A congregation or school planning doors around a grant should get quotes, file the application, wait for the contract, and only then order equipment. We hold grant-funded orders and deposits until the organization tells us its contract is signed.

“A key fob system is basically a fancier key.”

A fob that opens a door is the visible part. What a building actually buys is the ability to issue, schedule and cancel access for each person, and a record of which door was used.

That matters most where keys have spread over years: a synagogue whose former board members, caterers and custodians still hold keys, or a walk-up whose street-door key has been copied at every hardware counter on Avenue U.

Unencrypted 125 kHz fobs can be copied cheaply too, so a building that wants the problem to stay solved should choose encrypted credentials from the start.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

That is usually the price of a lock off the shelf. Installed systems cost far more because of the strike, the controller, the cable, the power, the door contact and the programming.

On our Brooklyn list one controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500, a keypad on a staff or service door is $1,500 to $2,000, and a congregation or apartment building system with several doors is $4,000 to $15,000.

An old door with a failed closer, a frame that has to be prepared for a strike or a long cable run from the cellar moves a job up within its range, not off the list.

“New buildings come with access control built in.”

Some do, and many small ones do not. A new eight-unit building has to meet the Multiple Dwelling Law’s self-locking and intercom rules, but the law does not say fobs, readers on the cellar or a parking door.

On small projects the access system is often left until the end, when the frames are set and the walls finished, and the cheapest options are gone.

Plan the street, vestibule, cellar, parking and roof doors with the architect and the electrician while the walls are open, and the system costs less and looks better.

“A doorman makes a reader unnecessary.”

A doorman watches one door. A tower by the Shore Parkway has a garage, a pool, a roof terrace, a gym, a bike room, a mail room, a day care and service doors that the desk cannot see.

Those doors are opened by residents at all hours, by staff who change, by valets and vendors, and some of them are exits the fire safety plan relies on.

Readers, contacts and schedules on those doors make the doorman the person who is told, instead of the person who has to notice. Named credentials also mean that when a vendor changes, one list is edited instead of a lock.

“The roll-down gate is a shop’s real security.”

The gate is the first thing a burglar defeats. Brooklyn Paper’s 61st Precinct report of the Avenue U burglary describes a gate lock that was “clipped” before the thief got inside and took cash from a safe.

Once the gate is up a few feet, nobody on the street can see what happens to the door behind it. That door, and the office or stockroom door inside, are where a forced-door alert and a record of who closed up actually help.

A reader is not an alarm and does not replace one. Paired with an alarm and a camera, it turns a quiet entry into a noticed one.

“Once the reader is installed, the door is secure.”

A reader controls only a door that closes and latches. The Comptroller’s 2022 audit of NYCHA entrances found 40.1 percent of entrance locks broken and, in Brooklyn, 66 percent of entrance doors not secured, many of them propped because intercoms and release buttons did not work.

Those were public housing doors, but private buildings fail the same way: a closer that no longer pulls the door shut, a latch that misses its strike after the frame settles, a strike taped open for deliveries.

We fix those first, add a door contact so the system knows whether the door is actually shut, and make the door easy enough to use that nobody wants to prop it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before You Price the Readers

This describes how we approach a shared door, not a particular customer. Before we talk about readers, we ask whoever runs the building how many keys to the door exist and who has them. In an organization that has used the same lock for years the honest answer is usually “we don’t know,” and that answer tells us the job: the credential plan matters more than the brand of reader. We write down the groups of people who need the door, the hours each group needs it, and the people who should never have had a key at all.

Then we open and close the door ten times. If it does not latch on its own every time, no reader will make it secure, so the closer and the latch come first. Only then do we go looking for the cable path, the controller location and the outlet, and only after that do we stand outside and decide where the device goes. The order sounds slow. It is the reason the door is still locked a year later.

Where does a synagogue with a main entrance, a side door and a social hall door land on the Brooklyn list?
+

Three or more doors on one controller fall under the multi-door building system line, $4,000 to $15,000, with cloud management included. Where a building ends up in that range depends mostly on the exits: a main entrance with panic hardware and a fire alarm release costs more than a side door with a strike, and a social hall door that needs a caterer schedule and a video station adds more again. A single side door on its own is the single controlled door line, $1,500 to $2,500. Sales tax applies unless the organization gives us its exemption certificate, and the written price follows the visit.

Does a security grant require us to collect several quotes before we choose an installer?
+

It requires you to follow public procurement rules, and for most purchases that means comparing prices. The state program’s request says procurement must follow General Municipal Law 5A and the federal rules in 2 CFR Part 200, which set thresholds above which quotes or bids are needed. Your grant adviser can tell you which threshold your door work falls under. We give every organization a written, itemized quote that can sit beside other bidders’ quotes in the file, and we price the same doors the same way whether or not a grant is paying.

Is a keypad or a card better for the staff door of a bakery or deli where hands are often wet or floured?
+

A card or fob is usually better there. Staff can tap it with the back of a hand or carry it on a lanyard or wrist coil, while a keypad gets dirty, wears unevenly on the keys people use most and takes longer with wet fingers. Where the owner also wants codes, a combined reader takes both, so a delivery driver can use a temporary code and the staff use their fobs. Mount the reader at the latch side, out of the path of carts, and choose one rated for outdoor use if the door opens onto a lot or an alley.

Can the system send our board a weekly summary of who came in after hours, instead of everyone checking an app?
+

Most current platforms can email a scheduled report, for example every entry on chosen doors between ten at night and six in the morning, to the people the board names. That keeps most members out of the day-to-day record while the people responsible still see anything unusual. In a residential building the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what can be kept about tenants and for how long, so a residential report should be built around door events rather than tenants’ comings and goings, and we set it up that way.

Can a stockroom or safe-room door be set so it opens only when two employees tap in?
+

Many controllers support a two-person rule, where the lock releases only after two different valid credentials are presented within a few seconds. It suits a cash room, a jewelry vault room or a records room where one person alone should never be inside. It slows the door down by design, so we use it only on the doors that need it, and the door still opens freely from inside so nobody can be shut in. Each pair of entries is recorded under both names.

On the High Holidays hundreds of people arrive within half an hour. Will tapping in slow the line at the door?
+

A tap takes about a second, but a crowd at one reader still bunches up, and it is not the right setup for a peak arrival. For services with a large attendance, most congregations put the main entrance on an unlock schedule for the arrival window, with ushers or security at the door, and keep the side and rear doors on credentials. After the window the main door relocks on its own. The schedule for each holiday can be set months ahead and checked during the pre-season review.

Our doorposts carry mezuzot. Can a reader and an electric strike go on those doors without disturbing them?
+

Yes. The strike goes into the latch side of the frame and the reader on the wall beside the door, so the doorpost where the mezuzah is fixed is normally left alone. If cable has to pass through that side of the frame, we plan the route around the case and never mount anything over it. Ask your rabbi about any question of placement before the visit, and tell the technician, so the plan is drawn with it in mind.

Can the readers tell us how many people are inside the social hall at any moment?
+

Not reliably. A reader counts credentials presented, not people, and most guests at an event come in through a door that is open or on a schedule without tapping anything. Counting occupancy needs people-counting sensors or staff at the door, which are separate products. What access control can tell you is which doors were opened by which credentials and when, and whether an exit was forced or held open during the event.

Can our store’s front door unlock at opening time only if a manager has already tapped in?
+

Yes. Many controllers offer a first-person-in rule: the door’s opening-hours unlock schedule does not start until someone in a chosen group, such as managers, has presented a credential. If the manager is late or the store is closed for a holiday, the door stays locked instead of opening to an empty shop. At closing the schedule ends on time, and a forced or held-open alert after hours still goes to the owner.

Do you handle the door hardware yourselves, or do we also need a locksmith?
+

We supply and fit the electrified hardware, meaning the strike, the electrified lock or the electrified panic device, along with the reader, the controller and the cable, and we adjust closers and latches so the door closes and locks properly. Where a door needs a new cylinder, a rekeyed override or a mechanical repair beyond adjustment, we say so in the plan; your locksmith can do that part, or we can include it if the hardware is in our range. The 120-volt outlet for the power supply is for your electrician.

How should fobs be labeled so we know whose is whose without writing names on them?
+

Most fobs and cards carry a printed number. When we enroll a credential, that number is linked to the person in the system, and the fob itself carries nothing else, so a lost fob found on the street tells the finder nothing about the building or its owner. Keep the issue list in the software rather than on paper, and when someone returns a fob, delete the link and reissue the fob to someone new.

What should we receive in writing when the job is finished?
+

An administrator login in the owner’s or organization’s name, not ours; a door schedule listing every controlled door, its hardware and what it does in a power cut and on fire alarm; a cable map showing where each run goes; the product listings for the locks and controllers on exit doors; the user groups and schedules as programmed; and a one-page guide for the office. With those, the system can be managed by your own staff or serviced by any qualified installer later.
