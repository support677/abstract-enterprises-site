Why Marine Park Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Marine Park is a neighborhood of one- and two-family houses on the south shore of Brooklyn, bounded roughly by Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway and named for the 530-acre park at its southern edge. Brick Underground's 2022 guide describes most of its housing as detached and semi-detached one- and two-family houses built from the 1910s to the 1940s, with a few co-ops on the south and west borders, a handful of small condos in the middle, and no rental apartment buildings; renters live in two-family houses or above the shops. Brownstoner's 2020 listing of a 1920s house on Avenue R describes the pattern on many blocks: a driveway beside the house, a garage behind it, a back yard paved over for more cars, and a basement reached by a stair off the kitchen or from the yard. That layout decides where the camera system lives and how it breaks. The recorder is in the basement or the garage, the cables run along the side of the house to the back, and the cameras that people actually check are the ones on the driveway and the car.

Video from those cameras has a track record here. In November 2009 the Brooklyn Paper reported that a man who had been going through parked cars in Marine Park at three in the morning was arrested after a resident's surveillance system near East 32nd Street and Avenue P recorded him breaking into a car. "In no time we got responses from two different officers in two different commands identifying the suspect," Deputy Inspector Frank Cangiarella told the paper. Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Marine Park's Architecture

Six kinds of property account for almost every camera repair call we take from Marine Park. Each one fails in its own way, and knowing which one you have tells us where to look first.

Brick houses from the 1920s and 1930s

Detached and semi-detached brick houses, many in the "English style" the period ads sold, with a basement under the whole house, a side driveway and a garage at the back. The recorder is usually in the basement and the cables leave through a basement window or a hole in the foundation.

Frame houses and duplexes

Smaller wood-frame houses and duplexes, many around 1,100 square feet, with siding, wooden eaves and attics. Cameras are often screwed into soft trim, and cables run through the attic to the eaves, where squirrels and summer heat get at them.

Two-family houses

Houses with an owner on one floor and a tenant or relatives on the other. Often two sets of cameras, one recorder in a basement both households use, a router that belongs to whoever pays for the internet, and questions about who sees what.

Houses with a detached rear garage

The garage at the end of the driveway, sometimes now a workshop, office or guest room. The camera on it reaches the house through a cable along the fence or overhead, or through a wireless link, and that connection is where most of these systems fail.

Co-ops and small condos

The co-op buildings on the south and west borders and the few small condo buildings in the middle of the neighborhood, with lobby, mailbox, laundry and parking cameras on one recorder in a basement room, and a board or managing agent who approves repairs.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Two- and three-story buildings on Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U, Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue with a store on the ground floor and apartments above, where the store's recorder and the landlord's cameras may share a cellar and a power circuit.

Brick houses: thick walls, a deep basement and one hole to the outside

The brick houses that fill so many Marine Park blocks are good for cameras in one way and hard in another. The walls are solid, so a camera screwed into a mortar joint stays put for years, and the basement gives the recorder a cool, dark room to work in. But brick also blocks Wi-Fi, so wireless cameras on the far side of the house or out at the garage often struggle to reach a router in the basement or the living room. And every camera cable on the house usually leaves the basement through the same opening, a drilled hole in the foundation or a gap at a basement window, which means one bad seal can let water run along every cable into the basement at once.

On these houses we start in the basement. We check where the recorder, the power box or PoE switch, the modem and the battery backup sit relative to the floor and the drain, and whether any of them has been wet. Then we look at the cable entry: whether the cables dip below the hole on the outside, so water drips off them instead of following them in, and whether the hole is sealed. On the outside, we look at how each camera is fixed to the brick and whether the cable is stapled along the wall, run in a channel or left hanging, because loose cable on a brick wall is what wind and ice pull apart first.

Frame houses and duplexes: soft trim, hot attics and chewed cable

On the frame houses and duplexes, cameras are usually screwed into wooden eaves, fascia or trim, and the cables run up through the attic. Wood moves with the seasons and softens with age, so the screws work loose and the camera slowly tips toward the ground. The attic adds heat in summer, which shortens the life of plug-in power adapters left up there, and it gives squirrels and other animals a dry place to chew. Cables that run along the outside of the siding are exposed to sun and to anyone with a ladder.

Two-family houses: one basement, two households, one set of passwords

Brick Underground notes that Marine Park renters live mostly in two-family houses. That makes the two-family the place where camera systems get complicated without anyone noticing. The owner put in cameras for the driveway and the yard; the tenant or a grown child upstairs added a doorbell camera; the recorder sits in a basement both households walk through; and the internet that carries the phone view belongs to whichever household pays the bill. When the tenant moves out or switches internet providers, the owner's cameras can disappear from every phone overnight, even though the recorder is still saving video.

Rear garages: the camera at the end of the line

The garage behind the house is where Marine Park cameras go to die. It is the farthest point from the recorder, so the cable is long; it is usually reached across a paved yard, along a fence or overhead from the back porch, so the cable is exposed; and the garage itself may have no heat, a leaky roof and an outlet that trips. Owners who have turned the garage into an office or workshop often add a camera inside as well, which doubles the load on whatever link carries the video back to the house.

We test the garage camera and its link separately. If the camera works on a short test cable, the problem is the run, and we find the break, replace the damaged length, or reroute it away from the gate, the fence posts and the overhead door. If the garage uses a wireless bridge or a Wi-Fi camera, we check its signal, its antenna and its line of sight, because a new extension or a grown tree between the house and the garage can cut a link that worked for years. Where the garage has its own outlet, we check whether the camera's power depends on a circuit that trips; a ground-fault outlet that cuts out in wet weather explains a lot of "it comes back on its own" complaints. If the circuit itself needs work, that goes to your electrician; we do not do electrical work.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Working a Marine Park Camera System From the Basement Drain to the Back Garage

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical Marine Park house: a brick or frame house with a side driveway, cameras on the front, the driveway side and the back, a detached garage at the end of the lot, and the recorder somewhere below the kitchen. It is the order we follow, not a story about one job.

Save the night first. If anyone needs video from a recent night, the technician exports it before restarting, unplugging or moving anything, and checks the recorder's clock against the real time so the timestamps can be explained to a detective or an insurer.

Look at the floor before the screen. In the basement, the technician notes where the recorder, the power box or PoE switch, the modem and any battery backup sit, how far they are from the floor and the drain, whether there are water marks, rust or green crust on connectors, and whether everything is plugged into one power strip on the floor. Half of the long-term fixes on a Marine Park house are decided right here, before any camera is tested.

Follow the cables out of the house. The technician traces every camera cable from the recorder to the point where it leaves the basement, checks the seal at the foundation or window, and then follows the run along the side of the house to the driveway cameras, up to the eaves and out to the garage, looking for chewed, crushed, stapled-through or sun-cracked sections.

Test at the far end. At each dark or weak camera, power and signal are tested at the camera itself, then at the connector, the junction box and the recorder, so we know whether the camera, the cable or the recorder input has failed. A camera that works on a short test cable has a cable problem, not a camera problem.

Price before parts, then leave a record. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, the technician labels the cables at the recorder, turns on warnings for lost video and drive errors, writes down which camera is on which channel, and shows you how to export a clip yourself.

Fault by Fault

Water, Power, Cars, Brick and Age: Why Marine Park Camera Systems Stop Recording

Five causes explain most of the camera failures we find on Marine Park houses, co-ops and storefronts. Knowing which one you are dealing with usually tells you what the repair will involve and roughly what it will cost.

Water: the basement drain, the foundation hole and the floor-level power strip

Most of the water damage we see here is not dramatic. It is a recorder that has sat for years on a shelf a foot above a floor drain, a power strip lying on the basement floor, a plug-in power box for the cameras hung low on a damp wall, or cables that enter through a hole at ground level and carry rain inside along their jackets. A sewer backup that leaves an inch of water for an hour can ruin a power strip and every adapter plugged into it, and damp air in a basement corner slowly corrodes the metal ends of the camera cables until the picture flickers and then stops. The THE CITY and Flood Form figures for the Marine Park drainage area are why we treat every basement here as one that may take water, whether or not the owner has seen it happen.

The repair is in two parts. First, the damage: we test the recorder, the drive, the power supply and the switch, replace what failed, and cut back and re-terminate corroded cable ends. Second, the placement: the recorder, the switch, the modem and a battery backup go onto a wall shelf or into a cabinet well above the floor, with the power strip mounted rather than lying down, and the cable entry is resealed with a drip loop outside so water falls away instead of following the cable in. If your plumber has fitted or is fitting a backwater valve, we can plan the camera equipment around that work.

Power: transformers, storms and the moment the lights come back

The 2015 transformer failure that cut about 825 Marine Park households for two and a half hours is typical of how power trouble arrives here: suddenly, on an ordinary day, for a few hours. During the outage, a system without battery backup records nothing. When the power returns, the inrush can finish off an old power supply, the recorder can come back with its clock wrong or its settings gone, a drive that was already weak can report errors, and some cameras can fail to reconnect until the switch is restarted. Owners often do not notice any of this until they look for a clip days later.

We test the power path from the outlet to every camera, replace a failed supply or switch, and check that the recorder kept its settings and its time. Then we look at what would make the next outage harmless: a battery backup sized to carry the recorder, the switch and the modem together for long enough to ride out a short cut and shut down cleanly in a long one, and warnings that tell your phone when a camera or the drive has stopped. A surge protector that has taken a hit stops protecting even if its lights stay on, so we check those too.

Cars: driveways, carports, curbs and the hour before dawn

The incidents that send Marine Park owners to their recorders mostly involve cars, and they happen in the dark: the 2009 break-ins at three in the morning, the August 2026 carjacking at 4:40 a.m., the converter thefts discovered on the way to school. A camera that sees cars well at night has to do several things at once: stay in focus on the spot where a person would stand, not flip between color and black-and-white every time headlights sweep across it, not be blinded by a porch light or a carport fixture, record at a frame rate that keeps a moving person sharp, and keep enough days of video that a theft discovered later is still on the drive.

Many driveway cameras we find here were set up in daylight and never checked at night. The repair often needs no new parts: re-aim the camera to look along the driveway rather than down onto car roofs, tighten the zoom onto the side door or the gap between cars, adjust exposure and day-night switching, set motion detection so it reacts to a person crouching beside a car and not only to someone walking upright, and lengthen how long the recorder keeps video. Where the camera simply cannot do the job, we say so and quote a replacement that can.

Brick and distance: why wireless cameras drop out here

Brick walls, foil-backed insulation, aluminum siding and the distance to a rear garage all weaken Wi-Fi. A battery or Wi-Fi camera that worked fine when it was tested beside the router may drop out for hours once it is mounted on the far side of a brick house or on the back of the garage, and its motion clips may arrive late or not at all. Microwaves, baby monitors and the neighbors' networks crowd the same radio band. Owners often read this as a broken camera, when the camera is fine and the signal is not.

We measure the signal at the camera's position, not at the router. Sometimes moving the router or adding an access point fixes it. Sometimes the camera can be switched to a less crowded band. Often the best repair is to replace the most troublesome wireless camera with a wired one on a single cable from the basement, which also takes care of charging batteries. We tell you which option will actually last, not only which costs least today.

Age: analog cameras, tired drives and systems nobody remembers setting up

Many Marine Park systems went up ten or fifteen years ago, often installed by a relative, a handyman or a contractor who has since retired or closed. The cameras may be analog on coax with a DVR that needs an old browser plug-in to view on a computer; the drive may have been recording around the clock for years; the passwords may be on a sticky note in a drawer or nowhere at all. These systems often still work, but they fail in small ways that add up: one camera dark, playback missing on another, the phone app unable to connect, the clock drifting.

For an older system we give you an honest choice. A drive replacement, a power supply and a few connectors can keep a sound analog system going for years at a modest cost. A hybrid recorder can keep the existing coax cameras and accept new network cameras later. When the cameras themselves are worn out, we quote replacements that fit the cables you already have, so the walls stay closed. You decide; we tell you what we would do if it were our own house.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Marine Park: Questions Answered

These are situations Marine Park homeowners, two-family owners, co-op boards and shop owners describe when they call. Each answer explains what is usually wrong and what the repair involves.

Driveways, carports and the garage

Our camera hangs under the carport roof, and every time we pull in after dark the picture flips between color and black and white for a minute. Is the camera failing?

No, it is reacting to your headlights. Most cameras decide between day and night mode by measuring light, so a car pulling in under a low roof floods the sensor, the camera switches to color, the car stops, the light drops and it switches back, sometimes several times. During those switches the picture is often blurred or blank, which is exactly the moment you would want it. The repair is in the settings: a longer delay before switching, a fixed night schedule, or a lower sensitivity, and sometimes a small change of angle so the headlights hit the camera less directly. We check it with a car pulling in before we leave.

The Wi-Fi camera on our garage has a small antenna, and it snapped off in a storm. Can a new antenna go on, or does the camera have to be wired?

Often a new antenna is enough. On many outdoor Wi-Fi cameras the antenna screws onto a standard connector, and a replacement of the right type and gain costs little. If the antenna was molded into the housing, or water got in where it broke off, the camera may need replacing. Either way, a garage at the back of a brick house is a hard place for Wi-Fi, so we measure the signal there before deciding. If it was marginal even before the storm, a single cable from the house, or a dedicated wireless link aimed at the garage, will be more reliable than a new antenna.

Basements, drains and water

Our plumber is putting a backwater valve in the basement next week and has to shut off power and clear the wall where the recorder and the camera power box sit. What should we do with the camera system that day?

Plan it rather than let it happen. Before the work, export any clips you might need, note how the cables are connected or photograph the back of the recorder, and shut the recorder down from its own menu rather than pulling the plug, which protects the drive. Keep the drive in the recorder and move the unit, the power box and the switch somewhere dry and out of the work area. Once the plumber is done, it is a good time to remount the equipment higher on the wall with a battery backup, well above the drain. We can do the disconnect and the remount around the plumber's schedule, so the cameras are down only for the hours they have to be.

We carried the recorder upstairs to keep it away from the basement drain, and now the screen says there is no hard drive. Did we break it?

Probably not. Moving a recorder can shake loose the small data or power cable that connects the drive inside the case, especially in older units, and the recorder then reports no drive or an unformatted one. Do not choose "format" or "initialize" when it asks, because that erases the video. With the power off, the case can be opened and the drive cables reseated; in most cases the drive reappears with its recordings intact. If it does not, the drive may have been on its way out already, and the move only exposed it. Moving the recorder upstairs also means the camera cables now have to reach it, so we check that the longer runs still carry a clean picture.

Water trickles into our basement along the camera cables where they come through the foundation wall. Is the cable carrying water inside?

Yes, that happens. Rain runs down a cable on the outside wall and follows it straight through the hole unless the cable dips below the hole first. Once inside, it drips onto whatever is beneath, often the power strip or the recorder. The fix is a drip loop on the outside so the cable goes down before it goes in, a proper seal in the hole, and, where needed, an outdoor entry fitting. We also check the connectors on the inside end, because cables that have been carrying water for a while usually have corroded ends that need to be cut back and redone. If water is also coming through the wall itself, that is a job for a waterproofing contractor.

Recorders, drives and screens

We bought a bigger hard drive online to get more days of video, but the recorder will not recognize it. Is the drive bad?

Usually the drive is fine and the recorder has a limit. Many recorders, especially older ones, accept drives only up to a certain size, and some need a firmware update before they can use a larger drive. Desktop drives meant for computers also fail sooner in a recorder that writes around the clock. We check your recorder's maximum supported size and its firmware, and fit a surveillance-rated drive it can use. Swapping the drive means the old video is no longer playable on that recorder unless the old drive is kept, so we export anything you want first.

All our cameras show live, but when we play back one of them there is nothing recorded. Why would one camera stop saving?

Usually because of a setting, not a fault. Recording is set camera by camera, and one channel may have been switched to motion-only with the motion zone drawn over a wall, or turned off entirely during an earlier repair, or set to record only during certain hours. Sometimes the camera's video settings exceed what the recorder will save, so it shows live but is refused for recording. We check the schedule, the motion settings and the stream settings for that channel, and confirm with a test that it records and plays back. Then we turn on a warning so a channel that stops recording tells you.

We bought a new 4K television for the den, and when we plug the recorder into it the screen stays black. The old TV worked fine. Is the recorder failing?

Very likely not. Many recorders output a fixed resolution that an older TV accepted but some newer sets do not, and some TVs need the right input selected or a setting changed before they show an older signal. The fix is usually to set the recorder's output to a resolution the TV supports, which sometimes means connecting a monitor briefly to reach the menu. A short HDMI cable or an adapter can also be the culprit. We test the recorder on our own monitor first, so you know whether the recorder or the connection is the problem.

All the older cameras on our house get their power from one metal box with a row of small fuses, and three of them went dark at the same time. Do we need three new cameras?

Probably not. A central power box feeds several cameras, and when it ages or a fuse blows, every camera on that output goes dark together. Three cameras failing at once points to the box, a fuse or the shared wiring rather than three cameras. We test the box's output, replace a fuse or the whole supply if it is weak, and look for the reason a fuse blew, such as a short in one camera's cable from water or chewing. If one camera caused it, we fix that run so the new fuse does not blow again.

Shops and home offices

Our store on Avenue U has an old recorder we used to watch from the office computer through a browser page, and now the page asks for a plug-in that will not install. Is the recorder dead?

No. Many older recorders were built to be viewed through a browser plug-in that modern browsers no longer run. The recorder is still recording; only the viewing method has aged out. The options are the maker's own desktop software for your recorder, if it still runs on your computer, a phone app that supports the recorder, or a firmware update that adds plug-in-free viewing. If none of those work, a newer recorder that accepts your existing cameras restores computer viewing. We check what your model supports before suggesting a replacement.

Our old network switch died, so we bought a new one at an electronics store and plugged everything back in exactly the same way, but none of the cameras came on. What did we do wrong?

Most likely the new switch does not supply power. Network cameras that run on a single cable get their power from a PoE switch, and many ordinary switches carry data only. Even a PoE switch may not have enough total power for every camera, especially ones with heaters or long-range infrared. We check the cameras' power needs, replace the switch with a PoE model that can carry them all, and put it on battery backup. If you still have the receipt, the store switch can usually go back.

The indoor camera on our kitchen counter, which we use to check on the dog, keeps going offline whenever someone runs the microwave. Can that be fixed?

Yes. Microwave ovens leak a little energy in the same 2.4 GHz band that many small Wi-Fi cameras use, and a camera close to one, or far from the router, loses its connection while the oven runs. Moving the camera a few feet away, moving the router closer, switching the camera to the 5 GHz band if it supports it, or using a wired camera all solve it. We test which fix works in your kitchen before changing anything.

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common on houses wired ten or fifteen years ago. Typical faults: corroded connectors in a damp basement, a failed central power box, and grainy night pictures.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Marine Park

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, forum threads and national repair guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each has some truth in it, but each misses something that matters on a Marine Park house or storefront.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

National averages put most camera repairs in a low range, and some quick answers repeat a single figure as if it applied everywhere.

That range describes the simplest jobs, such as a settings fix or a loose connector. HomeAdvisor's own Brooklyn page shows how wide the real spread is: an average of $1,416 for surveillance camera installation or repair, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and a low-to-high span of $160 to $3,500. Those numbers mix installation with repair, so they overstate what a repair alone usually costs, but they show that one number cannot describe every job.

In Marine Park the price follows the fault. A connector or a power adapter falls near the bottom of our Brooklyn list; a recorder that sat in a basement backup, with a new drive and a remount, falls in the middle; a long run to a rear garage that has to be replaced falls higher. The $150 service call tells you which one you have before you spend more.

"If the recorder got wet, the footage is gone"

Some answers treat any water damage as the end of the recorder and everything on it.

The recorder's electronics may not survive, but the hard drive inside is a sealed unit that often does, especially if the water reached the case and not the drive itself, and the power was cut quickly. Video on a drive pulled from a damaged recorder can usually be read in a working recorder of a compatible type.

What destroys the footage is usually what happens next: someone powers the wet recorder back on repeatedly, or a replacement recorder is told to format the old drive. If your recorder has been wet, switch it off, do not restart it, and call before you throw anything away.

"Wireless cameras solve the cable problem"

Quick answers often suggest that a failed wired camera can simply be replaced with a wireless one, with no cable to break.

On a Marine Park brick house, a wireless camera trades one problem for another. Brick, foil-backed insulation and the distance to the rear garage all weaken the signal, and a camera that drops off Wi-Fi records nothing to your phone. Battery cameras also need charging, and they drain faster in cold weather.

Wireless is the right answer in some spots, such as a camera inside a window or a garage close to the house. For a driveway or a rear garage that matters, a repaired wired run is usually more reliable for years. We test the signal where the camera will hang before recommending either.

"A surge protector keeps your cameras safe in a storm"

Many answers recommend plugging the recorder into a surge protector and leaving it at that.

A surge protector helps with spikes, but it does nothing when the power simply goes out, which is what happened to about 825 Marine Park households in the 2015 transformer failure. With only a surge protector, the system stops recording the moment the power drops. Surge protectors also wear out after absorbing hits, often without any sign.

What keeps recording through a short outage is a battery backup sized for the recorder, the switch and the modem together. We check both kinds of protection on a repair and tell you whether yours are still doing their job.

"Police have street cameras, so private cameras add little"

Some answers suggest that public cameras and doorbell networks have made private systems less important.

Marine Park is a neighborhood of side streets, driveways and back yards, and the places where people's cars are opened and their packages taken are mostly outside any public camera's view. The 2009 arrest came from one resident's camera, and the still that identified the suspect came from that footage.

A private camera that records the driveway, the side door and the curb in front of your house is often the only video of what happened there. That is why a dark driveway camera is worth repairing, and why it needs to be checked at night, not just in daylight.

"Forgot the recorder password? Just reset it to factory settings"

Password help in quick answers often jumps straight to a full reset.

On many recorders a full reset wipes every setting: camera assignments, recording schedules, motion zones, the phone app link and sometimes the network setup. On some, the reset process also asks to format the drive. For a system set up years ago by a relative or a contractor, rebuilding all of that takes time and can lose video.

Many makers offer a password recovery that keeps settings and footage, using the recorder's serial number and proof of ownership. We try that first, and we export the video you care about before any reset.

"Any handyman can fix a security camera"

Some answers treat camera repair as a simple swap that any handy person can do.

An owner can work on his or her own system, but in New York a company that installs or services security systems, including CCTV, for other people needs a license from the Department of State. Beyond the license, a repair often involves tracing faults through cables, power supplies, switches, drives and network settings, which is a different skill from mounting a camera.

FAQ

Marine Park Security Camera Repair FAQ

When the hard drive fills up, does the recorder stop recording or start recording over the oldest video?
+

It depends on one setting. Most recorders are set to overwrite the oldest video when the drive is full, so they keep recording and you always have the most recent days. Some were set, by accident or on purpose, to stop when full, and those quietly stop saving anything new. We check that setting on every repair, along with how many days your drive actually holds, and set it to overwrite unless you have a reason to keep everything.

How much electricity does a camera system use running day and night?
+

Less than most people expect. A typical home recorder with a drive and four to eight cameras draws roughly as much as one or two old incandescent bulbs left on, a bit more at night when the infrared lights come on. Cameras with heaters, floodlights or long-range infrared use more. If your bill seems high, the camera system is rarely the reason, but we can tell you what your equipment draws when we test the power supply.

If you take down a camera we no longer need, what happens to the hole where its cable went into the wall?
+

We seal it. Any cable hole we leave behind is filled and sealed against water and drafts so it does not become a leak into the basement or the wall. We do not patch, paint or refinish brick, siding or interior walls, so the mount's screw holes and any outline on the surface stay visible; if you want them hidden, a painter or mason can do that after. We tell you before taking anything down what will be left.

When you replace a camera, do you reuse the bracket and holes that are already on the house?
+

Whenever we can. Many replacement cameras fit the old bracket or a junction box that covers the old holes, which keeps new drilling to a minimum and keeps the cable entry sealed. If the old bracket is rusted, the screws have pulled out of soft wood, or the new camera needs a different mount, we show you before drilling anything new and ask first.

Do refurbished or used parts ever go into a repair?
+

Not without your agreement. Replacement cameras, drives, power supplies and switches we supply are new. Occasionally, for a discontinued recorder or camera where no new part exists, a compatible used or refurbished part is the only way to keep the system going, and in that case we tell you, explain how it differs and price it separately so you can decide whether to use it or move to new equipment.

If a camera has to be replaced, is a bullet or a dome better on a Marine Park house?
+

Each has its place. A bullet with a hood sheds rain and low sun well and is easy to aim down a driveway or along the side of a house, and its shape tells passers-by it is a camera. A dome is harder to knock out of position and hides where it is pointing, which suits a front door, a porch ceiling or a store. On most repairs we match what was there unless it was the wrong type for the spot, and we explain why if we suggest a change.

How can we tell roughly how old our camera system is?
+

The recorder usually tells you. A sticker on the back or bottom often shows the model and a manufacture date, and the recorder's system information menu shows the firmware version and sometimes the build date. Round connectors on the cameras usually mean an older coax system, while cameras on network cable are usually newer. If nothing is readable, our technician can usually date the system from the model during the visit.

Can we repair the most important cameras now and leave the rest for later?
+

Yes. Many Marine Park owners fix the driveway, side door and front cameras first and leave the yard or garage for another visit. We write the price for each part of the repair separately, so you can choose, and we make sure whatever we do now does not have to be undone later, such as choosing a recorder or drive that will still suit the cameras you plan to fix next.

Should our recorder connect to the router by cable or by Wi-Fi?
+

By cable whenever possible. A recorder on Wi-Fi depends on a signal that brick walls and floors weaken, and when the link drops, phone viewing and alerts stop even though recording continues. A single network cable from the recorder to the router, even a long one from the basement, is far more reliable. If the router is far away, a wired access point or a short move of the router often solves it.

Do we still need a monitor plugged into the recorder if we only watch the cameras on our phones?
+

Not to record, but it helps to have one. The recorder saves video with or without a screen. A monitor, even a small old one, lets you see warnings on the recorder, export a clip and fix settings when the phone app cannot connect, which is often exactly when you need it. If you do not want one permanently connected, keep one nearby that can be plugged in when needed.

What should we do if a camera power box or adapter smells hot or starts smoking?
+

Unplug it at the outlet right away if you can do so safely, and do not plug it back in. A power supply that overheats is failing and can damage the cameras or start a fire. Leave the recorder running if it is on separate power, so the other cameras keep recording, and call us. We replace the failed supply, check the cameras it fed, and look for the cause, such as a short in a cable or too many cameras on one adapter.
