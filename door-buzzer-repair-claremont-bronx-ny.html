The Neighborhood

Why Claremont Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Claremont grew up around a hill. NYC Parks records that the ground now inside Claremont Park belonged to the Morris family estate from 1679, that a mansion named Claremont was built there in 1859 with terraced lawns running down to the Mill Brook, the stream that is now Webster Avenue, and that the city bought the land between 1888 and 1890 under the New Parks Act. The mansion became the Bronx headquarters of the Parks Department and was torn down in 1938. The park itself, 38.54 acres in Community Board 4, is still the center of the neighborhood, and the avenues around it, Clay, Morris, Sheridan and Selwyn among them, are lined with five- and six-story apartment houses that PLUTO dates to the 1910s and 1920s, the years after the IRT subway opened in 1904.

East of Webster Avenue the picture changes. Four public-housing developments built in the early 1960s, Butler, Webster, Morris and Morrisania Houses, are known together as Claremont Village. Wikipedia records Butler Houses as six 21-story buildings completed in December 1964, Webster Houses as five 21-story buildings completed in September 1965, and the Morris Houses as a mix of 16-, 17- and 20-story towers completed in August 1965. Rob Stephenson’s June 2026 neighborhood newsletter describes the cluster as housing more than 10,000 people. Around those towers stand rehabilitated tenements, two-family brick houses from the 1990s, and newer affordable and supportive-housing buildings on Third Avenue, Park Avenue and Claremont Parkway.

Put the two halves together and Claremont is a neighborhood of big buildings. In the census tracts we used for this page, 66 buildings of fifty or more apartments hold 9,492 of the 14,750 homes, about 64 percent. A big building’s intercom is a big system: one lobby panel, an amplifier or power supply in a cellar room, risers running up six or twenty floors, and dozens or hundreds of apartment stations, many replaced one at a time over the years by whoever was handy. In a system like that, the part that fails is often not the part that gets replaced, and the complaint comes back.

The city’s own records show it. HPD inspectors have written 392 intercom violations on these blocks since January 2021, at 115 different buildings, and the number has risen almost every year. Tenants have filed 680 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints with 311 since 2020. A large share of both lists is the same addresses and the same apartments returning. That is the reason this page is organized around one question an owner or tenant in Claremont should ask before paying for any buzzer repair: will this fix hold, and how will we know.

Why It Comes Back

Claremont’s Buzzers Fail Twice: Reading the Repeat Record

Every HPD intercom violation names a building and, usually, an apartment. Matched against the tax-lot map, the Claremont record is unusual less for its size than for its repetition. Of the 561 apartments that have ever had an intercom violation here, 112 have had two or more, and those 112 account for 336 of the 785 violations on file. Eighty-four apartments were cited in two or more different years. On the building level, 59 of the 156 buildings ever cited were written up in at least three separate years. A complaint that comes back after a year is rarely bad luck. It is usually the original fault, still in the wall or the cellar, waiting behind a new handset.

Intercom violations in the study area climbed from 25 in 2021 to 102 in 2025, with 71 more written by October 22, 2026. The rise is real across both halves of the neighborhood, east and west of Webster Avenue.

The rise is not spread evenly. Since January 2021, one privately owned 17-story building of 321 apartments, completed in 1965, accounts for 64 of the 392 intercom violations, 61 of them written since 2023. The next four buildings add 56 more. The remaining 272 are spread across 110 buildings, mostly prewar walk-ups and six-story elevator buildings on the Grand Concourse, Sheridan Avenue, Clay Avenue, College Avenue and Fulton Avenue. The 311 file shows the same shape: of 680 buzzer complaints since 2020, 121 came from that one tower, and in 2026 so far, 21 addresses filed two or more complaints each, together accounting for 92 of the year’s 116.

Concentration. A single building can dominate a neighborhood’s numbers, but a hundred others still produce most of the violations. Both patterns point to faults that outlive the repairs made on them.

Repeats in the HPD file. Apartment-level repeats undercount the problem, because a fault in a riser usually shows up in a different apartment on the same line the second time.

Both halves rose after 2022. On the east side a large share of the 2024–2026 increase traces to one 1960s tower; on the west side it is spread across prewar buildings on the Concourse, Sheridan, Clay and College Avenues.

Most Claremont intercom violations are class B, which HPD gives 30 days to correct. A small number were written as class C.

What the record says about how the earlier repairs were made

HPD’s status codes are blunt but telling. Of the 392 violations written since 2021, 163 now read “violation dismissed,” 100 “violation closed,” 58 “NOV sent out,” 34 “not complied with,” and 15 “first no access to re-inspect.” A dismissal or closure ends the paperwork. It does not tell anyone whether a technician tested the apartment’s station, the line behind it, and the door it is supposed to open, and it does not stop the same apartment from being cited again the next winter. That is the gap a Claremont repair has to close.

The commonest patterns we look for in a building with a repeat record are ordinary ones. A handset is swapped when the break is in the riser splice on the landing, so the new handset works until the splice moves again. A replacement station from a different maker is wired onto a system it was never meant for and loads the line, so the apartments at the far end fade. A power supply sized for the building’s original stations runs hot after years of additions. A strike is replaced while the keeper it latches into stays bent, or the closer is left too weak to pull the door home. None of those faults is visible from the apartment that complained, and every one of them brings the complaint back.

How we turn the record into a repair plan

Before a visit to a building with a history, we read what is public: the HPD violation list, the 311 record and the building’s age, size and class in PLUTO. We sort the past violations by apartment line and floor, because faults that move up and down a line point to the riser or the supply rather than the stations. On site, we test the apartment that called, then every apartment on the same line, then the lobby panel, the relay, the strike, the keeper and the closer, in that order. The price we confirm is for the failed part we can show you, not for the symptom. And we leave a written record, line by line, so that the next complaint, if there is one, can be compared with this visit instead of starting from nothing.

None of that makes a big building’s intercom immortal. Lobby panels in buildings with hundreds of residents take hard use, and old risers do eventually need replacing. What it does is separate the faults that are new from the faults that were never fixed, which is the difference between one repair and five.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Claremont’s Architecture

Claremont has six kinds of residential building, and each one fails in its own way. The counts below come from the City Planning PLUTO file for the study area; the descriptions of how each kind is wired come from how these systems are built, not from any particular address.

Six-story elevator buildings of the 1920s

The Grand Concourse, Sheridan, Morris, Selwyn and the upper end of Clay Avenue. PLUTO lists 56 private elevator buildings in the study area with 3,944 homes. Long corridors, two or more risers, sometimes a second entrance on a side street, and a head-end in a cellar room.

Five-story walk-ups

Fulton, Washington, Clay, College, Teller and Findlay Avenues and the numbered streets between them. Walk-ups hold 4,783 homes here. One riser per line, stations replaced piecemeal, and a street door that takes every delivery in the building.

NYCHA’s Claremont Village towers

Butler, Webster and Morris Houses: towers of 16 to 21 stories, about 4,256 apartments on nine lots. Their entry systems belong to NYCHA, which repairs them through its own ticket system; we do not work on them at a resident’s request.

The 1965 tower and other mid-century buildings

A handful of privately owned elevator buildings from the 1940s to the 1960s, including the 17-story building that leads the neighborhood’s violation count. Big lobbies, long risers and decades of additions to the original system.

Newer affordable and supportive housing

Buildings from 2000 onward on Third Avenue, Park Avenue, Claremont Parkway and East 170th Street, often with a staffed desk, video entry panels, readers and magnetic locks. Thirty-nine newer buildings of eight or more apartments hold 2,656 homes.

Small houses at both edges

1890s and early-1900s houses on College, Teller, Clay and Findlay Avenues, and 1990s two-family brick houses east of Webster on Claremont Parkway, Third Avenue, Crotona Place and East 171st Street: 278 one- and two-family lots with 524 homes.

How the repair changes from one building type to the next

Prewar elevator buildings. These are the buildings where a repeat complaint is most likely to sit in the riser. A six-story building of 70 or 120 apartments may have three or four vertical lines, each fed from a closet or landing box where pairs from every floor meet. Decades of station swaps leave those boxes crowded with twisted splices and tape. When an apartment on the fourth floor of one line goes quiet twice in two years, we open the box for that line before we touch the handset, and we remake every splice we find there, not just the one that failed. Where one of these buildings has a second entrance on a side street, that door usually has its own panel and strike on the same supply; it tends to fail more often because it is used without anyone watching, and we test it on every visit.

Walk-ups. In a five-story walk-up with sixteen or twenty apartments, the system is small enough to test completely in one visit, and we do. The repeat faults here are usually at the two ends: the stations in the apartments, where years of replacement have mixed parts from several makers, and the street door, where the strike, the keeper and the closer take every push of every delivery. A walk-up’s cellar supply is often an old transformer on a plank beside the meters. We read it under load, with the release held, because a supply that tests fine at rest can sag far enough to drop a strike that is already tired.

NYCHA towers. We include Claremont Village here because residents ask, not because we repair it. The intercoms, doors and locks in Butler, Webster and Morris Houses are NYCHA equipment, and the authority repairs them through tickets residents file with its customer contact center or the MyNYCHA app. A private contractor working on that equipment at a resident’s request would be working on someone else’s property. What we can do is explain what a useful ticket looks like, and that is in the questions below.

Mid-century private towers. A 17-story building of 321 apartments is a different machine from a walk-up. Its lobby panel serves hundreds of households, its supply and amplifier carry the load of every call, and its risers run the height of the building. When a building like that has dozens of violations in a few years, the right first step is a survey of every line rather than another round of handsets, because the pattern of which apartments fail, and in which order, is the map to the fault.

Newer affordable and supportive housing. The buildings that went up after 2000 rarely have a hand-wired audio system. They have video panels that call apartment monitors or tenants’ phones, card or fob readers, magnetic locks with request-to-exit sensors, and in supportive housing a staffed desk with its own release. The repeat faults there are different: a panel that lost its network connection, a door controller whose schedule was changed and never written down, a desk release wired in series with something else. Even so, HPD’s file shows 43 intercom violations since 2021 at buildings recorded as built in 2000 or later, so a modern panel is not a guarantee.

Small houses. The 1890s houses on College and Teller Avenues and the 1990s two-family houses east of Webster mostly have doorbell-style circuits: a button or two at the door, a transformer in the cellar, a chime or a buzzer in each apartment, and sometimes an electric release in the front door. HPD has written only a few intercom violations at one- and two-family lots here. The faults are simple, the fixes are usually quick, and we price them the same way as everything else, on the $250 visit.

How we handle a shop door that shares a building with apartments

When a shop and the residents share a cellar, the most common repeat fault we look for is a shared supply: a store owner adds a door release or a chime to the building’s transformer, the load goes up, and the residents’ strike begins to drop out when two doors are released at once. We separate the circuits where they should be separate, label each one at the supply, and give the store its own release and supply where the building owner agrees. We schedule work on a shop door around opening hours, and we keep the residents’ street door locking throughout.

Big buildings produced just over half of Claremont’s 311 buzzer complaints. The smaller buildings, walk-ups and store buildings among them, produced the rest.

HPD’s self-closing door violations rose sharply after 2022. A door that does not close and latch on its own defeats any buzzer, so we check the closer on every lobby call.

Entrance and vestibule lock violations, 458 at 91 buildings, sit next to the intercom record. Twenty-four of them mention what happens to the lock in a power outage.

Rules that apply to Claremont entry doors

  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. The 1968 date splits Claremont in two. A class A building put up or converted after January 1 of that year owes its tenants entrance doors that shut and lock themselves, and, at eight apartments or more, a two-way voice intercom that can release the door. For the prewar buildings that make up most of the private stock, the intercom duty arises when a majority of the tenants ask for one.
  • Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005. HPD writes buzzer faults under the owner’s general duty to keep the building in good repair; the violation text typically orders the owner to “properly repair the broken or defective bell/buzzer intercom” from the building entrance to the apartment.
  • Correction periods. HPD’s penalties-and-fees page sets 30 days to correct a class B violation and 14 days for a class C self-closing door. Since 2021, HPD has written 3,399 self-closing door violations in the study area, almost all class C, and 458 entrance or vestibule lock violations.
  • Line-voltage work. New 120-volt circuits for a supply or a door operator need a licensed electrician and, where required, a permit from the Department of Buildings. We do the low-voltage side and coordinate the rest.
  • Accessibility. Where a panel or button is moved, we keep the operable parts within the 2010 ADA reach range, a maximum of 48 inches for a forward reach.
  • Egress doors. A magnetic lock on an exit door must release on a sensor and a manual exit device under the building code’s electrically locked egress rules; we never wire one to fail locked.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Claremont: Questions Answered

These are the questions Claremont owners, supers, boards and tenants bring to us, grouped by what is really being asked. Each answer is written for this neighborhood’s buildings and checked against the questions on our other neighborhood pages so that none is repeated.

When the same apartment is cited again

HPD wrote our apartment’s intercom in 2024, the landlord said it was fixed, and an inspector wrote the same apartment again this year. Is that one fault or two?

Usually one. When the same apartment is cited twice, the likeliest explanation is that the first repair replaced the part that was easy to see, the handset on the wall, while the break that caused the silence stayed where it was: in the run from the apartment to the riser, in a splice on the landing, or in the supply in the cellar. Those faults come and go with heat, cold and vibration, so a new handset can work for months before the old break opens again.

The way to tell is to test the line, not the station. We connect a known-good station at the apartment, then at the nearest splice, then at the cellar end, and measure at each point. If the old fault is still there, we can show you where it is. If the line tests clean all the way through and only the new handset is failing, then it really is a second fault, and that is worth knowing too, because it changes who should pay for it.

A brand-new handset worked for about three weeks and then the apartment went silent again. What fails that fast?

Three things, mostly. The first is a handset that does not match the system: some substitutes draw more current or use a different number of conductors, and they work at first on a line that is already weak and then drop out as the supply warms up or the weather changes. The second is a splice disturbed while the handset was changed; a twisted joint that was moved gets loose within weeks. The third is the supply itself, which may have been on its way out before the handset was ever touched.

We look at all three before replacing anything again. A handset that failed in three weeks is likely still within its maker’s warranty period, and if it is the wrong model for the building, putting the same model back will give the same result.

Our violation shows as “dismissed” in HPD’s online records, but the buzzer still does not work. What does dismissed actually mean for a tenant?

It means the violation is no longer open in HPD’s system. The record by itself does not say how that happened, and it is not a certificate that anyone tested your apartment’s station. Of the 392 intercom violations written in our Claremont study area since 2021, 163 now read “dismissed.” Some of those buzzers were certainly repaired. Others may not have been.

If yours still does not work, the practical path is the same as the first time: tell the owner or managing agent in writing, keep a copy, and file a new complaint with 311 if nothing happens. From the owner’s side, a dismissed violation on a buzzer that still fails is a strong reason to have the whole line tested rather than repeat whatever was done before.

Every time a technician comes, the buzzer tests fine. Tenants say it fails in the evenings. How do you find a fault that only appears some of the time?

Intermittent faults are the hardest to catch and the most common in big, old systems. Evening failures in particular often trace to load: more apartments are home, more calls are made, the lobby door is released more often, and a supply or a splice that holds during a quiet afternoon test sags under use. Temperature does it too, as cellar rooms cool at night.

So we test under load. We hold the release while calling several apartments, measure the supply voltage while the strike is energized, and flex each splice on the line while someone listens at the apartment. Where that still does not reproduce the fault, we can leave a simple log in place, or ask the super to note the time of each failure, and come back with the pattern. Most intermittent faults show themselves within one or two of those steps.

January leads, and late summer runs high. Seasonal swings like these fit faults that come and go with temperature and humidity in cellars and risers.

Buildings with fifty to three hundred apartments

Our building on the Grand Concourse has a side entrance with its own panel, and that is the panel that keeps failing. Why would one of two doors wear out faster?

Because nobody watches it. A main lobby door is used in front of a super, neighbors and often a camera; a side or rear entrance is used by people in a hurry, by delivery workers and, at night, sometimes by people who are not supposed to be there. It is pulled harder, propped more often and pried at more. The panel and the strike on that door take more abuse and get less attention.

The repair for a door like that is usually heavier hardware rather than another of the same: a flush metal panel with fixings that need a security bit, a strike rated for the door’s weight and use, a keeper fixed through the frame, and a closer strong enough to pull the door home against wind on the side street. We also check whether the side panel shares a supply with the lobby, because a fault at the side door can drag down the main entrance as well.

In a building with more than 300 apartments, how long does a whole-building intercom survey take, and does it disrupt tenants?

A full survey of a building that size is usually a day’s work for two people, sometimes longer when access to the apartments is difficult. One technician stays at the lobby panel and calls each apartment in turn; the other moves floor by floor, checking that each station rings, talks and releases the door. Where nobody answers, we note the apartment and test the line at the nearest landing box instead.

Tenants are not asked to do anything except answer if they are home. We ask management to post a notice a few days ahead with the date and hours. The result is a written list, line by line, of what works, what does not, and where the faults cluster, which is what an owner needs before deciding between repairs and a larger replacement.

Cable and phone installers work in our riser closets all the time. Can their work knock out apartment buzzers, and how would we know?

Yes, it happens. Intercom pairs, telephone pairs and old cable runs often share the same closets and sometimes the same terminal blocks in prewar buildings. An installer looking for a free pair can borrow an intercom pair, cut through a bundle, or pull a splice loose while working. The giveaway is timing: one or two apartments on the same line going quiet within days of a service appointment.

When we find that, we label the intercom pairs and terminals in that closet clearly, separate them from other services where there is room, and note it in the record we leave. Telling the super to check the intercom after any installer has been in the closet catches most of these the same day.

Our intercom power supply sits in a cellar room that takes water during heavy storms. What should be moved, and what can stay?

Anything powered should be off the floor and away from where the water runs: the supply or transformer, the amplifier if there is one, and the terminal blocks where risers land. Webster Avenue follows the old Mill Brook, and NYC Parks notes that the Black Swamp near the old Claremont estate was only filled in by the early twentieth century, so a cellar on low ground that floods in a storm is not a surprise. Equipment that sits in water once may keep working for a while and then fail, and a corroded terminal is a classic source of repeat faults.

Moving a supply up a wall to a board, extending the leads and re-terminating the risers on a raised block is usually a modest job priced from the transformer and riser lines on our list. The 120-volt feed to the supply, if it has to move too, is work for a licensed electrician, and we coordinate it.

The door that takes the traffic

After a new closer went on our lobby door, the door rebounds off the frame before the strike can hold it. Is that the closer or the strike?

Usually the closer’s settings rather than the strike. A closer has separate adjustments for its sweep speed, its latch speed at the end of the swing and, on many models, its backcheck. If the latch speed is set too fast, the door hits the frame, bounces, and the latch never settles in the strike’s keeper; the buzzer then seems to have stopped locking the door. A strike that is set slightly out of line with the latch makes the bounce worse.

We adjust the closer so the door arrives at the latch firmly but without slamming, then set the strike so the latch drops straight into the keeper. If the closer is the wrong size for the door’s weight, it needs replacing; a closer that has to be turned to its limit to shut a heavy lobby door will not hold its setting for long.

The keeper on our street door has been bent twice this year by people yanking the door. Is there hardware that survives that?

Yes. A bent keeper means the door is being pulled while the strike is locked, or the latch is catching on the keeper’s edge every time the door closes. Heavier strikes have keepers made to take that kind of load, and a strike body fixed through the frame with longer screws or through-bolts resists twisting far better than one screwed into a tired wooden or thin metal jamb.

We also look at why the door is being yanked. If residents are pulling because the release is weak or slow, fixing the supply or relay ends the yanking. If the latch is dragging on the keeper, a small adjustment to the door’s hinges or the strike’s position removes the edge it is catching on. A keeper bent twice in a year is very often the sign of a door that never closes cleanly.

A sketch of the door hardware we check on every lobby call: closer, hinges, electric strike, keeper and latch. A release that buzzes but leaves the door shut usually points to one of them.

Different owners, different buildings

I live in a Claremont Village tower. What should our tenant association write down about intercom outages so a NYCHA repair ticket is useful?

Specifics. For each outage, note the building and entrance, which floors or apartments are affected, whether the problem is no ring, no voice, or no door release, the date it started, and the ticket number NYCHA gave. If several apartments on the same line are affected, say so; that tells a technician to look at the riser rather than the stations. Photos of a damaged lobby panel help.

A floor-by-floor log kept by the association is far more useful than many separate complaints that each say “intercom broken.” It also creates a record that the association can bring to management meetings. News 12 Bronx reported in June 2025 that Butler Houses tenants spent weeks being sent between management and the post office over a broken mailbox; a dated log is what cuts through that kind of back-and-forth. We do not repair NYCHA equipment, but we are glad to explain what a log should contain.

Our building is run by a nonprofit with a front desk staffed around the clock. Should the desk be able to release the street door, and what goes wrong when the desk and the intercom are separate systems?

In most staffed buildings the desk should have a release, because the desk is where visitors actually check in. The trouble starts when the desk button was added later and wired into the intercom’s release circuit in a way the original system was not designed for. Then a fault at the desk can hold the door unlocked, or a desk release can stop working whenever the lobby panel is in use.

We trace how the desk release is connected, give it its own relay where it shares one, and make sure the door locks again after every release, from either place. In supportive-housing buildings such as 3500 Park Avenue, which the New York Housing Conference describes as 115 studio and two-bedroom apartments for formerly homeless individuals, seniors and families, the desk is part of how the building keeps residents safe, so its release has to be reliable without leaving the door open.

A fire on the top floor of our building was put out and the apartments are being repaired. What in the intercom should be tested before tenants move back?

Everything below the fire, as well as on the fire floor. The water used to fight a fire travels down risers, shafts and walls, and low-voltage wiring and stations on the floors below can be soaked even where nothing burned. In February 2026, ABC7 reported a four-alarm fire through the top floor and cockloft of a five-story building off the Grand Concourse in what it called the Claremont section, with 82 residents displaced; buildings recovering from fires like that need their entry systems checked from the top down.

We test each station on the affected lines, the risers through the wet floors, and the supply and panel, and replace what water has damaged. Stations that look dry can still have corroded contacts, so we test function rather than appearance. The strike and closer on the street door are checked too, because firefighters forcing a door can bend a frame or a keeper.

Our HDFC co-op is paying for the work out of reserves. Can the repairs be phased, so the street door is fixed this month and the apartment stations next year?

Yes, and in a building with a repeat record, phasing in the right order saves money. The street door, its strike, keeper, closer and release come first, because a door that does not lock is a safety problem for everyone and is usually the cheaper part of the job. The supply comes next, because a weak supply makes every other repair look unreliable. The risers and stations can then be repaired line by line as the budget allows, starting with the lines that fail most.

We put the phases in writing with each line’s price from the Bronx list, so the board can approve one phase at a time. Fifty-six residential lots in our Claremont study area are held by housing development fund companies, and a written phased plan is often what a volunteer board needs to vote on a repair.

Cost and what you get for it

Why does testing the whole line cost more than replacing one handset, and when is it worth it?

It costs more because it takes longer: a technician has to reach the other apartments on the line, open the landing or closet boxes and test at each one. On our list that work falls within the riser repair range of $850 to $2,400 when a riser fault is found, against $250 to $525 for an apartment station. For a first-time failure in one apartment of a small building, a station test and replacement is often all that is needed.

It is worth it when the same apartment or line has failed before, when several apartments on one line have complained, or when HPD has cited the building more than once. In those cases another handset is the expensive choice, because it buys a few months and another visit. We tell you before we start which situation you are in and what the test is likely to cost.

Why do two contractors quote very different prices for the same dead buzzer?

Because they are usually quoting different repairs. One quote may be for the part at the apartment, a handset at a few hundred dollars; another may include testing the line and fixing whatever the test finds; a third may be for a new lobby panel or a whole system. All three can be offered for the same symptom, and only one of them is aimed at the actual fault.

Ask each contractor what they expect has failed and how they will confirm it before replacing anything. A quote that names a test, a part and a price is comparable; a quote that names only a product is not. Equipment

Types of Door Buzzer Systems We Repair

Claremont’s buildings span 130 years, so the systems behind their front doors range from a push button and a bell to a networked video panel. These are the categories we work on, and where each one tends to turn up in the neighborhood.

Audio handsets and wall stations

The two-wire and multi-wire handsets in most prewar walk-ups and elevator buildings. Replaced one by one over decades, often with mixed makers, which is where mismatches start.

Lobby button panels

Rows of call buttons with a speaker grille and a directory, common on the Concourse and the side avenues. Buttons wear, speakers rot in the weather, and directory cards fade.

Supplies, transformers and amplifiers

The cellar end of the system. Transformers in small buildings, regulated supplies and amplifiers in big ones. A tired supply is behind many faults that look like they live elsewhere.

Electric strikes and keepers

The part in the door frame that lets the latch go. Fail-secure strikes stay locked without power; the keeper takes the force of every pull on a locked door.

Magnetic locks with exit sensors

Found mostly in the newer buildings, holding glass or metal doors with an electromagnet and releasing on a motion sensor, a push button and the intercom.

Video entry panels and monitors

Panels with a camera that call indoor monitors or tenants’ phones. Common in post-2000 affordable housing; faults are often network, power or configuration rather than wiring.

Card and fob readers

Readers beside or built into the entry panel, controlling side doors, laundry rooms and lobbies in staffed buildings. Their door controllers share supplies with the intercom more often than they should.

Front-desk release consoles

Buttons or small consoles at a security or reception desk, wired to the same door. Common in supportive housing and senior buildings.

Repair, or replace the station, or replace the system

For an apartment station, we replace it when it is the confirmed fault and a matching part exists. For a lobby panel, we repair buttons, speakers and faceplates when the panel’s electronics test good, and replace it when they do not. For the whole system, we recommend replacement only when a survey shows widespread failure and the parts are gone. In every case we show the test that led to the recommendation.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Claremont

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or similar answer boxes from our research environment, so we cannot quote them directly. Instead, this section tests the claims those answers tend to repeat, taken from the cost guides, vendor blogs and neighborhood guides that rank for buzzer repair and for Claremont itself, against the public records for this neighborhood.

Claim one: Claremont is basically public housing

A quick summary can make Claremont sound like towers and little else. The towers are real and large: NYCHA’s nine lots in Claremont Village hold about 4,256 apartments, roughly 29 percent of the homes in our study area. But that leaves more than 10,000 homes in private prewar walk-ups, 1920s elevator buildings, mid-century towers, 1990s houses and newer affordable buildings.

For buzzer repair the distinction matters more than anything else on this page. NYCHA repairs its own entry systems, and its buildings appear in neither HPD’s violation file nor 311’s buzzer complaints. Every enforcement number quoted here, the 392 violations since 2021 and the 680 complaints since 2020, comes from the private two-thirds.

So an answer that tells a Claremont tenant to “call NYCHA” may be right for a Butler Houses resident and wrong for someone on Sheridan Avenue, whose landlord is responsible and whose recourse runs through the owner, 311 and HPD.

Claim two: a buzzer repair is a small job, $150 to $400

That range comes from vendor content such as Swiftlane’s 2026 intercom cost guide, which says minor repairs typically run $150 to $400. For a single apartment in a small building, a minor fault can land near there. Our own Bronx list starts with a $250 service call that covers the diagnosis and is credited toward the repair.

In a Claremont building with a repeat record, the minor repair is often the reason the complaint came back. A handset swap fits the cheap range; finding and fixing the riser splice behind it falls in our $850 to $2,400 riser line. Quoting the first and skipping the second is how an apartment ends up in HPD’s file twice.

The useful question is not what a repair costs on average but what failed, and whether the price covers that part. We confirm the specific price on site, before any part is replaced.

Claim three: if it keeps breaking, replace the whole intercom

Replacement vendors make this case naturally. ButterflyMX’s guide for small buildings puts a replacement around $1,500 to $2,500, and HomeAdvisor’s national average for installing an intercom is about $2,500. Aggregators such as Angi and Fixr work the same way, from national averages that know nothing about a particular building. Those numbers describe small systems far from a Claremont tower of 300 apartments.

More to the point, many repeat faults are not in the part a replacement would change. A new lobby panel does nothing for a door whose keeper is bent or whose closer cannot pull it shut, and a new set of stations on a damaged riser inherits the riser’s faults.

Replacement makes sense when a survey shows failure across most of the system and parts are no longer made. Until that survey exists, it is a guess.

Claim four: once HPD closes the violation, the problem is solved

The record does not support this in Claremont. Eighty-four apartments in the study area have been cited for their intercom in two or more different years, and 59 buildings have been cited in three or more. A closed or dismissed status shows the violation is no longer open; it is not a test result.

Since January 2021, 163 of the 392 intercom violations here read “dismissed” and 100 read “closed,” while 34 read “not complied with” and 15 record that HPD could not get in to re-inspect. A violation that ends in paperwork and returns a year later is the pattern this page is built around.

For owners, the cheapest violation is the one that is not written twice. A written record of what was tested and repaired, line by line, is what lets an owner, a tenant or an inspector see the difference between a new fault and an old one.

Claim five: a modern video intercom ends buzzer problems

Newer systems remove some failure points, the long two-wire runs to every apartment among them, and add others: network connections, accounts, door controllers, phone-number directories and power for cameras and readers. In Claremont, buildings recorded as built in 2000 or later have drawn 43 intercom violations since 2021.

A video panel is still a panel on a door. The strike or magnetic lock, the closer and the latch have to work for any intercom to keep a building secure, and a modern panel on a door that does not latch fails the same inspection as an old one.

We work on both, and we say plainly when the problem is the door rather than the electronics.

Claim six: the neighborhood guides agree on what Claremont is

They do not. CityNeighborhoods.NYC bounds Claremont by East 167th Street, the Grand Concourse, the Cross Bronx Expressway, Fulton Avenue, Claremont Parkway and Webster Avenue, and places Claremont Village west of Fulton Avenue toward the Concourse; Wikipedia redirects “Claremont, Bronx” to its Morrisania article and lists Claremont Village as the Butler, Webster, Morris and Morrisania houses east of Webster. CityNeighborhoods dates the Claremont Village Houses to 1959; Wikipedia’s articles give completion dates in 1964 and 1965.

Details drift too. One guide says the park’s mansion has been stabilized as a landmark; NYC Parks says the Zborowski mansion was razed in 1938 and replaced with a gazebo. Parks dates the city’s purchase to 1888–90 and credits Elliott and Anna Zborowski with building the house in 1859, while The Neighborhoods newsletter argues that Elliott was born the year the house was finished and that his father built it.

For this page we used the city’s 2020 neighborhood tabulation areas and census tracts, described in the sources section, and we report the conflicts rather than choose a winner. An AI summary that states one boundary as fact is guessing.

Claim seven: tenants can get a dead buzzer fixed fast through 311

311 is the right channel, and its apartment-maintenance complaint explicitly covers doorbells, buzzers and intercoms that are broken or missing. But a complaint is the start of a process, not a repair. An inspector has to visit, get into the building and the apartment, and find the condition; then the owner has HPD’s correction period, 30 days for a class B violation.

Claremont tenants have used 311 heavily: 680 buzzer complaints since 2020, rising from 46 in 2020 to 145 in 2025. The repetition in that file, 21 addresses filing two or more complaints each in 2026 alone, shows that a complaint does not always lead to a lasting fix.

The fastest lasting repair still comes from an owner who calls a technician and asks for the cause, not the quickest silence.

Field Notes: Reading a Repeat Record Before the Visit

This is our standard approach for a building that has been cited before, not an account of a particular job. When a Claremont owner calls about a buzzer, we look the building up in HPD’s violation list and the 311 file first. Three things decide what we bring and how long we plan to stay. The first is whether the past violations stay in one apartment or move along a line; moving faults mean the riser or the supply. The second is whether HPD’s notes mention the entrance door, its lock or its self-closing hardware, because a door that does not latch makes every intercom repair look like a failure. The third is the building’s size and age in PLUTO, which tells us whether to expect a transformer on a cellar plank or an amplifier in a locked room.

On site, we ask the super what was replaced last and where the old parts went. A handset swapped twice in a year is a clue in itself. Then we test the line, not just the station, and we write down what each box on that line looked like before and after. That page of notes is often worth more to the next technician than the repair.

Yes. Some owners of big buildings prefer a scheduled test of every line to waiting for complaints. We agree the scope and price in advance; each visit ends with the same line-by-line record, so faults are caught while they are still small.

Does testing a whole line mean opening walls inside apartments?
+

Rarely. Most testing happens at the stations themselves and at the boxes on landings and in closets where risers are spliced. We open a wall only when a break is confirmed inside it and there is no other route, and we tell the owner and the tenant before doing so.
