The Neighborhood

Why Queens Co-op Properties Need Security Planned Building by Building

Queens holds a kind of cooperative housing the rest of the city mostly does not: the garden co-op. Instead of one tall building with one lobby, a garden co-op is a campus of two- and three-story buildings set around lawns, courtyards and parking courts, each with its own front door and often a rear door too. Glen Oaks Village, near the Nassau County line, is the extreme case. The cooperative's own history page says it was built in 1947 and covers 110 acres with 134 buildings and 2,904 garden-style apartments, and that it became a co-op in 1981 (Glen Oaks Village, About). A board that wants to replace the intercom there is not buying one lobby panel. It is buying a plan for well over a hundred entrances.

That is why we treat co-op building security in Queens differently from a Manhattan tower job. Brick Underground's March 2019 guide to Queens garden apartments, written by Leah Hochbaum Rosner, noted that most of these complexes sit in the more suburban parts of the borough closest to Nassau, named large co-op complexes in Bayside and Glen Oaks, and pointed to more in Fresh Meadows. It also described how residents reach their apartments: sometimes through a private entrance, other times through "a shared hallway with four or more apartment entrances." Michael Chadwick of Citi Habitats summed up what that means for security in one line: "There are never long hallways or attended lobbies like more urban-style living" (Brick Underground, Mar 7, 2019). No attended lobby means the door hardware, the intercom and the cameras are the doorman.

The borough's elevator co-ops have their own pattern. Jackson Heights is where the term "garden apartment" was coined, by the Queensboro Corporation, for blocks of buildings wrapped around shared interior gardens; the 1919 development there included the area's first co-op, and later complexes like the Château put 12 buildings around one garden on 34th Avenue (Wikipedia, Jackson Heights). In Kew Gardens and Forest Hills, 1920s and 1930s builders favored courtyard plans with several entrances so that only a couple of apartments shared a landing. Along Queens Boulevard in Rego Park and Forest Hills, postwar six- to twelve-story buildings, many now co-ops, have one main lobby but a garage, a service door, a laundry room and a rear yard gate. Each of those doors is a separate decision for the board.

Then there are the very large cooperatives. North Shore Towers on the Floral Park border is three 34-floor towers with 1,844 apartments on a 110-acre property, converted to a co-op in 1987 (Wikipedia, North Shore Towers). Rochdale Village in southeast Queens is 20 thirteen-story buildings in five circular groups on a 120-acre site, built under the Mitchell-Lama program and, when it opened, the largest private cooperative housing complex in the world until Co-op City was finished in 1971 (Wikipedia, Rochdale Village). The NYC Comptroller notes that a number of the city's remaining affordable cooperatives, "predominantly in Queens," were developed through federal loan programs such as HUD Sections 213, 221d and 236 (NYC Comptroller, Spotlight). Those boards often answer to a regulator as well as to shareholders, so their security bids need to be itemized and easy to audit.

The problems we are called about follow the building type. Garden co-ops call about courtyard and rear doors that never latch, intercom wiring that fails between buildings, laundry rooms used by people who do not live there, and cars broken into in the parking court. Elevator co-ops call about mailrooms. In April 2025, QNS reported that a single burglar hit building mail rooms across five Queens neighborhoods, including one Jackson Heights building three times in March, and that police released a surveillance image taken inside that building (QNS, Apr 16, 2025). Fox 5 showed a man forcing an Elmhurst lobby door with a screwdriver in April 2022 and emptying the mailroom (Fox 5 NY). The pattern is the same every time: a door that can be forced or picked, a room behind it with no alarm and a camera that captures the person but not what they did. That is the order a Queens coop security plan follows on this page: doors first, the intercom that controls them second, then fobs and cameras. Most Queens co-ops that fix the entry system add key fob access to the laundry and storage rooms within a year, so we plan the cable for both from the first visit.

Queens co-op facts that shape a security plan

  • Co-ops are a large share of the city's homes: the NYC Comptroller counts 450,000 occupied apartments in cooperative buildings citywide and notes that remaining affordable co-ops built with federal loans are "predominantly in Queens" (NYC Comptroller).
  • One co-op, 134 buildings: Glen Oaks Village has 2,904 garden-style apartments on 110 acres, built in 1947 and converted to a co-op in 1981 (Glen Oaks Village).
  • Intercom violations are lower in Queens, but not rare: NYC Open Data shows 342 HPD housing violations issued in Queens in 2025 whose description mentions an intercom (79 class A, 261 class B, 2 class C), against 1,637 in the Bronx and 1,157 in Brooklyn (NYC Open Data, HPD violations). Most come from rental inspections, so treat them as a measure of how often entry systems break, not a co-op count.
  • The landmark garden-apartment district: the Jackson Heights Historic District, designated October 19, 1993, was built mainly between 1914 and 1939 and is described as the first and still the largest garden-apartment community in the United States (Historic Districts Council).
  • State law on entrance doors: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, in buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, an intercom that lets each apartment talk to the entrance and release the door (NY Senate, MDL §50-a).
  • Mailrooms are repeat targets: the April 2025 QNS report described one Jackson Heights mailroom burglarized three times in a single month (QNS).

Intercoms

Co-op Intercom Upgrades in Queens: Repair, Replace or Phase It In

The intercom is where most Queens co-op boards start, and it is where the most money gets wasted when the decision is rushed. The first question is not which brand. It is whether the cable inside each building and between buildings is still good. In a 2025 CooperatorNews piece on replacing intercom systems, one installer advised boards to "retain the existing infrastructure with the exception of the panel" where they can, and noted that wiring from the 1960s to the 1980s can have "a typical lifespan of 25-50 years" (CooperatorNews, Nov 14, 2025). In a garden co-op that matters twice, because the cable is not just in the walls; it runs underground or through basements from one building to the next. If those runs test well, a co-op intercom upgrade can be mostly panels and stations. If they do not, the board is paying for a new path between buildings, and that is the line item that changes the budget.

So our walk-through on a coop intercom system starts at the building that fails most often and works outward. We test the lines to each building and a sample of apartments, look at how the existing panel talks to the apartments (two-wire, multi-wire, or already digital), check every door the panel releases, and note where a board could drop in-unit stations altogether and let shareholders answer on their phones. Some boards want a station in every apartment because older shareholders will not use an app. Others want phone answering only because it means no work inside apartments. Many Queens co-ops end up with a mix: app answering by default, with a simple indoor station for any shareholder who asks.

For a garden co-op with dozens of buildings, the realistic plan is often a phased co-op intercom replacement: one shared platform chosen up front, then buildings converted in an order the board sets, a few at a time, across one or two budget years. Everything on the new platform can be managed from one place even while old buildings still run the old system. For a single elevator building, the choice is simpler: replace the lobby panel and keep the riser, replace both, or add video at the lobby and leave the apartments on audio for now.

When the board is ready to bid it out, write down the same things for every contractor: the number of buildings, entrances and apartments, which doors must release from the intercom, whether in-unit stations are wanted, and what happens to the old equipment. Our longer guide for boards walks through the approval steps, the RFP and the shareholder notice (our co-op board intercom upgrade guide is being published now). For the technical side in this borough, see intercom installation in Queens, and if one building simply stopped working, start with intercom repair in Queens. Boards comparing two popular cloud video platforms can read Akuvox vs ButterflyMX first.

What a Queens board usually decides about the apartments

Every co-op intercom modernization ends with the same question: what goes inside the apartments. Removing an old audio handset usually leaves a hole in the wall. We cover old openings with blank plates wherever we can, but we do not patch or paint; that goes to the building's painter or the shareholder, depending on the co-op's rules. Boards that want to avoid apartment work entirely choose phone answering with no in-unit hardware. Boards that want every shareholder covered on day one choose a station per apartment, which means scheduling access to every unit, building by building.

Door Buzzers

Door Buzzer and Door Release Repairs Across Queens Co-op Buildings

A large part of our co-op work in Queens is not new systems at all. It is one building in a complex where the buzzer stopped, one entrance that releases but will not relock, or a lobby where residents hear the visitor but cannot open the door. These are door buzzer repairs, and they start with the $250 service call. If the fix is simple, the $250 covers it; if it needs parts or more labor, the $250 goes toward the repair.

Our published price ranges for the common parts apply in Queens too: an apartment station $250 to $525, a door release relay or button $250 to $475, a 24-volt transformer $325 to $650, lobby panel work $650 to $1,800, an electric strike replaced and the door aligned $450 to $950, and riser repair inside occupied walls $850 to $2,400 (door buzzer repair pricing). In garden complexes the underground run between buildings is its own case; we price it after tracing it, because the length and the path decide the cost.

Two Queens-specific causes come up again and again. The first is the door, not the buzzer: courtyard and rear doors on garden buildings take weather, kids and delivery carts, and once the closer or hinge wears, the strike never lines up and the release seems dead. The second is water. Basement junction points and underground cable between buildings get wet, and a system that works in August fails after the first freeze. We check both before we touch the panel. Access Control

Key Fob Access Control for Queens Cooperatives

Keys are the weak spot of every large Queens co-op. A garden complex with a hundred doors might have a handful of key patterns, and every shareholder who moved out in the last thirty years may still have one. Re-keying the whole property is expensive and solves the problem only until the next copy is cut. A co-op key fob system fixes it differently: each shareholder gets a credential, each door has a reader, and when a fob is lost or an apartment is sold, the managing agent or super turns that one credential off.

On a multi-building property, the board's first decisions are which doors get readers and who controls the list. We usually suggest starting with the doors residents use least carefully: rear and courtyard doors, laundry rooms, storage and bike rooms, and the parking gate, then the main entrances once residents are used to the fobs. Schedules matter, too. A laundry room that only opens to resident fobs between early morning and late evening stops most outside use on its own, and a contractor fob that expires on the last day of a job stops the rest.

Smart access also brings data rules. HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) says owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as key fobs must provide a data retention and privacy policy, obtain express consent, and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized (NYC HPD). How that applies to a particular co-op is a question for the co-op's attorney; we set up the system so the board can follow whatever policy it adopts. For readers, strikes, mag locks and controllers, see access control installation in Queens.

Elevator Access

Elevator Access Control in Queens Co-op Buildings and Towers

Elevator access control matters most in the borough's elevator co-ops and towers: postwar buildings on Queens Boulevard, Austin Street and Yellowstone Boulevard, and high-rise cooperatives near the city line. A reader in the cab can limit each fob to the lobby, its own floor, the laundry or garage level and the roof deck if there is one, so a visitor who slips in behind a resident cannot ride to every floor. In a building with a garage, the garage-level button is often the first one boards want locked down.

The elevator contractor controls the car. Before the board approves anything, we arrange a joint look with them so the board knows whether the controller can accept a reader directly or needs an interface priced by the elevator company. If the co-op has an elevator modernization coming, the cheapest time to add floor control is during that job, when the traveling cable and controller are already open. See elevator access control installation in Queens for how the readers tie in.

Cameras

Co-op Security Cameras for Queens: Mailrooms, Courtyards and Parking

Cameras are where we keep this page short, because our camera pages cover the detail. For a Queens co-op the priorities are usually the same: the mailroom (aimed at the boxes and the door, not just the door), the lobby and vestibule, the rear and courtyard doors, the path from the parking court to each building, and the parking area itself. In September 2026, PIX11 reported a string of car break-ins along 62nd Drive in Rego Park caught on surveillance video, with one resident saying, "They come multiple times, it's unbelievable" (PIX11). Parking cameras do not stop that on their own, but placed low enough and lit well enough, they give police a usable picture.

Footage belongs to the co-op, and the board should decide in writing who can view and export it. Cameras in common areas are a board decision under the co-op's governing documents, and audio recording raises separate legal questions; ask the co-op's attorney before turning on any microphone. For co-op camera layouts, recorders and pricing, see apartment building security camera installation in Queens (a co-op security camera installation page for Queens is on the way).

Mailrooms, Cabling & Scope

Mailrooms, Cable Between Buildings and What Stays Outside Our Scope

Mailrooms and package rooms

The Queens mailroom break-ins in the news share one detail: the door gave way. In the Elmhurst case Fox 5 covered, the lobby door was forced with a screwdriver. In a Forest Hills case QNS reported in November 2022, the suspect picked the lock on the outer door and then the lock on a large mailbox (QNS, Nov 4, 2022). A co-op mailroom plan therefore starts with the door: a strike or lock that resists prying, a latch guard where the gap is wide, a closer that actually closes, and a door contact that tells someone when the door was held or forced. Then a fob reader so only residents and carriers get in, and a camera aimed at the boxes and the parcel shelves.

Cable, network and the space between buildings

A multi-building co-op lives or dies on its cable. We run the low-voltage cable and network gear for the systems we install: Cat6 and fiber between buildings, PoE switches in basements, and protected runs through existing conduit, tunnels or crawl spaces. Under the NYC Administrative Code, signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under 50 volts generally needs no electrical permit, apart from life-safety exceptions (NYC Admin Code §28-105.4.7). For larger network work, see structured cabling installation in Queens.

Fire alarm and electrical

Fire alarm systems in co-op buildings are designed from engineer drawings, filed with the FDNY and tested before acceptance, and they are never bundled with cameras. If your co-op needs fire alarm work, that is a separate project; see fire alarm installation in Queens. High-voltage electrical work, including new circuits for gate motors, stays with the building's licensed electrician.

Building Specialists

Co-op Security for Queens' Architecture

Queens co-ops fall into six building types, and each one changes where the money goes. These cards describe the type, not any client building; we never name the co-ops we work in.

A

Postwar garden complexes

Two- and three-story buildings around lawns and parking courts in Glen Oaks, Bayside, Fresh Meadows, Little Neck and Floral Park. Many entrances, short hallways, no lobby staff. The plan is per-building door stations on one shared platform, fobs on rear and laundry doors, and cable between buildings.

B

Landmarked garden co-ops

Jackson Heights and Sunnyside Gardens buildings grouped around interior gardens, inside historic districts. Entrances face both the street and the garden, and anything on the facade follows Landmarks rules.

C

1920s–30s courtyard elevator buildings

Kew Gardens and Forest Hills buildings with a garden court and several entrances, so only a few apartments share each stair. Several small panels, or one main panel and slave stations, depending on the riser.

D

Postwar boulevard elevator buildings

Six- to twelve-story buildings along Queens Boulevard and its side streets in Rego Park and Forest Hills: one lobby, a garage or parking court, laundry and storage rooms. Classic candidates for elevator floor control and mailroom work.

E

Co-op towers

High-rise cooperatives with a staffed desk, a garage and hundreds of apartments, such as the towers near the Nassau line. The desk needs to see and answer every entrance, and the fob list runs into the thousands.

F

Limited-equity and federally financed co-ops

Mitchell-Lama and HUD-financed cooperatives, from multi-building campuses in southeast Queens to mid-rise complexes elsewhere. Large door counts, tight budgets and bids that must be itemized for a regulator.

Garden complexes: one platform, many front doors

The single most useful decision a garden co-op board can make is to choose one intercom and access platform for the whole property before replacing anything. Then every building that gets converted joins the same management screen, the same fob list and the same app, even if the conversion takes two years. We map the property building by building on the first visit: each entrance, which doors release from the intercom, where each building's cable enters, and how buildings connect today. Where there is no usable path between buildings, a small network switch and a cellular or point-to-point link is sometimes cheaper than digging, and we price both when it applies. Brick Underground's guide notes that many of the larger complexes were built for returning veterans after World War II, and the original copper often dates from that era or from a later 1970s or 1980s replacement; we test it before anyone assumes it has to go.

Landmarked garden co-ops in Jackson Heights and Sunnyside

Sunnyside Gardens was designated a historic district by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on June 26, 2007; it covers roughly 600 buildings built in the 1920s around large landscaped courtyards to plans by Clarence Stein and Henry Wright (CityLand). In both Sunnyside and Jackson Heights, the LPC permit guidebook allows intercom panels within or next to a door opening and limits a door to one intercom panel, with fixtures kept "neutral and discreet" (LPC Permit Guidebook). In practice we mount inside the vestibule wherever we can and choose slim, dark finishes when a panel has to sit at the door. If the building's architect or expediter needs to file, we supply the equipment sheets and mounting details.

Courtyard elevator buildings in Kew Gardens and Forest Hills

A Kew Gardens history survey describes the 1922 Kew Hall as a half-block building with an interior garden court and multiple entrances so only two apartments shared each landing, and notes that "this courtyard plan became a favorite among Kew Gardens developers throughout the 1920s" (6 to Celebrate, Kew Gardens). Every one of those entrances needs a working lock and a way to let a visitor in. Some of these co-ops still run each entrance as its own small audio system. Bringing them onto one platform lets the super see which door was used and lets a shareholder answer from any entrance on one phone app.

Postwar buildings on and around Queens Boulevard

The Queens Chronicle's history of Rego Park's postwar "apartments in the sky" describes a 1952 complex of three 12-story buildings at Queens Boulevard and 102nd Street with about 70 percent of its site given to lawns, gardens and playgrounds, now a co-op (Queens Chronicle). That layout is typical: big grounds, several buildings, a garage, and residents who cross open space between doors. These buildings often have an older elevator, which is why a Habitat story about a 98-unit, six-story Forest Hills co-op built in 1931 is useful context; its elevator modernization cost about $330,000 and took nine weeks (Habitat). A board planning that kind of job should add elevator floor control to the same schedule.

Towers and limited-equity campuses

In a tower co-op the desk is the center of the system: every entrance and garage gate calls the desk first, the desk can call any apartment, and visitor and delivery logs live in one place. In a limited-equity campus like the 20-building, five-group layout at Rochdale Village, the challenge is scale and budget: thousands of fobs, dozens of lobbies and a board that must show every dollar. In both cases we write the scope building by building and line by line so a board or its regulator can approve it in phases.

Commercial Co-op Security

Commercial Co-op Security in Queens: Shops, Offices and Mixed-Use Buildings

Many Queens co-ops have stores on the ground floor, and some cooperatives own whole commercial strips. That brings shop owners, deliveries and late hours into the same building as shareholders. A commercial co-op security plan keeps the two worlds apart: the store's doors and cameras on the store's system, the residential entrance, mailroom and stairs on the co-op's, and a clear rule about which cameras the co-op may see and which belong to the tenant.

The corridors where we see this most are the shopping streets beneath elevator co-ops: 37th Avenue and 82nd Street in Jackson Heights, Austin Street and Queens Boulevard in Forest Hills, Lefferts Boulevard in Kew Gardens, Main Street in Flushing, whose intersection with Roosevelt Avenue Wikipedia lists as the third-busiest in the city (Wikipedia, Flushing), and Bell Boulevard in Bayside. Each has buildings where the residential door sits a few feet from a store entrance, and where delivery drivers and customers are on the sidewalk all day.

For mixed-use co-ops we usually recommend three things. First, a residential door that cannot be reached from inside the store, or, where it can, a fob reader on that inner door. Second, a rear service door with a contact and a camera, because that is where store and residential trash, deliveries and contractors cross. Third, separate recording or at least separate user rights, so a store tenant's staff never see the residential lobby feed.

Compliance callouts for Queens co-op security work

  • Entrance doors and intercoms: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a sets the self-closing, self-locking door and intercom requirements for class A multiple dwellings; buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968 must add them when tenants of a majority of apartments ask in writing (MDL §50-a). We do not decide how a co-op conversion affects that; the co-op's attorney does.
  • Locks that release on power loss: NYC Building Code section 1010.1.9.8 covers sensor-release electric locks on egress doors, including an egress-side sensor, a push-to-exit device 40 to 48 inches high within 5 feet of the door, release on loss of power and on fire alarm activation; 1010.1.9.9 covers mag locks released by door hardware (NYC BC 1010.1.9.8, 1010.1.9.9).
  • Fob and app data: the Tenant Data Privacy Law sets rules for owners of multiple dwellings using smart access systems (NYC HPD).
  • Historic districts: intercom panels and cameras on landmarked facades in Jackson Heights and Sunnyside Gardens follow the LPC Permit Guidebook (LPC).
  • Permits for our cabling: the city exempts most under-50-volt intercom, data and signal cable from electrical permits, though life-safety systems are treated differently (NYC Admin Code §28-105.4.7).

We are a contractor, not a law firm. Treat this list as a starting point and have the co-op's attorney or architect confirm what applies to your property.

Board Worksheet

A Worksheet for the Queens Co-op Board Meeting Before Any Walk-Through

Boards get better bids when they walk in with the same facts every contractor will need. This worksheet takes a super about an hour on a garden co-op and less in an elevator building.

Your Questions Answered

Co-op Security Systems in Queens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Queens boards, supers and managing agents ask most, grouped by the kind of property they come from: garden complexes first, then elevator buildings and towers, then theft and misuse, then governance.

Garden complexes and multi-building properties

Our garden co-op has dozens of separate buildings. Does each one need its own intercom?

Each building needs its own door station, because each has its own entrance. It does not need its own system. The better design is one platform for the whole property with a station at every entrance, all managed from one screen, one directory and one app. That way a shareholder who moves from one building to another keeps the same credential, and the super can see every door from the office. Buildings can join the platform one at a time, so the board does not have to replace everything in one year.

Can one fob open every building, laundry room and parking gate in a Queens garden co-op?

Yes, if every reader is on the same access platform. Each shareholder's fob is then given only the doors that make sense: their own building, every laundry room or only the nearest one, the storage room and the parking gate if they rent a space. When the shareholder sells, one click removes all of it. The catch is network: every reader has to reach the controller, which brings us back to the cable between buildings.

How do you get intercom and camera cable between buildings that only share a courtyard?

First we look for what already exists: basement-to-basement runs, old intercom conduit, utility tunnels, or crawl spaces under connecting walkways. Reusing an existing pathway is usually the cheapest route. If none exists, the choices are a new underground conduit, which means digging and restoration the board has to approve, or a wireless link between rooftops or walls for network traffic. We price the options side by side so the board can choose, and we never trench without the board's written approval.

Which buildings should a large garden co-op upgrade first?

Start with the buildings that generate the most calls to the super, then the ones nearest the parking areas and public streets, where outsiders get in most easily. If the old system is failing everywhere, start where the cable between buildings is strongest, so the new platform is proven before the hard runs are tackled. A written phase plan, with the order and the cost per phase, gives the board something it can approve one budget year at a time.

What can a garden co-op do about rear and courtyard doors that never latch?

Fix the door before buying electronics. Most rear doors that stay open have a worn closer, a sagging hinge or a strike that no longer lines up after years of carts and weather. A properly adjusted closer, a strike set to the latch and, where needed, a latch guard solve most of it. Then a door contact can report any door left open more than a minute or two, and a fob reader means residents stop propping it for each other.

Elevator co-ops and towers

Our postwar Queens Boulevard co-op still has its original audio intercom. What can we keep?

Often the riser cable, sometimes the apartment boxes as junction points, rarely the lobby panel. Many modern panels can run over existing pairs, so the board can add video at the lobby and phone answering for shareholders without opening every wall. We test the riser floor by floor before recommending anything. If a few floors test badly, we replace those runs rather than the whole riser.

Our courtyard building has three or four separate entrances. Does each need its own panel?

Each entrance residents use needs a way for visitors to call up, so yes, each needs a station. They do not all need a full directory panel. A common setup is one main panel with a directory at the entrance visitors are told to use, and small one-button or keypad stations at the others that call the desk, the super or a code. That keeps cost down and still lets residents enter by fob at any door.

What does a tower co-op with its own security desk need from an intercom contractor?

The desk has to be the hub. Every entrance and garage gate should ring the desk first, the desk should be able to call any apartment and release any door, and after hours the system should route visitors directly to apartments if the desk is closed. Add a visitor and delivery log, fob control on the garage and elevators, and cameras the desk can pull up by door. We design that around the desk's actual routine, which we learn by spending time there on the walk-through.

Can lobby intercom calls reach shareholders who gave up their landlines?

Yes. Older telephone-entry systems dial each apartment's landline, which fails as shareholders drop home phones. Current systems can call a cell phone directly or ring a phone app with video, and most let a shareholder list a second number for a family member or caregiver. For shareholders without smartphones, a simple indoor station or a regular phone number still works.

What happens to an app-based intercom when the building's internet goes down?

It depends on the design, so ask every bidder. A well-built system keeps fobs and keypad codes working locally even with no internet, and the lobby panel can still call any indoor stations wired in the building. What stops is calling phones over the internet. Some panels can fall back to a cellular connection for calls. We put a battery backup on the network gear and controllers so a short power blip does not lock anyone out.

Theft, tailgating and misuse

What makes a co-op mailroom door harder to force open?

A solid door with a frame that does not flex, a strike or lock rated for the door, a latch guard covering the gap, and a closer that pulls the door fully shut. Then electronics: a fob reader so the door is never left on a key, a contact that alerts the super if the door is forced or held, and a camera aimed at the boxes so the footage shows what was taken. The Queens mailroom thefts in the news all began with a door that gave way to a screwdriver or a pick.

Is it worth putting cameras on a co-op parking lot or garage court?

Yes, if they are placed for faces and plates rather than for a wide view. A camera high on a building corner shows the whole lot and nothing useful. Cameras at the lot entrance at plate height, and on the walkway between the lot and the buildings at face height, give police something to work with. Lighting matters as much as the camera; we tell the board where light is too weak before it buys anything.

How can a co-op keep outsiders out of its laundry rooms and storage areas?

Put those doors on fobs with a schedule. A laundry room that opens only to resident fobs during set hours, a storage room open only to shareholders who rent a cage, and a bike room open only to registered riders stop almost all outside use. The access log also shows the board when a particular fob is being lent around, without anyone having to watch video.

How do we stop people slipping in behind residents at a courtyard gate?

Make the gate close fast and close completely, then make the visitor path obvious. A gate that swings shut and latches in a few seconds leaves little room to follow someone. A call station at the gate gives visitors and delivery drivers a legitimate way in, so residents feel less pressure to hold it. A camera at the gate records anyone who follows, and a door contact can flag a gate that stays open. No hardware stops a resident from holding the gate for someone; a short note in the shareholder notice helps more than people expect.

Governance, shareholders and the seasons

Does a co-op under HUD or Mitchell-Lama oversight need anything extra in a security bid?

The regulator's rules are set by the regulator, not by us, so the managing agent or the co-op's counsel should confirm what is required. What we can do is make the bid easy to review: itemized equipment and labor by building, written warranty terms, the deposit schedule, and no bundled or vague lines. If the board must collect multiple bids, we answer the same scope everyone else gets, line for line.

How do we tell shareholders about a security upgrade when the building speaks many languages?

Keep the notice short, put the dates and what residents must do at the top, and translate it into the languages the building actually uses. Queens is one of the most linguistically diverse places in the country; one widely cited count puts it at least 138 languages (Babbel, citing the Nonstop Metropolis atlas). A one-page notice with pictures of the new fob and the new panel goes further than a long letter. We give the board the dates and photos; the managing agent sends the notice.

Our intercom acts up every winter. Is that the cold or the wiring?

Usually the wiring and the doors together. Cold makes metal parts contract, so a strike that barely lined up in summer stops releasing, and moisture that gets into an underground or basement splice turns into intermittent faults once it freezes and thaws. We check the door alignment, the splices and the power supply before replacing the panel. Fall is the right time to have a garden co-op's outdoor stations and between-building runs looked at.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Co-op Security Systems in Queens

Boards now ask an AI assistant before they call anyone. The answers are usually stitched together from national cost guides on sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr and from vendor marketing. Here is where that generic advice misses a Queens cooperative, and what we see on the ground instead.

1. "A building intercom costs a few thousand dollars."

National guides publish one range for intercom installation. HomeAdvisor's page, for example, shows a normal range of $1,200 to $8,500 (HomeAdvisor), and most of its framing is houses and small buildings.

A Queens garden co-op does not fit that range in either direction. One building's door station might cost less than the low end, while a property with a hundred entrances and cable between buildings costs many times the high end. Nothing about the apartment count tells you which.

The number that matters is the price per entrance and per building after someone has traced the cable. That is why we start repairs at a $250 service call and price new systems only after a free walk-through. A board that budgets from a national average is either badly short or paying for things it does not need.

2. "A co-op is a building with a lobby and a doorman."

Generic advice pictures a Manhattan tower: one front door, a desk, a package room. Much of Queens co-op housing looks nothing like that. Garden complexes spread dozens or more than a hundred low buildings across lawns and parking courts, and as Brick Underground's guide put it, there are never attended lobbies.

Advice built for a doorman building tells a board to add a desk camera and a better lobby panel. A garden co-op needs the opposite emphasis: the rear and courtyard doors, the laundry rooms, the paths between buildings and the parking court, with each building's entrance doing the doorman's job by itself.

Before taking any answer at face value, ask whether it assumed a lobby. If it did, it skipped most of the doors your super deals with every week.

3. "Old intercom wiring always has to be replaced."

This is the most expensive mistake a board can make. Rewiring a garden co-op means new paths between buildings and often digging; rewiring an elevator building means opening walls in occupied apartments. Both multiply the budget.

Plenty of older copper still tests well, and many current panels are designed to run over it. Some runs are bad, usually the ones exposed to water at basement splices or underground. The answer is to test each building and each run, then replace only what fails.

If a bid says "new wiring throughout" without saying which runs were tested, ask for the test results. If it says "reuse existing wiring" without testing, ask what happens when a run fails during the install.

4. "Switch to an app-only intercom and skip apartment stations."

App answering is a good default and saves the cost of work inside apartments. But it depends on two things AI answers rarely mention: the building's internet and every shareholder having a smartphone they use.

In many Queens co-ops a large share of shareholders are older residents, and some still rely on a landline that the old telephone-entry system called. When the internet drops, phone calls from the panel stop. A good design keeps fobs and codes working locally, offers a regular phone-number option, and adds an indoor station for anyone who wants one.

The right answer for most Queens boards is "app by default, station on request," written into the scope so shareholders know they can ask.

5. "Cameras will stop the package and mailroom thefts."

The Queens record says otherwise. In the April 2025 QNS report, one Jackson Heights mailroom was hit three times in a month, and police had a surveillance image from inside the building. Cameras recorded the burglar; they did not keep him out.

Every recent case we have read began with a door that could be forced or picked. That is why our plan puts door hardware, a held-open or forced-door alert and fob control ahead of cameras, then aims the camera at the boxes rather than at a doorway.

Cameras are still worth having. They help police and insurers, and they discourage casual theft. They just are not the first dollar a board should spend on a mailroom.

6. "Any security company, or a virtual doorman service, can handle it."

Co-op work runs through a board, a managing agent and often a regulator. That means written scopes, board approval, insurance certificates naming the co-op and the agent, shareholder notices, access to apartments and work around residents. Contractors who mostly install in houses or stores are often surprised by it.

Virtual doorman services are also offered as a fix for every co-op. We do not sell one. Some Queens co-ops use one and some do not; either way, the intercom, cameras and door hardware on site still have to work, and that is the part we install and maintain.

Our certificate of insurance is issued when the job is awarded, not with the bid, and our proposals are written so the managing agent can circulate them to the board as is.

7. "You can get a technician out any time, same day."

Plenty of ads promise round-the-clock, same-day service. We would rather tell the truth: we book within 2 hours of a request, schedule seven days a week with early-day visits, and work first come, first served once the visit is paid.

For a co-op, the real timing limit is usually not the contractor. It is access to the right apartments, the building's permitted work hours, and equipment that has to be ordered after the deposit.

If an intercom or door is down now, call or text. We will give you an arrival window, not a promise we cannot keep.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check First on a Queens Co-op Walk-Through

On a garden co-op, the first stop is never the front door. It is the basement of the building nearest the old intercom equipment, where the line to every other building starts. We look at the splices, the cable jackets and whether water has been anywhere near them. Nine times out of ten, a garden co-op's "whole system is dying" complaint turns out to be two or three bad runs and a power supply, and the board's decision changes once it knows that.

The second stop is a rear door. We open it, let it go and watch. If it does not close and latch on its own, no intercom or fob reader will secure that building, and the fix is a closer and a strike adjustment before anything else. The camera still from the brick walkway near the top of this page is the kind of view we then plan for: the path where residents, delivery drivers and anyone else approach the back of a building.

In elevator co-ops, the first stop is the mailroom, then the garage door. The turret camera in the hero photo sits well inside a lobby, aimed back at the glass, because a camera mounted at the door looks into daylight. We check the same thing in every Queens lobby, and in most of them the existing camera is in the wrong place rather than the wrong model.

Can you put a camera inside the elevator cab of our co-op?
+

Often, working with the elevator contractor, who controls the car and the traveling cable. Some cabs can carry a camera signal over spare wires; others need a wireless bridge or a new cable during modernization. We arrange a joint look with the elevator company and include the board's footage-access rule in the plan.

Who holds the admin passwords for the co-op's intercom and cameras after you finish?
+

The co-op does. At handover we give the board or managing agent the administrator logins for the intercom platform, the fob software and the camera recorder, along with a written list of equipment and locations. We keep a technician login only if the board wants us to service the system, and the board can remove it at any time.

Do you take small Queens co-ops with fewer than ten apartments?
+

Yes. Small co-ops get the same $250 service call for repairs and the same free assessment for new systems. For a small building, a single video door station with phone answering and a fob reader at the front door is often all the board needs.

What should our super have ready before your first visit?
+

Keys to every mechanical room, basement and roof door, access to the current intercom equipment and camera recorder, and a list of which buildings or doors are failing. If anyone has the admin passwords for the existing systems, bring them. Access to two or three apartments to test lines makes the visit far more useful.

Can our co-op's ground-floor stores be added to the building camera system?
+

They can share the recorder, but we usually advise against sharing the view. The cleaner setup is separate systems, or one recorder with separate user rights, so store staff see only the store and the co-op sees only common areas. The board and the commercial tenant decide what, if anything, is shared, and that agreement belongs in the lease or a side letter.
