The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Heights Properties Need Security Cameras Planned Door by Door

Westchester Heights is one of those Bronx names that never quite settled on a map. Statistical Atlas draws it as a neighborhood of roughly 14,600 people with Morris Park, Parkchester, Pelham Bay, Pelham Gardens, Unionport and Van Nest around it and parts of three ZIP codes, 10461, 10462 and 10469. A 2011 piece in The Bronx Journal described the area east of Parkchester by saying some people call it Westchester Heights and others name it after its main attraction, Westchester Square. Untapped Cities, writing about Parkchester's history, notes that a 1943 description of Westchester Heights counted close to 48,000 residents and took in what is now Morris Park, Pelham Parkway and Van Nest, and admits little is written about where the name came from. Sources even disagree about which community board covers it. We do not try to settle the argument. What matters for a camera job is the building in front of us, and here that building is almost always one of two very different things.

The first is a Parkchester apartment. Before the complex existed, much of the site was the New York Catholic Protectory, which operated there from 1865 until 1938. MetLife then built Parkchester from 1938 to 1941 to plans drawn by Richmond H. Shreve, whose firm designed the Empire State Building: 171 brick buildings and 12,271 apartments on about 129 acres, most of them eight or thirteen stories tall, with five garages and some 500,000 square feet of commercial space that included Macy's first branch store, opened in 1941. The rentals became condominiums in two stages, in 1974 and 1986, which is why Parkchester today has separate North and South condominium boards and a patchwork of owners, renters and unit owners who rent to others. A camera here goes on or beside one door in a long corridor of identical doors, in a building whose walls, halls and entrances belong to the condominium rather than to the person who wants the camera.

The second is a house or a small building on the blocks running east toward Westchester Square and south toward Unionport and Castle Hill. Westchester Square itself is far older than Parkchester: English settlers from the New Haven Colony founded the village around 1654, and it served as the seat of the Town of Westchester until the area was annexed into New York City in 1895. The IRT Pelham elevated line arrived in 1920 with a station at Westchester Square–East Tremont Avenue, and housing and shops followed. Several 19th-century houses, including old Victorian mansions, still stand. In Unionport and the Castle Hill blocks nearby, the housing is a mix of attached and detached two- and three-story buildings on small lots, with many semi-detached brick two-families and attached multifamily rowhouses, some with a one-car garage built into the front. When the city rezoned the Westchester Square area in 2006, a stated goal was to protect the scale of its large detached homes. A camera plan for one of these houses looks nothing like a Parkchester door: it covers a stoop, a garage door, a driveway strip that may be shared with the other half of the building, a back yard, a rental unit and the curb where the car sits overnight.

What ties the two together is what residents have said in public. A Niche reviewer writing about Parkchester listed assaults, robberies, packages stolen from in front of doors and home invasions, and advised neighbors to put cameras in and in front of their doors. In September 2024 News 12 aired video of a man kicking down a Parkchester woman's door and breaking into her apartment, and tenants rallied for better security; one sign at the September 21 rally read, "I should not have to rely on my neighbor's Ring Doorbell." The Parkchester Watch Group has pressed the condominium boards for hallway cameras and has called out East Tremont Avenue for car break-ins, poor street lighting and a lack of NYPD cameras. PIX11 reported that parts of Virginia Avenue look utterly dark at night. None of that tells us what any single home needs, but it explains why our calls from this area so often start with a door, a dark block or a parked car.

What the record shows about Westchester Heights

  • A complex with its own entry system. Comelit Group USA describes Parkchester's street-door intercom as the world's largest app-based IP video intercom, covering 171 buildings, 12,271 apartments and about 48,000 residents, installed by Total Security and run through an app tied to the property management software.
  • A camera network that is not built around apartment doors. Testimony recorded in a 2014 New York court decision described Parkchester's security as including over 120 cameras, with the Parkchester South Condominium's Special Operations Unit handling video surveillance for the south, east and west quadrants.
  • Two layers of patrol. The 43rd Precinct covers the neighborhood, while the privately owned complex is also patrolled by the Parkchester Department of Public Safety.
  • Public cameras that stop at certain blocks. Bronx Times reported that the Assembly secured funding for cameras in Westchester Square in 2009 and that the Throggs Neck stretch of East Tremont Avenue later got eight new NYPD-operated cameras; the Watch Group says its stretch of East Tremont has none.
  • Package theft one neighborhood over. CBS New York reported a man taking packages from a Narragansett Avenue home in Morris Park and hitting two more homes nearby on Tenbroeck Avenue.
  • Growth on the way. The City Council approved the Bronx Metro-North Neighborhood Plan on August 15, 2024, allowing housing on parts of Parkchester and Van Nest that had been limited to nonresidential use, ahead of new Metro-North stations planned for late 2027.

Parkchester Apartments

A Camera for Your Own Door in a Parkchester Building

Most of the apartment-door work we are asked about in Westchester Heights is in Parkchester. The buildings were designed as a single planned community, so the corridors are long, the doors look alike, and the walls are brick and masonry built in the late 1930s and early 1940s. That uniformity makes some things easy, because a solution that works on one door usually works on the next, and some things hard, because a resident cannot simply decide what goes on the wall outside their own door.

What you own, and what the condominium owns

In a condominium, a unit owner owns the inside of their apartment. The corridor, the door frame's outside face, the walls between doors, the lobby and the street entrance are generally common elements controlled by the condominium. Parkchester has separate North and South condominiums, each with its own board and rules, and we do not assume one set of rules applies to every building. Before we quote a camera on the corridor side of a door, the person asking needs to find out from management or the board what is allowed on that side: whether drilling into the frame is permitted, whether a device may face the corridor at all, and whether anything has to be submitted for approval. Brick Underground's guidance for renters is blunt on the same point: putting any equipment in a building's common areas, including the hallway outside your own apartment, needs the landlord's permission. In a condominium, that permission comes from the condominium.

Owners who live there, owners who rent out, and renters

Three different people call us from Parkchester, and each one has a different first step. An owner who lives in the unit asks the condominium. A renter asks the unit owner, who is their landlord, and the unit owner in turn may need the condominium's approval for anything on the corridor side. A unit owner who rents to someone else and lives elsewhere often wants to protect the apartment, but a camera that watches a tenant's own door is watching the tenant's comings and goings, and that needs the tenant's agreement as well. We sort out which of these situations applies on the first phone call, because it decides what can be quoted at all.

Three ways a camera can live on an apartment door

The first is a peephole camera, which replaces the door viewer with a small camera and keeps everything inside the door's existing hole and on the apartment side. It changes the least, which is often why it is the easiest to get approved. The second is a door-mounted camera or video doorbell fixed to the face of the door or the frame beside it, usually on battery because these doors were never wired for a doorbell. It sees more of the corridor and can show a person's face from slightly above, but it sits on the common side. The third is a small indoor camera inside the apartment aimed at the inside of the front door, which records anyone who comes through it and needs nobody's permission beyond the household's. Many residents end up with the first or third, or both, because those keep the hardware on their own side of the door.

Getting a signal out through 1940s brick

A battery door camera depends on Wi-Fi, and the router is usually in the living room or a bedroom, several walls away from the front door. Parkchester's interior walls are masonry, and masonry swallows Wi-Fi. A camera that works on the kitchen counter during setup can drop out once it is on the door. We check signal strength at the door before choosing a camera, and where it is weak, the fix is usually moving the router or adding a small mesh point near the entry rather than buying a more expensive camera.

Living alongside the building's app intercom

The Comelit video intercom at the street doors is the condominium's system, managed through the property management side and the residents' app. It shows who is at the building entrance. It does not show your floor or your door, and it is not something we add private devices to. Your own door camera runs on its own app, records to its own storage, and stays under your control. Residents sometimes ask whether the two can be combined into one screen; in Parkchester, the honest answer is that the building's system belongs to the building, and your camera belongs to you, and keeping them separate is what protects your privacy and theirs.

What the 2024 break-in video teaches about placement

The clip News 12 aired in September 2024 was useful because it showed a person at the door, not just the aftermath. A camera that is only triggered once the door opens, or one mounted low on the door itself, can end up recording a shoe, a door swinging and an empty corridor. A view that catches a face is set slightly above head height and back from the door, looking at the approach, which is why a camera on the frame or a high indoor camera aimed at the door often gives a better identification than a peephole camera alone. One resident told Bronx Times she was thinking of reinforcing her door with boards or metal panels; a camera does not replace a stronger door, a better lock or a door that closes properly, and we say so when we are asked.

The Common Spaces

Hallway Cameras, the Condo Boards and the Complex's Own Network

When people in Westchester Heights talk about cameras, they often mean cameras in the halls. A Parkchester Watch Group petition says the doorbell-camera videos residents share in their Facebook group should push the North and South condominium boards to start adding cameras to the hallways, and the group lists cameras in the halls among its demands alongside new elevators, wheelchair ramps and properly fixing leaks and mold. That is a building-level decision, and it is worth understanding what it would actually involve.

What over 120 cameras across 171 buildings means

A network of more than 120 cameras, as described in the 2014 court testimony, sounds like a lot until it is divided across 171 buildings, five garages, the grounds and the walkways between them. Cameras on that scale are placed for entrances, paths and public spaces watched by a security unit, not for the thousands of apartment doors on the floors above. That is not a criticism of anyone; it is simply what a property-wide system is built to do. It is also why residents who want a record of their own door end up looking at their own camera, and why the rally sign about relying on a neighbor's doorbell struck a chord.

What a hallway system takes in a building like these

A corridor camera in an eight- or thirteen-story masonry building needs a cable path from every floor back to a recorder, usually in the cellar, through risers or chases that were never designed for low-voltage cable, with every penetration through a floor or rated wall fire-stopped. Each floor needs at least one camera at the elevator end of the corridor, and a corridor that turns or runs long may need two. Lighting matters as much as the camera, because a dim corridor gives a grainy, smeared image. And the board has to decide in advance who can watch, who can export clips, how long video is kept and how residents will be told what is covered. None of that is beyond reach, but it is a project for a board and its managing agent, not something a resident can commission for their own floor.

What a resident can do while a board decides

Residents who are waiting on a board can still protect their own door from their own side: a peephole camera, an indoor camera facing the door, or, with approval, a door-mounted camera. They can also make sure the videos they already have are kept properly: saved off the camera the same day something happens, named with the building, floor and date, and handed to the 43rd Precinct or the Parkchester Department of Public Safety as a file rather than as a phone recording of a screen. A clip that keeps its original time stamp and resolution is worth far more than one filmed off a phone.

Who should see corridor video

Hallway video is sensitive in a way that street video is not. It shows who visits whom, when people leave for work and when they come home. Any board that installs corridor cameras should limit live viewing to the people who need it, log who exports clips, and keep cameras aimed along the corridor rather than into any apartment when a door opens. Residents who share their own door clips online should think about the same things before posting: a clip that shows a neighbor's door number or a child walking past can travel further than intended.

After Dark

Virginia Avenue, Wood Avenue and the Streets That Go Dark at Night

PIX11 reported residents calling for more streetlights and security cameras after a reported assault, and described parts of Virginia Avenue as looking utterly dark at night. In that report, surveillance video showed a suspect following a woman to the corner of Virginia and Wood avenues and dragging her to a ramp of the Parkchester South complex. The Watch Group has raised the same lighting concern about East Tremont Avenue. For anyone planning outdoor cameras on these blocks, darkness is the main technical problem, and it is worth being honest about what cameras can and cannot do in it.

What darkness does to a camera

A camera needs light to make a picture. As light falls, it raises its own gain, which brightens the image but adds grain and smearing, especially on anything moving. A person walking quickly past a camera on a dark sidewalk becomes a soft shape. Color cameras rated for low light hold color longer, but they still need some light from somewhere: a streetlight, a doorway fixture, a shop sign or a porch lamp. Where there is none, the camera switches to infrared.

The limits of infrared

Infrared lights on the camera body illuminate a cone in front of it, in black and white. They work well across a driveway, a yard or a short stretch of sidewalk. Their reach is limited, and anything close to the camera, such as a wall, a fence, a gutter or a branch, reflects the infrared back and washes out the picture. Rain and snow show up as bright streaks. Infrared also cannot light a whole block. A camera on a house at the corner of a dark street will record its own frontage well and the far side of the street poorly, no matter what the box says about range.

Adding light is often the cheapest upgrade

On a dark block, a motion light or a dusk-to-dawn fixture over a door, a garage or a driveway often does more for the video than a more expensive camera. It puts light on faces where they matter, keeps the camera in color, and makes the spot less inviting. We point these out on the quote. For a streetlight that is out on the street itself, that is a city matter: report it through 311 by address, and let us know so we can plan around it in the meantime.

Ramps and garage entries

Many buildings in and around the complex have driveway ramps down to a garage or service entrance, and the PIX11 case ended at one. A ramp is hard to cover well: a camera at the top looking down sees roofs and the tops of heads, and a camera at the bottom looking up is staring into headlights and the sky. The usable view usually comes from a camera on the side wall partway down, aimed across the ramp at the height where people walk, with light on that stretch. Whether that camera can go up at all depends on who owns the wall, which in Parkchester is the condominium.

Aim at your property and the sidewalk, not the windows across the way

On narrow side streets, a camera aimed at the sidewalk often takes in a neighbor's windows across the street. Privacy masks black out any part of the frame that looks into a home, in both the live view and the recording. We draw them on every outdoor camera that needs them and show the owner what is masked before we leave.

The House Side

Semi-Detached Two-Families and Rowhouses on the Unionport Side

South and east of Parkchester, the Westchester Heights name drifts onto blocks of small houses. Homes.com listings for Unionport describe many semi-detached brick two-family homes and attached multifamily rowhouses, and a sold listing at 2159 Ellis Avenue describes a legal two-family, semi-attached and brick, with a one-car attached garage. That pattern, two households in one building, a garage built into the front, a narrow strip of driveway and a small yard behind, shapes almost every house camera plan we draw on this side of the neighborhood.

The front: stoop, door and garage together

On a semi-attached two-family with a built-in garage, the front of the house carries the stoop or steps up to the main door, sometimes a second door for the lower unit, and the garage door, all within a frontage that may be no wider than the lot. One camera set high on the front, near the line with the attached neighbor, can cover the steps and the garage apron together, and a doorbell or door camera at the main door handles faces at close range. We aim the front camera so it covers your steps and garage and stops at the line between the two halves, and mask the neighbor's door if it falls in the frame.

Inside the garage

A garage that opens straight into the house, or into the lower apartment, is an entrance in its own right. A small camera inside the garage covering the overhead door and the interior door records anyone who comes in when the overhead door is left open or forced. In a two-family, whoever uses that interior door should be part of the decision about who sees that camera, because it may show the lower tenant's own comings and goings.

The driveway strip between two halves

Semi-detached houses often share a driveway strip or a narrow passage with the other half of the building or the house next door. A camera that covers a shared strip shows the neighbor's movements as much as yours. The practical approach is to talk first: many neighbors are glad of it and some want to share the view or split the cost. If they object, the camera is aimed at your door, your garage and your half, and their side is masked.

The back yard on a small lot

The yards behind these houses are small and closely packed, often with three or four other yards touching them. A rear camera high on the back wall covers your yard and back door well, and easily looks over the fences into everyone else's. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. So the yard camera is aimed down at your own yard and rear entrance, mounted lower rather than higher where possible, and masked over any neighboring yard that is still in the frame.

The second household

Most of these houses hold an owner and a tenant household, or two tenant households under an owner who lives elsewhere. One recorder can give each person their own view: the owner sees everything, the tenant sees the street door, the steps, the sidewalk and their own entrance. Each adult gets a personal login. We give the owner a short written list of every camera and what it sees to hand to tenants, which heads off the suspicion that a camera exists to watch a particular person. When a tenant moves out, their access is removed that day.

Brick faces and mounting

Mounts on brick go into the brick, not the mortar joint, with anchors sized to the camera, and every cable hole is sloped outward, sleeved and sealed so water cannot follow the cable in. On aluminum- or vinyl-sided houses, mounts sit on a block that spreads the load so the siding is not crushed. Wherever possible, cable runs inside: through the basement, up a closet or along the garage ceiling, so the facade carries the camera and nothing else.

The Curb

Car Break-Ins on East Tremont Avenue and the Blocks Around It

The Parkchester Watch Group describes East Tremont Avenue as known for car break-ins and poor street lighting, and says its stretch has no NYPD cameras. Most households on the house side of Westchester Heights park on the street or in a short driveway, and most Parkchester residents who drive park on the street or in one of the complex's garages. A car is the one valuable that spends every night outside, which is why so many of our quotes here include a curb view.

What a curb camera can realistically show

A camera high on the front of a house, aimed along the curb in front of it, can show someone walking up to a car, trying handles, looking in with a light or breaking a window, and it can show which way they came from and went. It will usually not read a plate on a car moving down the street at night unless the camera is chosen and aimed for that job, with a narrow view and fast shutter, at a spot where cars slow down. Most homeowners are better served by a clear view of their own car and the sidewalk beside it than by trying to cover the whole block.

From a window above the street

Residents on upper floors sometimes want to watch their car from inside an apartment window. It can work, with limits. Glass reflects any light inside the room, so the camera has to sit flat against the glass with the room behind it dark, and infrared has to be switched off or it bounces straight back. From several floors up, a face at the curb is small and seen from above. A window camera is good at showing that something happened and roughly when; it is rarely good at showing who.

Garages and lots

Parkchester's five garages belong to the complex, and cameras inside them are the complex's business. For a private garage or a small lot behind a building on the house side, the useful cameras cover the way in and out, at a height and angle that sees faces and plates as vehicles slow down at the entrance, plus one or two views along the rows. Lighting at the entrance matters more than the number of cameras.

When the clip is needed

After a break-in, police and insurers want the original file, with its time stamp, not a phone video of a screen. We set every recorder's clock to sync automatically, show each household how to export a clip, and recommend saving any clip worth keeping the same day, because the recorder will overwrite it on its normal schedule.

Old Westchester

Victorian Houses and the Old Village Around Westchester Square

The oldest buildings in the area stand around the old village green, now Owen F. Dolen Park, bounded by Westchester Avenue, Lane Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. The congregation of St. Peter's Church was organized in 1693, and its Gothic church, designed by Leopold Eidlitz, was completed in 1855; the St. Peter's Church, Chapel and Cemetery Complex at 2500 Westchester Avenue was designated a New York City Landmark in 1976. The Huntington Free Library, with its front door on Lane Avenue, became an individual landmark in 1994. A Revolutionary War marker recalls the Battle of Westchester Creek on October 12, 1776, when 25 riflemen held off the British by taking up the bridge planks. Scattered among later houses, several 19th-century homes and old Victorian mansions remain.

Wraparound porches and deep eaves

Victorian houses tend to have porches that wrap around one or two sides, deep eaves, bay windows and several doors. A porch that turns a corner hides whoever stands around that corner from a camera at the front door. On these houses we usually put one camera in the porch ceiling near each end of the porch, each looking along its length toward the steps, so the whole porch floor is covered and the steps are seen from both directions. Deep eaves give a good, dry place for cameras covering the yard and the side of the house, and the attic above them often gives an easy cable path.

Old frame, old plaster and old wiring paths

Older wood-frame houses often have open bays between floors that let cable drop from the attic to the basement more easily than in newer construction, and sometimes old abandoned wiring that can be followed. An old churchyard or cemetery is one of the hardest places to cover. It is large, dark, full of shapes that throw infrared back at the camera, and it is a place of mourning by day. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Westchester Heights: Questions Answered

Parkchester apartments

I rent my Parkchester apartment from the person who owns the unit. Do I ask that owner, the condominium board, or both before a camera goes on my door?

Start with the unit owner, who is your landlord, and ask in writing. Anything inside your apartment and aimed at your own space, like a small camera facing the inside of your door, is usually a matter between you and the owner. Anything on the corridor side of the door or the frame is on the condominium's common elements, so the owner will typically need the condominium's approval too, under whichever of the North or South rules applies. A peephole camera sits in between, and it is worth asking both. Get the answers in writing before you buy anything.

The unit owner wants to put a camera in the corridor outside our door. Isn't the corridor the condominium's, not his?

Generally, yes. In a condominium, the hallway is part of the common elements controlled by the board, so a unit owner cannot simply mount a camera there on his own say-so, any more than a tenant could. He would need the condominium's approval for a device on the corridor wall. If his concern is the apartment, a camera inside the unit facing the door, with your agreement as the tenant, does the same job without touching the common areas.

The 2024 Parkchester video showed a man kicking a door until it gave. Where does a door camera go so it records his face before the kick, not just the door flying open?

Above and to the side of the door, looking back along the approach, so it sees a person walking up and standing at the door at face height. A camera mounted low on the door, or one that only wakes up when the door moves, tends to catch a shoe and a swinging door. If the corridor side cannot be used, a camera high inside the apartment facing the door records whoever comes through, and a peephole camera adds a close look at the person outside. Make sure motion recording starts early enough, or record continuously at the door.

I'm thinking of covering my apartment door with a metal panel after the break-ins. Will that stop a door camera from working?

It can. Sheet metal blocks Wi-Fi, so a battery camera mounted on a metal-faced door, or a peephole camera whose transmitter sits behind the metal, may lose its connection. Adhesive mounts also hold poorly on painted metal. If you reinforce the door, the more reliable camera options are an indoor camera facing the door, which connects through the apartment's air, or a corridor camera on the frame if the condominium allows it. Any change to the door itself should be cleared with management first.

Our building's street door already shows up in an app on my phone. Can my own door camera live in that same app?

Normally not. Parkchester's video intercom is the condominium's system, run through its own app and tied to the property management software, and residents do not add private devices to it. Your door camera has its own app and its own storage under your control. Keeping the two separate is also what keeps your door video private from the building and the building's entrance video out of your hands.

From my eighth-floor window I can see where I park on the street. Can a camera inside the window watch my car?

It can show that something happened and roughly when, but it rarely shows who. The camera has to sit flush against the glass with infrared switched off and the room dark behind it, or the glass turns into a mirror at night. From eight floors up, a person beside the car is small and seen from the top of the head. If identification matters, a camera at street level on a house or storefront you control will do far better.

Houses on the Unionport and Castle Hill side

The garage built into our Ellis Avenue two-family has a door straight into the lower apartment. Who should be able to see a camera inside the garage?

Whoever uses that interior door should be part of the decision, because a camera covering it shows the lower household's comings and goings. A common arrangement is that both the owner and the lower tenant see the garage camera, while the tenant does not see cameras covering the owner's own entrance. Put the arrangement in writing, give each adult their own login, and aim the garage camera at the overhead door and the interior door rather than into the apartment when it opens.

Our semi-detached house shares a driveway strip with the other half of the building. Whose camera should cover it?

Talk to the other owner before anyone buys a camera. A camera over a shared strip sees both households equally, so the cleanest options are one camera both owners agree on and can both view, with the cost split, or each owner covering only their own door and garage with the shared strip masked. A camera that one household controls and the other objects to tends to cause more trouble than it prevents.

Our old house near Westchester Square has a porch that wraps around two sides. How many cameras does a porch like that need?

Usually two for the porch itself: one in the porch ceiling near each end, each looking along the porch toward the steps on that side, so the whole porch floor is covered and nobody can stand around the corner out of view. A doorbell or door camera at the main door adds close-up faces. If there is a second door onto the porch, the two ceiling cameras usually cover it too.

Shops, institutions and buildings

Our store rents space on a Parkchester ground floor. Who controls the outside of the storefront for cameras, us or the landlord?

The landlord controls the building's exterior, including the facade above and beside your window and the sidewalk side of the building. Inside the store, you can generally plan your own cameras within the terms of the lease. If you want a camera outside the storefront, ask the landlord in writing, ideally before you sign or renew, and keep the outdoor view on your door and the sidewalk directly in front.

Our building has a driveway ramp from the sidewalk down to a garage, like the ramp at Parkchester South in the PIX11 report. Where on the ramp does a camera see faces?

Partway down, on the side wall, aimed across the ramp at walking height. A camera at the top looking down sees roofs and the tops of heads, and one at the bottom looking up stares into headlights. Add light on the ramp itself, because ramps are often the darkest spot on a building. On a condominium or landlord-owned wall, the camera needs their approval first.

Old gravestones and a churchyard are hard to light. What camera approach suits grounds like the ones at St. Peter's?

Cover the gates, the paths in from the street and the doors of the buildings, at heights where faces show, rather than trying to see the whole churchyard. Stones and monuments throw infrared back at the camera and create deep shadows, so lighting along the paths does more than extra cameras. Because the complex is a designated landmark, anything fixed to its exterior should go through the Landmarks Preservation Commission first.

The Law in Plain Words

New York Rules Behind a Westchester Heights Camera Job

Camera work in a condominium complex, a two-family house or a shop all runs into the same handful of New York rules. This is a plain summary of how we work within them, not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Private places stay off camera

Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people without consent in places where they reasonably expect privacy: inside homes, bedrooms, bathrooms and changing areas. A door camera may watch a corridor or a stoop; it may not look into a neighbor's apartment when their door opens, or through a window across the street. Where a view takes in a window or door that is not yours, we black it out with a privacy mask.

Sound is different from pictures

Recording a conversation without the consent of at least one person in it can be eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. Talking through a door camera to a visitor is a conversation you are part of. A corridor camera quietly recording what neighbors say to each other is not. We leave continuous audio off on corridor, shared-space and outdoor cameras.

Neighbors' back yards

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. On the small lots of Unionport and Castle Hill, this is the rule most likely to matter, so yard cameras watch your yard and the masks cover the rest.

Who gets to decide

In a condominium, the board controls the common elements, including corridors and entrances. In a rental, the landlord controls the building, and a tenant needs permission for anything in the common areas, including the hallway outside their door. In a two-family, the owner decides on the building and should tell tenants in writing where cameras are and what they show. One vendor blog claims the Real Property Law now requires landlords to allow tenant cameras; we could not find that in any primary source, and we do not rely on it.

Who may install

Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, a New York State license is required to install CCTV or video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. The Department of State exempts an owner who installs a system personally, or has their own employee install it, on their own property. The license itself requires at least 81 hours of qualifying education and a passed exam. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance; ask any installer for their number.

Landmarks

The St. Peter's complex and the Huntington Free Library are designated New York City landmarks, so exterior camera work on them is the kind of change the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews. Residents have called for Parkchester to be landmarked, but we found no designation of the complex in our sources.

"Parkchester's security cameras already cover residents' apartment doors."

This one sounds reasonable because Parkchester does run a camera network and has its own Department of Public Safety. But that network is spread across 171 buildings, five garages and the grounds between them. "New York changed the law so landlords have to let tenants install cameras."

Some AI answers repeat a vendor blog's claim that recent amendments to the Real Property Law require landlords to allow tenant cameras. We looked and could not find a primary source for it.

What reliable guidance says is the opposite in common areas: Brick Underground's advice is that renters need landlord permission to install equipment in a building's common areas, including the hallway outside their own apartment. In a Parkchester condominium, the corridor belongs to the condominium, which adds a second layer of approval.

Until someone can point to the actual statute, assume permission is needed for anything outside your apartment, and get it in writing. Inside your own space, aimed at your own door, is a different matter.

"Wired cameras in New York City must be installed by a licensed electrician."

One vendor's cost guide says this, and AI tools pick it up. It is a natural assumption, because cameras involve cable and power.

The New York State rules that speak directly to cameras point somewhere else. Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, installing CCTV or video surveillance used to detect or monitor intrusion or theft requires a state security and fire alarm installer license, which the Department of State issues after at least 81 hours of education and an exam. Owners who install on their own property, personally or through their own employee, are exempt from that license. Low-voltage camera cable is not the same as running a new electrical circuit.

If a job does need new line-voltage power, such as a new outlet for a recorder or a circuit for a new building's head end, that part is electrical work. The camera system itself is low-voltage work, and the license to ask an installer for is the state alarm installer license.

It does not light a whole street, it reflects off anything close to the lens, and rain and snow turn into bright streaks. Faces at the far edge of the advertised range are rarely identifiable.

On dark blocks, the reliable fix is light where faces will be, such as a motion light or a dusk-to-dawn fixture over the door, garage or ramp, combined with a camera aimed at a short distance. For a streetlight that is out, report it to 311.

A doorbell may also be mounted too low or face the wrong way to catch a face on approach.
