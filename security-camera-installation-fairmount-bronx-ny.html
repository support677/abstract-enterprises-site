The Neighborhood

Why Fairmount Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Fairmount is a name people still use for a set of blocks that the city maps inside East Tremont, and that matters before a single camera is chosen. NYC Parks records that Fairmount Playground and Fairmount Place take their name from a nineteenth-century estate here called Fairmount, owned in turn by Colonel Dunham, Robert Cochran and Thomas Minford. Parks also notes that Fairmount was one of three hills, with Mount Eden and Mount Hope, behind the name Tremont, which postmaster Hiram Tarbox suggested in the 1850s when the area was still Upper Morrisania. Today Wikipedia's entry for Fairmount simply redirects to East Tremont, a neighborhood bounded by East 180th Street, Southern Boulevard, the Cross Bronx Expressway and Third Avenue, in Bronx Community District 6. Real estate listings and building permits call the same Fairmount Place blocks East Tremont, West Farms, Crotona or Crotona Park East depending on who wrote them. None of that changes our price, which is the same Bronx rate on every block. It does change one thing: when you search for help, you will find pages written for a "Fairmount" that does not match the buildings on your street, and we would rather start from the buildings.

The buildings tell a story of heavy loss and slow rebuilding. Wikipedia puts East Tremont's 1950 population at about 60,000, packed into five- and six-story tenements and older multi-unit houses. Then the Cross Bronx Expressway was cut through the neighborhood. NYC Parks says 54 apartment buildings were razed and about 5,000 people evicted for the stretch beside the playground, and Robert Caro gave two chapters of The Power Broker to that "One Mile." Much of what survived was later damaged by arson and torn down by the city, leaving vacant lots that are only now filling. That history is why a single block of Fairmount Place can hold a frame two-family that StreetEasy dates to 1899, a 35-apartment walk-up built in 1931, a four-story, six-unit building finished in 2004, and permits for new mid-rise rentals. Each of those buildings needs a different camera plan, and they can sit within a few doors of each other.

The most common building here is the prewar walk-up, and it is the hardest one to watch. A walk-up like the one StreetEasy lists at 805 Fairmount Place, five stories and 35 apartments with no elevator, has a street door, often an inner vestibule door, a stair that every resident and visitor uses, a cellar door that may open onto the rear yard, and a roof door at the top of the stair. People move through all of those places with nobody at a desk to see them. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercom in many of these buildings, and HPD issued 3,151 self-closing door violations in fiscal year 2024 alone, according to its own report. But there is no general city rule requiring cameras. Cameras in a walk-up are a choice the owner makes, and they are most useful where the door hardware and the intercom cannot help: showing who came in behind a resident, which door was propped, and what happened on the stair at an hour when the building was quiet.

Most of the people living in those buildings rent. NeighborhoodScout describes the area around Fairmount Place and Marmion Avenue as mostly renter-occupied, with about 29.7 percent of residents born in another country. For a tenant, the rules are narrow and worth knowing early. Brick Underground states plainly that a renter needs the landlord's permission to put any equipment in a building's common areas, hallway included, and that cameras must never film the inside of anyone's apartment. A tenant who wants a camera on the hall without asking may end up taking it down, and one Bronx public housing tenant received a lease-breach notice over cameras in 2012. Inside the apartment, a tenant can do much more, and that is where we start. For owners, the same rule runs the other way: a hall camera has to stop at the threshold of each apartment door, and audio is usually best left off, because Connextivity notes that whether a hallway camera passively picking up conversations counts as eavesdropping has never been cleanly settled.

Fairmount's shops have their own reasons to want good video. The city's Commercial District Needs Assessment for East Tremont, prepared with the Neighborhood Initiatives Development Corporation, counted 529 storefronts along East Tremont Avenue, East 180th Street and part of Webster Avenue, with 67 of them empty. Tremont Avenue is mostly professional and medical offices, limited-service restaurants and beauty businesses; East 180th Street is mostly delis and bodegas; and most of the buildings are one or two stories, many built before 1950, often with apartments above. Merchants in that study named safety as a concern more than any other, at 82 percent. On the street itself, prosecutors in an April 2026 case covering 252 car, tire, rim and catalytic converter thefts across the Bronx said many of the crimes were captured on surveillance video, with nine of them in the 48th Precinct, which covers East Tremont. When something happens on these blocks, the video that helps usually comes from a private camera on a store or a building, aimed and recorded properly.

The new buildings add a third kind of customer: the developer. New York YIMBY has reported permits for a seven-story, 23-apartment building at 788 Fairmount Place between Prospect and Marmion Avenues, a four-story-plus-penthouse building with 25 small apartments at 785 Fairmount Place, and, in August 2026, a six-story, ten-apartment masonry building at 851 Fairmount Place with HPD as the owner and a rooftop terrace. 6sqft covered newly built affordable rentals at 863 Fairmount Place in 2016. Every one of those buildings needs camera cable somewhere, and in each case the difference between pulling it during framing and fishing it later through finished hallways is large. A builder who plans the camera rough-in with the electrical drawings gets a cleaner system for less work, and the residents move into a building whose entrance is already covered.

A camera will show that, but it will not fix it. The walk-up owner: five floors, four doors, one stair

For an owner, the useful way to see a walk-up is as a set of doors joined by a stair. The street door and the vestibule tell you who came in and whether they came in alone. The cellar door tells you who reached the boiler room, the meters and the rear yard. The roof door tells you who went up past the top floor. The stair joins them all, and every resident passes the same landings. A good plan covers each door with a camera that sees a face as a person walks toward it, and covers the stair with cameras placed so that a person climbing from the lobby to the top floor is never out of view for long. What the plan never does is look into an apartment. Hall cameras are framed on the stair and the landing, and the frame stops before any apartment door, or shows the door only as a closed shape at the edge of the picture. We also suggest the owner tell tenants in writing which cameras exist and what they show, because a building whose tenants know the rules has far fewer arguments about the footage later.

The tenant: what is yours to watch, and what is not

A tenant in a Fairmount walk-up can watch the inside of the apartment freely. A small camera on a bookshelf facing the inside of the front door shows anyone who comes in, and it does not touch the door, the frame or the hallway. A pet camera in the living room, or a camera that lets you check on a relative during the day, works the same way. Outside the door, the hallway belongs to the building. If you want a peephole camera or a door camera that looks onto the hall, ask the owner in writing first and keep the answer. We can draft a short note that names the device, where it would sit, how it attaches and what it would show, which tends to get a quicker yes than a vague request. If the owner says no, we will not install it anyway.

The developer: cameras belong on the drawings

The new buildings on Fairmount Place are the easiest camera jobs in the neighborhood if they are planned early, and some of the hardest if they are not. Before the walls close, a cable to every planned camera position can be pulled in a day, sleeved through fire-rated walls properly, and run to a telecom closet or locked room with power for a recorder. After the walls close, the same cable has to be fished through finished halls, surface-mounted in raceway, or carried through ceilings that were never designed to open. We work from the architect's floor plans, mark every entrance, the mailroom, the stair at each floor, the elevator lobby if there is one, any rooftop amenity and the trash and bike rooms, and give the general contractor a rough-in list they can price with the electrician's work.

Walk-up halls: plaster, stair walls and the cellar run

The halls of a 1930s walk-up are usually plaster on brick or on clay tile, and plaster punishes a careless drill. It cracks around the hole and can let a whole patch let go. So we drill slowly with the right bit, set anchors that grip the masonry behind the plaster rather than the plaster itself, and mount each hall camera on a small base so the cable is never left hanging out of a hole. Getting cable between floors is the real work. Most walk-ups have no empty shaft for low-voltage cable, so the usual path is up the stair wall in a small painted raceway that runs tight to a corner, or through a closet that stacks floor above floor. Every opening that passes cable from one floor to the next is sealed with rated fire-stopping, because a hole between floors in a five-story building is a path for smoke. At the bottom, the cables gather in the cellar and run along the ceiling to the recorder, clear of the boiler, the heating pipes and anywhere water has stood.

Frame houses: mounting blocks and the attic route

The wood houses from around 1900 that still stand on Fairmount Place and Marmion Avenue have been resided, patched and repainted many times, and the surface under a camera is often not what it looks like. Old wood siding may be covered with vinyl or asbestos-cement shingles, and none of those is a good thing to screw a camera onto directly. We mount to wood trim where we can, such as a corner board or the underside of a porch roof, and elsewhere use a mounting block that matches the siding and is screwed through to the framing. Before drilling into old shingles we ask what they are, and if there is any chance they contain asbestos we do not cut them. Cable in a frame house usually finds an easier path than in brick: up into an attic or down into a crawlspace and cellar, so the outside of the house shows only a small sealed hole under each camera.

The 2000s buildings: easier walls, tighter spaces

The small walk-ups built on Fairmount's empty lots in the 2000s are easier to wire than their prewar neighbors in one respect: the halls are usually drywall, and drywall can be opened and patched cleanly. Some have a hung ceiling in the lobby or the hallway that cable can travel above. But these buildings are often tight. A six-unit building on four floors has short halls, apartment doors close together and a stair that turns quickly, so a camera has little room to see a face before the person is past it. We place the camera at the end of a run where people walk toward it, not across from a door where they pass sideways, and we check that the frame does not show the inside of an apartment when a door opens. Where these buildings already have Fios or another fiber service, as StreetEasy lists for 797 Fairmount Place, remote viewing is simple to set up; the cameras still record locally first, so a dropped connection never means a gap in the footage.

Storefronts and big rooms: ceilings decide the plan

In the one- and two-story shop buildings along East Tremont Avenue, the ceiling decides almost everything about a camera job. A drop ceiling lets cable run out of sight to every camera and down a single wall to the back office. A solid ceiling, whether plaster, drywall or an old pressed metal ceiling, means cable runs in small painted raceway along the edges, planned so it looks like part of the room. In a large space like the former Loew's Fairmount, ceilings are high and runs are long, and cameras mounted too high see only the tops of heads. There, cameras over the entrances and registers come down on short drops to a useful height, while a few wide cameras high up give the overview, and the cable rides on proper supports across the ceiling rather than lying on the grid or the lights.

Offices and salons: privacy first, then coverage

Because so much of East Tremont Avenue is medical and professional offices and beauty businesses, many commercial camera jobs here start with what must not be recorded. In a medical or dental office, cameras belong on the street door, the reception desk and the waiting room, and on any door to a stock room or a back exit. They do not belong in treatment rooms, exam rooms, X-ray rooms or anywhere a patient undresses, and a reception camera should show people and the desk, not the screen or the paperwork on it. In a salon or barbershop, the register and the door are the priority, with a wide view of the floor if the owner wants one, while any room for waxing, treatments or changing stays off camera entirely. Many of these businesses are upstairs over a store, reached by a stair that the apartments may share, which means the stair camera has to respect the residents as well as the clients.

Delis, bodegas and late counters

The delis and bodegas on East 180th Street run long days with a small staff, often one person at the counter for hours. A camera plan for them has three parts. Over the counter, a camera that shows the customer's face, the hands and the register at the moment of a sale. At the door, a camera at about head height, aimed so people walk toward it on the way in, which is the view that identifies someone. And a wide view of the aisles and the cooler doors. Many owners also want a monitor where customers can see it, which says plainly that the store is recording. The recorder goes somewhere locked and out of sight from the counter, on a battery backup, so it cannot be carried off with the register or stopped by a short outage.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Fairmount

  • The landlord decides outside your unit. Before any camera goes on a facade, a shared vestibule or the stair to the apartments above, we ask for the landlord's answer in writing.
  • Private places stay private. New York Penal Law 250.45 makes it a crime to record people where they reasonably expect privacy, and the state Labor Law bars employers from video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms. We do not aim cameras at any of them.
  • Medical and treatment rooms stay off camera. In offices and salons, only the entrance, reception, waiting and register areas are covered.
  • Cameras cannot become a hazard. Under the ADA standards, anything mounted on a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a walking route, so a head-height door camera is chosen and placed with that in mind.
  • Sound is usually off. Whether a fixed camera picking up conversations counts as eavesdropping has not been settled, so we leave audio off unless there is a clear reason and a clear notice.
  • Electrical work is an electrician's. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable. If a store needs a new outlet for a recorder, the quote says so and names it as an electrician's job.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Fairmount: Questions Answered

Walk-ups and the doors nobody watches

The hallway lights in our walk-up switch on only when someone walks in. Does that leave the cameras blind until the light comes on?

For a second or two, yes, unless the camera can see in the dark on its own. A camera with infrared night vision keeps a black-and-white picture while the hall is dark, then switches to color when the motion-sensor light comes on. The moment of switching can blur a frame, so we place the camera where the person is still walking toward it after the light has come on, usually at the far end of the hall or the top of a flight. We also check whether the light sensor and the camera both cover the same spot, because a sensor that sees someone only at the stair turn can leave the landing dark for the first steps. If the hall stays dark too long, adjusting the sensor's timer is often the cheapest fix of all.

Our cellar door opens onto the rear yard, and it is the one door in the building nobody sees. Where does a camera go to cover it?

Usually two places. Inside the cellar, a camera facing the door from a few feet away shows anyone who comes through and their face as they walk in, and it is protected from weather and tampering. Outside, if the yard is dark, a camera with its own light high on the rear wall shows the path to the door and anyone waiting near it. The inside camera is the one that matters most. We also suggest an alert schedule on that camera, so the owner or managing agent gets a phone notice when the cellar door camera sees a person at hours when only the super should be there. The Multiple Dwelling Law expects cellar entrances to self-close and self-lock as well, and a camera will quickly show you whether yours actually does.

Pigeons settle on the ledge above our building's entrance. Will they foul a camera mounted there, and how do you work around them?

They can, and droppings on a dome or a lens will ruin the picture within weeks. We avoid mounting directly under a ledge pigeons use, and we prefer turret cameras, whose lens sits below a small visor, to domes with a flat top that birds can perch on. Where the only sensible spot is near a roosting ledge, a bird spike strip or a slope fitted to the ledge, put in by the owner or a pest-control contractor, keeps them off. Placing the entrance camera just inside the vestibule instead of outside also solves it, and inside the vestibule is often the better view of a face anyway.

Our super of many years set up the building's cameras himself and is retiring. How do we take the system over?

Start before he leaves, if you can. Ask him for the recorder's admin password, the app account it is linked to and any passwords for individual cameras, and write them down with him. We then log in, create a new owner admin account in the owner's name with two-step sign-in, give the new super his own limited login, change every old password and remove the departing super's access. If nobody has the passwords, many recorders can be reset, but some lose their footage or stay locked to the old account, and we will tell you which before we touch anything. We also trace and label every camera cable so the next person is not starting from scratch.

A Bronx building advertised "24-hour security" and it turned out to mean cameras, not a guard. What does a recorded camera system actually do?

It records, and it lets the right people look. Reporting on the Tremont Renaissance, a building a short way west of Fairmount, found its advertised 24-hour security meant cameras only. A recording system shows afterward who came in, at what time and what they did, and it can send alerts to a phone when it sees a person somewhere they should not be. It does not stop anyone at the door, and nobody is watching it live unless the owner pays someone to. We describe what a system will and will not do in writing on the quote, so an owner can tell tenants honestly what is in place, and a tenant knows what the cameras are for.

New buildings on Fairmount Place

We are building a seven-story rental on Fairmount Place. What camera rough-in should the electrician pull before the walls close?

A cable from every planned camera position to the room where the recorder will live, plus power for that room on its own circuit. Typical positions in a building that size are the main entrance outside and inside, the vestibule, the mailroom or package area, each floor's hall or stair landing, the elevator lobby and cab if there is an elevator, the cellar or service entrance, trash and bike rooms, and any roof door or roof terrace. Each cable should end in a box at the camera spot with solid backing behind it, and every pass through a rated wall or floor should be sleeved and fire-stopped. We mark those positions on the architect's plans and give the general contractor a list to price, so the camera cable goes in with the rest of the low-voltage work.

We are building on an empty lot on Fairmount Place. Can there be cameras on the site before there is permanent power or internet?

Yes. A small site system can run from the contractor's temporary electrical service with a recorder in a locked job box or site office, and a cellular router can carry remote viewing until the building has its own internet. Cameras on the fence or a temporary pole watch the gate, the material storage and the equipment. When the building is closed in, the site cameras come down and the permanent system, cabled from the rough-in, takes over. We plan both together, so some of the site hardware, such as the recorder, can move into the finished building rather than being thrown away.

The new building at 851 Fairmount Place is planned with a rooftop terrace. Can cameras cover a shared terrace without looking into the buildings next door?

They can, with care, and in any building with a shared roof terrace it is the owner who decides. A camera at the roof door, facing out onto the terrace, shows who comes up and when. The frame is set so it covers the terrace floor and the door, not the windows of neighboring buildings or the rear yards below, and privacy masking can black out any part of the picture that cannot be avoided. We also suggest a plain notice at the roof door that the terrace is recorded, because residents using a shared amenity should know.

Our building's lot runs through the block, with a door on each street. Does each entrance need its own camera?

Yes. Each street entrance is its own way in, and a camera at one tells you nothing about the other. New York YIMBY describes the lot at 788 Fairmount Place as a through lot, and buildings like that often end up with a main entrance on one street and a service or second entrance on the other. Each gets an outside camera above the door and an inside camera facing the door, and if one entrance is used less, its camera is the one to set for after-hours alerts. Both cable back to the same recorder.

Offices, salons and big stores

Our dental office on East Tremont Avenue has a waiting room and three treatment rooms. Where can cameras go?

At the street or stair door, over the reception desk, across the waiting room and on any door to the supply room or a rear exit. Treatment rooms stay off camera. The reception camera is aimed to show patients and staff at the desk, not the computer screen, the appointment book or paperwork on the counter, and the waiting room camera is a wide view rather than a close one. Footage access is limited to the practice owner and one manager, and audio is left off. If the office shares a stair with apartments upstairs, the stair camera is the landlord's decision, not the practice's.

Our hair salon wants the register covered. Can the camera do that without recording clients in the chairs?

Yes. A camera above and slightly behind the register looks down at the counter, the cash drawer and the customer paying, and the frame can be kept tight so the styling chairs are outside it. If you want a wide view of the floor as well, we place it at the entrance looking in, so it shows who came and went, and use privacy masking on any chair or station a client would rather not have recorded. Any room used for waxing, treatments or changing stays off camera entirely, which New York law expects.

Our store is in a very large old space with a high ceiling, the way the former Loew's Fairmount is now a supermarket. Do cameras that high see anything useful?

A camera mounted at the ceiling of a tall room sees the tops of heads and the floor, which is good for watching movement through the store and poor for recognizing anyone. So a big-room plan uses both. A few wide cameras high up give the overview of aisles and the floor. At the entrances and the registers, cameras come down on short pendant drops or wall mounts to roughly ten feet, where they see faces. Long cable runs in a room this size often need a switch partway across the store, and the cable rides on supports across the ceiling to stay clear of the lights and the sprinklers.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Fairmount

These are claims that turn up again and again in quick online answers and installer pages about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about Fairmount and what we see in buildings like these. Cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they give a rough sense of scale, but none of them has climbed a Fairmount walk-up.

"Fairmount is a neighborhood with its own boundaries and its own kind of buildings."

Quick answers tend to treat every name on a map as a separate neighborhood with a character of its own. Fairmount is a name that survives mostly in Fairmount Place and Fairmount Playground.

NYC Parks places it inside East Tremont, Wikipedia's Fairmount entry redirects there, and listings call the same blocks East Tremont, West Farms or Crotona. There is even a separate Fairmount Avenue in Throggs Neck that has nothing to do with these blocks.

For cameras, the label matters less than the building. A 1931 walk-up, an 1899 frame house and a 2004 infill building can share a block here, and each needs a different plan. We price every one on the same Bronx rate and plan from the walls.

"Old Bronx walk-ups are too hard to wire, so wireless cameras are the only practical choice."

This one sounds sensible, because nobody wants to open plaster on five floors. Many answers recommend battery or Wi-Fi cameras for prewar buildings for that reason.

In practice, plaster and brick are exactly what makes Wi-Fi unreliable in a walk-up. A router in the cellar or the super's apartment rarely reaches the top landing, and a battery camera that has to wake and connect often misses the first seconds of anyone who walks past.

Most walk-ups can be wired with small raceway on the stair wall or through stacked closets, fire-stopped at each floor. It takes planning, but the result records every time and needs no charging.

"Landlords in New York City are required to put cameras in their buildings."

This claim shows up often, usually blended with the intercom rules. People hear that buildings must have certain security features and assume cameras are among them.

What the Multiple Dwelling Law actually requires in many buildings is self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, for buildings of eight or more apartments, an intercom. There is no general city rule that requires cameras in an apartment building.

So a walk-up owner adding cameras is making a choice, and the owner sets the scope. If the street door does not close and lock, that is the legal obligation to fix first; a camera will only record it failing.

"A camera system means someone is keeping watch around the clock."

Listings and quick summaries use "security" and "cameras" as though they meant the same thing. The Tremont Renaissance, near Fairmount, advertised 24-hour security that turned out to be cameras only.

A recorder keeps footage. It can send alerts to a phone. It does not answer the door, call the police or stop anyone, and nobody watches it live unless the owner pays for monitoring.

That is not a reason to skip cameras. It is a reason to be honest about them. We write on the quote what the system does, so owners promise tenants only what is true.

"Anyone can install security cameras in New York; no license is needed."

Sources disagree here. Some installer sites say a state Security or Fire Alarm Installer license is required to install cameras in New York City.

The Department of State's own application says installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require a license, while General Business Law 69-m licenses the business of installing security systems more broadly. Where a camera system ties into an alarm or access control, the line is easy to cross.

We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance, so an owner does not have to work out which side of that line a job falls on.

"Turning on the microphone makes a hallway camera more useful."

Many cameras come with a microphone switched on by default, and online answers often treat audio as a free extra.

In a shared hallway, audio is a legal gray area. Connextivity notes that whether a camera passively recording hallway conversations counts as unlawful eavesdropping has never been cleanly settled, which is why audio is usually left off in apartment buildings.

We switch audio off on building cameras unless the owner has a specific reason, a clear notice and advice from a lawyer. Video alone answers nearly every question an owner asks of hall footage.

"Cameras fix a building's door problems."

Some answers suggest a camera at the entrance as the cure for a door that is always propped or never latches.

HPD issued 3,151 self-closing door violations in fiscal year 2024, and a camera does not clear any of them. A door that does not close needs a closer adjusted or replaced; a strike that does not hold needs repair.

A camera is useful alongside the repair: it shows who is propping the door and when, and whether the fix is holding. We look at the door on the survey and say when it needs attention first.

FAQ

Fairmount Security Camera Installation FAQ

We run a deli on East 180th Street. Does a monitor that lets customers see themselves on camera actually help?
+

It tells everyone who walks in that the store is recording, and many owners find that alone changes behavior at the counter. It is not a substitute for well-placed cameras, because the monitor only shows what the cameras already see. We usually put the monitor near the entrance or above the counter showing the door camera, so customers see their own face as they come in. The recorder still sits somewhere locked and out of sight, so the monitor never tells anyone where to grab the footage.

Websites show how many incidents happen within a block of an address. Can that data tell us where to point our building's cameras?
+

Not really. Block-level counts describe the area around a point, often including a busy avenue and several buildings, and they cannot tell you which of your doors people use to get in. A camera plan comes from your own building: the street door, the cellar door, the roof door and the stair. What you know from living or working there, such as which door is always propped or which hour the halls are busy, is worth more for placement than any map.

I rent a studio in one of the newer buildings and want to check on my dog while I am at work. What kind of camera suits that?
+

A small indoor camera on a shelf or counter, on your own Wi-Fi and your own account, does the job and needs no permission from the building, because it stays inside your apartment. Pick one with two-way talk if you want to speak to the dog, and a privacy shutter or a schedule that switches it off when you are home. We set it up on your phone, add two-step sign-in, and place it where it sees the room without pointing out of a window into the building across the way.

Our new store lease on East Tremont Avenue says we must keep working cameras. What should working mean in practice?
+

Read the lease for specifics, and ask the landlord if it is vague. In practice, working usually means every camera shows a clear picture, the recorder is saving footage for a set number of days, the clock is right, and someone can export a clip when asked. We set the recorder to warn you when a camera stops or the drive has a problem, show you how to check it each week, and write down the days of storage, so you can answer the landlord or an insurer with facts.

Can the camera at our clinic's front door hang at head height without becoming something people walk into?
+

Yes, if it is chosen and placed correctly. The ADA standards limit anything mounted on a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to sticking out no more than 4 inches into a walking route. A slim wall camera, a camera set into a corner beside the door, or one mounted just above 80 inches and angled down all meet that. We check the door swing and the path people take, so the camera sees faces without anyone bumping into it, including someone with low vision or using a cane.

Will a camera system on every floor of our walk-up push up the building's electric bill much?
+

Not much. Each wired camera draws only a few watts through its cable, and the recorder and switch together use roughly what a handful of ordinary light bulbs would. Cameras with built-in floodlights draw more, but only when the light is on. The bigger cost question is the battery backup, which is a one-time purchase rather than a running cost. We list the power needs on the quote, so the owner knows what will be drawing current and from which outlet.

If we add a burglar alarm to the building later, will the cameras we put in now work with it?
+

Usually, if that is planned now. Many recorders and alarm panels can share events, so an alarm on the cellar door can tell the recorder to mark the clip, or a camera can send an alert when the alarm is armed. The cable for the cameras stays separate from the alarm wiring, but we can leave spare capacity on the switch and room in the enclosure for it. Tell us on the first visit if an alarm is likely, and we will choose a recorder that can talk to one.

How sharp does our entrance camera need to be to recognize a face, not just see that someone came in?
+

Sharpness depends less on the camera's resolution than on how much of the picture the face fills. A high-resolution camera watching a whole lobby makes each face small, while a modest camera aimed at the door from a few feet away fills the frame. For identification we set the entrance camera so a face crossing the threshold fills a good part of the picture, at about head height, with light on the face rather than behind it. We suspect stock is going missing from our back room. Can we use a hidden camera to find out who is taking it?
+

A business can record its own stock room, and a camera there does not have to be obvious. What it can never do is record restrooms, locker rooms or changing areas, which New York law bars, or anywhere people expect privacy. We usually recommend a visible camera on the stock room door plus one inside, because people stop taking things when they know the room is recorded, which is often the real goal. If you want something discreet, talk to a lawyer about your staff policies first.
