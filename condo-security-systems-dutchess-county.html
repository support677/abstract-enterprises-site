The Neighborhood

Why Dutchess County Condo Communities Need Security Planned Around Clubhouses, Pools and Six Decades of Townhouses

Ask someone in Fishkill where the condos are and they will point you to the side roads off Route 9 and Route 52, where clusters of two-story townhouses sit around a clubhouse, a pool and a tennis court. That is the typical Dutchess County condominium. There are a few buildings with a shared front door and a hallway, and a handful of newer developments with garages and gated entrances, but most associations here own roads, lawns, parking, a recreation building and some shared doors rather than a lobby. Condo security in Dutchess County is therefore less about one front door and more about the half-dozen places every owner shares.

The market is larger than people outside the county expect. In the twelve months through September 2026, OneKey MLS recorded 307 condo sales in Dutchess, up from 295 the year before, with the median price rising from $318,000 to $355,000. Co-ops, by comparison, closed 42 sales at a median of $114,000 over the same period. Condos are the main form of shared ownership in this county by a wide margin, and every one of those 307 closings handed somebody a set of pool fobs, a clubhouse code and, in some communities, a gate remote that the seller may never have returned.

Where Dutchess condos actually sit

A county-wide directory of condo and townhouse complexes that we read while writing this page listed forty-one communities by town: thirteen in Fishkill, nine in Beacon, seven in the Poughkeepsie area, five in Wappingers Falls, two each in Beekman, Pawling and Hyde Park, and one in Rhinebeck. Their build dates ran from 1965 to developments still adding homes after 2019. Most of them cluster along the Route 9 and I-84 corridor, close to the highways, the shopping and the train stations, which is where our crews spend most of their Dutchess days.

The condo listings on Redfin the same day told a similar story. Seventy-one condos were for sale at a median list price of about $279,000, in Fishkill, Wappingers Falls, Poughkeepsie, LaGrangeville, Beacon, Hyde Park and Pawling. Listing notes mentioned community pools, tennis courts, clubhouses, fitness centers and a scenic pond. Three described gated communities, two of them in Poughkeepsie and one a 55-plus community in Fishkill with a clubhouse and fitness center. Monthly association fees quoted ran from roughly $300 to almost $600. Several listings were tagged as near a train station.

Sixty years of construction in one county

The spread of build dates is what makes Dutchess condo work different from a newer suburb. A garden complex from the late 1960s may still have its original door hardware on the laundry room and a 1980s burglar panel in the pool house. A 1990s townhouse community usually has a clubhouse with a key lock, a pool gate with a combination lock and nothing networked at all. Communities finished in the 2010s often came from the builder with a keypad gate, a handful of consumer cameras and a developer account nobody transferred. One association can contain all three eras when it was built in phases. Our first job is often simply to list what is there, what still works and who holds the passwords.

What is being built now

New condo supply in Dutchess comes in small numbers and slowly. Westfair Communications reported in December 2023 that a developer was planning a condominium project called Fishkill Landing on the Hudson in the Town of Fishkill: 116 condo units in four buildings of two and a half and three stories on 7.5 acres, next to a 10-acre town park with public parking and a riverfront esplanade. In Beacon, The Highlands Current reported in May 2025 that the Planning Board had granted two more 90-day extensions to a 37-townhouse project, at least the sixth and seventh since 2022. Boards of new communities like these inherit decisions made long before the first owner moved in, which is why a plan written early saves money later.

Towns, villages and two cities

Dutchess has two cities, Beacon and Poughkeepsie, plus towns and villages that each keep their own codes. That matters for owners' alarms and for any wiring that needs a permit. The City of Beacon requires an alarm user permit from its police department. The Village of Fishkill revokes an alarm permit when ten or more avoidable alarms are recorded in a single permit year. The Town of East Fishkill requires a separate alarm registration for each alarm site and charges no fee for the first two false alarms at the same premises. The Town of Poughkeepsie adopted a new electrical inspection law in February 2026 that makes it a violation to install wiring for light, heat or power without a permit from its Building Department. None of New York City's housing rules apply here; the state's Multiple Residence Law covers every town, village and city under 325,000 people, which includes all of Dutchess.

Winters, storms and the long drive

Weather shapes every Dutchess proposal. Central Hudson serves more than 315,000 customers across its territory, and when the July 4, 2026 thunderstorm hit, the county reported over 33,000 Central Hudson outages and more than 7,400 NYSEG outages, with the worst damage in the City and Town of Poughkeepsie, East Fishkill, LaGrange, Beekman, Union Vale and Pawling. Winter is the other half of the story: ice, plowed snowbanks and freeze-thaw cycles work on outdoor equipment for months at a time. For a condo community that means battery backup that outlasts a long outage, locks that fail the right way for each door, readers rated for real cold, and cable runs between buildings that will not be cut by the next snowplow or irrigation repair.

What Dutchess condo boards call us about

The same complaints come up again and again. The clubhouse has a key lock and nobody knows how many keys are out. The pool gate combination has been the same for years and every former renter knows it. The board wants cameras on the parking areas after a string of car break-ins, but there is no power or network anywhere near the lot. The management company is an hour away and cannot add or remove a fob without a trip. A burglar panel in the pool house beeps every time the power blinks. An owner wants a monitored alarm and asks whether the town needs to be told. None of these require a new system on the first day, but each one is a reason to have a written plan.

Clubhouse, pool and amenity access control

In a Dutchess townhouse community the clubhouse door and the pool gate are the real front doors, because they are the only places every owner shares. A metal key or a four-digit combination works until a few years of sales and rentals have spread it to people the board has never met. Electronic access ties each credential to a unit, so the managing agent can turn off one household's fobs in one step and the board can see who opened the pool after hours. Readers can follow a season: the pool opens Memorial Day weekend and locks itself again in September, the fitness room runs early morning to late evening, the party room opens only for a booking. Because state law expects pool enclosure gates to close and latch on their own and to be locked when the pool is not in use, we add a reader and electric lock without removing the self-closing hinge or latch. Cable, network and links between buildings

The single biggest cost driver on a Dutchess condo property is distance. Condo vs co-op in Dutchess

Co-ops in Dutchess are a smaller market with a different decision process: shareholders, a proprietary lease and a co-op board that controls who buys. Building Specialists

Condo Security for Dutchess County's Architecture: Six Property Types

Dutchess condo properties fall into six broad types. Most associations recognize themselves in one, and the phased communities built over twenty years often see two or three. Each card says what usually needs attention first.

1

1980s and 1990s townhouse loops

Two-story wood-frame units on private roads around a clubhouse, pool and courts. First priority: replace keys and combinations at the amenities with credentials tied to units.

2

Late-1960s and 1970s garden complexes

Brick or block buildings with a shared door for several units and a laundry room. First priority: the building door hardware and a simple entry station per door.

3

Gated 55-plus communities

Newer homes behind a vehicle gate, with a clubhouse and fitness center. First priority: a call box residents' guests and caregivers can use without trouble.

4

Station-area and downtown buildings

Small elevator or walk-up condos near Beacon and Poughkeepsie stations, sometimes over shops. First priority: a lobby entry panel and a separate residential entrance.

5

Riverfront and park-side proposals

New low-rise condo buildings planned beside public parks and the river. First priority: decide the security plan while the buildings are still on paper.

6

Rural and hamlet communities

Smaller associations in Beekman, Pawling, LaGrange and Rhinebeck with long drives and wooded grounds. First priority: remote management so nobody drives out to change a code.

Townhouse loops: amenities are the front door

In the communities built across Fishkill, Wappingers Falls and the Town of Poughkeepsie in the 1980s and 1990s, owners enter their own homes through their own doors, and the association's security job is everything else. The clubhouse usually has a single glass entry door and a back door to the pool, the pool sits behind a fence with one or two gates, and the courts have a chain-link gate. These doors were built for keys. Converting them is straightforward: a reader on each, an electric strike or a mag lock suited to the frame, a request-to-exit device where the code calls for one, and a small controller in the clubhouse utility room. The cost lies in the cable from the clubhouse to the pool gate and courts, which is why we look for existing conduit before anything else.

Garden complexes: one door for many units

The older garden buildings from the late 1960s and early 1970s, some of them listed with association fees above $500 a month, typically have a steel or wood door at each stair hall, a mailbox bank inside, and an audio buzzer or nothing at all. The doors themselves are often the weak spot: closers that no longer pull the door shut, frames that have shifted, latches that do not catch. We check the door before quoting any electronics, because an entry station on a door that does not latch secures nothing. Where the masonry is solid, cable goes through the basement or crawl space rather than the stair hall walls.

Gated 55-plus communities: easy in, hard to fake

The age-restricted communities in Fishkill and Wappingers Falls were built with a gate, a clubhouse and fitness rooms, and listings describe them that way. Their residents' needs are specific. Visitors, home-care aides and delivery drivers need a call box that is simple to use from a car; residents want large-button indoor stations or a phone app with big text; and the board wants a record of who came in. Many of these gates were installed by the builder with a phone-line call box and a keypad. When the phone line is cancelled or the keypad code leaks, a cellular call box with the directory managed online is the usual replacement.

Station-area and downtown buildings

The handful of Dutchess condos with a true lobby are mostly near the Beacon and Poughkeepsie train stations or in downtown blocks with shops on the ground floor. They behave like small city buildings: a front entrance with an entry panel, a rear or garage door, a mailroom and sometimes an elevator. Two problems come up often. The residential entrance and the commercial tenants share a door or a hallway, and the original panel calls a landline that no owner still has. In Beacon, the city's historic preservation chapter excludes security-related changes that do not substantially detract from a historic property from its definition of an alteration, which usually keeps a discreet panel or reader simple, though we confirm with the city before mounting anything on a historic facade.

Riverfront and park-side proposals

Projects like the condo buildings proposed next to a new town park in Fishkill raise questions that older communities never faced: a public esplanade and parking a few yards from residents' doors, visitors who are perfectly entitled to be in the park but not in the buildings, and a developer who may control the board for years. The cheapest moment to settle these is before the walls are closed. Empty conduit to each door, a dedicated room for the controller and recorder, and a decision about which camera views belong to the town park and which to the association save the future board thousands.

Rural and hamlet communities

Smaller associations in Beekman, Pawling, LaGrangeville, Hyde Park and Rhinebeck often have fewer than fifty homes, a volunteer board and a management company based in another town. Their priority is not the number of devices but the ability to run them from a distance: adding a new owner's fob from a laptop, checking whether the pool gate is locked, seeing why a camera went offline. Cloud-managed controllers and recorders that report their own health make this possible. We also plan for wooded grounds, which block wireless links in summer, and for longer power outages on rural lines.

Commercial Condo Security

Clubhouses, Offices, Mixed-Use Blocks and Utility Buildings: Business-Side Condo Security in Dutchess County

Every Dutchess association runs a small business on the side. It owns a recreation building that is rented for parties, a pool that hires lifeguards, a maintenance garage full of mowers and plows, and sometimes an office with payroll files. A few condos also share a building with shops or offices. Each of these places has its own security needs, separate from the homes.

1

The clubhouse

Party room bookings, a fitness room, a kitchen and televisions. Readers with schedules, a monitored alarm at night and one or two cameras on the entry.

2

The management office

Files, a computer and the master credentials. A separate door schedule, a PIN plus fob, and an alarm code only staff hold.

3

The maintenance garage

Equipment, fuel and tools. A reader that logs which contractor came in and a camera on the overhead door.

4

Pump house or water building

Where a community runs its own water or wastewater equipment: a door contact, a reader and an alarm that tells the board if the door opens.

5

Shops under condos

Downtown buildings with stores below. Separate entrances and schedules so customers never reach residential halls.

6

Office and medical condos

Commercial condominiums along Route 9 and Route 55. Suite-level access and after-hours alarms for each owner.

Clubhouse rentals and the party-room problem

The clubhouse is where most Dutchess associations lose control of access. An owner books the party room for a Saturday, borrows the key on Friday, and returns it Monday, or a copy of it. An electronic system solves this by issuing the booking owner a code or app credential that works only for the reserved hours and only on the party room door, while the fitness room keeps its own schedule. The board sees which booking was active when the kitchen was left a mess. If the room has a monitored alarm, the booking credential can disarm it for those hours and no others.

Lifeguards, pool staff and seasonal workers

Pools bring a summer staff the association may employ directly or through a pool management company. Each staff member can get a credential that works from late May to early September, on the pool gate, the guard room and the chemical storage door, and expires on its own. When a staffing company changes guards midseason, the managing agent swaps names without touching anyone else's access.

Mixed-use and downtown condos

In the few Dutchess condo buildings with ground-floor shops, the residential board and the commercial unit owners usually share a rear door, a trash room or a basement. The fix is to separate entrances where possible and, where a door must be shared, to give the store owners credentials that work only during business hours and only on that door. Commercial units carry their own alarm accounts so a store's false alarm never becomes the association's problem.

Office and medical condominiums

Commercial condominiums are common along Route 9 in the Town of Poughkeepsie and Fishkill: small office parks where a dentist, an accountant and an insurance agent each own a suite. The association controls the building doors and the common hallway; each owner controls a suite. We install building-wide access with suite doors on the same platform, so the association sets the front-door schedule and each practice manages its own staff.

Compliance notes for Dutchess condo boards

The rules below are the ones that come up most on Dutchess condo work. They are summaries with sources, not legal advice; the association's attorney has the final word on how they apply to a particular property.

  • State law, not city law: Beacon and Poughkeepsie are both far below the 325,000 population line in MRL §3, so the Multiple Residence Law is the state housing statute here, as it is in every Dutchess town and village. The city-only Multiple Dwelling Law and Tenant Data Privacy Act stay inside the five boroughs.
  • Who owns the shared equipment: the Condominium Act's definition of common elements includes "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" unless the declaration provides otherwise (RPL §339-e). Whether a particular device inside a unit belongs to the owner or the association depends on the declaration and bylaws.
  • Readers on pool gates: Executive Law §378 already dictates the spring hinge, the latch and the lock; our reader and electric gate lock sit beside that hardware and never stand in for it.
  • Owners' alarms: Beacon requires an alarm user permit from its police department and expects central stations to attempt a callback before notifying police on residential alarms; the Village of Fishkill revokes a permit after ten or more avoidable alarms in a permit year; East Fishkill requires a separate registration for each alarm site. Other towns have their own chapters.
  • High-voltage wiring: the Town of Poughkeepsie requires an electrical permit for wiring for light, heat or power (Town Code Chapter 91, adopted 2026). We do not do electrical work; any new 120-volt circuit is the association's electrician.
  • Digging: underground cable waits for the utility locate under Code Rule 753.
  • Contractor credentials: ask any bidder for the state alarm installer license that GBL §69-m requires; a town business permit is not the same thing.
  • Insurance: we carry general liability insurance and issue a certificate of insurance naming the association and managing agent once a job is awarded.
Your Questions Answered

Condo Security Systems in Dutchess County: Questions Answered

These are questions Dutchess condo boards, owners and managing agents bring to us that other pages on this site do not already answer. They are grouped by topic.

Rules, towns and paperwork

Can the board cap how many fobs and app users each unit gets?

Yes, and in communities with heavy turnover it is one of the most useful settings. Most platforms let the administrator set a limit per unit, for example four credentials of any kind, so a household cannot quietly hand fobs to friends, former tenants or a dog walker. When an owner asks for a fifth, the agent sees the existing four and can retire one first. The number itself belongs in the house rules, adopted the way the bylaws require; the system simply enforces whatever the board decides.

The clubhouse and several owners all have alarms in East Fishkill. Can they share one registration?

Not under the town's wording. The East Fishkill burglar alarm article says a separate alarm registration is required for each alarm site, so the clubhouse is one site and each owner's unit with its own alarm is another. The same chapter also says no fee is charged for the first two false alarms at the same premises, which is why we spend time at handover teaching staff and owners how to cancel an accidental alarm. Other Dutchess towns word their rules differently; we check the town that applies before the system goes live.

Grounds, paths and parking

Should cameras watch the association's pond and walking path?

A camera can show who was on the path and when, which helps after vandalism or a dispute, but it cannot prevent an accident or replace fencing, signs and lighting near water. Boards that add one usually aim it at the path entrance rather than the water, so it records who goes in and out, and they post the pond rules where people can read them. Paths without power can take a camera fed over cable from the nearest building or a solar unit if the canopy allows. We record common areas only, never a view into an owner's windows or yard, and we let the board's insurer weigh in on anything near the water.

After a divorce, one former spouse still has fobs for a unit the other now owns alone. Who can have them turned off?

The association acts on the owner of record, so the owner who now holds title asks the managing agent, and the agent removes the other person's credentials from that unit. Boards usually want to see the deed or the unit file updated first, and a written policy saves the agent from refereeing. Where title is still shared or a court order is involved, the board's attorney decides; we only carry out the change in the system, which takes a few minutes once the agent approves it.

An owner's car was keyed twice in the same space. Can we point a camera at that spot?

Yes, if the space is part of the common elements, and it is one of the more common reasons a Dutchess board adds its first parking camera. Aim it at the whole row rather than the single space so it reads as general coverage and does not look into anyone's home. A camera with built-in light and a recorder in the clubhouse keeps the footage on site. If the owner would rather pay for it, the board can decide whether that is allowed under the declaration and whether the camera then belongs to the association.

Amenities and residents

Owners want to check the pool camera from their phones to see if it is crowded. Is that a good idea?

It is possible but rarely wise. Giving every owner live view of the pool means anyone with the app can watch children in swimsuits from anywhere, and recordings can be shared further. Boards that want to show occupancy usually do it another way: a low-resolution or wide view shown only on a screen in the clubhouse, or a simple count of how many fobs opened the gate in the last hour. Live and recorded camera access stays with the board, the managing agent and anyone they authorize in writing.

Can our clubhouse cameras record sound as well as video?

Most cameras can, but sound is where boards should slow down. New York's eavesdropping laws treat recording conversations differently from recording video, and building-industry guidance has flagged that audio recording in common areas can raise those issues. Our default is video only. If the board wants audio somewhere specific, such as the management office front counter, it should get its attorney's view first; we can switch the microphone on or off in the settings either way.

Many owners rent to tenants on twelve-month leases. Can a tenant's fob turn itself off when the lease ends?

Yes. Most access platforms let the managing agent set an end date on any credential, so a renter's fob and app access stop working the day after the lease ends unless the owner renews it. The owner sends the lease dates when the tenant moves in, the agent enters them once, and the system does the rest. This keeps the list clean in communities where a large share of units turn over every summer or around college semesters.

Can owners use their phones instead of fobs at the pool and clubhouse?

Usually yes. Many readers accept a phone credential over Bluetooth or NFC alongside fobs and cards, so owners who prefer the phone can use it and those who prefer a fob keep one. The board decides whether phone access is offered to renters and guests. A fob still matters for anyone without a smartphone and for the moment a phone battery dies at the pool gate, so we never plan a system that relies on phones alone.

Buildings and equipment

Our community runs its own water treatment building. Should it be on the same access system as the clubhouse?

It can share the platform, but it should have its own door schedule and its own list of who may enter, usually only the operator, the maintenance contractor and one or two board members. A door contact that alerts the board when the door opens outside the operator's visits is often worth more than a camera. If the building has chemicals or electrical gear, any lock we install keeps free exit from the inside, and we coordinate with the operator before cutting into the door.

What happens to our camera recordings if someone steals the recorder from the clubhouse?

If the recorder is the only copy, the recordings go with it. That is why we place recorders in a locked closet or cabinet out of sight, not on the shelf under the clubhouse television, and why many boards add a second copy: a cloud backup of selected cameras, or a recorder in another building linked over the network. Even without a backup, a camera aimed at the closet door records the theft to the cloud or to a second device if one exists.

Who keeps the cameras' and entry panels' software up to date after installation?

Somebody has to, and boards should decide who in writing. Manufacturers release firmware updates that fix security holes; a device that never gets them becomes the easiest way into the network. During installation we change every default password, put the devices on their own network and record the firmware versions. After that, updates are part of a maintenance visit or a paid support call, or the board's own IT volunteer can do them with the credentials we hand over. The worst outcome is the common one: nobody owning the job at all.

Credentials that cannot be copied

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems in Dutchess County

Chatbots, AI search summaries and national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give quick answers about condo security, and most of them are written with city apartment buildings or single-family houses in mind. Here are seven claims we hear repeated by Dutchess board members, and what the work actually looks like here.

1. "Condo security means a lobby intercom and a doorman"

That picture fits Manhattan, not Fishkill. A Dutchess condominium is far more likely to be a loop of townhouses with a clubhouse and pool than a building with a lobby. The county directory we read lists forty-one condo and townhouse complexes, current listings talk about pools, clubhouses and tennis courts far more than lobbies, and the gated communities put their entry point at the road, not at a front desk.

Because of that, the first dollars usually go to the shared amenities and the credential list, then to cameras in parking areas, and only then, if at all, to anything that looks like a lobby system. A board that starts by shopping for a lobby intercom often discovers it does not have a lobby.

The exceptions are real: the station-area buildings in Beacon and Poughkeepsie and the downtown blocks with shops below do behave like small city buildings. For those, a lobby entry panel is the right starting point.

2. "An intercom system costs $1,200 to $8,500"

HomeAdvisor's national guide gives a "normal range" of $1,200 to $8,500 for installing an intercom system. The range is not wrong, but it describes a house or a single building, not a condominium property with a clubhouse, a pool gate, a vehicle gate and a dozen shared doors spread over several acres.

In Dutchess, the drivers that move a condo price are distance between buildings, whether existing conduit can be used, how many doors and gates are controlled, and whether owners want indoor stations or the app. A small garden building with one door can sit near the bottom of a published range; a gated community with long underground runs can need a separate line just for trenching.

The only figure anyone can honestly quote without seeing the property is the $250 service call for a repair. Everything community-wide is a written quote after a free assessment, and our published Dutchess ranges, shown in the pricing section below, apply to individual systems.

3. "New York's intercom and smart-access privacy laws apply to your building"

AI answers often borrow New York City rules: the Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom provisions and the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act for smart-access buildings. Both are written for the city. The Multiple Residence Law, not the Multiple Dwelling Law, covers all towns and villages and every city under 325,000 people, which includes Beacon and Poughkeepsie.

That does not mean Dutchess condos have no rules. The Condominium Act governs common elements and bylaws, state law sets pool gate requirements, and each town and city has its own alarm chapter and building department. Privacy questions about face readers or phone-based entry are still worth putting to the association's attorney.

What we tell boards is simple: local rules come from the municipality the property sits in, and we check them for each job rather than applying a city template.

4. "Wireless battery cameras are all a condo needs"

Battery cameras sold for houses look like an easy answer for a townhouse community, and AI summaries recommend them often. On a Dutchess property they run into three problems: cold drains batteries fast in winter, recharging means a ladder on a snowy end wall, and Wi-Fi from a clubhouse rarely reaches a parking court three buildings away.

Cameras that take power and data over one cable, recorded on a recorder the association controls, keep working through the winter and keep the footage on site. Where a camera truly cannot be wired, a solar unit or a point-to-point wireless link is a better choice than a battery camera.

Consumer cameras also tie footage to one person's account. When that board member leaves, the association can lose access to its own recordings.

5. "Any electrician can install it, and no permits are involved"

Two different trades are involved, and AI answers tend to blur them. Security and alarm installers in New York are licensed by the state under General Business Law §69-m. Electrical work for light, heat or power is a separate trade that local codes regulate; the Town of Poughkeepsie, for example, adopted a 2026 law requiring an electrical permit for that wiring.

For a condo project that means a low-voltage contractor installs the readers, locks, cameras and controllers, while the association's electrician adds any new 120-volt outlet a power supply needs. We do not do electrical work; we tell the board exactly where a circuit is needed so its electrician can quote it.

Confusing the two leads to delays: a project waiting on an outlet nobody ordered, or an electrician asked to program an access system they have never seen.

6. "A false alarm is only a nuisance"

In parts of Dutchess, false alarms carry consequences. The Village of Fishkill revokes an alarm user permit when ten or more avoidable alarms are recorded in a permit year. East Fishkill charges no fee only for the first two false alarms at the same premises. Beacon expects residential alarm notifications to include an attempted callback by the central station before police are notified.

For an association, the clubhouse alarm is the usual offender: a cleaner who does not know the code, a party guest who opens the wrong door, a motion detector aimed at a balloon display. Setup and training at handover prevent most of these.

We set entry delays to fit how the building is used, give each user a personal code, and make sure the central station has a current call list before the system goes live.

7. "Cloud systems never go down"

Cloud management is useful for Dutchess associations whose managing agent is an hour away, and AI answers are right to recommend it. But the cloud sits on top of local power and a local internet connection, and both fail in storms. When the July 4, 2026 thunderstorm hit, the county reported over 33,000 Central Hudson outages and more than 7,400 on NYSEG's lines.

A system that needs the internet to unlock a door leaves owners outside during exactly those outages. The controllers we install store the credential list on site, keep doors working without the internet, and sync changes when the connection returns. Recorders keep recording locally whether or not the cloud is reachable.

Cloud management is the right tool for remote administration; it is the wrong place to keep the only copy of anything a door or a recorder needs to work.

Will owners lose power or water while you work?
+

No. Our work is low voltage and does not touch the water supply or owners' electrical panels. The only interruption is to the door or gate being changed, usually for a few hours, and the owner notice says how people get in during that time.

Will our existing door closers and handles work with new electric locks?
+

Often, if they are in good shape. An electric strike usually works with the existing lever or knob, and a sound closer can stay. A worn closer that no longer pulls the door shut gets replaced, because an electric lock cannot secure a door that does not close. We list any hardware changes on the proposal.

Should the association post signs saying cameras are recording?
+

Many associations do, because signs discourage trouble and tell owners and guests what to expect. Whether signs are required in a particular place is a question for the association's attorney. We can recommend wording and placement at entrances, the clubhouse and the pool.

+

Yes. The panic bar stays exactly as it is, so anyone inside can always push out. On the outside we add a reader and either an electric strike or an electrified version of the existing exit hardware, chosen to suit the door and the code requirements for that exit.

+

No. The app works over the phone's own cellular data or whatever Wi-Fi the phone is on, and nothing new is wired into the unit. The entry station, gate box or reader is what needs a connection, and we provide that from the clubhouse network or a cellular modem.
