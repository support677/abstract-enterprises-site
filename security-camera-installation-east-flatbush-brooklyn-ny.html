The Neighborhood

Why East Flatbush Properties Need Security Camera Installation

East Flatbush was built fast, mostly in brick, and mostly for families who shared walls with their neighbors, and that history decides where a camera can go and what it can see. Between the 1890s and the 1930s, developers cut the old Lefferts, Vanderveer and Remsen estates into narrow lots, and the subway reaching Utica Avenue and running down Nostrand Avenue in the 1920s set off the main burst of building, according to CityNeighborhoods and the Brooklyn Public Library. What went up were solid brick row houses, freestanding two-family homes and small apartment buildings, many of them between 1910 and 1940, with rows of Colonial Revival and Neo-Renaissance brick houses along streets such as East 51st Street, Schenectady Avenue and Remsen Avenue. After the Second World War came larger blocks: Flatbush Gardens, the former Vanderveer Estates, a 59-building complex erected in 1949 on the old Flatbush Water Works site in the Farragut section, and six-story elevator buildings along Lenox Road, New York Avenue and Linden Boulevard. The neighborhood runs from Empire Boulevard and East New York Avenue down to the Bay Ridge Branch rail line, and from New York and Brooklyn avenues east to East 98th Street. Community Board 17 counted 162,446 people in 2020, and Wikipedia describes the area as the single largest West Indian neighborhood in the city.

For a camera, an attached brick house is a good and a difficult place at once. It is good because the walls are solid, mortar joints hold a mount well, and most houses have a cellar running front to back that carries cable out of sight. It is difficult because the front of the house is a few steps from the sidewalk, the stoop or porch is often shared with the next house, the back yards sit side by side in a long strip, and the parked car that the owner most wants covered is on a public street, between other people's cars. A camera that is aimed carelessly here records the sidewalk, the neighbor's stoop and three back yards, and still misses the person leaning into the owner's car door. The art of an East Flatbush camera job is narrowing each view to the owner's own frontage and then setting it tight enough that what it records is a face, a hand or a plate, not a blur.

The precinct numbers say where to point first. The 67th Precinct, which covers East Flatbush and Remsen Village from its station house at 2820 Snyder Avenue, published a CompStat report for the week of October 26 to November 1, 2026. Year to date, burglaries were 109, down 29.7 percent from 155 a year earlier. Robberies were 204, down from 241. Grand larceny was 500, down from 581. Car theft, which the NYPD lists as grand larceny auto, was 154, down only 7.8 percent from 167, and over the most recent 28 days it was up, 22 against 14. Shooting incidents had fallen to 11 from 26. The city marks all of these figures as preliminary. Read together, they say that the household problem most likely to bring a camera call in late 2026 is the car at the curb, followed by the front door and the shop counter, and that the drop in burglaries is welcome but not a reason to leave a back door uncovered.

The local news over the last two years points at the same three places. In April 2026, News 12 reported that three men struck a man in front of a deli at 787 Albany Avenue just before 1:30 in the morning, one of them holding a hatchet, and drove off in his 2004 BMW. In February 2024, a man showed a gun at a deli and grocery on Church Avenue and took $600 from the register. ABC7 has reported car windows smashed and consoles searched along East 38th Street and Avenue D, with one resident hit twice in a month. And in February 2025, tenants at Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street told News 12 their building doors were left unlocked, and the station's crew walked into several buildings without a key. In September 2024, a woman at Flatbush Gardens was robbed after a man came into her building and kicked at her apartment door. None of those stories says a private camera would have prevented anything, and we do not claim that. What a well-placed camera does is leave a clear record of the person, the time and the direction they went, which is what a detective, an insurer or a landlord asks for first.

The Curb, the Counter, the Common Door

Three Places Where East Flatbush Footage Gets Used, and How We Aim a Camera at Each

Read the East Flatbush incidents in the sources side by side and a pattern shows up. A car taken or broken into at the curb, on Albany Avenue, on East 38th Street, on Clarendon Road. Money taken across a counter, on Church Avenue. A building door left unlocked or not secure, at Rutland Plaza and at Flatbush Gardens. Those are the three places where a recording is most likely to be asked for here, by a detective, an insurance adjuster, a landlord or a tenants' association. They are also three very different problems for a camera, so we plan them separately rather than buying one kind of camera and putting it everywhere.

The curb: one car, not the whole street

On most East Flatbush blocks the owner's car sits on the street in front of the house, or on a short driveway beside a semi-detached house on the side streets toward Remsen Village. A wide camera over the front door sees the whole block, and at night under a sodium streetlight it turns a person at a car door into a grey shape. What helps is a varifocal camera mounted on the front wall, the porch roof or under the cornice, eight to ten feet up, zoomed along the owner's own frontage so the car fills most of the picture. It records all the time, not only on motion, because a person walking up slowly may not trip a motion setting and because the seconds before a window goes are usually the ones that show the face. A separate alert zone is drawn tight around the car, so a phone does not ping for every pedestrian on the sidewalk. Where the owner parks in a different spot every night, which is common on blocks with no driveways, we aim along the curb in the direction most cars approach from, and say honestly that a car parked three houses away will be small in the frame.

Plate reading is a different and more expensive job. A camera that reads plates needs a narrow, predictable spot where every car passes slowly, such as a driveway entrance, a garage door or a parking lot gate. On an open residential street with cars moving in both directions it rarely earns its cost, and we will tell you that rather than sell one.

The counter: hands, cash and the face at the door

A shop on Church Avenue or Utica Avenue needs fewer cameras than most owners expect, as long as each has a single job. The first sits just inside the front door at about head height, looking at the door, so everyone who walks in is walking toward it and their face fills the frame on the way in. The second is above and a little in front of the register, aimed down at hands, goods and cash, with the card reader and keypad kept out of the frame so no customer's PIN is ever recorded. The third looks along the main aisle from a high corner and also sees the other two cameras, so anyone reaching up to cover one is recorded doing it. The fourth watches the stock room or the back door from the inside. The recorder goes in a locked box away from the register and the office, because a recorder sitting next to the cash drawer leaves with the cash drawer.

The common door: who came in behind the tenant

In a walk-up or an elevator building, the most useful camera is the one at the street door, and the most common mistake is mounting it high on the vestibule ceiling looking straight down. That view records the top of every head. We put the camera at the inside end of the lobby or the vestibule, facing the street door at a gentle downward angle, so every person coming in walks toward it and shows a face as the door opens. A second camera at the mailboxes looks along the row from the side, so a hand and a face are in the same frame. Where the door is known not to latch, as tenants described at Rutland Plaza, a camera does not fix the door, and we say so; what it does is show the building's owner and the tenants exactly how often the door stands open and who walks through it, which is the kind of record a tenants' association can take to a managing agent.

Row houses: the cellar is the cable route, the mortar is the mount

The attached houses of the 1910s to the 1930s nearly always have a cellar that runs the full depth of the house, and that is the best cable path in the neighborhood. Cable for the front and rear cameras travels along the cellar joists and comes up through a small hole just inside the front and back walls, so nothing is clipped across the brick. Mounts go into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, because mortar drills cleanly and can be repointed if a camera ever moves, while a hole in a face brick is there for good. The party wall shared with the next house is never used for a mount. Where the stoop or porch is shared, the door camera is aimed at the owner's own door and the half of the stoop in front of it, and we mask the neighbor's door in the camera settings. If the cellar is damp, the recorder does not live there; it moves to a closet on a living floor with a battery unit, and the modem beside it if that can be arranged.

Frame two-families: a block behind every camera

Some of the freestanding houses are frame with vinyl or aluminum siding. A camera screwed straight into siding works loose and cracks it within a season or two, so we fix a mounting block through the siding into the sheathing and framing behind it, seal around it, and mount the camera to the block. The cable passes through the middle of the block into the wall, so the hole is hidden and sealed. These houses often stand a few feet from the next one, so a side-alley camera is aimed along the alley rather than across it, and its infrared light is turned down so it does not bounce off the neighbor's wall and wash the picture out white.

Walk-ups: the building with nobody watching

A three- or four-story walk-up with five or six apartments often has no one on site. That changes the camera plan. The recorder cannot sit in an open cellar where anyone with a cellar key can reach it, so it goes in a locked, ventilated box, ideally beside the internet line, with a battery unit. The owner or the managing agent gets a phone login with an alert that fires if any camera loses its picture, so a dead camera is noticed in hours, not weeks. Cameras go at the street door, the mailboxes, each stair landing looking down the flight, the cellar door and the rear yard door, and tenants can be given a view of the street door if the owner chooses.

Elevator buildings and complexes: risers, closets and a written plan

In a six-story building we look first for an existing riser or chase that runs from the cellar to the roof, because a new path through six floors of plaster is the most expensive part of the job. Every camera gets its own run back to one locked closet, and openings through fire-rated floors are sealed with rated firestop. In a garden complex with several buildings around courtyards, each building gets its own small switch and the buildings are linked back to one recorder room by cable through the cellars where they connect, or by fiber or a wireless bridge where they do not. Flatbush Gardens is a large example; reporting in the New York Sun said its management had put security cameras in as part of an earlier capital program, so a resident there should treat the building's cameras as the owner's system and talk to management about anything outside their own door.

Corner stores and delis: open late, with the cash at the front

The two East Flatbush store incidents in the sources happened at night or in the early hours: the Church Avenue deli robbery at about 10:40 p.m., and the car theft in front of the Albany Avenue deli just before 1:30 a.m. A store that trades late needs its door and counter cameras to see well under the store's own lighting and the glare of the street outside. That means low-light color cameras, a camera inside the door rather than outside it so headlights do not blind it, and a view over the register set for a fast shutter so a hand grabbing cash is not a smear. Many corner stores also want a view of the sidewalk and the curb out front, because what happens to customers and their cars right outside is often what the police ask about. We aim that camera from inside the glass or under the awning, and keep it on the store's own frontage.

Bakeries, groceries and takeout counters: crowded floors and deliveries at dawn

Shops along Church and Utica avenues often have a narrow, deep floor with a counter at the back, a cellar below for stock, and deliveries coming through the front door or down sidewalk cellar doors early in the morning. The camera plan follows the goods. One camera on the front door, one on the counter, one high at the back looking forward over the whole floor, and one at the cellar stair or the sidewalk doors, which in many buildings are the weakest entrance of all. A recorder in a locked cabinet in the back, not the cellar, keeps the footage dry and out of reach. Where the shop owner rents from the building owner, the cameras inside are the shop's decision and anything on the facade, the awning or the sidewalk doors is the landlord's.

Medical and professional offices near Clarkson Avenue

Practices around the hospital campus have their own limits. Entrance, reception and the back or service door are the views that matter, plus any room where drug samples, records or equipment are kept. Treatment and exam rooms, restrooms and staff changing areas are never on camera, and screens at reception are kept out of the frame so patient information on a monitor is not recorded. Field Notes

Field Notes: Standing at the Curb of an East Flatbush Row House Before Choosing a Single Camera

This is how we work on these houses, not the story of one job. We start where the owner parks, because on most calls here the car is the reason the phone rang. Standing beside the car at night, we look back at the house and pick the spot on the wall or the porch roof from which a person at the driver's door would be big in the frame, then check where the nearest streetlight sits, because a light behind the car turns faces into silhouettes and a light in front of it helps. Then we walk up the stoop. If it is shared, we stand at the property line on the steps and agree with the owner where the door camera's view stops. At the door we look at any doorbell camera's picture on the owner's phone to see what it already covers. Inside, we ask who lives where: which floor the owner uses, which is rented, and whether the tenant shares the front door, the hall and the yard. Down in the cellar, we look along the joists for a clean route front to back, find where the internet line comes in, trace any old alarm or camera wire left by earlier owners, and check whether the space is dry enough for a recorder. If it is not, we find a closet upstairs. Last, we go out to the back yard, stand at the rear fence and look back at the house, which shows us where a rear camera sees the whole yard and the back door and where it would spill into the neighbors' yards on either side. By then the camera count, each cable length, the recorder spot and who gets which view are all settled, and the written quote is built from those notes.

Cars, stoops and yards

Car thefts in the 67th Precinct rose over the last 28 days even as burglaries fell. Which camera should a row house owner buy first?

The curb camera. CompStat for the week ending November 1, 2026 counted 22 car thefts in the previous 28 days against 14 a year earlier, while burglaries for the year were down 29.7 percent. On an attached house where a doorbell already covers the stoop, the next camera is the one zoomed on your own car from the front wall or porch roof, recording all the time. The back yard comes second and the cellar door third. If the budget allows only one camera this year, make it the curb and use the doorbell you have for the door.

We share a stoop and a porch with the house next door. How do we stop our door camera from recording our neighbors coming and going?

Aim it at your own door and set a privacy mask over theirs. On a shared stoop we mount the camera on your side of the door frame or under your half of the porch roof, angled toward your door and the steps in front of it. Most recorders and cameras let us black out part of the picture permanently, so the neighbor's door and window never record, even though they are in the lens's view. We show you the masked picture on your phone before we leave, and if your neighbor ever asks, you can show them exactly what the camera sees.

Our back yards are a long strip of side-by-side gardens. Is it legal to point a camera down our yard if it also sees the yards next door?

Recording your own yard is fine, and we frame it so it stops at your fence. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets an owner or tenant sue someone who puts a camera on adjoining property and records the recreational activities in their backyard without written consent, with intent to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. A camera set up to watch your own back door is not that, but on these narrow yards a wide view can still take in two neighbors' gardens. We mount the rear camera high on your back wall looking down your yard toward the door and the gate, and mask anything over the fence, which keeps you clear of complaints and gives you a sharper picture of your own space.

A thief smashed car windows along East 38th Street and Avenue D and took almost nothing. Would a camera have helped anyone there?

It would not have stopped the glass breaking, but it would have given the owners something to report. ABC7 described about six cars hit on East 38th Street and three on Avenue D, consoles searched and little taken, and one resident hit twice in a month. A row of houses whose owners each had a curb camera zoomed on their own car would have recorded the same person moving down the block, at times that line up across several clips if the recorders' clocks are synced. That is why we set every recorder's clock to an internet time server and name each camera with the address and its job, so a clip from your house can sit beside a neighbor's and make sense.

Walk-ups, elevator buildings and complexes

Tenants at a complex on East 93rd Street told News 12 their building doors were left unlocked. Can cameras do anything about a door that stays open?

Cameras do not repair a lock, and anyone who says otherwise is selling the wrong fix. What a camera at the inside end of the lobby does is record every time the door is propped or left open and everyone who walks through it, with a face. For an owner, that shows which entrances fail and when; for a tenants' association, it is a record to bring to a managing agent or to HPD. Rutland Plaza, the complex in that February 2025 report, had been placed in HPD's Alternative Enforcement Program. In a building like that, the door hardware comes first and the camera second, and a good installer will say so.

Our six-family walk-up has nobody living on site. How do we find out a camera died before a tenant tells us something was stolen?

Turn on video-loss alerts and send them to someone who will act. Most recorders we install can send a phone notification within minutes when a camera stops sending a picture, and a second alert if the recorder itself goes offline. In a building with no super, those alerts go to the owner and the managing agent, and we test them at handover by unplugging a camera. We also put the recorder on a battery unit so a short power cut does not stop recording, and set it to restart by itself when power returns, which is the most common reason a walk-up's cameras go quiet for weeks.

A tenant on our third floor wants to put a camera in the hallway outside her door. As the owner, do we have to allow it?

The hallway is a common area, so it is the owner's call, not the tenant's. Brick Underground notes that landlords may place cameras in common areas but not in a way that sees into apartments, and the same logic means a tenant needs the owner's permission for equipment in a shared hall. Many owners prefer to put a building camera on that landing themselves, aimed down the hall rather than at one door, and give the tenant a phone view of it. Inside her own apartment, a camera looking at her own rooms and the inside of her own door is her business.

After packages kept disappearing from our lobby, the managing agent said the footage is only for the police. Can tenants make them look at it?

Not easily. Brick Underground's December 2023 answer to a Brooklyn renter in that situation was that a landlord is generally not required to show tenants footage outside a legal process, such as a police request or a court order in a lawsuit. It suggested that tenants organize and ask together for the footage to be reviewed, and for fixes such as better locks or package lockers. If you are the owner, the simplest answer is a mailroom camera aimed from the side at the shelf or boxes, so a face and the package are in one frame, and a written policy on who reviews the footage and when.

Shops, offices and churches

A deli on Church Avenue was robbed at the register at night. Where should a late-night store put its first two cameras?

Inside the front door, facing it, and above the register, facing down. News 12 reported that in February 2024 a man showed a gun at a deli and grocery on Church Avenue at about 10:40 p.m. and took $600 from the register. In a robbery like that, the useful images are the face as the person walks in and the hands at the counter. A door camera at head height facing the entrance catches the first, and a counter camera set with a fast shutter catches the second, with the card reader kept out of the frame. The recorder should be locked away from the counter so the footage stays even if someone goes behind it.

Our medical office is a few blocks from Kings County Hospital. Which rooms can have cameras and which cannot?

Entrances, the waiting room, reception, corridors and the back door can, and exam rooms, treatment rooms, restrooms and changing areas cannot. We also keep reception screens and any whiteboard with patient names out of the frame, set every camera to video only, and keep the office's recorder separate from the building's system so only your staff can review it. A camera on the room where samples or records are kept, aimed at its door, is often the most important one in a practice.

Our congregation meets in a storefront on Church Avenue and wants to stream services. Can the same cameras handle security and the livestream?

They can share cable, but they should be separate cameras. A security camera is set for faces at the door and a steady picture day and night; a streaming camera is set for the pulpit, the choir and good color under the sanctuary lights, and someone often moves it during the service. We put the security cameras on the entrance, the office, the collection room and any side or rear door, and run a separate cable for the streaming camera to wherever the stream is sent from, so a change to one never breaks the other. FAQ

East Flatbush Security Camera FAQ

How many cameras does a typical East Flatbush two-family house end up with?
+

Usually four to six. A common layout is one zoomed on the car at the curb, one at the stoop or front door, one in the shared hall at the foot of the stairs, one on the back door and yard, and one at the cellar door if the cellar has its own outside entrance. A semi-detached house with a side alley or a driveway often adds one more looking along it. We would rather install four that each do one clear job than eight that overlap and record the street, and the quote names each camera by its job so you can decide what to leave out.

If a detective from the 67th Precinct asks for our video, what exactly should we hand over?
+

The original recording, not a phone video of the screen. Export the clip from the recorder to a USB stick or straight to your phone in the recorder's own format, covering a few minutes before and after the event, from every camera that saw anything, and write down the camera names and the time on the recorder's clock. Lock the clip on the recorder so it is not overwritten. At handover we show you how to do this and have you practice it once, and we set the recorder's clock from an internet time server so the time on your clip matches the police report.

How many days of recording will the system keep before it starts writing over old footage?
+

That depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, whether they record all the time or on motion, and the size of the hard drive. For most houses and small buildings we size the drive for two weeks or more of round-the-clock recording, because an owner who is away or a managing agent who checks only after a complaint can easily need footage that is ten days old. The quote states the drive size and the number of days we expect, and we check the real figure once the system has been running for a few days.

Do the cameras you install record sound as well as video?
+

We set security cameras to record video only, on houses, buildings, shops and offices alike. Many cameras have a microphone built in, and recording other people's conversations raises legal questions that a picture of a door does not, so we switch audio off as a standard setting and leave it off unless there is a clear, lawful reason and the owner asks in writing. A video doorbell that lets you talk to someone at your own door is a different matter, because you are part of that conversation.

Can our upstairs tenant see the front door camera on her phone without seeing the rest of the system?
+

Yes. The recorder belongs to the owner, and each person gets their own account with only the views the owner chooses. A tenant can be given live view of the front door and the stoop, with or without playback, and nothing else, so she cannot see the back yard, the owner's own entrance or any settings. When a tenant moves out, her account is switched off in about a minute and the recordings are untouched. We set these accounts up at handover and show each person their own view on their own phone.

What happens to the footage if a burglar finds the recorder and takes it?
+

That is why the recorder never sits next to the register or in an open cellar. We put it in a locked cabinet or closet away from the obvious places, often with the internet modem, and run its cables so they do not lead straight to it. For shops and buildings where the risk is real, we can also send the alert clips from one or two key cameras, such as the door and the register, to a cloud service as they happen, so a copy leaves the building even if the recorder does. The quote names that service and says whether it carries a fee.

Will a camera on our block be able to read license plates at night?
+

Usually not from an ordinary house camera, and we do not promise it. Reading a plate at night needs a camera set for that one job, with a narrow view, a fast shutter and its own infrared light, placed where every car passes slowly at the same spot, such as a driveway entrance or a lot gate. On an open street with cars passing in both directions under streetlights, plates blur or flare. A curb camera set for the person at your car door, which is what most East Flatbush owners need, is a different camera with a different aim.

Should our shop sign up for the city program that links business cameras to the police?
+

That is entirely your choice. When the city announced its business camera pilot in May 2024, 1010 WINS reported that it was free and voluntary, and that businesses could either register where their cameras are so police know whom to ask, or give live access on terms the owner sets. We have not confirmed whether East Flatbush was among the pilot's ten neighborhoods. Installing cameras with us does not enroll you in anything, and if you decide to join a program later, we can help make sure only the cameras you choose are shared.

Our managing agent wants a copy of your license and insurance before you touch the building. Can you send them ahead?
+

Yes, and we encourage it. We send the managing agent or the board a copy of our New York State license, NYS license #12000287431, and a certificate of insurance before the work date, along with the written scope: which cameras go where, which openings are made in which walls, and where the recorder and its cabinet will sit. For buildings with house rules on contractor hours or elevator use, tell us the rules and we schedule around them.

Will the cameras keep recording when the power goes out on our block?
+

For a short outage, yes, if the recorder, the switch and the modem are on a battery backup unit, which we include on our systems. How long it lasts depends on the size of the battery and how many cameras draw power from the recorder, and the quote states what we expect. Just as important is what happens when power returns: we set the recorder to start up and resume recording by itself, and test that at handover by cutting the power once, because a system that stays off after an outage is the most common reason cameras go dark for days.

Can you hide the cable on the front of a brick row house so nothing shows?
+

On most attached houses, yes. The cable for a front camera comes up from the cellar through a small hole just inside the front wall and out through the wall directly behind the camera, so the only thing visible is the camera itself. Where a camera sits under a cornice or porch roof with no clear path inside, we run the cable in a short, slim cover painted to match, tucked into a corner or beside a downspout. Every hole through the brick is drilled in a mortar joint and sealed against water.
