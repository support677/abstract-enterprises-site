The Neighborhood

Why Dumbo Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Dumbo was built for industry, and nearly every camera decision here traces back to that. For most of the city's history the area was counted as part of Vinegar Hill, a place of ferry landings, warehouses and factories. Robert Gair, who had invented the folded cardboard box in 1879, moved his business here from Tribeca in 1888, and his buildings eventually covered enough ground that people called the area Gairville. Brownstoner notes that Gair called himself the "Father of Concrete Construction" and built in concrete to protect his equipment and goods. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report describes three kinds of construction across the district: slow-burning mill buildings with massive wooden columns, beams and joists; steel frames with terra-cotta floor tile; and reinforced concrete, including Gair factories that rank among the earliest large-scale reinforced-concrete factory buildings in the United States. 1 Main Street, finished in 1914, was the tallest reinforced concrete building in the world when it went up. Gair's son moved production to Piermont in 1927, and by the 1970s artists were working in the empty floors, coining the name DUMBO, Down Under the Manhattan Bridge Overpass, in 1978. David Walentas bought eight buildings in 1981, and the rezoning that let residential conversion take off took 17 years. The LPC designated the DUMBO Historic District on December 18, 2007, covering roughly 91 buildings. Wikipedia places the neighborhood between Brooklyn Bridge Park, the Brooklyn Bridge, Brooklyn Heights and Vinegar Hill, in ZIP 11201 and Community Board 2. The 84th Precinct covers it from 301 Gold Street.

What sits inside those walls now is unusually mixed. The DUMBO Improvement District counts about 6 million square feet of commercial space, 15 office buildings, more than 1,000 creative and tech companies, more than 125 retail storefronts, 11,266 employees in 2022, more than 20 galleries and 152 artist studios, alongside 3,338 residential units and about 10,200 residents in 2023 across its area; the 2020 census counted 4,975 people within Wikipedia's narrower boundary. Two Trees turned Gair's concrete factories into offices and apartments, Alloy Development reworked the Arbuckle brick factories, and towers such as Olympia, at 33 stories with 76 condos, and One John Street, with 42 condos and a Brooklyn Children's Museum annex on its ground floor, now stand near the water. A single converted loft can hold condo owners upstairs, an office tenant on the lower floors, a shop at the sidewalk and a cellar shared by all of them. That means a camera plan in Dumbo starts with a question that never comes up on a one-family house: who controls each door? The condo board, the managing agent, the commercial landlord, a coworking operator and a shop tenant may each own a piece of the same entrance, and the cameras have to follow those lines.

The incidents reported in recent years show where the weak spots are, and they are rarely the dramatic ones. Brooklyn Paper's police blotter described a thief who got into a Washington Street apartment building by following a resident through the front door, went down to the basement, broke the mailroom lock, took packages and left toward Water Street. In the same report, two laptops disappeared overnight from a locked office on Bridge Street with no sign of a break-in. In August 2025 someone shattered a large glass door at the Aveda Arts and Sciences Institute on Pearl Street and took two cash registers. In July 2025 two people walked into the Alo shop on Front Street around 3:30 in the afternoon, and the next day three men came back and took items worth more than six thousand dollars. Someone used a rock to break the front doors of Ignazio's Pizza on Water Street, and police reviewed the restaurant's security cameras. A car was stolen from a Jay Street parking lot one night in August. Years earlier, the paper logged more than half a dozen break-ins at a restored warehouse full of artists, start-ups and importers. Followed doors, basement mailrooms, unforced office losses, daylight shop thefts and smashed glass: each is a different camera job, and each one depends on the right view existing before anything happens.

The precinct-wide numbers are moving the right way, and we would rather say so than lean on fear. The NYPD's CompStat report for the week of September 21 to 27, 2026 shows 116 burglaries in the 84th Precinct so far that year against 173 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 32.9 percent. The precinct also covers Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn and other neighborhoods, so these figures say nothing about a particular block. Package loss is a citywide problem; News 12 reported the mayor's office estimate that 90,000 packages are stolen or lost in transit across the city every day. And Dumbo has a pressure few residential neighborhoods share: crowds. Brooklyn Bridge Park, which the BID says averages more than 600,000 visitors a week, has its most-used entrance at Pier 1 on Old Fulton Street, and people gather at Washington and Water Streets to photograph the Manhattan Bridge framing the skyline. A lobby door in a quiet residential neighborhood sees a few dozen faces a day. A door on Washington Street can see thousands, which changes how a camera has to be aimed and how its alerts have to be set.

Dumbo facts that decide where a camera goes

  • A landmark district: about 91 buildings fall inside the DUMBO Historic District, designated in 2007, so cameras on street-facing walls fall under LPC rules.
  • Concrete, brick and timber: the district mixes early reinforced concrete, slow-burning mill construction and steel frames with terra-cotta floors, and each takes a camera mount and a cable run differently.
  • Mixed-use buildings: lofts often hold condos, offices and shops behind shared doors, so separate accounts and clear camera ownership matter from day one.
  • A bridge overhead: the Manhattan Bridge, opened to traffic in 1909, carries B, D, N and Q trains above Water, Front, York and Plymouth Streets, throwing shadow by day and moving light by night.
  • A flooded edge: AP photographers recorded flooded streets under the Manhattan Bridge during Sandy on October 29, 2012, and Jane's Carousel's electronics basement took five feet of water.
  • A falling burglary count: 116 burglaries year to date in the 84th Precinct in late September 2026 against 173 a year earlier.

The Frontage Budget

Counting Cameras by the Foot: How Dumbo's Landmark Limits Shape a Facade Plan

Outside a historic district, an owner can add a camera to every corner of a building and nobody counts. Inside the DUMBO Historic District, the facade is a shared civic asset, and the LPC's Permit Guidebook treats security cameras as minor health and safety equipment, in the same family as window security bars, lighting and intercom panels, with the details set out in Section 2-20 of the Commission's rules. The guidebook text we were able to read caps facade cameras at two for every 25 feet of street frontage, sets a separate allowance for large residential, commercial and institutional buildings, and asks for manufacturer cut sheets for some installations. The text we could open was fragmented, so before we draw a facade plan we read the current rule in full against your building, because the exact numbers matter. A third-party guide reports that about 95 percent of LPC permits are approved at staff level without a public hearing, and that applications that clearly meet the rules are often approved in about ten business days. The practical effect is simple. On a Dumbo facade, every camera has to earn its place, because there are only so many places to give out.

The Commission does enforce. Its 2012 hearing calendar listed 29 Jay Street over a metal roll-down security gate and an awning, and a 2016 agenda listed 20 Jay, 55 Washington, 45 Main and 25 Jay Streets over entry infill installed without permits. Neither involved cameras, but both show that street-facing hardware on these buildings gets looked at. A camera installed in the wrong place on a landmark facade is not a cheap mistake to fix: it means a second visit, patched masonry and possibly an application after the fact. We would rather spend twenty minutes measuring frontage on the first walk-through.

Every outside camera gets one doorway

When the number of facade cameras is limited, the temptation is to buy the widest lens available and hope it covers everything. On a Dumbo street that fails twice: the picture of any one face is too small to recognize, and a wide view of Washington or Water Street on a weekend fills with hundreds of people who have nothing to do with the building. We do the opposite. Each exterior camera is assigned to one doorway and framed so that a person at the door fills a usable share of the picture, from about head height, at walking pace. Everything the facade camera cannot do, such as watching the sidewalk at a distance or showing which way someone walked, we try to get from inside: an upper window looking down the block, a vestibule camera behind the outer door, or a shop camera behind its own glass.

Small, dark and close to what is already there

The guidebook's emphasis on minor, unobtrusive equipment is easy to work with if the camera is chosen with the facade in mind. A compact turret or mini dome in a dark finish disappears against old brick or painted cast iron far better than a large white bullet. We mount next to existing hardware, such as a light fixture, an intercom panel or a door frame, rather than in the middle of a clean masonry field, and we bring the cable in at the camera's own base so nothing runs across the face of the building. Where a mount has to go into masonry, we use the mortar joint rather than the brick or stone face, because mortar is softer and easier to repair if the camera ever comes down.

Who files, and what we bring

In a condo building the board or managing agent usually files with the LPC, in a rental loft the owner does, and a shop tenant almost never files alone. Our part is to give the applicant what the Commission tends to ask for: the camera model's cut sheet, a photo of each proposed location marked with the mount, a note of the finish and size, and a count of cameras against the frontage on each street. Indoor cameras, cellar cameras and cameras on walls that cannot be seen from a street can usually proceed while the facade paperwork is in review, and we sequence the job that way so the building is not left waiting with nothing.

Light Under the Bridge

Shadow, Glare and Passing Trains: Getting a Usable Picture Under the Manhattan Bridge

Few places in Brooklyn are as hard on a camera's exposure as a Dumbo sidewalk. Under the Manhattan Bridge, Water, Front, York and Plymouth Streets sit in deep shadow for much of the day, while a block away the same streets open onto the East River and catch direct reflected light. A doorway on Washington Street can have dark shade under the bridge on one side of the frame and bright open sky down the block on the other. At night the conditions flip: the street goes dark, but trains on the bridge sweep moving light across facades, and lit windows, shopfronts and the restored archway lighting throw hard pools of brightness beside dark corners. A camera set up with default settings will often expose for the brightest part of the scene and turn the person at the door into a dark outline, or expose for the shade and burn the street out to white.

The fix is mostly in the choice of camera and the patience to set it up on site. We use cameras with true wide dynamic range, which combine short and long exposures so a face in shadow and a bright street behind it can both be read. We aim the doorway camera so the brightest part of the scene sits at the edge of the frame rather than behind the subject's head, which often means mounting to the side of a door rather than straight in front of it. We set the shutter fast enough that a person walking briskly does not smear, which matters more at night than raw resolution does. And we test each view at three times: midday, dusk and full dark. On streets under the bridge we also watch a few train crossings at night, because a camera whose exposure jumps every time a train's lights sweep the wall will produce a flickering, useless recording unless its settings are steadied.

Trains also matter for alerts. The MTA's New York City Transit Noise Reduction Report, prepared after a 2023 request from Assembly Member Jo Anne Simon, logged hundreds of train passages at each Dumbo residence it measured, for example 520 at 56 Adams Street and 503 at 68 Jay Street during the monitoring periods. Every one of those passages throws moving light and, near the anchorage and approach, some movement in nearby structures. A camera set to alert on any change in the picture will fire constantly. We switch alerts to person and vehicle detection, draw the detection zone tight around the door or the curb in front of it, and leave the camera recording continuously regardless, so nothing is lost to a filter. Where a camera is mounted on a wall that visibly shakes when a train crosses, we use a short, rigid mount close to the wall rather than a long arm, which keeps the picture steady.

The river side of the same building

Buildings facing Brooklyn Bridge Park and the river have the opposite problem from those under the bridge. Big factory windows and newer glass walls fill interior views with daylight, and a lobby camera looking toward a glass front will see silhouettes until its exposure is set for faces rather than for the sky. We aim interior cameras away from the glass where we can, toward the inside face of the entrance, so that people coming in are lit from the front by the daylight behind the camera. That single change, choosing which way a camera looks, does more for picture quality in a riverside lobby than any upgrade in resolution.

Gair concrete: when there is no hollow space at all

In most buildings, camera cable disappears into a hollow wall or above a dropped ceiling. In a Gair-era concrete loft there may be neither. Walls, ceilings and columns are solid, and owners who bought or rented here usually did so precisely because the concrete is exposed. That leaves four ways to reach a camera, in the order we prefer them: an existing opening, such as an old pipe sleeve, a disused freight lift shaft, a chute or a chase left by earlier tenants; the inside of an existing soffit, bulkhead or column wrap built during conversion; a neat run of metal conduit or painted raceway fixed tight to the concrete and following its lines; and, only when nothing else works, a planned core through the slab. Before any core, we scan for reinforcing steel and ask the building whether a structural engineer needs to sign off, because cutting a reinforcing bar in a century-old slab is not something to discover afterwards. National Park Service guidance on historic concrete notes that early mixes sometimes used cinder or crushed brick as aggregate and that corroding reinforcement causes cracking and spalling, so we drill at slow speed, keep holes back from slab edges and seal each one.

Brick-and-timber warehouses: respecting old wood

The warehouses along Water Street were built with massive timber posts and beams, and those beams are often the best place in the building for a camera. They are high, solid, and run where people walk. But old timber splits if a large screw is driven in without a pilot hole, and in a building like this the beam is part of what owners and tenants are paying for. We fix to timber with clamps or straps where we can, pre-drill where we must, and keep fixings small and in one line so they can be filled if the camera is ever moved. Cable follows the top or side of the beam in clips rather than being stapled to its face. Where cable has to pass through brick, the opening is made in a mortar joint and sealed, and where it runs near the cellar, it is kept off the floor and away from any spot that has taken water.

Steel-framed lofts: clamps, not holes

A building like 68 Jay Street, described by the BID as brick with a steel frame and reinforced concrete floors, gives us steel beams to work from. Beam clamps let a camera bracket or a cable support grip the flange without drilling the steel or disturbing the fireproofing on it. The floors are where care is needed, because some buildings of this era used terra-cotta tile in the floor system, which cracks if hit hard or drilled at the wrong spot. Between floors we use existing penetrations wherever possible and firestop every opening we touch.

Towers: fire-rated corridors and garages

Newer towers are easier in one way and stricter in another. Cable can usually travel above corridor ceilings to a riser closet, but corridors and shafts are fire-rated, so every penetration gets a rated sleeve and firestop. Garage cameras face headlights straight on, so we choose cameras that handle strong point light and aim them along the ramp rather than into it. Corridor cameras are framed so they show the length of the hall and the elevator landing without looking straight into any apartment door, and amenity rooms and roof terraces get views of their doors, not of residents relaxing inside.

Office lofts: the losses that leave no broken lock

The laptops that vanished from a locked Bridge Street office overnight are the kind of loss Dumbo offices most often face: no broken door, no alarm, just something missing in the morning. An office camera system here has a precise job. It needs a camera on the suite entry from inside, at head height, so everyone who enters is recorded front-on with a clear face; a view of reception and any visitor sign-in area; a camera on the server or equipment closet door; a view of any back door, stair door or freight landing that opens into the suite; and, on open-plan floors, overview cameras aimed along aisles rather than down onto desks. Screens, whiteboards and documents stay out of frame, and conference rooms, phone booths and restrooms are not covered. In a multi-tenant floor, the suite's cameras stay inside the suite's walls. Where the landlord's lobby system already records the building entrance, we make sure the suite recorder's clock matches it, so a clip from the lobby and a clip from the office door line up to the second.

Shops on Front and Water Streets: doors, racks and the cash

The two thefts at the Front Street shop were daylight walk-ins, and the Pearl Street and Water Street break-ins came through glass doors. Both point to the same core views for a small Dumbo storefront: a camera just inside the door, at head height, recording one clear face per person; a camera aimed along the racks or tables nearest the door, where goods can be lifted and carried out in a few steps; a camera above and slightly in front of the register that shows hands and the drawer but not the card pad's keys; a camera on the stock room or back door from inside; and one view looking out through the glass to the sidewalk, set back from the window to avoid reflections. The recorder goes somewhere the staff cannot easily reach from the counter, on battery backup, so it keeps recording if the door is smashed and the power flickers. In a landmark building, all of this can be done from inside the shop without touching the facade.

Galleries, studios and venues: protecting work that is part of the room

A gallery's walls are its product. Cameras have to cover the door, the desk, the walls and the storage room without appearing in every installation photograph or casting shadows on the work. We use small dome cameras in a finish that matches the ceiling, mount them at room corners or on existing track, and route cable along the lines of the ceiling or inside existing raceway. Storage and packing rooms, where works sit between shows, often matter more than the exhibition floor and get their own camera. Recording and photo studios need equipment that makes no noise in the live room and settings that ignore bursts of strobe light, and venues need entrance and box-office views that hold up when a crowd arrives at once.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Dumbo

  • Private rooms stay unrecorded. Restrooms, changing areas, locker rooms and lactation rooms are never covered, and we check each view for mirrors and glass that might reveal one.
  • Screens, card pads and documents stay out of frame. Register and reception cameras are aimed at hands and faces, and unavoidable areas are blacked out in the camera itself.
  • The facade belongs to the building owner. In the historic district, any camera on a street-facing wall is the owner's application to the LPC, not the shop tenant's.
  • Coworking operators control the common areas. Shared lounges, kitchens and corridors in coworking floors are the operator's to camera, and a member's camera stays inside a private office.
  • Employees deserve to know. One compliance source we read points to New York Labor Law Section 203-c on notice of video monitoring; we draft a plain posted notice for office and shop clients and suggest a word with counsel.
  • Video only, no audio. Business cameras are set to record pictures, not conversations.
  • Rated openings stay rated. Every hole through a fire-rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • New power is an electrician's work. If a camera location needs a new outlet or circuit, a licensed electrician does that part, and the quote says so.
  • Proof on paper. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and we send proof to landlords, boards and coworking operators before the visit when they ask.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Dumbo Loft From the Cellar Up

On a Dumbo walk-through we start in the cellar, not the lobby, because the cellar tells us how the rest of the job will go. It shows us where the building's power, phone and internet lines come in, whether there is a high, dry place for a recorder, and whether the floor has taken water before; stains on walls and equipment raised on blocks usually say so plainly. From there we follow every existing path upward: old pipe sleeves, disused shafts, the conduit an office tenant left behind, the chase beside the freight elevator. In a concrete building those paths decide where cameras can go far more than the floor plan does. Only after that do we go out to the sidewalk, count the frontage on each street and mark the doorways. We look at the street door at midday and, where we can, again after dark with a train or two crossing the bridge, because a view that looks fine at noon under the bridge can be useless at nine at night. The last stop is the person who will open the recorder after an incident, a super, a manager or a shop owner, and we ask them which views they would need. Their answer usually trims the plan.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Dumbo: Questions Answered

Facades, frontage and old materials

The landmark guide counts cameras by street frontage. Our corner loft has a short face on Washington Street and a long one on Front Street. How does that shape the plan?

Each street face is measured on its own, so the long Front Street side gives you more facade spots than the short Washington Street side, and the large-building allowance in the LPC's guidebook may apply if your building qualifies. We measure both faces, read the current rule against them and then assign the spots to doors, not to wall length. On a corner like yours, the usual plan is one compact camera at each working entrance, with the corner itself watched from inside an upper window looking down both sidewalks, which uses no facade spot at all. The Washington Street side, where crowds gather to photograph the bridge, gets the most tightly framed camera on the building, so it records faces at the door rather than every visitor on the sidewalk.

Our ground-floor shop is in an early building with a painted cast-iron front. Can a camera be fixed to cast iron?

It can, but cast iron is brittle and often thinner than it looks, and on a landmark facade it is exactly the kind of feature the LPC protects. We would rather not drill it. The better options are usually inside: a camera mounted on the inside of the door frame or the ceiling just behind the glass, set back far enough that the window does not reflect into the lens. If the building owner wants an exterior camera at the shop entrance, we look first for an existing fixing point, a light bracket or a sign mount, and only then consider a small tapped hole in a thick section, with the owner's approval and any permit in place. Plain brick or a mortar joint nearby is almost always a better choice than the iron.

Our Gair-era concrete loft has flat slab ceilings and mushroom-capped columns. Where does a hallway camera mount, and how does its cable stay out of sight?

The flared top of a mushroom column is often the best mount in the room. It sits high, gives a long view down a corridor or across an open floor, and lets a camera tuck into the angle where the capital meets the slab. Cable can then run down the column inside a slim painted raceway, or across the slab in conduit following the line between column bays, to the nearest chase. Where the building has a soffit or bulkhead built during conversion, we use it. We scan before any hole, drill slowly into old concrete, keep back from edges and seal everything. The aim is a run that looks deliberate, like part of the building's other services, rather than cable that wanders.

Our Water Street warehouse still has its heavy timber posts and beams. Is it all right to fix a camera mount into old timber?

Yes, with care. Old dry timber splits if a large screw goes in without a pilot hole, and many owners prize those beams. We prefer clamps or straps that grip the beam without piercing it. Where a fixing has to go into the wood, we pre-drill, use the smallest fixing that holds the camera securely, and set the holes in one tidy line along the side of the beam rather than its face. Cable runs along the top or side in clips. Camera weight is small, so the structural load is negligible, but appearance and the life of the wood matter, and that is how we treat it.

Doors, mailrooms and lobbies

A thief followed a resident through the front door of a Washington Street building, went down to the basement and broke the mailroom lock. Which cameras would have shown that?

Three, in sequence. A vestibule camera inside the street door, at head height, records each person coming in and whether two people entered on one door opening, which is how tailgating shows up on video. A camera at the top or bottom of the cellar stair, or at the corridor leading to the mailroom, shows who headed downstairs. And a camera inside the mailroom, aimed at its door and the package shelves, records the lock being forced and what was taken. With synced clocks, those three clips tell the whole story in under a minute of footage. Many buildings have only a lobby camera, which proves someone came in but not what they did next. In a basement mailroom we also check lighting, because a camera in a dim cellar corridor needs a light on a motion sensor or good low-light performance to record a face.

Two laptops disappeared overnight from a locked Bridge Street office with no sign of a break-in. What should our office camera show so a loss like that can be traced?

A loss without forced entry usually means someone with access, a key, a code or simply a door left on the latch, so the camera's job is to record every entry and exit, not to watch desks. One camera inside the suite door, facing it at head height, recording continuously, covers most of the need. Add a view of any second door, such as a stair or freight landing, and a camera on the equipment closet. Set the recorder to keep at least two weeks, because losses like this are sometimes noticed days later. If your building has a fob or keypad log, make sure the camera clock matches it, so an entry event at 11:42 p.m. can be checked against the face at the door at 11:42 p.m.

Our loft has condos upstairs and an office tenant on the lower floors. The office's security person wants to see our lobby cameras. Who should be allowed to watch shared-lobby footage?

The owner of the lobby decides, which in a condo is usually the board through the managing agent. A common arrangement is that the building holds the lobby, vestibule, mailroom and freight cameras on its own recorder, and gives an office tenant's designated contact live view of the street door only, with no playback or export, or provides specific clips on written request after an incident. Residents' comings and goings are then not routinely visible to a commercial tenant. We set this up as separate accounts with clearly limited permissions, and we suggest the board put the policy in writing so the next manager inherits the same rules.

Shops, galleries and studios

A Front Street shop lost more than six thousand dollars of stock to people who walked in during the afternoon. Where should a small shop aim its cameras so the clip shows the goods leaving?

At the stretch between the racks nearest the door and the door itself, because that is where goods change from on display to on the way out. A camera just inside the entrance records each face at arrival; a second camera, mounted high at the back wall and aimed toward the door, shows the racks, the hands that lift items and the route to the exit in one frame. Together they show who came in, what they took and that they left with it. Keep the most valuable stock in the view of both. Record continuously all day rather than on motion, since a busy shop triggers motion constantly anyway, and keep a few weeks of footage so a pattern across visits can be checked.

Our Main Street gallery hangs work worth far more than the camera system. Where can cameras go without showing up in every installation photograph?

Small domes in the ceiling's own color, at the corners of each room and on the line of the existing track lighting, are nearly invisible in photographs and cover walls from above. One camera faces the entrance and front desk; the others are angled along the walls so that each piece is in at least one view. The storage and packing room, where works wait between shows, gets its own camera. We avoid putting a camera where its infrared lights reflect off glazed frames at night, and we can switch off the infrared in rooms where the gallery lighting stays on. Cable follows the ceiling lines or the track, never across a wall used for hanging.

Our recording studio's live room has to stay silent. Can cameras run in there without fans or clicking?

Yes. The cameras themselves are silent; a fixed PoE camera has no fan and no moving parts unless it is a pan-tilt-zoom model or has a motorized lens that refocuses, and we avoid both in a live room. The noise in a camera system comes from the recorder's hard drives and any switch fans, so those go in the machine room or a closet outside the live room, connected by cable. Infrared lights are silent too, but if the room has dark sessions we confirm the camera's day-night switch makes no audible click. Audio recording stays off, which also keeps the camera system separate from your session audio.

Offices, coworking and the waterfront

We are adding cameras to our Jay Street office. Do our employees have to be told in writing?

One compliance source we read says New York Labor Law Section 203-c requires employers to post notice of video monitoring and get written acknowledgment from employees. We are not lawyers and suggest you confirm the details with counsel, but the practical step is simple and sensible either way: post a short notice at the entrance and in common areas saying cameras are in use, describe where, and include it in your employee handbook with an acknowledgment. What is never allowed is video in restrooms, changing rooms or similar private spaces. We keep those off any plan and can give you a written list of each camera's location and view to attach to your notice.

Our company rents a private office on a coworking floor in Dumbo Heights. Can we put our own camera at our office door?

Usually inside your office, yes; in the shared corridor, usually no. Coworking operators control the common areas and normally have their own cameras there, so a member's camera is limited to the space it rents. The neat solution is a small camera mounted inside your office, above or beside the door, aimed at the door and the inside of the room near it, recording to a compact recorder or a camera with local storage, and powered from your own outlet. Ask the operator first, since most member agreements cover fixtures and network use, and keep the camera on its own network rather than the shared floor Wi-Fi. When you move out, it comes down with a couple of small holes to fill.

Our building has a retractable flood wall at its doors, like the one at the Empire Stores. Where do door cameras go so they still work when the barrier is up?

Above the barrier line and on its dry side. The Urban Land Institute describes the Empire Stores' AquaFence, a retractable flood wall staff can deploy when needed, and buildings with barriers like that have to think about cameras in two states: open for business and sealed for a storm. We mount door cameras high enough that the barrier does not block their view of the doorway when it is up, bring their cable in above the expected water line, and keep switches and the recorder on an upper floor rather than the cellar. A camera aimed at the deployed barrier from inside also lets the building see, remotely, whether water is reaching it during a storm.

Licenses, Landmarks and Permission

The Rules Behind a Dumbo Camera Job, Plainly Set Out

Most camera work inside a Dumbo apartment, office or shop needs no city paperwork. The rules that do apply come from five places: the state's installer licensing law, the Landmarks Preservation Commission, New York's privacy laws, the ownership and governance of the building, and the building code's rules about fire-rated walls and electrical work. Here is how each plays out in this neighborhood.

Who may install. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license under that article. A camera system used to monitor intrusion or theft falls within the security system work the law describes. An owner putting up cameras on their own property is not in the business of installing; a contractor doing it for pay is. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and we send a copy to a board, a managing agent or a landlord who asks. New line-voltage power, such as a new outlet beside a lobby camera or a circuit for a recorder closet, is a licensed electrician's work, and when a job needs it the quote says so.

Landmarks. Inside the DUMBO Historic District, cameras on street-facing walls fall under the LPC's rules for minor health and safety equipment, set out in Section 2-20 and described in the Permit Guidebook, including the frontage-based cap discussed above and, for some installations, manufacturer cut sheets. Interior cameras do not change a building's exterior. The city's recent restoration of 26 blocks of Belgian block streets in Dumbo and Vinegar Hill, begun in 2019 and finished in December 2025, was approved by the LPC as well, a reminder that the streetscape here is treated as part of the district. We check every address against the district map before planning any exterior work.

Where a camera may look. New York does not prohibit recording what anyone can see from a public place, such as a building entrance or a sidewalk. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives residents a claim against a neighbor who records them where they reasonably expect privacy, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places such as bathrooms or bedrooms without consent. In a loft building where apartment doors open onto shared corridors and windows face each other across narrow streets, we frame every view on the door, hall or room it is meant to watch and black out any window or apartment interior that falls inside the frame. Cameras record video, not sound.

Who decides. In a condo, the board decides about common areas and usually about anything on the exterior. In a rental loft, the owner does. In a mixed building, the owner of each part decides for that part, and shared doors need agreement from both sides. A shop tenant controls the inside of the shop and needs the landlord for anything on the facade, in a shared cellar or on the residential entrance. A coworking member controls only the private office it rents. An office tenant's cameras stay inside the suite. Some loft buildings in the city are interim multiple dwellings under the Loft Law, Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law; if yours is one, tell us, and we will confirm with the owner how any common-area work fits into the building's legalization obligations before planning it.

Walls and openings. Every opening through a fire-rated wall, floor or shaft is sealed with rated firestop. Every hole through brick or concrete facing the weather is sealed against water. Before any core through a reinforced concrete slab, we scan for reinforcing and ask the building whether its engineer needs to review the location.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Dumbo

Quick answers from search tools, cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and AI summaries tend to treat Dumbo either as a generic Brooklyn apartment area or as a postcard. These are claims we see repeated about cameras here, written as summaries rather than quotations, and what the sources and the work itself show instead.

"Dumbo is upscale and quiet, so cameras are mostly about packages."

Package loss is real, and the mayor's office estimate of 90,000 packages stolen or lost across the city each day shows how common it is.

But the incidents Brooklyn Paper reported here are wider: a tailgater forcing a basement mailroom on Washington Street, laptops gone from a locked Bridge Street office, glass doors broken at a Pearl Street school and a Water Street pizzeria, two daylight thefts in two days at a Front Street shop, a car stolen from a Jay Street lot.

Each needs a different camera in a different place. A package camera alone covers one of them.

"Burglaries are falling, so cameras matter less."

The fall is real: the 84th Precinct's CompStat report showed 116 burglaries year to date in late September 2026 against 173 a year earlier, down 32.9 percent.

The precinct covers several neighborhoods, so the figure ranks no block. And many of the losses Dumbo buildings deal with, a missing laptop, a shoplifted rack, a damaged freight elevator, are not burglaries at all.

A falling count is good news. It is not evidence that the doors which do get tested are covered.

"You can't put cameras on a building in a historic district."

The LPC's Permit Guidebook says the opposite: it treats security cameras as minor safety equipment alongside lighting and intercom panels, under Section 2-20 of its rules.

There are limits, including a cap on cameras tied to street frontage and, for some installations, cut sheets. A third-party guide reports that most LPC permits are issued at staff level, often in about ten business days when they clearly meet the rules.

The real constraint is how many facade cameras a building gets, which is why each one is assigned to a doorway.

"Wireless cameras are the simple answer for a loft."

Wireless cameras avoid drilling, which sounds ideal in a building made of concrete.

The same concrete is the problem. Thick slabs and walls block Wi-Fi between floors and down to cellars, and a wireless camera still needs power. In a mailroom two floors below the router, it is often the first camera to drop out.

Wired cameras on PoE, run along existing chases or neat conduit, are the reliable choice for anything beyond a single apartment door.

"A building with a concierge doesn't need cameras."

A staffed desk sees the lobby while someone is at it and looking up.

It does not see the basement mailroom, the freight landing, the cellar door or a stair three floors up, and it keeps no record. In the Washington Street case, the thief went past the front door to the basement.

Cameras give the desk eyes where it cannot be and a record of what happened when nobody was watching.

"Cost sites give a per-camera price that works for Dumbo."

Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages built from jobs across the country, usually on wood-framed houses with attics and hollow walls.

A camera on a drop ceiling in a fitted-out office and a camera on a century-old concrete slab with no hollow space above it take very different labor. Landmark paperwork, firestop and flood-aware equipment placement are not in those averages either.

That is why we price each Dumbo job line by line after seeing it, and say so plainly instead of quoting a number in advance.

"Anyone can mount cameras for an office; no license needed."

An owner may install cameras on their own property.

A business doing it for pay is different: General Business Law §69-m bars anyone from being in the business of installing security systems without a license under that article.

Ask any installer for a license number and check it. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

Our Installation Process

Every Dumbo camera job, from a single camera inside a coworking office to a full loft building with lobby, mailroom, freight and cellar coverage, follows the same eight steps. Steps two and four carry the most weight here: confirming who signs off in a building with several owners and a landmark facade, and counting the frontage before any exterior camera is drawn.

1. Send photos, the address and who controls the building

Photos of the street door, lobby, mailroom, freight entrance, suite or shop floor, plus who owns, manages or leases each part. Requests are booked within 2 hours.

2. Confirm who signs off

The condo board, managing agent, landlord, coworking operator or shop owner, in writing, and whether any facade camera needs an LPC permit.

3. Walk the building from street to cellar

Sidewalk, vestibule, lobby, mailroom, stairs, freight elevator and cellar, checking light at different times, wall material and every existing cable path.

4. Give every camera one job and count the frontage

Street door, mailroom, register: one task per camera, named in plain words, with facade cameras counted against each street face.

5. Write the plan and the price

Each camera, mount, cable run, wall or slab penetration, recorder, drive, switch, battery unit, viewing screen and setup task listed before anything is ordered.

6. Install through concrete, brick and timber

Cable along existing chases, ceilings and conduit, holes only where planned, every opening sealed and firestopped, recorder locked away above flood level.

7. Set clocks and test by day, night and train

Recorder synced to a time server, each cable certified, every view checked at midday, dusk and after dark with trains crossing, unique passwords and two-step sign-in.

8. Hand over logins, an export test and a camera map

Each group gets only its own views, the manager or owner exports a test clip while we watch, and we leave a written map naming every camera and port.

FAQ

Dumbo Security Camera FAQ

Film crews park trailers on our block for days at a time. What happens to our street camera when they do?
+

A trailer parked at the curb can block a low street view completely, and NY1 has described Dumbo shoots running for several days with trailers on the cobblestones and no-parking signs on the lampposts. That is one more reason we frame the exterior camera tightly on the doorway itself, which a trailer rarely hides, and take any wider street view from an upper window inside, where it sees over parked vehicles. If a shoot is scheduled outside your building, check that the door camera is still recording a clear view on the first day, since lighting rigs can also change its exposure.

Our building is an interim multiple dwelling under the Loft Law. Does that change how lobby cameras get approved?
+

It can affect timing and who coordinates, because an interim multiple dwelling under Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law is working through legalization with its own obligations. Low-voltage cameras are usually separate from that work, but the owner still controls the common areas and may want camera work sequenced around other building upgrades. We ask the owner to confirm how a camera project fits before we plan it, and we keep cameras out of residential units and framed away from apartment doors.

Movers keep scraping our freight elevator and hallway walls on move days. Can cameras show which crew caused the damage?
+

Yes, if the cameras cover the freight landing on each floor and the inside of the cab, and the recorder keeps a few weeks of footage. A camera in the cab, mounted in a hard-cased dome in an upper corner, records loads going in and out; landing cameras show the corridor walls nearest the elevator, where most scrapes happen. With synced clocks and a booking log for each move, the building can match a scrape found on Tuesday to the crew that used the elevator on Saturday.

Our company has its own IT team. Can you install the cameras and then hand full administration over to them?
+

Yes. We agree the network details with your IT team before the visit, such as a separate network segment for cameras, fixed addresses and which ports are allowed, then install, configure and test. At handover we give your administrator the master account, a written list of every camera, its address and its port, and the recorder settings, and we remove our own access unless you want us kept on for support. Your team then controls passwords, updates and who can view.

I rent one artist studio on a floor shared by a dozen others. Can I have a camera inside my studio aimed at my door and my work?
+

In most cases yes, inside your own space, with the landlord's agreement for any fixing to walls or ceilings. A small camera mounted high inside the studio, aimed at the door and the area where work is stored, powered from your own outlet and recording locally, covers most needs. It stays out of the shared corridor, which belongs to the building. When you leave, it comes down with a couple of small holes to fill.

We paid for soundproofing in our office facing the Manhattan Bridge. Will drilling for camera cable weaken it?
+

It does not have to. We keep camera cable out of the acoustic walls and windows altogether, using the ceiling, existing chases or surface raceway instead. Where a cable must pass through an acoustic partition, the opening is kept as small as the cable allows and sealed with acoustic sealant, so the gap does not leak sound. Outdoor cameras on a bridge-facing wall are fed from the inside through one sealed opening near the camera, not through the window frames.

Our photo studio fires strobes all day. Will the flashes keep setting off the camera alerts?
+

They will if the cameras alert on any change in brightness. We switch the studio cameras to person detection only, draw detection zones around the doors rather than the shooting area, and schedule alerts for hours when nobody should be there. Recording continues regardless. We also set the cameras so that a strobe burst does not push them into a long exposure adjustment, which keeps the recording steady through a shoot.

We are opening a pop-up shop in Dumbo for a few weeks. Can a camera system be put in temporarily and taken out cleanly?
+

Yes. For a short lease we design for removal from the start: cameras on clamps, existing fixings or a few small screws, cable in surface raceway or along existing supports, and a recorder on a shelf with battery backup. Nothing goes on the facade. When the pop-up ends, we take everything down, fill the small holes and leave the space as it was, and the equipment can go to your next location.

Our auditors want proof of who went into our server closet. Can a camera on that door give them what they need?
+

A camera inside the closet door, recording continuously with a synced clock, gives a dated visual record of every entry that can sit beside your badge or key log. For audits, the important parts are retention long enough to cover the review period, restricted viewing and export rights, and a written record of who can access the footage. We set those up and give you a short document describing the camera, its view, retention and permissions, which is the kind of thing auditors usually ask for.

Could one of our cameras double as a live skyline view we share with clients?
+

It can be done, but not with a camera from your security system. Publishing any view from the security recorder means opening it to the internet in ways that weaken its protection. If you want a public skyline or bridge view, we set up a separate camera on its own connection, never linked to the recorder that watches your doors, so sharing one never exposes the other.

We run a small gym in a Water Street loft. Can cameras cover the training floor while leaving the locker rooms alone?
+

Yes, and the locker rooms, showers and restrooms are never on camera under any plan we draw. We cover the entrance and front desk, the training floor from its corners, any storage area for equipment and the back door. Each view is checked so that the doorway into a locker room shows only the corridor outside it, never the inside, and mirrors on the gym walls are checked for reflections into private areas.
