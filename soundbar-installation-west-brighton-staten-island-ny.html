The Neighborhood

Why West Brighton Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Most soundbar jobs are decided by two things: what the TV wall is made of, and who is in the room when the sound is on. In West Brighton both answers change from one block to the next. The neighborhood sits in the middle of Staten Island's North Shore, between New Brighton and Port Richmond, running from the Kill Van Kull up past Forest Avenue toward Clove Lakes and Silver Lake parks. It was industrial before it was suburban. A local history newsletter, The Neighborhoods, notes that it earned the plain nickname Factoryville, and that its first big employer was a textile dyeing works run by Barrett, Tileston & Co., opened in 1819 just north of Castleton Avenue.

That makes West Brighton old by Staten Island standards. Point2's summary of census survey data for the neighborhood puts the median year of construction at 1952 and says 42 percent of its homes were built in 1939 or earlier — a far older stock than the high ranches and townhouses of the Mid-Island and South Shore. The same figures show 37.8 percent detached single-family houses, 22 percent two-unit buildings and 14.2 percent attached houses, with roughly one home in ten in a building of fifty or more apartments. A Compass agent quoted in Homes.com's neighborhood guide sums the streets up as older Cape Cods, detached duplexes and colonial-style homes, with Tudors mixed in, and calls it a big investment neighborhood with a lot of multifamily homes.

For a soundbar, those numbers translate into three very different walls. In a house from before the war, the TV usually goes on plaster laid over wood lath, which can be sound or can be quietly coming away from its lath, and the wiring behind it may be original. In a postwar Cape Cod or colonial the wall is more likely to be plaster on gypsum lath or early drywall, with studs that a scanner can find. And in the 1960s brick buildings of West Brighton Houses the wall is masonry behind a coat of plaster or paint, which takes a completely different drill bit and anchor.

Then there is the household. With more than a fifth of buildings holding two units, a living room in West Brighton very often has another family above it or below it. Richmond University Medical Center on Bard Avenue, a teaching hospital licensed for 448 beds, and the shipyards and tugboat companies along Richmond Terrace mean plenty of residents work nights, early mornings or rotating shifts, so the TV is on while somebody else in the house is trying to sleep.

Last, one corner of the neighborhood is being rebuilt from the inside: West Brighton Houses, covered in its own section below.

How each West Brighton wall takes a bracket and a cable

Plaster on wood lath. The fixing goes into the studs, never into the lath alone. Between studs we use toggles that clamp behind the plaster. Drilling is done with sharp bits, at low speed and without hammer action, so the plaster's grip on its lath is not shaken loose.

Plaster on gypsum lath and early drywall. Many houses from the 1940s and 1950s used gypsum board lath under a thinner coat of plaster, which drills cleanly and scans reasonably well. These walls are the most likely in West Brighton to have an open cavity from floor to ceiling, which is what an in-wall power and cable kit needs, as long as there is no fire blocking or insulation in the way.

Brick and block in apartment buildings. Masonry is drilled with masonry bits and fixed with sleeve or screw anchors set into whole brick or solid block, not the mortar joint. Holes are depth-limited and the first one is checked before the rest. Masonry walls cannot take an in-wall kit, so the leads go into a paintable channel or straight down into the cabinet, and anything that management prohibits is not drilled at all.

Opening a wall for other work? Ask about a network cable at the same time. A wired connection keeps a streaming bar steady when plaster and distance weaken Wi-Fi, and our Staten Island structured cabling crew can run it on the same day.

Before the war: what old plaster asks of us

A pre-1940 house in West Brighton was plastered by hand, coat over coat, onto strips of wood lath nailed across the studs. When it is healthy it is harder and denser than modern drywall and holds a well-placed fixing for decades. Its weakness is the bond to the lath, which can fail unseen after years of settling or leaks. So the first thing we do on an old wall is find out where it is sound and where it is not, and the bracket only ever goes where the studs are solid and the plaster is firm.

After the war: why the 1950s house is usually the easy one

The Cape Cods and colonials that fill much of West Brighton were built in a period when plasterers increasingly used gypsum board as their lath, and drywall was taking over. The result is a flatter, more uniform wall over studs at predictable spacing, which a scanner reads well. These are the houses where a bar can most often be hung in under an hour, and where hiding the cables inside the wall is most likely to be possible.

The 1960s: masonry, management and a fresh coat of paint

West Brighton Houses were built as brick apartment buildings, and in buildings of that era interior and exterior walls are often masonry or block behind paint: a solid fixing with the right anchor, but no room for an in-wall kit. The renovation adds new paint, windows and doors, and a property manager whose contractor rules come first — more on that below.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in West Brighton: Questions Answered

These are questions West Brighton households and businesses raise that a generic soundbar guide skips: tired plaster in old houses, instruments in the living room, matches streamed from a phone, fast games consoles, a day-sleeping shift worker, and the screens in clinics, pubs, yards and animal waiting rooms. Practical booking questions are in the FAQ further down.

Old houses and musical households

Knocking along our TV wall, some patches of the old plaster sound hollow and others sound solid — is it safe to hang a bracket there?

Often it is, but not on the hollow patches themselves. In a house built before the war, a dull, drummy sound usually means the plaster has lost its grip on the wood lath behind it, while a hard, dense knock means it is still keyed in. A bracket screwed only into loose plaster will eventually pull a piece of it away. The fix is to make sure every fixing that carries load goes through the plaster into a stud, which is solid regardless of how the plaster is doing, and to keep any toggles between studs to the firm areas. We map the wall by knocking and scanning, mark the loose areas, and confirm each stud with a small pilot hole inside the bracket's footprint.

If the loose area is large, sits right where the bracket must go, or the plaster is already cracking and bulging, it is worth repairing before anything is hung on it — a plasterer can re-attach it with plaster washers or adhesive, or cut out and patch the section. Until then, the bar can sit on the console or hang from the TV's own mount so the wall carries nothing new. Either way we tell you what we found and leave the loose area alone rather than drilling into it and making it worse.

We play guitar and keyboard at home — can an instrument plug into the soundbar, or should it?

Technically some can, but a soundbar is a poor instrument amplifier. Most bars take sound in over HDMI, optical, Bluetooth or Wi-Fi; only some have an analogue input, and an electric guitar's pickup needs an amplifier or interface before it reaches any line input. Bluetooth and Wi-Fi add a delay that you cannot hear while watching a film but can certainly feel while playing a note.

What works well is the other direction: a digital piano or keyboard with a line output, or a guitar going through a small audio interface or modelling amp, can feed the bar's analogue or optical input for casual playing along with a video lesson, with the bar's sound modes switched off. For practice that you want to sound right and feel immediate, a proper amp or headphones from the instrument are better. West Brighton has a musical streak — the Forest Avenue instrument shop Mandolin Brothers, opened in 1971, drew famous players across the harbor for decades.

Matches, streams and consoles

We watch cricket and football streams on a phone and cast them to the TV — does the sound still come out of the soundbar?

Yes, as long as the cast lands on the TV or on a streaming stick plugged into it. Casting hands the stream to the device on the TV, which plays it like any other app, and the TV then sends that sound to the bar over ARC or eARC like the rest of its audio. Where people run into trouble is when they mirror the whole phone screen instead of casting from the app, or when the bar itself is also a cast target and the phone sends the audio to the bar while the picture goes to the TV — that is when voices drift out of sync with lips.

On the visit we check which casting system your apps support, make sure the TV or stick is the target, set the TV's audio output to the bar, and test with whatever you actually watch. Cricket at Walker Park and football at Corporal Thompson Park are part of the neighborhood's weekends, and the matches people follow from abroad are often only available as streams, so this is a setup we get asked to sort regularly.

Our new console can run at 120 frames a second — will routing it through the soundbar hold the picture back?

It can, depending on the bar. A console plugged into a soundbar's HDMI input relies on the bar passing the video through to the TV, and many bars pass 4K at 60 frames per second but not 4K at 120, variable refresh rate or every HDR format. If the bar cannot pass a signal, the console quietly drops to something the chain can handle and you lose the mode you paid for. The safer arrangement on most current TVs is the reverse: plug the console straight into one of the TV's HDMI 2.1 ports, and let the TV send the game's audio back down to the bar over eARC.

There is a small catch: some TVs only offer their fastest modes on one or two ports, and one of those can be the eARC port the bar needs. We sort the ports so the console and the bar each get what they need, and confirm 120 Hz is active before leaving.

Shift work and quiet hours

I work nights at the hospital and sleep through the day while the kids watch TV — can the soundbar keep a daytime setting and an evening setting, one button apart?

Usually, yes. Most current bars remember a sound mode and let you switch features like night mode, dialogue enhancement and bass level from the remote or the app, and several let you save preferences or presets. A daytime "quiet" setting typically means night mode on to squeeze the loud passages, voice enhancement on so the kids can keep the volume down and still follow the show, and the subwoofer turned well down or off, because bass is what travels through floors and walls to a bedroom. The evening setting reverses it.

We set both up, show everyone in the house where the button is, and, where the bar supports it, put the quiet setting on the remote's shortcut so it is a single press rather than a menu. If the sleeper's bedroom shares a wall with the TV, moving the sub to the opposite side of the room and onto a pad makes a bigger difference than any setting. Richmond University Medical Center is a 24-hour hospital in the middle of the neighborhood, so we hear this request more in West Brighton than almost anywhere.

Screens that work for a living

Our medical office near the hospital uses a commercial display rather than a TV, with no tuner and no ARC — can a soundbar still be added to it?

Usually, but the connection is different. Commercial displays and digital-signage screens often have no built-in speakers worth using, no ARC socket and limited HDMI-CEC, because they are meant to be fed by a media player and controlled from a network. The simplest answer is to take the sound from the source rather than the screen: many media players and streaming boxes can output audio over a second HDMI or optical connection, or an HDMI audio extractor can split the sound off the cable before it reaches the display. Some displays do provide an optical or analogue audio output, which a bar can take directly.

Without CEC, the screen's remote will not control the bar's volume, so we either let the bar learn the display remote's volume commands where it can, or set a fixed, sensible level and leave the bar's remote at the front desk. For a waiting room we tune for speech, turn bass down or off, and fix the bar where it does not stick out into the walkway.

Our Forest Avenue pub runs a quiz night with its own microphone and speaker — can the TV soundbars drop out automatically whenever the host talks?

Not by themselves. Consumer soundbars have no way of hearing a separate microphone system and ducking under it; that feature belongs to mixers and commercial audio processors. What is practical is a one-press mute: with the bars on universal remotes or grouped in the same app, staff can silence every screen at once when the host picks up the microphone and bring them back afterwards.

If true automatic ducking is wanted — music and TV sound dipping whenever someone speaks — the TVs' sound needs to run through a small mixer or processor along with the microphone, which is a commercial audio job rather than a soundbar job. We can price the simple version on the day and pass the bigger one to our AV team. Either way, the bars over the bar stay pointed at the tables and set so the sound does not carry to the apartments upstairs after the pub empties.

The dispatch office at our marine yard keeps a VHF radio running all day — will a soundbar interfere with the radio, or drown out the calls?

It will not interfere with the radio itself. Marine VHF works on frequencies around 156 to 162 megahertz, while a soundbar's wireless links run on the Wi-Fi and Bluetooth bands at 2.4 and 5 gigahertz, far apart, so the two do not tread on each other.

Drowning out the calls is the real question, and it is solved by placement and settings. We mount the bar so it faces the seating rather than the radio desk, turn the bass down, which carries furthest and adds least to understanding speech, and set the bar's dialogue mode so the TV can be followed at a lower level. If the screen is mainly for weather and traffic, a fixed volume locked at a low level, with the remote kept by the dispatcher, keeps the radio the loudest thing in the room.

Our veterinary waiting room has nervous animals in it — should the soundbar's subwoofer be on at all?

We would leave it off, or very low. Deep bass is the part of a soundtrack that travels furthest and that people and animals feel as much as hear, and a waiting room is no place for sudden rumbles from a film trailer or an advert.

The other practical points: mount the bar high enough that a large dog cannot knock it and clear of the walkway, keep cables in a covered channel that cannot be chewed, and put the remote behind the desk.

AI answers like to say that a soundbar is light, so a pair of drywall anchors will do.

On old plaster it is the wrong instinct. The weight of the bar is not the issue; the plaster's hold on its lath is. An anchor in a patch that has lost its keys will turn and loosen, and every cable plugged into the bar adds a little tug each time someone moves the console.

Claim three: Dolby Atmos needs eARC

Partly true. Lossless formats such as Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio from discs and some downloads need eARC's extra bandwidth. Streaming services deliver Atmos inside Dolby Digital Plus, which plain ARC carries.

So a TV that is several years old with ordinary ARC is not a reason to skip an Atmos bar for Netflix or Disney+. What matters is the bar on the socket marked ARC, CEC enabled at both ends, and the TV's digital audio output set to pass-through or auto rather than PCM.

Claim four: casting from a phone is the same as playing on the TV

There are really three different things: casting from an app to the TV or a stick, mirroring the phone's whole screen, and sending audio straight from the phone to a bar that is itself a cast target.

Only the first reliably keeps sound and picture together through the soundbar. Mirroring adds lag and often drops to stereo; splitting audio to the bar and video to the TV produces the drifting lip-sync that people then blame on the bar.

So we make the TV or stick the cast target, send its sound to the bar over HDMI, and test with the apps you actually use.

Two Households, One House

Setting Up a Soundbar in a West Brighton Two-Family

More than one in five West Brighton homes is a two-unit building, and most of them stack one household on top of the other on wooden floors. That arrangement shapes a soundbar setup as much as the wall does, whether you are the owner downstairs, the tenant upstairs or a family that bought the house to live on one floor and rent the other.

Why the bass, not the dialogue, reaches the other floor

Voices and most music travel through the air, and plaster walls and ceilings stop a good share of them. Low bass behaves differently: it sets the floor joists and wall studs themselves vibrating, and a wood-framed house carries that vibration from one unit to the next with very little loss. So the household downstairs usually feels a film as a low thump without catching a single word, and the household upstairs hears a bass line through its floorboards. When a soundbar causes friction in a two-family, the subwoofer is almost always the reason.

Where the subwoofer goes, and where it should not

Keep it away from any wall shared with a hallway or stair the other household uses, and out of the corner above or below their bedroom, because corners and walls reinforce bass. Stand it on an isolating pad instead of straight on the floorboards, so less of it reaches the joists. Then set its level by ear from the sofa, ideally with someone listening in the other unit, since most subs leave the factory set far too high.

+

Virtual surround is processing: the bar uses angled drivers, reflections off nearby walls and timing tricks to make sound seem to come from beside and behind you, all from the front of the room. It works best in a small, regular room with walls close to the sofa, and it fades in a wide or open space. Real rear speakers put actual sound behind you and are clearly more convincing for films, but they need floor space and power outlets near the seats.

Can the soundbar's power cord or HDMI cable be cut shorter so there is no loop hanging behind the TV?
+

No. Cutting a moulded power cord removes its safety rating and can void the bar's warranty, and an HDMI cable cannot be shortened and re-terminated reliably. The tidy answer is the right length to begin with: a shorter HDMI lead, a power lead coiled and strapped out of sight behind the TV or inside the console, or an in-wall kit or channel that takes the slack. We bring several HDMI lengths so the run is neat.

What is the USB socket on the back of our soundbar for?
+

It depends on the model. On many bars it is a service port used to install firmware from a memory stick; on some it plays music files from a USB drive; on a few it can power a small streaming stick. It is almost never a way to connect the TV's sound. The manual will say which, and if a firmware update is due we install it from the bar's network connection or that port during the visit.

Does a new soundbar need a “break-in” period before it sounds its best?
+

Not in any way you would notice. Small speaker drivers may loosen very slightly with early use, but the difference is tiny next to where the bar sits, the room's shape, the subwoofer's position and the settings. What does change the sound in the first days is calibration and adjustment, which is why we run the bar's room calibration where it has one and set the bass and dialogue levels with you listening from your seat.

Do we need to register the soundbar with its manufacturer for the warranty to count?
+

Usually not; most makers honour the warranty with proof of purchase, so keep the receipt or order email. Registering, often through the bar's app during setup, can still help: it records the serial number, brings firmware notices and makes a claim quicker. We complete the app setup under your own account on the visit, so registration is a tap away. The bracket and our work are covered separately under our service terms.
