The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Heights Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Prospect Heights grew up quickly once its neighbor was finished. Most of the neighborhood was built after Prospect Park was completed in 1873, and by the 1890s the name Prospect Heights was in use, traced to a letter to the editor in 1889. Before that the land was farms and woodland, with an older pattern underneath it: Patch's history of the "old grid" explains that in 1833 the landowners Hoyt and Underhill laid out 25-by-100-foot lots at roughly a 45-degree angle, oriented to the Flatbush Turnpike, which followed a Lenape path. When the 1839 street grid arrived, those lot lines did not entirely disappear. Some buildings still sit at odd angles, a few can only be reached through an alley and a doorway in another building, and the effect is most visible between Underhill and Washington Avenues. None of that history changes our price, which is the Brooklyn rate. It changes the cellar, and the cellar is where nearly every Prospect Heights cabling job begins.

The houses built on that ground are mostly masonry. The Historic Districts Council describes the city's Prospect Heights Historic District as about 850 buildings, predominantly single-family row houses and apartment buildings constructed mostly from the mid-19th to the early 20th century, with the oldest from the 1850s. Brooklyn Paper's coverage of the designation names Neo-Grec rows from the 1860s and 1870s and Romanesque and Renaissance Revival houses from the 1880s and 1890s. One real estate guide puts the core brownstone stock on Park Place, Prospect Place, St. Marks Avenue and Underhill Avenue. For a cabling crew that means party walls of solid brick, plaster applied straight onto masonry or onto furring, wooden floor joists that span from party wall to party wall, and a front that, inside the district, must not acquire exposed conduit. The walls that run between front and back rooms are usually framed and can be fished. The walls between your house and your neighbor's cannot.

The houses are also changing shape. New York YIMBY reported that 119 St. Marks Avenue, designed by John V. Porter and built between 1870 and 1872, went before the Landmarks Commission in August 2026 for a rear addition, in a 44-foot-tall building yielding three condominium units. The LPC's January 2026 docket included 628 Carlton Avenue, built in 1871. When a row house becomes three units, the single network that once fed one family has to become three networks with a shared starting point, and someone has to decide who owns the cable in the shared parts of the building. When a house gets an addition, the new rooms need a path back to the old cellar. Both are cabling decisions that are cheap to make before the plaster goes up and expensive to make afterward.

Along the parkway and the plaza, the buildings are bigger and the rules come from a board. A building history from The Roebling Team describes Eastern Parkway in the mid-1920s becoming a corridor of 15-story elevator buildings; CityRealty lists Turner Towers at 135 Eastern Parkway as a 16-story building from 1926, converted to a cooperative in the 1980s, with 186 units. Real estate guides name 41 and 135 Eastern Parkway and 50 and 60 Plaza Street East among the prewar co-ops, and others mention 10 and 34 Plaza Street East and 230 Park Place. In these buildings a cable to the back bedroom is a question for the alteration agreement as much as for the walls: what hours work may happen, whether the super must be present, which shafts belong to the building, and what the board needs to see before anyone drills.

North of the row houses, the scale changes again. BKReader reports that Pacific Park has brought about 3,000 apartments in eight new buildings, and Brooklyn Speaks notes that six planned buildings, B5 through B10, are slated to stand on platforms built over the LIRR rail yard between Carlton and Vanderbilt Avenues and Pacific Street and Atlantic Avenue. In June 2026, 6sqft reported a new plan for seven buildings on six sites with about 5,600 homes, the tallest at 799 feet, and construction possibly starting in 2028. Off Grand Army Plaza, the Richard Meier tower at One Grand Army Plaza was completed in 2008, and 550 Vanderbilt is a 17-story, 278-unit condominium. In these buildings, the developer's wiring is whatever the developer chose, the floors are concrete, and changes go through management. Then there are the avenues: Vanderbilt Avenue, which one guide notes had apartment buildings from the 1870s combining street-level commerce with apartments above, and which Commercial Observer described as a restaurant row from about Park Place to Pacific Street.

The reason people call us is usually that the network never kept up with the building. A brownstone router on the parlor floor has to push its signal through plaster, brick and thick floors to reach a garden-level kitchen and a top-floor office. A co-op apartment has one outlet by the front door and a desk at the far end of a long prewar hallway. A condo conversion leaves three owners sharing a cellar that holds one tangle of provider cables. A restaurant takes over a space on Vanderbilt and inherits the last tenant's wiring along with the walk-in. Every one of these is solved the same way, by deciding first where cable can travel in that building without damaging it or breaking a rule, and then putting a tested cable exactly where the connection is needed.

Six Prospect Heights facts that shape a cabling plan

  • A large share of the housing is landmarked. The 2009 district of about 850 buildings runs roughly from Flatbush Avenue to Washington Avenue between Sterling Place and St. Marks Avenue, with a section reaching north to Pacific Street. Inside it, any conduit or wiring extended on the exterior needs an LPC permit and has to be concealed.
  • Not every block is in it. The neighborhood runs from Atlantic Avenue to Eastern Parkway, so blocks outside the district boundary exist, including the non-landmarked block where Brownstoner covered a stoopless early-20th-century row house renovation.
  • The party walls are brick. In the row houses, the walls shared with neighbors offer no cavity, so vertical routes have to come from closets, shafts and interior partitions.
  • The old grid survives in places. Lots laid out in 1833 at an angle to today's streets left angled buildings and a few mid-block buildings reached only through other properties, most visibly between Underhill and Washington Avenues.
  • Prewar co-ops line the parkway and plaza. Buildings like Turner Towers, with 186 units over 16 stories, govern work through boards and alteration agreements.
  • Some new towers stand over a rail yard. Pacific Park buildings planned for platforms over the LIRR yard mean a floor is not always just a floor, and nobody cores it without the building's engineer.

Structured Cabling for Prospect Heights' Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every cabling call in Prospect Heights. They differ in what their walls are made of, who owns the space a cable must cross, and who besides you has to agree. Those three answers decide the route before anyone talks about cable categories.

The landmarked row house

Italianate, Neo-Grec, Romanesque and Renaissance Revival houses inside the 2009 district, where the street front may not gain exposed conduit and the party walls are brick.

The row house becoming condos

Houses like 119 St. Marks Avenue, divided into units during a gut renovation, often with a rear addition under LPC review.

The row house outside the district

Houses on blocks beyond the district boundary, including stoopless early-20th-century rows, where exterior rules are lighter but the walls are just as solid.

The avenue apartment house

1870s and later buildings on Vanderbilt and the other avenues, and larger apartment houses on Lincoln and St. Johns Places, often with a store at street level.

The prewar parkway co-op

Mid-1920s elevator buildings on Eastern Parkway and Plaza Street East, such as Turner Towers at 135 Eastern Parkway, run by boards and building staff.

The new tower

Pacific Park buildings, some on platforms over the rail yard, plus 550 Vanderbilt and the glass tower at One Grand Army Plaza, with concrete slabs and management rules.

The landmarked row house: brick on two sides and a front that stays as it is

A Prospect Heights row house is a long box with two solid sides. The party walls carry the floors and separate the house from its neighbors, and they are brick from cellar to roof. Plaster on the inside face of those walls sits directly on the masonry or on thin furring, which leaves no space to drop a cable. The front and rear walls are masonry too. What is hollow is the interior: the partitions between front and back rooms, the walls around closets and bathrooms, the stair enclosure, and the floors themselves, whose wooden joists run from one party wall to the other. A cable route in one of these houses is therefore built from the inside out, using those framed partitions and the spaces between joists, and avoiding the brick wherever possible.

The front of the house is a separate matter. The Landmarks Commission's permit guidebook says that where installation requires conduit, the conduit must be concealed, and that conduit or other wiring extended on the exterior requires a permit from the Commission. A guide from The Brownstone Boys makes the point that exterior work on a landmark property can need LPC approval even when it needs no Department of Buildings permit. In practice we keep every new cable inside the house, use the service entry the provider already has, and if a provider line truly needs to move, we plan its new path at the rear or below grade and flag whether a Landmarks filing is likely before anyone drills. Interior partitions, closets and the cellar do the rest.

The row house becoming condos: three owners, one cellar and a decision about the middle

When a house is split into condominium units, the cable becomes property. Each owner will want a network that belongs only to them, but the cable between the cellar and their front door crosses space that belongs to everyone, and someday one of the units will sell. The cleanest design gives the condo one shared, labeled entry point in a common area of the cellar where providers connect, a separate bundle from that point to each unit, and nothing inside one unit that serves another. The details of who owns what are for the condo's offering documents and its lawyer, not for us, but we draw the plan so it can be described simply: the shared part ends at a labeled panel, and everything beyond it belongs to a single unit.

Timing matters even more than in a single-family house. A conversion usually means walls, ceilings and floors open at once, often alongside a rear addition, and that is the cheapest moment the building will ever offer. We pull cable after the framing inspection and before insulation and drywall, place an empty pathway with a pull string between the cellar and each unit for whatever comes next, and leave each unit's cable coiled and labeled where its equipment will go. When the finishes are done we return to terminate and test. If the architect's drawings are being filed with the Commission, the interior cable plan does not normally appear on them, but any exterior entry does, so we raise it early.

The row house outside the district: lighter rules, the same masonry

Prospect Heights is bigger than its historic district. The neighborhood reaches from Atlantic Avenue to Eastern Parkway, while the city district covers 21 blocks roughly between Flatbush and Washington Avenues and Sterling Place and St. Marks Avenue, with a northern section toward Pacific Street. The 1983 National Register district, with 305 contributing buildings from 1865 to about 1900, is a separate listing with different consequences. Brownstoner's coverage of a stoopless, early-20th-century row house on an eclectic, non-landmarked block is a reminder that some houses here sit outside the city's review. For an owner on one of those blocks, a sealed rear entry or a protected run on the back wall is a decision for you and good practice rather than for the Commission.

The construction inside, however, is the same story: brick party walls, wooden joists, plaster on the masonry. So is the habit we follow in every house regardless of the map. We place one exterior entry if one is needed, keep it on the rear or under an overhang, sleeve it through the wall, seal it inside and out, and route everything else indoors. We check the city's district map against your address before quoting, because owners are sometimes surprised in both directions, and we tell you which side of the line you are on so the plan matches it.

The avenue apartment house: stores below, tenants above, a shared cellar

Vanderbilt Avenue's apartment buildings date to the 1870s, and some combined street-level commercial space with apartments above. Larger apartment houses stand on Lincoln Place and St. Johns Place between Underhill and Washington Avenues. These buildings bring two problems that a row house does not. The first is the law around entry systems: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercommunication system in Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and HPD's guidance says owners should immediately repair broken intercoms. The second is that the cellar usually serves several masters at once, the store's walk-in and storage, the building's boiler and meters, and the provider handoffs for every apartment.

For an owner, the practical answer is to treat the building's own systems, the entry panel, any cameras and the common-area network, as a separate plant with its own cabinet, its own cable and its own power, kept apart from the store's network and from any tenant's. Riser cable climbs in a shaft or a stacked closet that belongs to the building, each apartment's run ends at its own door or inside the unit, and the store's equipment lives in the store's space. Where an owner is replacing an intercom anyway, the same visit is a good moment to give each apartment a proper data run, because the walls in the stair hall are already being opened. Mail theft reported at 55 Eastern Parkway, where the super checked the building's cameras, is a reminder that a camera is only as useful as the cable and recorder behind it.

The prewar parkway co-op: a board, an alteration agreement and the building's shafts

The elevator co-ops on Eastern Parkway and Plaza Street East are a different world from the row houses a block away. A cabling job here begins with paper: the alteration agreement, the board's approval of the scope, the certificate of insurance and the managing agent's schedule. Inside the apartment, partitions are usually plaster on a framework that can sometimes be fished and sometimes not, and the long prewar plans that make these apartments attractive also put the bedroom office far from the single outlet near the entry. Cable inside an apartment stays inside the apartment's walls, closets and ceilings; anything that passes into a building shaft, a hall ceiling or the basement belongs to the co-op and needs the board's agreement.

We plan these jobs around the building's habits. The super usually controls basement access and may need to be present. Working hours are set by the house rules. Elevator use for materials is booked in advance. Inside the unit, the route generally follows the apartment's own corridor, either behind its baseboards or within its partitions, to a small equipment spot in a closet, from which each room is fed. Where an apartment has been combined from two original units, old outlets and abandoned cable often turn up in unexpected places, and we trace before we drill. The board sees our written scope before work starts, so nobody is surprised by what happens on the day.

The new tower: concrete, curtain walls and a slab that may sit over a rail yard

The Pacific Park buildings, 550 Vanderbilt and the tower at One Grand Army Plaza were built with cable already in them, but what that cable does varies from building to building and unit to unit. Some apartments have a panel in a utility closet with a jack in every room; some have one outlet near the windows. Adding to them means working with drywall partitions on metal studs, which can be fished, and concrete slabs above and below, which management almost always forbids cutting. Where a building stands on a platform over the LIRR yard, as Brooklyn Speaks says the planned B5 through B10 buildings will, the structure under the floor is even less negotiable. Concealed routes come from soffits that already hide ductwork, closets that back onto the rooms that need jacks, and slim, painted raceway where nothing else works.

For buildings that have not been built yet, the time to ask about wiring is before closing or lease signing, not after move-in. The June 2026 plan reported by 6sqft would add seven buildings and about 5,600 homes, with construction possibly beginning in 2028, which means future buyers will have a long window to ask developers what each unit receives, where its panel sits and whether spare pathway is included. For ground-floor retail and office tenants in these towers, the conversation is with the landlord's building engineer, who controls the telecom rooms and any sleeves into them.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order a Technician Surveys a Prospect Heights Building

These notes describe how our technicians survey row houses, co-ops, towers and shops like those in Prospect Heights. They are our method, not an account of any particular address or customer.

Check the map before the building. Before the visit, the address goes against the city's historic district map. If it falls inside the 2009 district, the survey treats the street front as fixed and looks for a rear or below-grade solution for anything that must cross an exterior wall. If it is outside, the technician still assumes the owner cares about the front, because in this neighborhood owners nearly always do.

Start where the service enters. In a row house that is usually the cellar, under the stoop or along the front foundation wall. In a co-op it is the provider's equipment in the basement, which the super has to open. In a tower it is the unit's utility closet. The technician photographs the provider equipment, notes every cable leaving it and follows each one as far as it will go.

Look for the line that stacks. The technician walks every floor looking for something vertical: closets one above another, a bathroom wall over a bathroom wall, an old dumbwaiter or a service shaft, a framed wall beside the stair. In a brownstone, finding one of these is the difference between a hidden job and a visible one, because the brick on either side offers nothing.

Measure the angle. On the blocks between Underhill and Washington, the technician checks whether the house sits square to the street and to its neighbors. On the old 1833 lot lines it may not, which changes the length of each run and sometimes the direction the joists span. A long diagonal across a cellar is fine; an assumption that it is square is not.

Find out who signs. The survey records whether the owner, a condo association, a co-op board, a managing agent or a commercial landlord approves the work, and who can open each door on the day. In buildings set back from the street, it also records whose property the service line must cross.

Sketch it with you. Before leaving, the technician sketches the route floor by floor with the owner, marking each jack, each opening and anything that needs someone else's approval, so the written quote describes a plan you have already seen.

Pathways

Party Walls, Wedge Rooms and the Parlor Floor: How Cable Travels Through a Prospect Heights Row House

Every structured cabling system has the same pieces: a point where service arrives, a place where the main equipment lives, cable from there to each jack or access point, and the jacks themselves. In a Prospect Heights row house the service point and the equipment are usually in the cellar, and almost all the difficulty lies in getting from there to the upper floors without touching the party walls or the front. Here is how each level of the house usually answers that.

The cellar. The joists over the cellar span from one party wall to the other, so a cable heading from front to back can run between two joists without a single hole, supported on its own straps, while a cable heading sideways has to pass through the joists in small holes near the middle of their depth. Cellars here hold boilers, water heaters, gas meters and, in houses with tenants, laundry and storage. Data cable stays high, away from flues and gas lines, off water pipes and away from the electrical panel, and each floor's or each unit's bundle travels together so it can be identified later.

The climb. The vertical route is the heart of the job. A closet stack, one closet above another from the garden level to the top floor, is the best gift a brownstone can give, because a small hole through each closet floor carries the cable upward with nothing visible anywhere. Bathroom walls that stack, the framed wall beside the stair and old service shafts can do the same. The Q&A further down covers the old dumbwaiter and the pocket-door walls, two features owners often ask about. When none of these exists, the honest choice is between a small opening in a framed partition, patched afterward, and a neat raceway in a closet or along a stair stringer, and we show both on the drawing.

The parlor floor. This is the floor owners worry about most, with its tall ceilings, plaster cornices, wood trim and often original floors. Cable reaching it usually comes up from the cellar through the bottom plate of an interior partition, or arrives inside a closet and turns along the top of a baseboard. Jacks go low in framed partitions, inside built-in cabinets or beside existing outlets, and the brick party wall is left alone. Where the parlor's front room faces the street in a landmarked house, nothing we add is visible from outside, because nothing we add is on the outside.

The upper floors and the roof. Upper floors are fed from below, through the same stack, or from above if the house has a reachable space under the roof. Bedrooms and offices at the top of a four-story house are often the farthest point on the job, and a ceiling access point on that floor, fed by its own cable, usually does more for the house's Wi-Fi than any router upgrade. Any cable that has to cross the roof space stays clear of flues and is sealed where it passes through.

Wedge rooms and angled cellars

On some blocks the 1833 lot lines left houses and rooms that are not rectangular. Patch's history points to 323 Prospect Place as an angled example and notes the effect is most visible between Underhill and Washington Avenues. In a cellar, that can mean a back wall that meets the party walls at an angle, joists of different lengths, and a run from the front of the house to a back room that is longer than the house's frontage would suggest. We measure the actual route, not the lot depth, before choosing cable and equipment positions, and in a wedge-shaped room we place jacks and access points where the room's shape actually puts the furniture and the people, which is rarely in the corner the router installer guessed.

Openings that are closed the same day

Every hole through a floor or a wall is a hole in something that slows smoke and fire. In a row house the floors are not usually rated the way an apartment building's are, but an open hole from cellar to bedroom is still a chimney, so every penetration is closed around the cable with listed fire-rated sealant on the day it is made. In the co-ops, the avenue apartment houses and the towers, where unit walls, shafts and floors are rated assemblies, sealing with listed firestop is required and is usually a condition of the board's or the building's approval. Each sealed opening is marked on the as-built drawing so the next contractor can find it.

Separation from power, gas and heat

Old houses are crowded behind the plaster. A century of plumbers, steamfitters and electricians have used the same bays, and some of what they left is still live. Data cable is kept apart from electrical wiring and crosses it at right angles where the two must meet, never shares a bored hole or a box with power, stays away from steam and hot water piping and is never strapped to a gas line. If the bay we hoped to use is already full, we choose another. That discipline is invisible when the job is done, and it is the reason a cable keeps working through the next three renovations.

Service Entry

Where the Internet Comes In: Cellar Handoffs, Co-op Basements and Tower Utility Closets

Cabling inside a building only helps if it starts in the right place, and in Prospect Heights the right place depends on which of the neighborhood's building types you live or work in. Getting the starting point right is often the part of the job that saves the most trouble later.

Row houses. A provider bringing service to a brownstone usually lands its line in the cellar, often near the front foundation wall or under the stoop. Everything from that point inward is the owner's. Over the decades, many installers took shortcuts, running their own cable straight up the front or through a window frame to wherever the customer wanted the modem, which is exactly what the Landmarks rules on exposed conduit and wiring are meant to prevent on designated houses. A good cabling job lands the provider's line at a tidy, labeled spot in the cellar and runs clean cable from there to each floor, so the next technician has an obvious place to connect and no reason to touch the front of the house.

Condos and multi-unit houses. In a house with several units, each household may choose its own provider. That is normal. The cellar becomes a small shared entry where every provider's line lands in its own labeled spot and every unit's bundle starts. If one unit switches providers, the new line connects at the cellar, and the cable upstairs stays exactly where it is. The building's front stays clean because nobody needs a new route.

Co-ops and larger apartment buildings. In the parkway co-ops and bigger avenue buildings, provider equipment lives in the basement and the risers belong to the building. An older Patch story on Verizon's Fios rollout quoted a company spokesman saying that in larger buildings the company needs to deal with the landlord, the property owner or the realty company that owns the building. For an apartment owner, that means service arrives at your door on the building's terms, and our work begins at the point where the building's riser enters your apartment. Everything we add stays on your side of that line unless the board agrees otherwise.

Towers. In Pacific Park and the other new buildings, service usually reaches each unit from the building's telecom rooms through cable or fiber the developer installed, ending at a panel or terminal in a closet. That terminal is the provider's or the building's, and the cable from it to your rooms is yours to add to. We start there, trace what the developer installed, and extend it to the rooms that need a connection.

Where the equipment should live in a brownstone

In a single-family brownstone, the best home for the router and switch is often not the cellar. A closet on the parlor or second floor sits in the middle of the house and makes every run shorter. When the equipment stays in the cellar, it goes on the wall, off the floor and away from the boiler, the water heater and the washing machine, in a small ventilated cabinet if the cellar is shared. In a house with tenants or condo owners, each household's own router lives in its own space, fed from the shared cellar panel, so nobody has to knock on a neighbor's door to restart a box.

The service drop that belongs to someone else

In a few places, the old grid means a building does not touch the street at all. Patch's history describes three buildings reachable only by an alley and a doorway through another building. For those, the service line has to cross a neighbor's property, and the arrangement should be in writing. We cover how that works in the Q&A below. For every other building, the point is the same: the line should arrive once, in one place, and everything after it should be clean, labeled and inside.

Rules and Licenses

Landmarks, the City Code, Boards and Building Owners: Who Has a Say in a Prospect Heights Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before work begins.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission. The Commission designated the Prospect Heights Historic District on June 23, 2009, and the City Council affirmed it later that year. The LPC's permit guidebook, in its chapter on health, safety and utility equipment, says that where an installation requires conduit, the conduit must be concealed, and that conduit or other wiring extended on the exterior requires a permit. Security cameras, lighting and intercom panels are covered by the Commission's rules in Section 2-20, and the 2018 amendments describe restricting many of these items on a primary facade. A brownstone security guide notes that visible alterations, including mounts and conduit, may require a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter. Work that meets the rules is approved by staff; work that does not goes to a public hearing.

For a cabling job that stays entirely inside the house, the Commission is usually not involved. Where a cable has to cross an exterior wall, the rear is generally less sensitive than the front, but we do not guess. We tell you before the quote whether the plan touches the exterior, and if it does, whether a filing is likely, so you can decide with full information. The National Register listing from 1983 is a separate designation and does not by itself add city review.

The city's low-voltage rule. Under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and in effect since December 21, 2025, an electrical permit is not required for installing, altering, maintaining or repairing wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. For ordinary data cabling in a house or apartment, that means no electrical permit filing. Fire alarm wiring falls outside the exemption and needs specialized licensing and its own permits. In the co-ops, avenue buildings and towers that have building fire alarm systems, our data cable never shares a fire alarm raceway.

Licensed security work. Cameras, door access and burglar alarms are licensed work in New York. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, so when a cabling job includes a camera on the rear garden, a reader at a building door or a video entry panel, the same licensed company handles the cable and the device.

Entry systems in multiple dwellings. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercommunication system in Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments that were erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Smaller buildings are not covered by the intercom requirement, though self-closing and self-locking door rules still apply. HPD's guidance says the owner, manager or super should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms. We mention this because owners planning an intercom upgrade often want data cable in the same stair hall at the same time, and it is easier to plan both together.

Boards, condo associations and landlords. In a co-op, the alteration agreement and the board govern anything beyond your walls and often what happens inside them. In a condo conversion, the association's documents will eventually say who owns shared cable. In a rental building or a commercial lease, the owner decides. We send a certificate of insurance to whoever needs it before booking, and we put our scope in writing so that the person approving it knows exactly what will be opened and where.

Old paint and old materials. Most of the neighborhood's housing predates 1978, and painted trim, floors and walls may contain lead. A small cable hole disturbs very little, but we drill through sheeting, collect dust at the bit with a HEPA vacuum and clean as we go. If a route would require opening a larger painted area, the federal lead-safe rules for pre-1978 housing apply, and we raise that before starting rather than after. If anything in a cellar or ceiling looks like old insulation that should not be disturbed, we stop and tell you.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Prospect Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Prospect Heights owners, residents and businesses bring up: old brownstone features that might carry a cable, houses on the old grid, condo conversions, buildings hidden behind other buildings, radiators in prewar co-ops, towers over a rail yard and restaurants inheriting a previous tenant's wiring. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Brownstone features and routes

Our row house still has its old dumbwaiter shaft. Can cable use it to climb between floors?

Often it is the best route in the house. A dumbwaiter shaft is a ready-made vertical chase from the cellar or garden level toward the upper floors, and in a brownstone with brick party walls it can save opening any plaster at all. We check first whether the shaft is still open its whole height, whether it has been filled or partly reused for plumbing or ductwork, and whether it is closed off at the top. Cable goes up on its own supports fastened inside the shaft, enters each floor through a small opening into a closet or framed wall, and every floor crossing is sealed with fire-rated sealant, because an open shaft from cellar to roof carries smoke as well as cable. If the shaft still has its car or pulleys and you want to keep them, we work beside them.

Can a cable pass through the wall pocket that holds our parlor's sliding doors?

Usually not inside the pocket itself, but often right beside it. The pocket that receives a sliding door is a hollow space the door travels into, and a cable in it risks being scraped or caught every time the door moves. What we can often use is the framed space at the ends of the pocket, or the wall above the door head, where the door does not travel. Before drilling, the technician checks with an inspection camera how the pocket and the wall around it were framed, and whether the door's track hardware is in the way. If the pocket wall is the only route between front and back parlors, we look instead at the floor below, crossing in the cellar and coming up on the far side.

Could the run to our back bedroom travel behind the baseboard molding instead of inside the wall?

Yes, and in brownstones with brick party walls it is often the neatest answer. One Yelp review we read praised an installer who ran two ethernet cables under molding, and that is a real technique: a baseboard is removed carefully, a channel is cut in the plaster or the cable is laid in the gap at the floor, and the baseboard goes back. It works best on interior walls and on runs along the same floor. It is slower on a landmark-quality interior with original woodwork, because old baseboards can split coming off, so we test one board before committing to the whole room. Where the baseboard cannot come off, a small groove behind the shoe molding or a run through the cellar below is the alternative.

Our house sits at an angle to the street because it follows the 1830s lot lines. Does that change how cable crosses the cellar?

It changes the measurements more than the method. Patch's history of the old grid describes lots laid out in 1833 at about 45 degrees and buildings that still follow those lines, most visibly between Underhill and Washington Avenues. In an angled house the back wall may meet the party walls at a slant, the joists may vary in length, and a run from the front of the cellar to a back room is longer than the house's frontage suggests. We measure the real route instead of the lot depth, which matters for where the equipment sits and how much cable each run needs. Where joists change direction or a wedge-shaped room has a back wall at an angle, we choose crossing points where the framing is simple and the holes can stay small.

Our brownstone is about twenty feet wide and very deep. How many access points does each floor really need?

In most row houses here, one ceiling access point per floor, placed near the middle of the floor's length, covers the floor well, because a narrow house is long rather than wide and the access point sits between the front and back rooms. The exceptions are floors split by a solid brick wall across the house, garden levels half below the sidewalk, and top floors where the bedrooms are at opposite ends. Those sometimes need a second access point or one at each end. The cable is what makes this work: each access point gets its own run back to the switch, so it does not have to relay signal through the floor above. We place them on the survey with you, looking at where you actually sit, work and watch.

Conversions and hidden buildings

We are dividing our row house into three condominium units. How should the cable between the cellar and each unit be split up?

Into a shared part that is small and clearly marked, and three private parts that never cross. We would place one labeled entry panel in a common area of the cellar where providers connect, run a separate bundle from that panel to each unit, and end each bundle at that unit's own small panel or equipment closet. Nothing inside one unit would serve another, so a sale, a renovation or a provider change in one unit never touches the others. Who formally owns the shared panel and the bundles is a question for your condo documents and your attorney, and we give you a drawing that makes it easy for them to describe. With walls open, we also leave an empty pathway with a pull string to each unit for whatever arrives next.

Our building sits behind another one on the old grid and has no frontage on the street. How does an internet line reach us?

Through the property in front of you, with permission in writing. Patch's history mentions buildings reached only by an alley and a doorway through another building, and any service line to them has to cross land or a building that belongs to someone else. In practice the provider needs a route from the street to your wall, which usually follows the passage you already use, in conduit that is protected and as inconspicuous as the setting allows. The two owners should agree in writing who may access that conduit and how it is maintained. Once the line reaches your building, the inside is like any other job. We can design the route and the conduit, but the access agreement is between you and your neighbor.

Co-ops and towers

Can cable follow the radiator pipe up through the floor of our prewar co-op apartment?

We would not use it. The sleeve around a heating riser is the building's, it is there so the pipe can move as it heats and cools, and the pipe itself gets hot enough to damage cable jackets over time. A board would also reasonably object to anything new in a building riser. Inside a co-op apartment, cable belongs in the apartment's own partitions, closets and ceilings, and if it has to cross between floors in a duplex, through a separate small opening the board has approved and that is sealed afterward. Where a radiator sits under a window right where a jack is wanted, we place the jack to one side, clear of the radiator and its cover, and route the cable away from the heat.

Our tower stands on a platform built over the LIRR rail yard. Is drilling the floor for cable allowed?

Assume not, and plan as if the floor were untouchable. Brooklyn Speaks notes that the planned B5 through B10 buildings are slated to stand on platforms over the rail yard between Carlton and Vanderbilt Avenues, and any slab in a building like that is a structural element the building's engineers control. Even in towers on ordinary foundations, management rarely allows coring. That still leaves plenty of room: drywall partitions can be fished, soffits that hide ducts can carry cable, closets often back onto the rooms that need jacks, and slim painted raceway handles the last few feet where nothing else works. We trace the developer's existing cable back to your unit's panel first and send management a written scope before any work.

We are thinking of buying in a Pacific Park building that has not been built yet. What should we ask the developer about wiring before we sign?

Ask four things in writing. Where the unit's network panel or utility closet will be, and what reaches it from the building, including whether fiber is brought to each unit. How many rooms get a data jack, and where. Whether there is any spare conduit or pull string inside the unit for adding cable later. And what the building's rules will be on alterations, especially concrete. The June 2026 plan reported by 6sqft describes seven new buildings and about 5,600 homes, with construction possibly beginning in 2028, so buyers will have time to ask. A unit planned with a jack in every bedroom and spare pathway is far easier to live with than one where every addition means surface raceway on concrete.

Shops and offices on the avenues

Our new restaurant on Vanderbilt moves the host stand and the kitchen pass. Can the old cables that now end a few feet short simply be extended?

Sometimes, but rarely the way people imagine. Twisting or taping a short piece onto the end of a data cable creates a weak point that fails sooner or later, usually during a busy service. The proper way to extend a run is to end the old cable at a fixed connection point in a protected box, a jack or a coupler mounted where it will not be disturbed, and run a new length from there, then certify the whole link. That only makes sense if the old cable tests well and the connection can sit somewhere accessible. In restaurants, where kitchen renovations tend to damage cable in walls, it is often cheaper over a few years to pull new runs to the new positions and remove the old ones. We test before recommending either.

Our office is on the second floor over a Washington Avenue store. Can our cable pass through the store's ceiling to reach the building's cellar?

Only with the agreement of the building owner and, in practice, the store as well. The route from a second-floor office down to a cellar entry often runs through the ceiling of the shop below, which is the landlord's building but the store's leased space. The cleanest answer is a route through building-controlled space, a stair hall, a service shaft or a closet stack, so the store is never involved. If the store's ceiling really is the only path, the owner has to approve it, the store has to agree to the hours, and the cable belongs in a protected pathway so the store's future renovations do not cut you off. We draw the options and you take them to the landlord.

"Prospect Heights is a neighborhood of landmarked brownstones"

"Old houses have hollow walls you can fish a cable through"

Many how-to answers assume framed walls and describe dropping cable from an attic into any wall cavity.

In a Prospect Heights row house, the party walls are brick with plaster on the masonry, and the front and rear walls are masonry too. "A luxury condo comes fully networked"

"A mesh Wi-Fi kit replaces wiring in a townhouse"

Consumer advice often presents mesh units as the modern alternative to cable in a multi-story home.

Mesh units relay signal through the air, and a four-story brownstone with brick party walls, deep plaster, heavy floors and a half-below-grade garden level is a hard place for that signal. Mesh systems work far better when each unit has a cable behind it, which is the job structured cabling does.

FAQ

Prospect Heights Structured Cabling FAQ

Does it matter what color the network cable is?
+

Not for performance, but it helps later. We often use one color for room jacks, another for access points and another for cameras, so anyone opening the cellar panel in a Prospect Heights row house or a shop cabinet can tell at a glance what each bundle serves before reading a single label.

Can the jacks sit at desk height instead of down near the floor?
+

Yes, on framed partitions that is easy, and many people prefer it behind a desk or a media console. On brick party walls we usually keep jacks low and feed them from below, because a high jack on masonry needs a surface box and a visible run. We mark the height with you on the survey.

Can the lights on a bedroom ceiling access point be switched off at night?
+

On most business-grade access points, yes. The status light can usually be dimmed or turned off in the settings while the access point keeps working. We set that up at handover for bedroom and nursery ceilings, and leave the lights on for access points in hallways or offices where they help with troubleshooting.

Does summer heat under the roof hurt the cable or an access point on the top floor?
+

Cable rated for indoor use tolerates the heat of a closed roof space well, though we still keep it away from flues and hot pipes and avoid laying it directly on the roof boards. Access points go on the ceiling of the room below, not in the roof space, because electronics last longer where people are comfortable.

If a cable has to get past a doorway, does it go over the frame or under the floor?
+

Under the floor whenever there is a cellar or an accessible ceiling below, because that leaves the door casing untouched. On upper floors of a brownstone with no access below, the cable can cross through the framed wall above the door head. We avoid cutting into original casings and say in advance which doorways the route passes.

Can my work computer be kept on a network separate from the rest of the household?
+

Yes. The cable to your desk can end on a switch port that belongs to its own network, kept apart from the household's phones, televisions and smart devices, or it can run to a separate router your employer supplies. We label that jack clearly so nobody plugs a game console into it by accident.

Can the job be split into a rough-in visit and a finish visit around our plasterer?
+

Yes, and in a renovation that is usually the best way. We pull and protect the cable while walls are open, leave each end coiled and labeled at its box, and return after the plaster and paint to terminate, test and label. The quote shows both visits so you know what happens at each.

Will the firestop sealant around the openings leave a smell or a visible mark?
+

The sealant has a mild odor for a short time while it cures and then none. It is usually hidden inside a closet, a cellar ceiling or behind a plate, and where it shows, it can be painted once cured. We tell you where each sealed opening is, because it is also there for the next contractor to see.

Do you need our internet provider's account information to do the cabling?
+

No. The cabling runs from the provider's handoff to your rooms and does not involve your account. If you want the router moved to the new equipment spot and set up, we need access to the router itself, and if the provider's own equipment has to move, that is something the provider arranges with you.

Do you staple cable to the joists the way the cable company did?
+

No. Staples can crush or kink network cable and hurt its performance. We support cable on straps, hooks or loose ties fastened beside it, so it is held without being squeezed, and we remove old stapled runs along the cellar ceiling when they are part of the job.

Is it better to run every jack back to the cellar, or put a small switch on each floor?
+

In most Prospect Heights houses we run every jack back to one place, because it keeps the system simple and every port visible on one panel. A small switch on a floor makes sense when the vertical route can carry only one or two cables, as it sometimes can in a brownstone with no closet stack, and we explain that trade-off on the drawing.
