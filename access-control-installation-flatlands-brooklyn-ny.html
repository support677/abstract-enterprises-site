The Neighborhood

Why Flatlands Properties Need Access Control

Flatlands is mostly houses with a ribbon of mid-size apartment buildings along its busiest roads, and that mix shapes every door job here. The usual description puts its edges at Nostrand Avenue on the west, the Bay Ridge Branch rail line on the north, Ralph Avenue on the east and Avenue T on the south, with blocks of brick and frame one- and two-family houses built mostly between the 1920s and the 1950s. Avenues J, K and L run through it as residential streets, while Flatbush, Utica and Ralph Avenues, Avenue N and Kings Highway carry the shops. There is no subway station inside the neighborhood; people ride buses or walk to the 2 and 5 at Flatbush and Nostrand Avenues. That matters more than it sounds: residents come home on foot from a bus stop, often after dark, and the door at the end of that walk is the one they want to trust.

The apartment buildings are where we spend most of our time. Building records for the stretch of Kings Highway near the old Dutch church read almost like a set: 3319 Kings Highway, a six-story rental with 72 apartments, built in 1951; 4149 Kings Highway, 102 apartments, built in 1952; 4011 Kings Highway, between East 40th and East 41st Streets, a six-story rental with 96 apartments, built in 1959. On Nostrand Avenue between Avenue J and Avenue K, 2425 Nostrand is a six-story elevator building with 178 apartments, built in 1955. None of the listings we read mention a doorman. These are buildings with a great many households behind each door, run by a super and a management office, and in our experience a building of this type typically has one or two street doors, a rear or yard door, a cellar, a laundry and a mail area. Every one of the four was built before 1968.

That date is not trivia. Under section 50-a of the state Multiple Dwelling Law, the street entrance of a class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have a self-closing, self-locking door, and buildings with eight or more apartments must have an intercom with voice and a remote door release. For older buildings, like the postwar rentals on Kings Highway, the same equipment becomes required when a majority of tenants ask for it in writing. Either way, once a building has a locking door and an intercom, it has to keep them working; Habitat Magazine’s 2024 summary notes that New York courts treat a working intercom as an essential service under the warranty of habitability. A fob reader does not replace that intercom. It sits alongside it, and the two have to be planned together.

The neighborhood also lives next door to one of the city’s largest experiments in electronic entry. NYCHA’s Glenwood Houses, bounded by Ralph Avenue, East 56th Street, Farragut Road and Glenwood Road, is 20 six-story buildings with 1,186 apartments that opened in 1950. NYCHA told the City Council in 2023 that it had installed “layered access controls – new entrance intercoms, key fobs, and doors” at more than 400 buildings across 80 developments, and described keeping those doors repaired as costly and time-consuming. A year earlier the City Comptroller’s field visits to 262 NYCHA developments found 40.1 percent of building entrance door locks and 28.5 percent of rear or side door locks broken. Private owners on the blocks around Glenwood see the same lesson on a smaller scale: an electronic lock on a door that is slammed, propped and kicked all day fails like any other lock unless the door and closer are built for it.

The houses ask different questions. Many are semi-detached or attached, so a side door shares a wall or a driveway with the house next door; many detached houses have a driveway and a garage at the back, which makes the side door the family’s real front door. Two-family houses are common, often with an owner on one floor and relatives or a tenant on the other. The concern we hear most from homeowners is packages and visitors rather than break-ins: News 12 reported in April 2023 that a porch thief in Flatlands took the packages holding toys and decorations for a two-year-old’s birthday party. A keypad does not stop someone lifting a box from a stoop, but a station that lets you answer the door from your phone, and a code you can give a neighbor for one afternoon, changes where packages end up.

Business owners here talk about counters and late hours. News 12 reported that around 11 p.m. on a Wednesday in June 2026, three people robbed a Popeyes in Flatlands; one climbed over the counter and shoved an 18-year-old worker, and about $1,200 was taken. Access control cannot stop a robbery in progress and we will not pretend it can. What it can do is control the doors that should never be open to the public: the door behind the counter, the back door to the lot, the cellar stair, and the customer door once the last customer leaves. And in the industrial zone off Flatlands Avenue, where a city planning survey counted 128 open industrial sites, most of them vehicle and material storage, auto salvage and carting, the doors are gates, and the problem is usually that too many people have the padlock combination.

Street Door Won’t Latch, Reader Beeping Red, Gate Code Leaked: Repair in Flatlands

A lot of Flatlands calls are not about new systems at all. A street door on a Kings Highway building that has to be pulled shut by hand, a keypad on a house side door that has stopped lighting up, a fob reader that beeps red for everyone after a storm, or a yard gate whose code has been passed around: ring (347) 934-8335 and describe what the door is doing, or send a short video. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. A large share of these faults come down to a door that has dropped on its hinges so the latch misses the strike, a closer that has lost its oil, a backup battery that has run flat, or a power supply sharing a circuit with something else in the cellar. Those are usually fixed on the first visit. We also take over systems that other companies installed, and if a reader or controller is too old to be worth repairing, we say so before you pay for parts.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Flatlands’s Architecture

A reader is the cheapest part of a controlled door. The door, the frame, the wall it sits in and the route back to a controller decide most of the price and most of how long the system lasts. These are the six kinds of property we meet most often between Nostrand and Ralph Avenues.

Postwar six-story rentals

Brick buildings from the early and late 1950s on Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue, 70 to nearly 180 apartments, elevator, super, no doorman. Heavy original street doors in steel or aluminum frames, a vestibule, a buzzer riser that has been patched for decades, and a cellar that makes cable routes easy.

Semi-detached and attached brick houses

Pairs and rows of brick houses with a party wall on one side, a stoop at the front and a shared or side driveway. The side door is often the everyday door, and the controller has to stay on the owner’s side of the party wall.

Detached frame houses and bungalows

Wood-frame houses with siding, a front porch that is often enclosed, a driveway and a garage behind. Readers mount on a block fixed to the frame, never on the siding alone, and cable usually reaches the cellar through the floor below the door.

Two-family houses

An owner on one floor and relatives or a tenant on the other, sometimes with their own entrance. A shared street door needs a button per household; separate entrances need separate credentials and separate power decisions.

Mixed-use buildings on the shopping streets

Stores at street level with apartments above on Kings Highway, Flatbush, Utica and Ralph Avenues and Avenue N. The store door, the tenants’ street door and any shared cellar each need their own schedule and their own administrator.

Yards and low industrial buildings

Fenced lots and one-story buildings in the Flatlands/Fairfield zone, many of them storage, salvage and carting yards. Gates on steel posts, roll-up doors with a personnel door beside them, and offices that are sometimes a trailer.

What the walls and doors mean for the install

Postwar street doors are heavy, and seventy years of tenants and deliveries have usually bent something. The most common finding on a Kings Highway building is a frame worn where the old latch has hammered it, a closer that slams or barely moves, and hinges that have dropped so the door rubs at the bottom. An electric strike needs the latch to meet it at the same spot every time, so we straighten the door, set the closer and fit a strike rated for the door’s weight before any reader goes up. Where the frame is filled with grout behind the strike, we cut it out cleanly rather than enlarging the opening by force.

Brick house fronts are the easy case. Stations and keypads are fixed into the mortar joints, not the face of the brick, so a later change leaves a joint to repoint rather than a broken brick, and cable reaches the cellar through a small sleeve. Frame houses take more care: siding hides the wood behind it, and a reader screwed through siding alone will loosen and let water in. We fix a mounting block to the framing, flash it, and seal the cable entry from above. On semi-detached houses we look for the party wall before drilling anything, and keep controllers, power supplies and cable on the owner’s side.

Yard gates are their own trade. A chain-link gate flexes; a steel slide gate weighs hundreds of pounds and moves on a track that fills with grit. A lock that works on a door will not survive either, so gates get gate locks or operator locks made for them, and readers stand on their own posts where a driver can reach them without touching the gate.

Seventy to 178 Households Behind One Door: The 1950s Rentals on Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue

The postwar buildings are the largest single group of doors in Flatlands, and they share a set of problems that come from being seventy years old and full. The street door is opened hundreds of times a day. Keys have been cut for every tenant since the building opened, and for many of their relatives, home attendants and contractors. The buzzer plate has been repaired one button at a time. And the building is old enough that it predates the 1968 cutoff in the Multiple Dwelling Law, so its owner may assume, wrongly, that none of the door rules apply.

A fob system answers the key problem directly. When a tenant moves out, their fobs stop working that day instead of the lock being changed and new keys handed to everyone. When a fob is lost, it is cancelled and replaced. The super’s ring of keys for the cellar, the boiler room and the roof can shrink to one credential that opens only what the super needs. Habitat Magazine’s account of a 32-door co-op system in Manhattan describes exactly this reasoning: the board was tired of re-keying every resident each time a key went missing, and wanted to limit doors such as the bike room to the people who used them.

But a large rental is not a co-op, and the rules for switching are stricter. In rent-stabilized buildings the state housing agency, DHCR, treats the way tenants get into the building as a required service. Brick Underground’s 2018 summary of DHCR rulings reported that owners had to give fobs free to every lawful occupant, could not cap the number of fobs per apartment, could charge only a capped fee for a lost one, and could not collect new identity information to issue them. In a 2019 case at 951 Carroll Street in Brooklyn, DHCR approved an owner’s switch from metal keys to fobs on conditions, after tenants complained that the system often failed and HPD had issued violations; the owner had kept the mechanical locks working until the approval came. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law adds a written privacy policy, tenant consent and a 90-day limit on keeping authentication data. We do not give legal advice, but we build every Flatlands rental job so that it can meet those conditions: free fobs in the numbers the owner chooses, a key cylinder that still opens the street door, and logs set to the shortest retention the owner’s policy allows.

Then there is the rollout itself, which is where large buildings go wrong. CBS New York reported in April 2025 that when NYCHA switched Queensbridge Houses from keys to fobs, doors jammed, keys no longer worked and neither did some fobs, residents taped doors open, and the resident association counted 78 door complaints in one month on one side of the complex. Council Member Julie Won put it plainly: “There are seniors, there are young adults, there are children who are locked out of their own homes.” The cure is unglamorous. Fix the door before the reader goes on it. Keep the old key working until every apartment has collected its fobs. Issue fobs at times working tenants can actually come, including an evening and a weekend morning. And give the super a way to issue a fob on the spot, so that a tenant who missed the handout is not standing outside at night.

What we usually propose for a 1950s Flatlands rental

A first phase covers the street door, the vestibule inner door if there is one, and the rear or yard door, on one controller in the cellar with a battery that keeps the doors working for hours in an outage. The intercom stays, or is replaced with a video panel that has the reader built in. Encrypted fobs replace keys for tenants, and the 125 kHz kind that can be copied with an inexpensive device is not used. A second phase adds the laundry, the storage area and a package room, which is where most of the day-to-day theft and arguments happen. The roof door stays self-closing and openable from inside without a key, as section 50-a requires; at most it gets a contact that tells the super when it has been opened.

Four in Ten Broken: What the Doors Around Glenwood Houses Teach a Private Owner

Glenwood Houses, at the northeast corner of the neighborhood, is the largest group of controlled doors in Flatlands, and it belongs to NYCHA, not to anyone who hires us. We do not work on NYCHA property. We mention it because private owners on the surrounding blocks often point to the public-housing fobs and ask whether they should get the same thing, and because the city’s own reports are the clearest public record anywhere of how electronic entry fails in buildings that look a lot like the postwar rentals nearby.

The Comptroller’s 2022 field visits did not find that fobs fail. They found that doors fail: 40.1 percent of entrance door locks and 28.5 percent of rear and side door locks were broken across the 262 developments visited. NYCHA’s chief operating officer, Eva Trimble, told the Council in 2023 that the agency had to issue protocols for staff to monitor and promptly repair damaged doors, called the work costly and time-consuming, and said the agency notifies the NYPD when a door is not working. A reader on a door that will not close is a decoration.

For a private building of seventy or a hundred apartments, the practical lessons are three. First, spend on the door: a closer sized for the door’s weight and traffic, hinges that hold it square, and a strike rated for abuse. Second, make a propped or forced door visible: a door contact that alerts the super when the street door has stood open for more than a minute or two does more for security than any credential. Third, plan the repair before the break. Keep a spare reader and strike on the shelf, know who is called when the door fails at night, and make sure the key cylinder still works, so that a failed lock means a tenant uses a key, not that the door is taped open.

Owners also ask about cut-through traffic, because the blocks around a large campus carry a lot of foot traffic toward the buses on Ralph Avenue and Flatbush Avenue. A building’s yard gate or side passage can be put on fobs, but any gate or door that is also part of a way out of the building in a fire has to open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. We design those gates to lock against people coming in and open freely for people going out.

Driveway Doors and Party Walls: Access Control for Flatlands Houses

Most of Flatlands lives in houses, and most house calls start with a simple wish: no more keys under the mat, no more copies with relatives who moved away, and a way to let someone in without driving home. The postwar building boom left the neighborhood full of semi-detached brick houses and bungalows, and a lot of older one- and two-family houses on the side streets off Avenues J, K and L. Many have a driveway and a garage, so the door the family uses every day is the side door at the top of a few steps off the driveway, not the formal front door.

That changes the plan. The side door gets the keypad or reader, because that is where people arrive with groceries and children. The front door gets a video station or simply stays on its key, because visitors and deliveries come there. Where the side door faces a shared driveway, the keypad and any camera on the station are angled along the owner’s side, not toward the neighbor’s door. And on a semi-detached house, everything with power, the controller, the power supply and the battery, stays in the owner’s half of the cellar, away from the party wall, so that a future dispute or renovation next door never touches it.

Two-family houses add a second household. If both floors share a stoop door, each household gets its own call button and its own codes, and neither can see the other’s entries. If the upstairs or downstairs unit has its own entrance, that door is the tenant’s front door: the tenant holds the credentials, the owner keeps an emergency method that is written into the lease, and the door always opens from inside by hand. In a two-family, the question of whose electric meter runs the tenant’s lock should be settled in writing before the installation, not after the first bill.

Wireless smart locks are fine for some houses, and we say so. A battery deadbolt on an interior door to a home office, or on a back door that is used twice a week, needs nothing more. For the door that the whole family uses every day, we prefer a wired strike with a battery-backed controller: it relocks every time the door closes, it does not depend on the home Wi-Fi to open, and it keeps working through a power cut long enough for everyone to get in.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Flatlands

Flatlands shops line a handful of roads: Kings Highway through the middle of the neighborhood, Flatbush Avenue running south toward Kings Plaza on the Marine Park and Mill Basin border, Utica and Ralph Avenues, Avenue N, and Flatlands Avenue on its way to Canarsie. Forgotten New York describes Flatlands Avenue as blocks of detached homes broken by an occasional shopping strip and apartment building, which is a fair description of most of these roads. North of Flatlands Avenue toward the rail line sits the Flatlands/Fairfield Industrial Business Zone. Each strip gives a door job its own shape.

Kings Highway ground floors

Shops and offices in the ground floors of the postwar apartment buildings, and in smaller mixed-use buildings between them. The common job is a store door and a tenants’ street door on the same façade that must never share a schedule or an administrator.

Flatbush Avenue to Kings Plaza

Storefronts, food counters and service businesses on the approach to the indoor mall. Counter doors, back doors to parking lots and staff schedules that end at closing. Stores inside the mall itself follow the mall’s rules; see the FAQ below.

Utica Avenue

A commercial edge of the neighborhood with storefront retail and offices. Back-room readers, buzz-in front doors for businesses that see customers by appointment, and a release button at the front desk.

Ralph Avenue

The eastern edge, beside Glenwood Houses, with shops and community spaces. Side doors that open only for scheduled programs, and a door contact on any rear exit that staff need to know about.

Avenue N and Flatlands Avenue

Shorter shopping strips set among houses. Small businesses with one front door, one back door and a handful of staff, where a single controlled back door and individual codes are usually the whole job.

The Flatlands/Fairfield zone

Yards and low buildings used for vehicle and material storage, auto salvage, carting and a few concrete operations, by the city’s own survey. Gates, walk gates, roll-ups with personnel doors, and offices that are sometimes trailers.

Inside the industrial zone

NYC Planning’s survey of open industrial uses in and around the Flatlands/Fairfield zone gives an unusually exact picture of who uses its gates: 128 sites on 57.17 acres, averaging less than half an acre each. Fifty stored operable vehicles and usable materials, thirty-two held inoperable vehicles or discarded materials long term, twenty-two were auto dismantling or salvage yards, six were commercial carting, and the rest a mix of scrap, concrete and cement, debris transfer and recycling. The same map shows parts of the area inside the FEMA 100-year floodplain and the area flooded by Hurricane Sandy.

Those uses mean a lot of gates, a lot of drivers and a lot of padlock combinations. A long-running yard usually has keys cut for employees who left years ago, a gate remote in a truck that was sold, and a code written on the inside of the office door. The fix is individual credentials: each person gets a card or a code, each truck can get a tag, and each one has a schedule and can be cancelled the day the person leaves. Where a yard is in the flood area, the controller and battery go high inside the building, not in a box at the foot of the gate post, and cable runs in sealed conduit. A yard gate that opens to the street is also a vehicle safety device, so motorized gates get the photo eyes and edges their standard requires; we do not wire a reader to an old operator that has none.

Rules that apply to Flatlands business doors

  • Exits stay exits. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a way out must release when its sensor detects someone approaching from inside, when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, with a manual “push to exit” button near the door that cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock also needs a release built into the door hardware itself.
  • Listed equipment. Access controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294; we fit listed parts and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above stores. Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law covers the tenants’ street door in a class A building: self-closing and self-locking in buildings built or converted after 1968, and on a written majority request in older ones, with an intercom where there are eight or more apartments.
  • Tenant data. Where fobs, phone apps or other smart access serve apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires a written policy, consent, collection of only what the system needs, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install or service security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
  • Tenant locks. Section 51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law lets a residential tenant add their own lock to their apartment door, giving the owner a key on request; an access system on the street door does not change that.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Flatlands: Questions Answered

Postwar apartment buildings on Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue

Our Kings Highway building went up in the early 1950s and still has its original steel-and-glass street door. Does the door have to be replaced before a reader can go on it?

Usually not. Most original street doors in these buildings are sound slabs hung on worn hardware, and a good door with new hinges or pivots, a properly sized closer and a heavy-duty strike will outlast a cheap new door. We check three things on the visit: whether the frame is straight and solid where the strike will go, whether the door closes and latches by itself from fully open, and whether the glass and its stops are secure enough to take the jolt of the latch catching every few minutes all day.

If the frame has been chewed by decades of lock changes, or the door has sagged so far that it drags on the saddle, the repair is quoted separately before any electronics. Replacing the door outright only makes sense when it is cracked, rusted through or no longer closes even with new hardware, and in that case the new door is ordered with the strike preparation already in the frame.

Our 178-apartment building has one super on site and a management office somewhere else. Who should hold the master login, and who should only be able to hand out fobs?

Split the roles. The managing agent, or the owner, holds the administrator account that can change door schedules, add administrators and set how long records are kept. The super gets a narrower account that can issue a fob to a named tenant, cancel a lost one and see whether a door is open, but cannot change schedules or read the full history. A second administrator at the management office covers holidays and sick days.

This protects the super as much as the tenants. When a tenant disputes when a fob was cancelled, or why a door was unlocked during a repair, a clear division of who can do what, with every change logged under a name, ends the argument quickly.

When the power supply in our cellar failed last winter, the intercom and the new fob reader went dark together. Should the two systems share power at all?

No. They can share a cable path and a wall, but each should have its own power supply, its own battery and preferably its own circuit, so one failure never takes out both. The intercom is the tenants’ legal way to let a visitor in, and the reader is their way in; losing both at once is the situation that leads to doors being propped open.

We also put the controller’s battery on a size that rides out a typical outage for the street door, and we set the lock so that when its own power is gone the door can still be opened with the key cylinder from outside and the handle from inside. On the handover sheet the super gets the location of both supplies and the breaker for each.

We manage two buildings on Kings Highway a few blocks apart. Can the porter carry one fob for both lobbies while each building’s tenants open only their own?

Yes. Both buildings go on one management account as separate sites, each with its own doors and its own tenant list. Staff who work at both, such as a porter, a handyman or the super, get a credential that is valid at both sites on their working hours, while tenants’ fobs are valid only at their home building.

Keep the sites separate in everything else. Each building should have its own privacy policy, its own retention setting and its own list of administrators, because a tenant in one building has no business seeing records from the other.

If tenants in our stabilized Nostrand Avenue building file a service-reduction complaint about the new fobs, what is the agency likely to look at?

We cannot predict a ruling, and you should talk to your attorney. The published record gives an idea of what has mattered. In the 2019 DHCR review for 951 Carroll Street, tenants said the fob system often failed and that HPD had issued violations; the agency still approved the switch on conditions, noted that the owner had kept the mechanical locks working until approval, and said tenants could file service-reduction complaints if problems continued. Earlier DHCR rulings summarized by Brick Underground looked at whether fobs were free to every lawful occupant and whether new identity information was demanded.

What we can control is reliability and records. A door that latches every time, a battery that carries the door through an outage, a working key cylinder and a log of every repair visit are the facts an owner wants to be able to show.

Living beside Glenwood Houses and other large campuses

The Comptroller found four in ten NYCHA entrance locks broken in 2022. What keeps a private building’s reader door from ending up the same way?

The same thing that keeps any door working: hardware rated for the traffic and someone who notices when it fails. The 40.1 percent figure was about broken door locks, not failed fobs, which tells you where the money should go. We size the closer and strike for a door that opens hundreds of times a day, fit a door contact so a door held or forced open sends an alert, and leave a spare reader and strike with the super.

The other half is a repair plan agreed in advance: who is called when the street door fails at night, how fast they come, and what tenants do in the meantime, which should be to use their key, not to wedge the door. A building that has those answers written down rarely ends up with a taped latch.

People cut through our small building’s rear yard on the way to the bus. Can the yard gate lock against them without blocking our tenants’ way out?

Yes, if the gate is designed as an exit first. If the yard is part of a path tenants would use to leave the building in a fire, the gate must open from the yard side with a simple push or handle, no key or code, and it must not lock tenants in when the power fails. From the street side it can be locked and opened only by fob or code.

The usual hardware is a gate lock with free exit from inside, a self-closing hinge so the gate does not stand open, and a reader on a post outside. A contact can tell the super when the gate has been left open. If the gate is not part of a way out, there is more freedom, but we still prefer free exit on any gate people walk through.

Houses on the residential avenues and side streets

Our semi-detached brick house off Avenue K shares a party wall with the house next door. Can the controller and every cable stay entirely on our side?

They should, and on almost every semi-detached house they can. The controller and power supply go on a cellar wall away from the party wall, the cable runs along your own joists to a point under the door, and it comes up through the floor or a small sleeve in your own front or side wall. Nothing is fixed into or drilled through the shared wall.

If the only practical route crosses the party wall, for example on an attached house with no cellar under the door, we stop and show you the alternatives, such as a neat surface raceway inside your own hallway, before drilling. A neighbor should never find our cable on their side.

The front porch on our Flatlands bungalow is enclosed with a glass-and-aluminum door. Should the keypad go on the porch door or on the house door behind it?

Usually on the house door. A light aluminum porch door flexes, its frame is too thin for a strike, and the porch is not heated, so it is the house door that actually keeps people out. The porch door can stay on a simple latch, or take a small video station on the porch wall so visitors are seen before anyone opens anything.

There is one good reason to control the porch door instead: if deliveries are left on the porch and you want the porch itself locked. In that case the porch door needs a sturdier frame or a lock designed for storm doors, and we quote it as a second door rather than squeezing a strike into aluminum.

Relatives stay with us for a month or two at a time. Can each visitor get a code that stops working on the day they fly home?

Yes. Each visitor gets their own code or fob with a start date and an end date, and it stops working at midnight on the end date without anyone having to remember. If a stay is extended, the end date is moved in the app in a few seconds.

Keep them as separate users rather than sharing a family code. When a code is shared, it lives on after the guest leaves; when it is personal, it ends cleanly, and the record shows whose code opened the door if a question ever comes up.

Counters, yards and the industrial zone

After the June robbery at a Popeyes in Flatlands, our late-night takeout counter wants to lock the customer door from behind the counter. Can one button do that and still let people out?

Yes. A button at the register can switch the customer door from free entry to locked, so that from then on people outside are let in only when staff release the door, while anyone inside can always leave with one push on the bar or handle. The door also locks itself at the scheduled closing time if nobody presses the button.

Be clear about what this does. It does not stop a robbery that starts while the door is open, and staff should never confront anyone. It does let a crew close the room to new arrivals once it empties late at night, keeps the door behind the counter locked to anyone but staff, and makes the back door to the lot a staff-only door that records who opened it.

Our salvage yard in the Flatlands/Fairfield zone lets parts buyers walk in on weekday mornings. Can their walk gate follow a schedule while the truck gate stays on cards?

Yes. The walk gate and the vehicle gate are separate doors on one controller. The walk gate can stand unlocked during posted buying hours and lock itself afterward, with staff codes still working outside those hours. The vehicle gate stays on cards or tags at all times, so a buyer’s car never comes in on a staff schedule.

Add a contact on each gate and an alert if either is open after the yard closes. In a yard that holds parts and vehicles, the most common problem is not a forced gate but one that was left open by the last person out.

The only building on our concrete yard is an office trailer at the back of the lot. Where does the controller go?

Inside the trailer, mounted high on a wall in a locked enclosure, with cable to the gate run underground in conduit or overhead on a messenger wire. A trailer works well as long as it has steady power; if the power to the trailer is switched off at night, the controller needs a battery big enough to keep the gate working until morning, or its own permanent feed.

Dust is the bigger enemy on a concrete or aggregate site. We use sealed enclosures for the controller and the reader, and keypads with sealed buttons, because grit wears out an ordinary keypad within months.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Flatlands

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. We do not attribute any particular claim or figure to any one of them. Each is set against what a Flatlands door actually needs.

“Fobs fix an insecure building.”

This is the most common assumption, and the city’s own records show why it is incomplete. The Comptroller’s 2022 visits to 262 NYCHA developments found four in ten entrance door locks broken, even as the authority was putting fobs and new intercoms on hundreds of its buildings.

A fob decides who may open a door. It does nothing if the door does not close, if the closer has failed, or if someone has taped the latch. In a seventy-year-old Kings Highway building those mechanical faults are the first thing we find.

So the order is door, closer and strike first, then a contact that reports a propped door, then credentials. A building that skips the first two steps buys a reader that watches the door stand open.

“Older buildings are exempt from the door rules.”

Answers that skim the Multiple Dwelling Law often stop at the 1968 date in section 50-a and conclude that a building from the 1950s has no obligations at its street door. That misses the second half of the section.

For pre-1968 class A buildings, a self-locking door and, where there are eight or more apartments, an intercom become required when a majority of tenants ask in writing. And once a building has a working intercom, courts have treated it as an essential service, as Habitat Magazine’s 2024 summary explains; attorney Nancy Kourland put it as “the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom.”

For the postwar rentals on Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue, which were all built between 1951 and 1959, the safe planning assumption is that the street door and intercom have to work, and that any fob system has to be added without disturbing either.

“Phone-only entry is the modern way.”

App-based entry is convenient, and many tenants like it. But a door that opens only with a phone fails every time a phone dies, a tenant has no smartphone, or the building’s internet goes down.

Brick Underground published a tenant’s complaint about exactly that: a fob system that failed when the internet went out or it rained, and a landlord who refused a backup. The attorneys it asked said tenants have a right to get into their homes at all times and that a backup such as a conventional lock is needed.

We never install a door that opens only by phone. Every person gets a second way in, a fob or a code, the controller keeps working without the internet, and a key cylinder stays as the last resort.

“Facial recognition is the next step up from fobs.”

Product pages often present face scanning as an upgrade. In Brooklyn rental housing it has been a fight. At Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville, about 700 apartments, more than 130 residents objected when the owner sought to replace its fob system with facial recognition; tenant Tasliym Francis said, “It’s my biometrics, it’s my identity and we don’t want that in anyone’s hands.”

The Department of Buildings said then that facial recognition was not banned but could only be used alongside a key-based system, and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law now puts biometric data under its consent, minimum-collection and retention rules.

For a Flatlands rental, the practical answer is that encrypted fobs and codes already solve the copied-key problem without the legal exposure. We do not recommend biometric readers for residential street doors.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a keypad lock off the shelf and call it a door. That is the price of one part, and it is the right price only for a battery lock on a light interior door.

A controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, cable, a programmed controller with battery backup and ten fobs runs $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. A heavy postwar street door with a worn frame and a closer that needs replacing sits toward the top of that range, and the door repair is quoted separately when it is needed.

A whole six-story building with street, rear, cellar, laundry and package doors runs $15,000 to $40,000. The figures are published below so you can compare them with any quote you receive.

“Houses don’t need access control, only a smart lock.”

Generic advice treats access control as an apartment and office product. Most Flatlands doors belong to houses, many of them shared by two households or two generations.

A battery smart lock is a reasonable choice for a lightly used door. On the side door a whole family uses every day, a wired strike relocks every time it closes, does not depend on the home Wi-Fi, and carries through a power cut. In a two-family house it also lets each household keep its own users and its own administrator.

We will tell you honestly when a battery lock is enough. It often is for a back door or a home office. It rarely is for the door the family comes home through.

“A yard just needs a better padlock.”

For a small lot with two people and one key, perhaps. The yards in the Flatlands/Fairfield zone are usually busier: the city’s survey found storage, salvage and carting operations dominating 128 sites, which means drivers, buyers and staff coming and going on different hours.

A padlock has one combination, and everyone knows it within a month. It cannot be changed for one person, cannot follow a schedule, and cannot tell you who opened the gate on a Sunday.

A reader or keypad on a post, a gate lock made for the gate, and a contact that reports the gate left open give a yard individual credentials, schedules and a record, which is what an owner actually needs when something goes missing.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Let the Street Door Go and Watch It Close

This describes how we approach a large postwar building, not a particular customer. Before we open a catalog, we stand at the street door with the super and do one test: open it all the way and let go. On a door that has carried a building for seventy years, one of three things usually happens. It slams, because the closer has been turned up to make it latch; it stops a few inches short, because the hinges have dropped or the closer is spent; or it closes but the latch rides over the strike and does not catch. Any of those means a reader would be wasted money until it is fixed.

Then we count what opens the door today. The super’s ring, the management office’s copies, the keys tenants have handed to relatives and attendants over the years, and any fobs from an earlier attempt. That number, set against the number of apartments, tells everyone in the room why individual credentials matter. Only then do we go down to the cellar, find the intercom riser and the panel, and pick a wall for the controller away from both the boiler and the intercom’s own power supply.

+

Each household should administer its own people. Does access control do anything for a Flatlands storefront once the roll-down gate is down?
+

The roll-down gate protects the glass at night, and access control protects the doors people use when the gate is up, so they do different jobs. Where a store has a back door or a cellar door that stays usable after the front gate comes down, a reader and a door contact on that door is what tells you if anyone used it overnight. A camera aimed at the gate and the back door covers the rest, and we can quote both together.

What happens to the old buzzer plate and wiring when a Flatlands building gets a new panel?
+

We remove the old plate, patch or cover the opening neatly, and take the old equipment away. The old riser cable is tested before anything is decided: where it is sound, it is often reused for the new panel or the apartment monitors, which saves opening walls. Where it is damaged or too thin for the new system, new cable is run and the old wire is cut back and labeled so nobody mistakes it for a live circuit.
