The Neighborhood

Why Remsen Village Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Remsen Village is the eastern end of East Flatbush, a name that came into use in the mid-1990s for blocks that were farmland in old Flatlands until the subway reached Nostrand Avenue in the 1920s. Brownstoner's 2013 piece on East 91st Street describes it as built mainly between the 1920s and the 1940s, and the housing still reads that way: brick rows, semi-detached and detached two-families with small front yards, and garden apartment buildings. What matters for a buzzer is not the history itself but what it left behind at the doors. Almost every property here was built for one or two families who answered the door in person, and many of them now hold more households, more deliveries and more visitors than anyone planned for.

The apartment buildings tell the sharper story. Along Remsen Avenue there are low brick buildings from the mid-1960s with ten to twenty apartments behind one street door. 1106 Remsen Avenue, toward the Canarsie end of the avenue, went up in 1965 with two floors and 18 units, and LucidRents lists "Door/Lock" as its leading HPD violation category, with more than a hundred entries over about seven years, along with 59 "DOOR/WINDOW" complaints to 311. 431 Remsen Avenue, which openigloo files under Remsen Village, is an 11-unit, three-floor building of the same year. These are not grand lobbies with a doorman. They are a glass-and-metal door, a short vestibule, a panel of buttons and a long hall, and when the door does not latch, the buzzer is the only thing that looks like security.

That is why our first job on most building calls here is not the panel. A buzzer can only control a door that closes and locks by itself. HPD treats a door that fails to "swing closed and latch by themselves after being opened" as a Class C, immediately hazardous condition, and gives the owner 14 days to fix it. A building at 525 Rockaway Parkway, the avenue Brownstoner names as one of the neighborhood's borders, received exactly that kind of violation on March 27, 2026, for self-closing doors with missing or defective hinges at the entrance. In a building like that, a new door station on a door that drifts open is money spent on the wrong part.

The neighbors' experience points the same way. In February 2025, News 12 reported from Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street, in East Flatbush, where resident Patricia Walters listed her complaints as "everything the unlocked doors, the leakage," and the reporters entered several buildings without a key. A year later the station reported that locks in the buildings had not been working. Those are large buildings under a single management company, but the lesson is the same for a ten-unit walk-up: a door that opens to anyone who pulls it makes every apartment's buzzer pointless.

Ownership is changing too, and that touches the entry system. In April 2026 News 12 visited a four-story rent-stabilized building on Linden Boulevard in East Flatbush whose registered owner had changed in December 2025, where tenants said calls to the new company went unanswered and the building carried more than 900 HPD violations. When a building changes hands, nobody may know where the buzzer's power supply is, which pair goes to which apartment or who holds the codes for a newer panel. We document all three during an installation for exactly that reason.

Repair or Replace in Remsen Village

Need Repair in Remsen Village? Decide Whether the Door, the Wire or the System Has Failed

Many of the calls we get about "a new buzzer" in Remsen Village are really about a building or house that already has one. The panel at the street door has buttons that do nothing for half the apartments, the handset in one unit hums all night, or the release clicks and the door stays put. Before we recommend a new system, we work out which of three things has actually failed, because each one leads to a very different price.

The first possibility is the door. If the closer, hinges or latch no longer bring the street door shut and locked, no amount of electronics will secure it, and the right first step is door work, often a closer, a hinge or a strike keeper that has worn out of line. The second is the wiring or the power: a transformer that has failed in the basement, a pair cut during an apartment renovation, a corroded connection at the panel. Those are repairs, and our Brooklyn door buzzer installers price them as repairs. The third is the system itself: handsets that can no longer be matched, a panel with failed buttons that cannot be replaced individually, or a building that now needs functions the old system never had.

ButterflyMX's guide for small buildings puts the rule plainly: "Do not replace a system based on age alone." We agree. A 1965 audio system whose cable still tests clean can sometimes take new stations and a new panel on the old pairs for much less than a full replacement. A system that fails in a new apartment every few months, on cable that meters badly, is usually cheaper to replace than to keep chasing. We tell you which you have after testing, not before.

Low 1960s walk-ups: one street door, a vestibule and a hall that runs the length of the lot

The buildings along Remsen Avenue from 1965 are wide and low rather than tall. 1106 Remsen Avenue fits 18 units into two floors; 431 Remsen Avenue fits 11 into three. That shape changes the cable work. Instead of a riser climbing six or seven floors, the cable runs a long way horizontally, usually along the basement ceiling and then up into each line of apartments, and the apartments at the far end of the building can be as far from the panel as the top floor of a much taller building. When an old system fails in "the back apartments first," distance and a tired splice in the basement are usually the reason.

The mistake here is pricing the job from the apartment count alone. An 18-unit building with a short, dry basement and clean pairs is a modest job; the same count spread along a long building with a basement ceiling that has been boxed in during repairs is a much bigger one. We walk the basement end to end before quoting, meter every pair we can reach, and tell the owner which apartments will need new cable. On a low building, new cable usually runs along the basement rather than through the halls, which keeps the work out of tenants' sight.

Semi-detached one-families: a porch door, a driveway door and a hatch to the basement

The 1920 semi-detached house on Lenox Road is a good picture of the typical one-family job here. Visitors use the porch door; the family often comes and goes by the driveway; and the basement has its own way in through steel Bilco doors. The household wants to hear the door from the back of the house and the bedrooms upstairs, see who is there and let a relative or a courier in without walking to the front. A single-button station at the porch door, a strike in the frame, stations in the kitchen and upstairs, and a keypad at the side door for the family covers it.

The mistake is trying to put every door on the buzzer. A Bilco hatch is not a visitor's entrance and cannot take an electric strike in any sensible way; the door that matters is the one at the bottom of the basement stair, inside the hatch, and it usually just needs a good lock. We plan the doors people actually use and leave the rest locked and free to open from inside.

Two-families with a garden rental: two households, one stoop and sometimes two doors

The 1945 two-family on East 55th Street, an owner's duplex over a garden-level rental, is the arrangement we meet most often on the side streets. Sometimes both homes share the door at the top of the stoop; sometimes the garden unit has its own door at ground level. Either way the plan is the same: each household gets its own button that rings only its own home, and each household can release only the door it uses. Where there are two doors, each gets its own small station.

There is no law behind this. A two-family house is not a multiple dwelling under New York's housing laws, so §50-a does not reach it. The reason to do it properly is practical: a tenant who can answer their own visitors and let them in is a tenant who does not leave the door on the latch or hand out keys. We put the plan on paper first so the owner and the tenant both know which button does what.

Detached two-families with garages behind: doors at both ends of the house

Brownstoner's 2013 Building of the Day on 649 to 677 East 91st Street, between Avenues A and B, describes detached two-families built in 1932 by architect Irving Kirshenblit, each with a two-car garage at the back, one for each apartment. In houses laid out like that, the back of the property is as busy as the front: one household parks behind the house and walks in through a rear or side door, while visitors arrive at the front. A buzzer that only works at the front door leaves the household walking the length of the house every time someone they are expecting comes in the back.

For these houses we usually plan two small stations, front and back, each ringing the right household, and keep the cable run between them along the basement, which in these houses usually runs the full depth of the building. Where the back door sits a long way from any basement wall, we say so on the quote rather than at the install.

Brick rows off Kings Highway: a short stoop, a narrow front and a deep cellar

The attached brick rows that cityneighborhoods.nyc describes as typical of the area, with small front yards, are the simplest houses here to wire. The front door is a few steps above the sidewalk, the cellar runs under the whole house, and the bell wire, if it survives, drops straight down from the door to the cellar. The question in these houses is usually the door itself: an aluminum or steel storm door in front of the main door, or an older wood door that has been patched, and which of them should lock and release.

Our rule is that the release goes on the door that actually keeps people out. If the outer storm door is the one that locks, the station goes outside it and the strike goes in its frame; if the outer door is just a screen, the release goes on the main door and the station beside it.

Shops with apartments above: two doors a few feet apart and two separate systems

On Ralph Avenue, where cityneighborhoods.nyc describes Caribbean bakeries and barbershops, and along Rockaway Parkway and Church Avenue, the typical mixed building has a shop at the sidewalk and two or three apartments upstairs, with the residents' door right beside the shopfront. The apartments need a station at their own door that calls only the apartments; the shop may need a buzzer on its back or delivery door. We wire them as two separate systems with separate power, so a new shop tenant, a late-night repair or a fault in the store never touches the apartments' door.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Push the Street Door, Then Walk the Basement to the Last Apartment

This is how our technician spends the first part of a visit to a Remsen Village walk-up, before anything is priced. None of it involves opening the panel.

First, the door. The technician lets the street door close on its own from fully open and from halfway, then pushes and pulls it while it is locked, at the latch side and at the top corner. A door that closes but bounces off the strike, a closer that runs out of force in the last inch, or a latch that can be eased back with a firm pull all show up in a minute. On a door with that problem, the quote starts with the door, and we say so in writing.

Second, the record. For a building, we read the HPD violation history line by line with the owner or agent. A building with dozens of "Door/Lock" lines may have very few that involve the intercom; most will be apartment doors, closers, locks and peepholes. Separating them tells the owner which problems the buzzer job will clear and which need a locksmith or the super.

Third, the basement. The technician finds the old power supply or transformer, then follows the cable along the basement ceiling to the farthest line of apartments, noting splices, wet spots, boxed-in sections and anything that was cut during a renovation. Each reachable pair is metered. Only then is the cable plan drawn, and the apartments that need new runs are named on the quote by number.

The Door Before the Buzzer

Reading a Remsen Village Building's Door-and-Lock Record Before Choosing a Buzzer

Owners of small buildings here are often surprised by how long their HPD record is, and by how little of it is about the intercom. LucidRents groups violations into categories, and for 1106 Remsen Avenue the top category is "Door/Lock," with more than a hundred entries. That label covers a great deal: apartment entrance doors that do not self-close, closers that need adjusting, locks that need a key to get out, missing peepholes, latches on public hall doors. Only some of those touch the entry system at all.

The wording of an actual intercom violation is different, and it is worth knowing. In a Bronx building in February 2026, HPD issued a Class B violation that reads "§ 27-2005 HMC: PROPERLY REPAIR OR REPLACE THE BROKEN OR DEFECTIVE INTERCOM SYSTEM," and in August 2026 another Class B in the same building to "PROPERLY REPAIR OR REPLACE THE BROKEN OR DEFECTIVE BELL BUZZER." Those are the lines a buzzer installation clears. The self-closing door lines, which HPD treats as Class C and immediately hazardous, are cleared by door work, even when that door is the building's main entrance.

The two do meet at the street door. If the main entrance has a self-closing violation and a dead buzzer at the same time, the order matters. The door should close and latch by itself first, because the strike or lock has to hold a door that is actually shut. HPD gives 14 days to correct a self-closing door and then "must reinspect all self-closing door violations within 20 days of the date the violation should be corrected," and it can make emergency repairs and bill the property if the owner does not. A buzzer replacement takes planning and ordering; door hardware usually does not. Our practice is to put the door right in the first visit and install the system on the next.

Tenants have duties too. HPD's page says tenants must not prop doors open, tape latches or use wedges or door stops. In our experience, a buzzer that works and a door that is easy to open from the inside are the two things that stop people propping the street door, because the reason they prop it is almost always that somebody they are expecting cannot otherwise get in.

  • Lines a buzzer job clears: broken or defective intercom system, broken or defective bell buzzer, a dead release at the street door.
  • Lines door work clears: self-closing doors with missing or defective hinges or closers, latch sets at public hall doors, entrance doors that do not lock.
  • Lines neither clears: peepholes, apartment door locks, lighting in halls and stairs, which 431 Remsen Avenue's openigloo record shows among its open violation categories.
Rules and Responsibilities

Which Remsen Village Properties Must Have a Buzzer, What It Has to Do and Who Decides

  • Buildings of eight or more apartments built after 1968 must have one. Under Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, a Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have an intercom at its self-locking main door that lets a resident speak to a visitor and release the door. Any building that rises on the East 98th Street site will be in this group from the start.
  • The 1965 walk-ups fall on the older side of that line. For buildings put up before 1968 with eight or more apartments, such as 1106 and 431 Remsen Avenue, the duty to install a self-locking door and intercom arises when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing. Brick Underground's guide to buzzer rights notes that the tenants bear the cost in that case.
  • A system that exists has to be kept working. HPD's tenant guide says "the building manager, superintendent, or owner should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms," and tenants can report "Broken locks and intercoms" to 311. Habitat magazine's February 2024 report notes that courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service tied to the warranty of habitability.
  • Houses are the owner's choice. A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, so whether to put in a buzzer, and what kind, is up to the owner.
  • Every door must open from inside without a key. HPD's tenant guide lists "Door locks that require a key to exit" among the conditions inspectors look for and calls double-cylinder locks that need a key from inside "dangerous, illegal and must be removed." Any lock or release we add leaves the door opening freely from inside.
  • Keyless entry in an apartment building brings data rules. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) covers owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as fobs, cards, apps or biometrics. Owners "must provide tenants with a data retention and privacy policy," must get express consent "either in writing, or through a mobile application," and must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.
  • Magnetic locks need more than a lock. Electromagnetic locks are only made fail-safe, and the building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual "PUSH TO EXIT" device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door that keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, release on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and UL 294 listed equipment.

None of this is legal advice about a particular building. An owner facing a tenants' written request, a new owner inheriting a system or a developer planning a lobby should confirm the details with a housing lawyer or the managing agent. What we provide is a system that meets the requirement that applies, and a written record of what was installed.

Rules that apply to business entry doors around Remsen Village

  • Anyone inside can always get out. A business door with a buzzer, code or reader still opens from inside without a key. As Allegion's guide puts it, with an electric strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure.
  • Fire-rated doors keep their rating. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes, so a rated door to a stair or basement stays latched in a fire, and the release hardware must carry the right listing.
  • Magnetic locks bring the full sensor-release package. A shop that wants a mag lock on its front door needs the egress sensor, the "PUSH TO EXIT" button, the 30-second unlock and the fire alarm release, which is why a strike is usually the simpler choice on a storefront.
  • Buttons within reach. Call buttons outside and release buttons at a counter go no higher than the 48-inch high forward reach in the 2010 ADA Standards.
  • Tenants need the landlord's written approval. A shop or office that rents its space orders a buzzer only with the building owner's written permission, especially where the work touches a shared street door or the apartments' cable.
  • Big new buildings plan entry from the drawings. Any building of eight or more apartments on a redeveloped site falls under §50-a, and its lobby, garage and service doors cost least to wire before the walls close.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Remsen Village: Questions Answered

The low walk-ups along Remsen Avenue

Our building on Remsen Avenue is only two stories tall but runs far back on the lot, with eighteen apartments off long halls. Does a long, low building need a different kind of system than a tall one?

It needs a different cable plan more than a different system. In a tall building, the cable climbs a riser and the hard part is the height. In a low building like 1106 Remsen Avenue, 18 units on two floors, the hard part is distance along the basement: the apartments at the back can sit as far from the panel as a sixth floor would in a taller building, and older audio systems lose volume and reliability on long runs, especially through old splices.

So we start by measuring. If the existing pairs meter well to the far end, a two-wire system that reuses them is often the best value, because two-wire equipment is built to work over long distances on one pair per apartment; Aiphone, for example, describes its GT series as made "for new builds and retrofit projects" and as able to use existing wire. If the back of the building meters badly, we run new cable along the basement ceiling to those apartments only, and the quote lists them by number. Either way, the power supply goes near the middle of the run rather than at one end where we can arrange it, which keeps the far apartments as strong as the near ones.

A rental site lists more than a hundred door-and-lock violations for our building. How do you tell which of them a buzzer job will actually clear?

By reading them one at a time with you, because the category label hides very different problems. "Door/Lock" on a listing site covers apartment doors that do not self-close, closers, hinges, latches on hall doors, locks that need a key to get out and more. An intercom violation reads differently, typically "PROPERLY REPAIR OR REPLACE THE BROKEN OR DEFECTIVE INTERCOM SYSTEM" or the same wording for a "BELL BUZZER," and those are the lines a buzzer installation clears.

We sort the open lines into three groups before we quote: the ones our work clears, the ones door work at the entrance clears, and the ones that belong to a locksmith or the super inside the apartments. That gives the owner a realistic picture. A new buzzer will not lower a long door-and-lock list by itself, but a street door that closes, latches and releases properly, with a working panel, removes the condition behind many of the repeat complaints from the entrance.

Our system went in when the building was new in the mid-1960s. Is its age by itself a reason to replace it?

No. Age tells you to test it, not to throw it out. ButterflyMX's guide for small buildings says it directly: "Do not replace a system based on age alone." What decides it is whether the fault is in one place with parts available, or spread across the building. Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith told Brick Underground that the core wiring of older systems is often fine even when the stations are dated, and that most problems are "all in the wire connections."

In practice, a 1965 building tends to fall into one of two camps. Either the cable is sound and the handsets and panel are worn out, in which case new stations and a new panel on the old pairs is the economical answer, or the cable has been cut and spliced through decades of apartment renovations, in which case new cable is part of any lasting fix. We meter before we recommend, and the quote shows both options when both are realistic.

Only a few apartments in our building have dead handsets. Can just those stations be replaced while the panel at the street door stays?

Sometimes, if replacement stations exist that work with your panel and power supply. ButterflyMX's repair guide makes the same point: station-only replacement is possible only when the new stations are compatible with the panel, power supply, controls and wiring you keep. For some older audio systems, matching handsets are still made; for others they are not, and a mismatched station can upset the rest of the line.

Before buying anything, we test the dead apartments' pairs from the panel. If the pair is good and a matching station exists, we replace just those stations and price it as a repair. If the pairs are bad, a new station will not help. If no matching station exists, we explain the cheapest way forward, which is sometimes a new panel and power supply that accept modern stations, installed in stages as old handsets fail.

Street doors and vestibules

News 12 reporters walked into buildings on East 93rd Street without a key. How do you check whether our own street door actually holds when it looks locked?

We test it the way a stranger would, before anything else on the visit. The door is let go from fully open and from halfway to see if it closes and latches on its own every time. Then, with it locked, we push and pull hard at the latch side and the top corner, try it with the handle turned from outside, and look at the gap between the door and the frame. A latch that barely catches the strike, a closer that runs out of force at the end, a frame that has shifted, or a strike keeper screwed into worn wood all show up in a minute or two.

The fix depends on what we find, but it comes before the electronics. HPD treats a door that does not close and latch by itself as a Class C, immediately hazardous condition, so this is not a cosmetic point. A door that holds, with a strike that releases it on command, is what makes the panel worth having. If the door needs more than adjustment, we price it as its own line before the buzzer.

The owner put a lock on the outer vestibule door, and now the buzzer panel is behind it on the inner wall where visitors can't reach it. Where should the panel go?

Outside the first locked door. It sounds obvious, but this exact situation turns up in buildings everywhere: a tenant in Jersey City described on Reddit how a second locked door left the building's buzzers "100% unusable," with couriers leaving "could not deliver" notices. The panel has to be wherever a visitor first meets a locked door, and the release has to open that door.

There are two common fixes. One is to move the panel to the outside of the outer door and put the release on that door, leaving the inner door on a key or a second release triggered by the same button. The other is to keep the panel in the vestibule and take the lock off the outer door. The first is usually what owners want. Moving the panel means new cable through the outer wall or the door frame, and on brick it is planned so the cable is hidden behind the panel.

Some residents call relatives to walk them in at night. Can people check the lobby on their phones before they open the street door?

Yes, if the system includes a camera inside the vestibule or lobby that residents can view, as well as the camera in the door station. At Rutland Plaza, resident Patricia Walters told News 12 she now calls her son to make sure she gets to her apartment safely. A resident who can look at the vestibule on their phone from the sidewalk, before putting a key in the door, is in a better position than one who finds out on the way in.

There are limits we explain up front. A vestibule camera viewed on residents' phones means an app, an internet connection at the building and, in a multiple dwelling that uses keyless entry, the city's data rules about what is stored and for how long. We set it up so the live view is available to residents while recordings are kept only as long as the owner's written policy says.

Houses, two-families and visiting family

Our 1920s semi-detached has a basement entrance through steel Bilco doors. Can that hatch be put on the buzzer?

Not usefully. A Bilco-style hatch is a pair of heavy steel leaves over an outside stair, built to keep weather out and to be opened from below or with a key. It is not where visitors arrive, and its leaves are not a place for an electric strike. The door that matters is the one at the foot of that stair, between the stairwell and the basement, and it usually needs nothing more than a solid lock that opens freely from inside.

If the basement is a lived-in space, such as a family room or a relative's room, and people do arrive that way, we look at putting a small station and a release on that inner door instead, with the cable kept inside the basement. Houses like the semi-detached on Lenox Road, with a porch, a driveway, a detached garage and a hatch, usually need two stations at most: one at the porch and one at the side door the family uses.

Relatives often stay with us for a few months at a time. Can a visiting family member answer and open the door on their own phone, and lose that when they go home?

On systems with phone answering, yes. Most app-based door stations let the owner add a user, who can then see and speak to visitors and release the door from their own phone, and remove that user again in a few taps. While your relative is staying, they get the same calls you do; when they leave, you delete them and the next visitor is never routed to their phone.

Two cautions. First, a guest's phone may be on a foreign number or limited data, so a wall station in the room where they spend the day is still worth having; it works without any app or internet. Second, only the household should hold the admin login. We set the system up so the owner controls who is added and removed, and we show them how on the handover visit.

A tenant in our two-family wants to wire a smart relay into the handset so the street door opens from their phone. Should we allow it?

Ask to see the plan first. A relay wired across the release contacts of an existing handset can work, and ButterflyMX lists adding smart control to an existing buzzer as a common renter question. But the relay has to match the system's voltage, it adds a way to open the shared door that you do not control, and if it is wired badly it can leave the door releasing at random or damage the handset.

If you are open to the idea, the better route is usually to give the tenant phone answering through the building's own system, set up and removed by you, rather than a device you cannot see. If you do allow a relay, have it installed by a low-voltage installer, put it in writing, and make sure it comes out when the tenant leaves. We are glad to look at the specific product and tell you whether it suits your system.

The proposed towers

The buildings proposed for East 98th Street and Rockaway Parkway would hold about a hundred apartments each. What does an entry system for a building that size need that a walk-up's never does?

A great deal more planning. The Real Deal reported 972 units across buildings of 14 or 15 stories, or about 108 per building if split evenly. At that size the street door needs a directory or code panel rather than a wall of buttons, every resident needs a way to answer that works without an app, there are usually several controlled doors (main entrance, garage, package room, service door), and the system has to stay usable when the building's internet is down. Swiftlane's cost guide notes that cloud subscriptions commonly add "$5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month," which at a hundred apartments is a real operating line.

Most of that is decided long before installation: where the panel sits, how cable reaches each floor, which doors are fail-secure and which have sensor-release hardware, and where the power supply and battery live. Developers and general contractors who involve a low-voltage installer while the drawings are still changing avoid opening finished walls later. For a building that size, a buzzer is part of the building's design, not an add-on.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Remsen Village

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary what a door buzzer costs or what the law requires, and you get confident answers assembled from national cost guides, vendor blogs and New York articles written about Manhattan buildings. Some of it is right in general and wrong for a neighborhood of 1960s walk-ups and semi-detached two-families. Here are seven claims we see repeated, each checked against its source and against what we find at doors here.

"Hard-wired intercom systems cost $1,000 to $15,000"

That range is from HomeAdvisor's national guide, which puts wireless systems at $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired ones at $1,000 to $15,000, with an overall average of $2,500. It is a fair description of the whole country and an unhelpful one for a specific Remsen Village door.

On our Brooklyn list, an audio buzzer with a new release on a house runs $400 to $900, video with stations or phone answering for a house or two-family runs $700 to $1,800, and a building system starts at about $2,500 and can pass $10,000. Most houses here land well below the national average; most 1960s walk-ups land in the building range.

What moves a price here is the street door's condition, how many old pairs still meter clean and how far the back apartments are from the panel, none of which a national range can see.

"Buildings with at least eight apartments must have an intercom"

That is how the rule is often summarized, including in Brick Underground's guide, but the same guide adds the part that matters here: pre-1968 buildings must provide self-locking doors and intercoms "if requested by a majority of tenants." Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a makes the requirement automatic only for buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968.

The walk-ups along Remsen Avenue were built in 1965, three years before that line. For them, the duty to install a system starts with a written request from tenants occupying a majority of the apartments, and the guide notes that the tenants bear the cost in that case.

Once a building has a system, though, HPD expects it to work, and its tenant guide tells owners to "immediately repair broken locks or intercoms." The date decides installation, not maintenance.

"Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY"

HomeAdvisor says this, and for a single-family doorbell it can be true. A wireless buzzer still needs power at the door and a wired connection to whatever releases the lock, and the release is the part that does the work.

In a brick semi-detached house or a walk-up, the hard part is not the signal to the apartments; it is the strike in the frame, the closer that makes the door latch and the power supply in the basement. Those are wired whether the stations are wireless or not.

Wireless stations can save a difficult run to a top-floor bedroom. They do not let anyone skip the door.

"Intercom prices run anywhere between $2,000 and $50,000"

A security company representative gave Brick Underground that range, and it is honest for New York City as a whole, where the top end means a large tower with cloud video at every door.

Remsen Village's apartment buildings are small. A 10- to 20-unit walk-up on Remsen Avenue falls in our building range of about $2,500 to past $10,000, depending mainly on how much cable must be replaced and whether the owner wants video or phone answering. The $50,000 end belongs to buildings like the ones proposed for East 98th Street, not to a 1965 walk-up.

The useful question is not the citywide range but which pairs in your building can be reused.

"Video intercoms cost around $500 per apartment"

Brick Underground repeats a New York Times figure of about $500 per apartment for video intercoms in large buildings. Per-apartment figures are a quick way to compare bids for a big building with many identical units.

They mislead in small buildings, where the fixed costs dominate: the panel, the power supply, the strike and the door work cost about the same whether a building has eleven apartments or eighteen. A per-apartment figure makes an 11-unit building look cheaper than it can be done for, and a two-family look more expensive than it is.

We price by the building, with the fixed parts and the per-apartment parts shown separately, so an owner can see what adding video to every unit actually adds.

"A buzzer repair costs $150 to $250"

ButterflyMX quotes that figure, and labels it carefully as a benchmark from one NYC installer that "should be treated only as a local benchmark." Brick Underground's repair guide reported service calls at $195 per hour and $250 per hour plus parts.

Those figures describe a service call, not a new system. Our in-person diagnosis of an existing system is the $250 service call booked online, and that $250 is applied toward the work if you go ahead.

In a building where the street door, the panel and several apartments' handsets have all failed, a single repair call is the start of the conversation, not the price of the answer.

"A new intercom fixes a building's door problems"

Search summaries often treat the buzzer and the door as one thing. HPD does not: a door that fails to "swing closed and latch by themselves" is its own Class C, immediately hazardous violation with a 14-day correction period, separate from any intercom violation.

525 Rockaway Parkway's March 2026 violation was about hinges at the entrance, not the buzzer. A new panel on that door would have left the violation open and the door unsecured.

The door comes first. We test it before quoting the system, and if it needs work, that work is its own line on the quote.

Is a door buzzer the same thing as an intercom?
+

Not quite, though people use the words loosely. A buzzer, strictly, is the part that rings and releases the door: the button outside, the sound inside and the release in the frame. An intercom adds two-way voice, and often video, so the resident can speak to the visitor before letting them in. Most systems we install in Remsen Village buildings do both, and the law for larger post-1968 buildings asks for both voice and release.

The handset in one apartment makes a constant buzz even when nobody is ringing. Is it safe to leave it like that until the visit?

Someone has to let the crew into each apartment that gets a new station, so the owner or super arranges access and tenants are told in advance which day we need their unit. Tenants do not need to stay while we work. Apartments we cannot reach on the day are left on the old station if it still works, or scheduled for a short return visit, and the test sheet shows which units are finished.

If we take the door station off our brick front someday, will it leave a mark?
+

It leaves the screw anchors and the cable hole behind it. On brick we set anchors in the mortar joints where we can, because mortar is easier to repoint than a drilled brick face, and the cable goes straight back into the wall behind the station so nothing runs across the front. We do not patch or paint, so if a station is ever removed, a mason or painter closes the holes.

Can the old telephone wire in our building carry the new buzzer?
+

Sometimes. Older buildings often have spare telephone pairs running to each apartment, and some two-wire buzzer systems will run on them if they meter clean and are not shared with anything live. We test them like any other pair before relying on them. If they pass, reusing them can save new cable to the back apartments; if they fail or are in use, the quote shows new cable instead.

Do courts treat a dead buzzer in an apartment building as a habitability problem?
+

Habitat magazine reported in February 2024 that courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service, and that failing to maintain one can breach the warranty of habitability, which affects both an apartment's livability and its security. That is general information, not legal advice. For an owner, the practical point is that a building system that exists should be kept working, and repaired promptly when it fails.

Our new door station has a camera. Will it see into the neighbor's windows across the shared driveway?
+

A door station's camera is built to show the person standing in front of it, with a fairly narrow view, so it rarely reaches far past the step or stoop. We mount it facing the visitor and check the picture with you before we leave. If part of the view takes in a neighbor's window or door, we adjust the angle or choose a model with a narrower view so it covers only your entrance.

My father can't easily get out of bed. Can a release button go beside his bed instead of on a station across the room?
+

Yes. On most wired systems a separate release button can be wired near the bed, alongside or instead of a station, and on video systems a small station can be mounted within reach of the bed. Phone answering is another option if he uses a phone comfortably. We place every button within the reach range the 2010 ADA Standards use, no higher than 48 inches, so it is easy to press from a bed or a chair.

For a family event, can the street door be left on release for a few hours and then lock itself again?
+

Many panels and some door controllers can hold the release open on a timer and then lock automatically at a set time, which is much better than propping the door with a chair. The door still has to close by itself; it simply is not locked during that window. In an apartment building the owner decides whether that feature is enabled at all. In a house, we can show the household how to start and stop it.
