The Neighborhood

Why Marine Park Properties Need Intercom Installation

Marine Park takes its name from the 530-acre park of grassland and salt marsh on its southern edge, and the neighborhood to the north of it was built mostly as houses for families who intended to stay. Brick Underground's 2022 guide describes the housing as mainly one- and two-family detached and semi-detached homes from the 1910s through the 1940s, with co-ops along the southern and western borders, a few small condos in the middle and no rental apartment buildings to speak of. Renters here, the guide says, live in two-family houses or above shops in low-rise mixed-use buildings. That last detail is the one that matters most for an intercom. In much of Marine Park, the person ringing the bell is trying to reach one of two households in the same house, and the house was usually built with one bell in mind.

Listings show how often that second household has its own way in. A semi-detached two-family on Ryder Street is described as three stories plus an attic, with "a 1 bedroom apartment with its own private entrance" on the lower level and a shared driveway. An attached brick two-family on Gerritsen Avenue, built in 1955 according to its listing, has a ground level that "has a separate entrance and features a studio apartment." Brownstoner's description of a 1931 brick Tudor on Avenue T, part of a semi-detached row, notes a basement reached by a staircase from the kitchen or an entrance from the rear yard. Every one of those doors is a place a visitor, a courier or an aide will eventually stand and look for a button. If the only button is the original one by the front door, the upstairs household ends up answering for the downstairs one, or the downstairs tenant hangs a battery doorbell beside a door nobody can see from the street.

The apartment buildings are fewer but much larger. Land records show only 27 lots in the area that hold apartment-type buildings other than houses and store buildings, together about 711 homes, and all of them sit on the edges: the six-story buildings near Kings Highway and Flatbush Avenue, the 1950 garden courts on Fillmore Avenue and Avenue R, a row of six-family walk-ups from 1928 to 1930 on Flatlands Avenue, and store buildings and a 2016 building on the east side of Nostrand Avenue. South of Avenue U, facing the park, the Brooklyn Marine Park Development Co-op on Burnett Street adds 264 residences. In those places an intercom is the lobby: the panel a visitor reads, the riser that carries every call, and the release that decides who gets inside.

Front doors also carry more traffic than they used to. Residents in a Marine Park Facebook group have posted about packages taken from front doors and porches, including one on East 31st Street who wrote that it was "the second time in a month." News 12 reported in March 2026 that police were looking for four people who entered a home near East 34th Street and Avenue N at about 4:30 on a February afternoon and carried out two televisions. An intercom does not stop either of those things by itself. What it does is let the right person see and speak to whoever is at the right door, without opening it, and that is the piece many Marine Park houses still lack at their second entrance.

Six Marine Park facts that shape an intercom job

  • Houses outnumber everything else by a wide margin. Of about 6,050 residential lots in PLUTO between Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway, 4,232 are one-family houses and 1,470 are two-family houses. Another 112 are three-family houses. The average year built is between 1925 and 1940 for every house type, so most bell wiring we meet is old, thin and often spliced.
  • Attached and semi-detached rows are common. About 1,394 of the one-family lots are recorded as attached or semi-detached. In those rows a door station shares a facade with a neighbor's, and the cable route into the house usually has to stay on your own side of a shared wall.
  • The apartment buildings are on the edges, and almost all of them are old. The largest are 3502 Kings Highway (84 apartments, 1950), a 72-apartment building on Kings Highway from 1939 and 3715 Kings Highway (66 apartments, 1938), plus a 41-apartment building on East 37th Street from 1925. Each predates the 1968 line in New York's intercom law, which changes how the law reaches them.
  • Garden courts have many small entrances. Two-story garden apartments built in 1950 include 2801 Fillmore Avenue, with 52 apartments, and buildings of eight apartments each at 3001 and 2995 Avenue R. A court like that has many doors, each serving a handful of households, which is a different design problem from one lobby.
  • City complaints about intercoms are rare here. NYC Open Data shows 21 HPD intercom, buzzer or door-bell violations on the Marine Park side of the area in 23 years, and eight 311 complaints about bells, buzzers or intercoms since 2018. Few violations does not mean the systems are new. Most Marine Park homes are houses, which HPD rarely inspects for this.
  • There is no historic district. The Historic Districts Council lists one designated landmark in Marine Park, the Hendrick I. Lott House at 1940 East 36th Street, designated in 1989 as an individual landmark. No historic district covers the house rows, so a door station on a Marine Park house does not go to the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

Where this page applies. Wikipedia describes Marine Park as roughly squared off by Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway, in Community Board 18 and ZIP codes 11229 and 11234. This page also covers the co-ops on the park's southern edge that are sold as Marine Park. Buildings on the west side of Nostrand Avenue are usually counted as Madison, and the blocks across Flatbush Avenue are Bergen Beach; for those, see our Bergen Beach intercom installation page, our Flatlands intercom installation page for the blocks north of Kings Highway, and our Brooklyn intercom installation page for the borough.

Repair Calls in Marine Park

Already Have an Intercom That Half Works? Marine Park Repairs Before Replacement

Not every Marine Park call should end in a new system. In a house, the usual complaint is that the upstairs station rings but cannot talk, or the downstairs tenant's button rings the owner's floor instead of their own. In the edge buildings, it is a handful of apartments on one riser that stopped getting calls, or a lobby panel whose buttons have gone stiff. A technician traces the fault first, at our service rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and tells you in plain terms whether the existing equipment is worth keeping. If one station has failed on a system that is otherwise sound, it is usually cheaper to replace that station. If the power supply has been replaced three times, the wire has been spliced by every owner since the 1930s and the panel is a model nobody makes parts for, the repair money is better put toward a new system.

A repair visit also answers the questions that make a replacement quote accurate. Which pairs of wire still test clean from the basement to each floor. Whether the old transformer is feeding only the doorbell or several other things as well. Whether the electric strike in the door is the original one, and whether the closer brings the door back to latch. Once those are known, a new system can reuse what passed and replace only what failed, and the quote does not carry a guess for wiring nobody has looked at.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Marine Park

Every Marine Park intercom starts from three counts: how many households, how many doors visitors use, and how many of those doors need to unlock from inside. A one-family Tudor with one front door is one count; a two-family with a lower-level entrance is two households and at least two doors; a garden court can be fifty households and a dozen doors. These are the services that cover them.

Door stations for one-family houses

A video or audio station at the front door, an indoor station where the household actually spends its time, and an app on the phones that want it. For the many Marine Park houses entered from the driveway or the rear yard, a second station there on the same system.

Two-household systems for two-family houses

A station at each entrance and an indoor station for each household, with calls routed so the lower-level tenant's visitors ring the tenant and the owner's visitors ring the owner. Each household releases only its own door unless both doors are shared.

Three-button panels for three-family houses

A small panel with a labeled button for each floor, an indoor station on each, and a strike on the shared front door, fitted to the brick or frame facade without disturbing the original door surround.

Entrance panels for garden courts

One small panel at each entrance of a two-story garden building, wired back to the apartments that entrance serves, with a consistent layout so every resident in the court learns one system.

Lobby panels and risers for elevator buildings

Directory or keypad panels for the six-story buildings near Kings Highway, apartment stations on every floor, and riser work planned floor by floor so residents are not left without a working entrance.

Co-op systems with a front desk

Panels that call a desk, apartments or both, with schedules for when the desk is staffed, for the larger co-ops on the park edge and along Gerritsen Avenue and Troy Avenue.

Apartment doors beside storefronts

A separate panel, power supply and cable path for the apartments over shops on Avenue U, Flatbush Avenue and Nostrand Avenue, so the residential door never depends on the store's equipment or hours.

Video on existing wire

Two-wire video systems that use an old bell or intercom pair once it tests clean, which is often the only way to add video in a brick house without opening plaster on every floor.

Takeovers of systems nobody can log into

Documentation, reset and reprogramming of networked panels left by a previous installer or developer, so the owner or board holds the administrator account from now on.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Marine Park's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every intercom request we get from Marine Park. They differ in how many households share a door, what the walls are made of, and where the old wire goes, and each of those decides the system before any brand is chosen.

Brick two-family houses

Attached and semi-detached two-families, many with a lower-level apartment that has its own door. Two households, two or more entrances, and calls that must reach the right floor.

Tudor and bungalow rows

One-family brick houses in semi-detached rows from the 1920s and 1930s, often with a basement door to the rear yard and a side driveway leading to a garage.

Three-family houses

About a hundred houses with three apartments behind one front door, where a single row of bells has to become three labeled calls and three ways to answer.

1950 garden courts

Two-story apartment buildings on Fillmore Avenue and Avenue R with an entrance for every small group of apartments and a basement that links them.

Edge walk-ups and store buildings

The six-family walk-ups on Flatlands Avenue from 1928 to 1930, the 1927 store buildings on the east side of Nostrand Avenue, and apartments over shops on Avenue U.

Elevator buildings and co-ops

Six-story buildings from 1938 to 1952 near Kings Highway and on Burnett Street, with lobbies, risers and dozens or hundreds of apartments.

Brick two-family houses: two households, more than one door

The typical Marine Park two-family is brick, either attached on both sides or semi-detached with a driveway on one side, and it was usually built with one bell button by the front door and one chime or buzzer inside. Over the decades the lower level or the ground floor became a separate apartment with its own entrance, often under the front stoop or off the driveway, and the bell never caught up. When we design a system for one of these houses, we start with the doors. The upstairs household's visitors come to the top of the stoop. The lower apartment's visitors may come to a door under the stoop, beside it, or along the driveway. Each of those doors gets its own station, or one station gets two clearly labeled buttons if both households truly share a single entrance.

Inside, each household gets its own indoor station, placed where the people actually are. In the upstairs apartment that is often the kitchen at the back, not the front hall. In the lower apartment, it is wherever the tenant will hear it with the television on. The wiring path is usually the hardest part. Most of these houses have plaster on brick or block, and the original bell wire, if it still exists, runs through the basement ceiling and up a single path. We test that pair first. If it carries a clean signal, a two-wire video system can use it, and the walls on the upper floor never need opening. If it fails, the new cable goes up through a closet, beside a riser pipe, or along a route the owner approves on the survey.

Two-families also raise a question of who controls what. The owner may want to see visitors at both doors, while the tenant should not be able to release the owner's door. Most current systems can be programmed so that each indoor station sees and releases only its own entrance, while an owner's station can be given a view of both. We write that arrangement into the quote so both households know what they are getting.

Tudor and bungalow rows: one household, a front door nobody uses

Homes.com describes most Marine Park properties as red brick, including bungalow and Tudor Revival rowhomes. Many are one-family houses where the family itself comes and goes by the driveway, the side door or the rear yard, and the front door is mostly for guests and deliveries. The intercom design follows that habit. The front door gets the station a visitor will see from the sidewalk. If the household wants to know when someone comes up the driveway or to the back, a second station or a camera-only call point goes there, on the same system, ringing the same indoor stations and phones.

The facades on these rows are often detailed: brick laid in patterns, small entry porches, stone or cast trim around the door. A door station is surface-mounted on a flat section of brick or on the door frame, with the cable entering behind the unit so nothing runs down the face of the house. Where there is no flat section near the door, the station can go on the side wall of a porch or entry, or on a short post, and we show the owner the options on the survey rather than drilling first.

Three-family houses: one door, three answers

Land records show about 112 three-family houses in the area, roughly 336 homes. Most have a single front door with three bell buttons in a row and, behind it, a stair hall that serves all three floors. The intercom here is a small multi-unit system: a three-button panel at the door, a station on each floor, and an electric strike so each household can let a visitor in without walking down. The city's intercom rule in the Multiple Dwelling Law begins at eight apartments, so it does not reach a three-family house, but tenants still expect to be called and to buzz someone in, and an owner who has to come downstairs for every delivery to the third floor usually wants it fixed. The common route for cable is the stair hall itself, where a riser can run in a corner or behind trim to each floor's door.

1950 garden courts: many entrances, one complex

The garden apartments built in 1950 on Fillmore Avenue and Avenue R are two stories high and spread out. Their entrances each serve a small group of apartments, and the buildings are often joined by a basement or a shared yard. That arrangement favors one small panel per entrance, wired to only the apartments behind it, over a single panel at the edge of the property. Visitors go to the door their host actually lives behind, and a fault at one entrance does not take down the others. The supply for each group of entrances is placed in the basement where the super or the board can reach it, with battery backup sized for the panels and the strikes.

Edge walk-ups and store buildings: shared walls, separate doors

The six-family walk-ups along Flatlands Avenue were built as a row between 1928 and 1930, and the store buildings on the east side of Nostrand Avenue date from 1927. On Avenue U, listings describe corner store buildings with apartments on the floor above. In each case the apartments' door sits beside, or around the corner from, a business. We give the residential door its own panel, its own power supply and its own cable path, so a store renovation, a change of tenant or the shop's hours never decide whether residents can be called. Where the walk-ups have a narrow vestibule, the panel goes at the outer door, the strike on the door that actually locks, and the mailboxes stay where the post office expects them.

Elevator buildings and co-ops: lobbies that serve hundreds

The six-story buildings near Kings Highway, from 1938, 1939 and 1950, and the 1952 co-op on Burnett Street are the only places in Marine Park where an intercom looks like the lobby systems in other parts of Brooklyn. They have a directory panel at the street door, a riser that climbs past every floor, apartment stations in every unit, and often a second entrance for service or parking. Replacing a system like that is planned as a building project: survey of every floor, testing of every pair in the riser, a schedule that keeps one working entrance at all times, and a written plan for who holds the administrator logins. The sections below on the 1968 date in the law and on the Burnett Street co-op cover the specifics.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks on a Marine Park Survey

Count the doors before the buttons. The old bell plate tells you how many households someone once planned for. The doors tell you how many households live there now. On a Marine Park two-family, the technician walks the whole perimeter: the stoop, the space under it, the driveway side and the rear yard, and notes which doors visitors actually use.

Find where the bell gets its power. In houses of this age, the bell transformer is usually screwed to a basement joist or wall, sometimes beside the electric panel and sometimes far from it. It is found, checked for what else it powers, and the new intercom gets its own supply rather than sharing a transformer that may also feed a chime, a garage bell or a forgotten button.

Test the old pair end to end. A pair of bell wire that rings a chime may still be too damaged to carry video. Each pair is tested from the basement to each floor before anyone promises a two-wire system, and the result goes on the survey sheet the owner sees.

Look at the door, the jamb and the closer. An electric strike only works if the latch lines up and the door closes all the way by itself. Many Marine Park doors are original, or have been replaced with steel doors in old openings. The technician checks the latch, the jamb material and whether the closer brings the door back every time.

Ask who lives where, and who signs. In a two-family, the owner signs; in a co-op, the board does; in a self-managed co-op, a committee may. The survey notes who has authority over each door so the quote goes to the right person and nobody is surprised by the scope.

Check the light at each door. A video station shows little at a lower-level entrance with no light above it. If the door is dark at night, the survey notes it so the owner can add a light, or so the station chosen has its own illumination.

Switch off the power and try the door. With the intercom and the strike unpowered, the door must still open from inside and lock from outside the way it did before. That test is run on the survey for the old system and again at handover for the new one.

The 1968 Line

Built Before 1968: What That Date Means for Marine Park's Apartment Buildings

New York's intercom requirement is written around a date, and nearly every apartment building in Marine Park sits on the older side of it. The Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, as summarized by Parker Custom Security, requires every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 that contains eight or more apartments to have an intercom at its self-locking main entrance door. The six-story buildings near Kings Highway were built in 1938, 1939 and 1950. The Burnett Street co-op dates from 1952. The garden courts on Fillmore Avenue and Avenue R are from 1950. The 41-apartment building on East 37th Street is from 1925. Of the larger buildings in the area, only the 19-apartment building on the east side of Nostrand Avenue, completed in 2016, falls clearly on the newer side.

What applies to the older buildings. For a building with eight or more apartments erected before 1968, the same summary says an intercom must be installed once the tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request one in writing, with work begun within 90 days and completed within six months. Most of Marine Park's older elevator buildings already have intercoms, so the more common question is not whether one must be added but whether the existing one must be kept working. Habitat magazine reported in 2024 that the Housing Maintenance Code requires a functioning intercom where one is provided, and that courts treat intercoms as essential under the warranty of habitability. Parker's summary describes a non-working intercom or buzzer as a Class B hazardous violation, enforceable on tenant complaint.

Co-ops are not exempt. Shareholders sometimes assume that because they own the building together, the rules for landlords do not apply. Habitat quoted attorney Nancy Kourland on this point: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." For a Marine Park co-op board, that means an intercom replacement belongs in the capital plan, not in the list of things to try when there is money left over.

The garden courts and the eight-apartment count. A garden building of eight apartments, like the ones at 3001 and 2995 Avenue R, sits exactly at the threshold. A larger court such as 2801 Fillmore Avenue has 52 apartments but many separate entrances. How the city counts a complex like that depends on how it is registered, which an owner or board can check on the building's HPD record. Our design does not depend on the answer: each entrance gets a working call point and release regardless, because that is what residents and visitors need.

The houses and the small walk-ups. The intercom section of the Multiple Dwelling Law does not reach a one-, two- or three-family house, and it does not reach a six-family walk-up on Flatlands Avenue. Those owners install intercoms because the household or the tenants need them, not because the law demands it. The door still has to close and lock properly, and an owner who adds an electric strike takes on keeping it working.

Mounting heights. The city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. We mount lobby panels in the edge buildings to that rule, and house stations to the same heights unless the owner asks otherwise, so the station is reachable for a visitor in a wheelchair and for a child sent to the door.

The local record. NYC Open Data shows 21 HPD violations for intercoms, buzzers or door bells on the Marine Park side of the area since 2003, never more than four in a year, and eight 311 complaints about bells, buzzers or intercoms since 2018. The addresses that appear most are store buildings on the east side of Nostrand Avenue and the walk-ups on Flatlands Avenue. A quiet record does not mean every system is sound. It mostly reflects how few Marine Park homes are in buildings HPD inspects for intercoms.

The Park-Edge Co-ops

Burnett Street, Gerritsen Avenue and Troy Avenue: Planning a Co-op Intercom

The largest single intercom job in Marine Park is the Brooklyn Marine Park Development Co-op, which homes.com lists at 2220 Burnett Street and also at 2240, 2260 and 2280 Burnett Street, across from the park between Avenue U and Avenue V. The listing describes a red-brick complex from 1952 with 264 residences in two six-story wings, terra cotta tiling above the lobby doors, a parking garage and a surface lot. It says "there are elevators and a video intercom system, which lend a sense of security," and lists "Virtual Doorman," "Intercom to Front Desk," "Controlled Access" and "Secure Lobby" among the building's features. Homes.com's neighborhood guide adds that along Gerritsen Avenue "a few mid-rise postwar co-ops round out the housing inventory," and the Marine Cooperative Apartments at 2239 Troy Avenue, where Marine Park meets Flatlands, describes itself this way: "We are self-managed and self-governing. The people who live here take an active role in caring for the property."

Those three descriptions point to three questions every Marine Park co-op board should settle before choosing equipment.

One complex, several street numbers. A visitor told to go to 2260 Burnett Street will look for that number, not for the co-op's name. Each lobby panel should carry its own street number clearly and should call the apartments behind that door. Whether the panel also lets a visitor search the whole complex is a board decision; it helps guests who arrive at the wrong door, and it shows more names to anyone who walks up. We set it up whichever way the board chooses and write the choice into the programming notes.

The desk, the service and the apartments. A system that calls a front desk has to know what to do when the desk is not staffed. A "virtual doorman" service, where one exists, may be a contract with a monitoring company rather than equipment the co-op owns. Before anything is replaced, we list which pieces belong to the co-op, which belong to a service provider, and which contracts would need to end or continue. That list is often the most useful document the board gets from the survey.

Who holds the keys to the software. In a self-managed co-op like the one on Troy Avenue, there may be no managing agent to keep the administrator account. We recommend that the board, not the installer and not one volunteer, hold the main login, with a second login for whoever handles move-ins and directory changes. The login details go into the handover binder along with the labeled riser map.

Two Households, One House

The Lower-Level Apartment Door: Giving Marine Park's Second Household Its Own Call

Real estate listings for Marine Park two-families keep describing the same arrangement: a lower-level or ground-level apartment with its own entrance. The Ryder Street listing calls it "a 1 bedroom apartment with its own private entrance." The Gerritsen Avenue listing says the ground level "has a separate entrance and features a studio apartment." For the household that lives there, the intercom is often an afterthought. For the owner, it can become a daily nuisance when every visitor, courier and repair person for the downstairs apartment rings the upstairs bell.

Start with where the visitor stands. If the lower door is under the stoop, a visitor coming up the walk may never see it. A station at that door helps only if there is also a sign or a second button at the main entrance that sends visitors to it. Sometimes the cleaner answer is a two-button station at the main walk, one button for each household, with the lower apartment's call ringing only downstairs and its release opening only the lower door.

Keep each household's controls its own. The tenant's indoor station should see and open the tenant's door. The owner's station can be set to see both doors, which many owners want for packages and repairs, and the tenant should know that when the lease is signed. Current two-wire and networked systems can be programmed either way; the decision belongs to the owner and goes in writing.

Plan the cable for the lower door. A door under the stoop is often only a few feet from the basement, which makes its cable short and simple. A door at the back of a driveway can be the length of the house away. We survey both and price the route the owner prefers, and where the run would cross a shared driveway or a neighbor's side of a semi-detached pair, we stop at the property line.

Think about the empty months. A lower apartment is sometimes vacant between tenants or while it is being renovated. The release on that door can be turned off from the system's settings while the unit is empty and turned back on for the next tenant, so the door does not stay openable from a station nobody is watching.

Compliance notes for Marine Park shops, offices and mixed-use buildings

  • Free exit at every controlled door. Any door fitted with an electric strike or lock must still open from inside without a key, a code or power, and we check that with the power off before we leave.
  • Apartment doors that latch on their own. HPD enforces self-closing door rules at building entrances. Where apartments share a building with a business, the residential street door gets a closer that brings it back to latch every time.
  • Controls within reach. Push buttons stay no higher than six feet and the speaker between four and five feet from the floor, following 1 RCNY 42-01, which also keeps them within reach for a visitor in a wheelchair.
  • Separate supplies for separate occupants. The apartments' intercom and the store's door station each get their own power supply and cable path, so a shop renovation or a change of tenant does not cut off the residents.
  • Life-safety equipment stays clear. Stations and cable are never mounted over an exit sign, a fire alarm device or a sprinkler line, and penetrations through rated walls are sealed.
  • Written approval in leased space. A business that rents its space gets the landlord's written approval before we drill the facade or the door.
  • No landmark review needed on the corridors. No historic district covers Marine Park's commercial streets, so a new door station on a storefront does not go to the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Marine Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Marine Park owners, tenants and co-op boards describe when they first get in touch, grouped by the kind of building they come from.

Co-ops and elevator buildings

Most shareholders in our co-op walk in from the parking garage rather than the front lobby. Should the garage door get a call station, or only a reader for residents?

Usually a reader for residents and no call button. A visitor who is not expected should be sent to the lobby, where the directory, the camera and any desk can see them. A call button at the garage door invites guests and couriers to the least supervised entrance. If the board wants deliveries to come in that way, the station there can be set to call only the desk or the super rather than every apartment, and the garage door's strike can be placed on a schedule.

Our six-story 1950 building has the main lobby door plus a service door by the trash room. Does the service door need to be on the intercom?

It needs to lock and close reliably more than it needs a call button. A service door is used by the super, contractors and residents taking out trash, and visitors should not be calling apartments from it. We usually fit it with a strike or a lock that residents open with the same fob or code as the lobby, a closer that brings it back every time, and, if the board wants, a single call button that rings the super's station only.

Garden courts and newer buildings

The buildings in our garden court are joined by a basement. Can the intercom cable for each entrance run through it?

Often, yes, and it is usually the cleanest route. A shared basement lets one power supply and one set of cable paths serve several entrances, with each entrance's run going straight up to its own panel and apartments. We check the basement for dampness, for rooms residents lock, and for any wall that is rated and needs sealed penetrations. Cable crossing a boiler room or storage area goes in conduit, and every run is labeled with its entrance and apartment.

Our building on the east side of Nostrand Avenue opened in 2016 with a networked intercom the developer chose, and nobody here has the administrator password. Can someone else take it over?

In most cases, yes. Many networked panels can be reset to factory settings and reprogrammed by a new installer, though some brands require proof of ownership or a dealer account to do it. We identify the make and model, check whether the manufacturer's support will release the account, and if not, reset and rebuild the directory and apartment settings. The new administrator login goes to the owner or board, not to us.

Two-family and three-family houses

The studio in our attached brick two-family is entered through a door under the front stoop. Will a camera there show a visitor's face?

It can, but the spot under a stoop is dark and low, so the station has to be chosen and placed for it. We mount it at the height of a standing visitor's face rather than on the stoop's underside, choose a station with a wide lens and its own light, and check the picture with the door's own light on and off. If the visitor will stand farther out because of steps down to the door, the station goes on the side wall facing the approach.

Can a battery video doorbell simply be stuck over the old buzzer button so the upstairs floors can see who is there?

It can sit there, but it will not do what the upstairs households need. A battery doorbell usually talks to one app account and one Wi-Fi network, does not ring the existing indoor stations, and cannot release the door. In a Marine Park two- or three-family, each household needs its own call and its own way to open the door. In a Brownstoner forum thread from a multi-family owner who asked much the same thing, the setups that worked were wired, not a battery unit stuck over the old intercom.

We are gutting the upper floor of our semi-detached two-family. Which cables should go into the open walls now, even if the intercom comes later?

Pull a cable from the basement to the spot where the upstairs indoor station will hang, and a cable from the basement to each entrance, including the lower-level door. Use cable rated for the purpose, run it away from electrical wiring, and leave a few feet coiled at each end. A Brownstoner reply to a two-family owner gave the same advice and added one point we agree with: run a wire for a door release even if you do not want one yet, because a later owner may.

When is a store-bought wired doorbell the right answer for a Marine Park house, instead of an installed intercom?

When there is one household, one entrance visitors use, and no need to unlock the door from inside. Many one-family Tudors and bungalows fit that description, and a good wired doorbell on the existing transformer can be enough. Once there is a second household, a second door that visitors use, or a reason to open the door without walking to it, a doorbell stops being the right tool and an intercom with separate calls and a release takes over.

Our three-family still has three working bells, but the talk-back on every floor died years ago. Can the bells stay while only the voice part is replaced?

Rarely in a way worth doing. Bells and the talk-back usually share the same buttons and wires, so fixing one means touching the other. A three-button audio or video system replaces both at once, uses the old pairs if they test clean, and adds a release on each floor. If the owner's budget is tight, audio-only stations cost less than video and can be changed to video later on many systems.

We run a small office in our basement with its own door from the yard, and clients arrive there. Can that door ring only the office?

Yes. The office door gets its own station, wired to an indoor station in the office and, if you want, to a phone, and it rings nowhere else in the house. The release on that station opens only the office door, so a client never has a way into the rest of the house. If the office closes for the day, the station can be set to ring the household upstairs instead.

Our attached house's original front door was replaced with a steel door in the old opening. Can an electric strike go into it?

Usually, yes, but the strike goes into the frame, not the door, so the frame decides it. A steel door is often hung in a new steel frame or in the original wood jamb with a steel insert. We check the frame material, the latch type and the gap between the door and frame, then choose a strike made for that combination. The closer is adjusted so the door returns to latch every time.

We rent the upper floor of a Marine Park two-family and want video at our door. What should we ask the owner first?

Ask whether the owner wants a whole-house system or is willing to add a station for your floor, who will pay, and who will own the equipment when you move. A station for one floor of a two-family usually touches the shared entrance and the house's power, so the owner has to approve the work in writing. If the owner agrees, we quote the job to the owner and coordinate access with both households.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Marine Park

Quick answers about intercoms tend to describe a generic New York apartment building or a generic suburban house. Marine Park is neither, and several common claims miss what we find on its blocks.

"Marine Park is single-family, so a video doorbell covers it"

Summaries of the neighborhood describe quiet blocks of houses, and from there it is a short step to recommending a video doorbell for everyone.

The land records tell a fuller story. About 1,470 lots are two-family houses and another 112 are three-family houses, and listings regularly describe lower-level apartments with their own entrances. On the edges are 66- to 84-apartment elevator buildings and a 264-residence co-op. A doorbell serves one household at one door.

For a one-family house with a single entrance, a doorbell can be the right choice. For the roughly one in four Marine Park houses with more than one household, and for every apartment building, a system with separate calls and a release is what actually works.

"Intercom installation costs about $2,500"

Cost guides publish national averages, and HomeAdvisor's intercom guide gives an average of $2,500 with a low of $1,500 and a high of $15,000.

An average across homes and buildings of every size says little about any one Marine Park property. A two-family with a clean bell pair and two doors, and a Kings Highway building with seventy apartments and a riser that needs testing floor by floor, are not points on the same scale.

The useful number is the one on a written quote for your house or building, with each panel, station, strike, cable run and hour of labor listed separately. That is why we quote per job after a survey rather than publishing a single figure.

"Older buildings have to be completely rewired for video"

Many answers assume that an old building's intercom wire is useless for anything modern, so a video upgrade means opening every wall.

That is often not true. When the Park Vanderbilt co-op in Park Slope replaced its audio intercom with video, Security Sales & Integration reported, the installer reused the existing cable everywhere but two apartments. Dan Arnold of the installing company said: "By using the existing cable – with the exception of two units – we were able to save $20,000 on the job."

The same test applies in Marine Park's 1930s houses and its 1938 to 1952 elevator buildings. Each pair is tested first. Pairs that pass carry the new system; pairs that fail are replaced. The test result, not the building's age, decides how much new cable is needed.

"New York requires an intercom in every apartment building"

Answers often state the intercom rule as if it applied to every multiple dwelling regardless of size or age.

The Multiple Dwelling Law requirement reaches buildings with eight or more apartments, and its direct rule is for buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Almost every apartment building in Marine Park is older than that, so for them an intercom must be installed when a majority of tenants request one in writing. Houses and small walk-ups are outside the rule altogether.

What catches owners out is the other half: once an intercom is there, the Housing Maintenance Code expects it to work, and a dead one can be written up as a Class B violation. Planning a replacement before the old system fails is cheaper than answering a violation.

"Old Brooklyn neighborhoods have landmark rules for anything on the facade"

Because many older Brooklyn neighborhoods have historic districts, quick answers sometimes warn that any exterior device needs Landmarks approval.

The Historic Districts Council lists one designated landmark in Marine Park, the Hendrick I. Lott House on East 36th Street, designated in 1989 as an individual landmark. No historic district covers the house rows, the edge buildings or the commercial corridors.

That does not mean anything goes. A co-op's own rules, a condo's bylaws or a landlord's lease can limit what goes on a facade. But a Marine Park homeowner adding a door station does not need a Landmarks permit.

"A smart gadget on the old handset makes any intercom app-ready"

Forum threads, including several on Reddit, describe clipping a button-pusher onto an apartment handset or wiring a small relay board inside it so a phone can open the door.

Those tricks need an apartment handset and a building system to attach to. Most Marine Park two-families have a doorbell and a chime, not a handset, so there is nothing for the gadget to press. In a co-op, the board may not allow residents to modify building equipment at all.

Where phone answering is wanted, the cleaner answer is a door station or panel that supports an app directly, set up so the households that want an app have one and the households that do not still have a wall station.

"The number of apartments sets the size of the job"

Price estimators often ask only how many units a building has and scale the answer from there.

ButterflyMX's own guide for small buildings makes the opposite point: "a 10-unit building with one entrance may be a much smaller project than a 10-unit building with several controlled doors." In Marine Park, a 52-apartment garden court with many entrances and a 66-apartment elevator building with one lobby are very different jobs, even though their apartment counts are close.

Entrances, the condition of the wire between them and the apartments, and the doors themselves drive the work. A survey that counts doors as carefully as apartments is what keeps a quote honest.

FAQ

Marine Park Intercom Installation FAQ

After our co-op board signs the contract, how soon can the installation begin?
+

That depends on whether the equipment is in stock. Common panels and stations can usually be scheduled soon after the deposit. Some brands are special order and ship from overseas, and their shipping time is outside our control. In that case we order after the deposit, tell the board the arrival date once we have it, and book the installation only after the equipment is in hand.

What should we do around the house the day before the technician arrives?
+

Clear a path to the basement wall where the bell or intercom power is, and to the spots where each indoor station will go. Move anything stored against those walls or under the stoop. If a tenant lives in the lower level, let them know the time so the technician can reach their door and station. Have the Wi-Fi password ready if any station will use an app.

Should a new lobby panel be set into the wall or mounted on its surface?
+

Set into the wall is neater and harder to pry off, but it means cutting into the masonry or tile around the old panel. Surface mounting is faster and leaves the wall intact, which can matter in a 1950s lobby with original brick or tile. Many replacement panels come with a plate that covers the old opening. We show the board both options and their costs on the survey.

Who puts together the list of names for a brand-new lobby directory?
+

The board or managing agent, because they hold the shareholder and tenant records. We send a simple sheet with every apartment number, and the board fills in how each household wants to be listed. We program the panel from that sheet and give the board a copy, so later changes start from a correct list.

In our two-family both indoor stations can be heard from the shared stairs. Can each household's call make a different sound?
+

On most current systems, yes. Each indoor station can be given its own ring tone and volume, and a call from a second door can ring differently from a call from the front. That way the upstairs household learns to ignore the lower apartment's ring without having to check the screen every time.

What happens if one shareholder won't allow a new station in their apartment?
+

Access to apartments is a matter between the board and the shareholder, and we do not force it. We finish the rest of the building, leave the cable for that apartment ready and labeled at the wall, and note it in the handover. The station can be installed on a later visit once the board arranges access.

If someone damages the outdoor station, is the repair covered?
+

The one-year parts-only warranty covers AESS-supplied products that fail in normal use. Damage from impact, vandalism or a power surge is not normal use, so a replacement in that case is quoted as a repair. Choosing a station with a metal housing and mounting it securely makes that kind of damage less likely.

Can we see what the panel and stations will look like before we decide?
+

Yes. The quote comes with the manufacturer's product sheets and photos for each panel and station we propose, so the owner or board can see the finish, the size and the screen before approving. If a board wants to compare two options, we include both on the same quote.

If one apartment in our building loses power, does its intercom station go dead too?
+

It depends on the system. On most two-wire and networked systems, apartment stations are powered from the building's central supply, so they keep working when one apartment's breaker trips. Some video monitors need a local outlet, and those go dark with the apartment. We tell you which kind each station is before you choose.

We have a wall-mounted mailbox beside our front door. Where does the door station go?
+

On the other side of the door if there is a flat surface there, so the mail carrier and a visitor are not reaching for the same spot. If the mailbox side is the only flat section, the station goes above or below it at the right height, with enough space that opening the mailbox lid never covers the camera or the button.
