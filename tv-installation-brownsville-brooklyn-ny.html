The Neighborhood

Why Brownsville Properties Need TV Installation

A TV job in Brownsville is decided by two facts before anyone opens a toolbox: most households rent, and a large share of them rent from the city. The NYU Furman Center's profile of the district, updated in May 2026, puts the homeownership rate at 15.8 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent for the city as a whole, and counts 21.3 percent of rental units as public housing. Wikipedia's account of the neighborhood describes NYCHA as holding roughly 10,000 apartments in 18 developments and more than a hundred buildings, about a third of the housing stock. So on most Brownsville calls the person who wants the TV on the wall is not the person who owns the wall. That is not a reason to leave the screen on the floor. It is a reason to sort out permission, pick a fixing that can be cleanly undone, and choose a cable plan the building will accept, all before the drill comes out.

The buildings themselves are unusually varied for a neighborhood of about a square mile. The NYCHA developments range from the 27 six- and seven-story buildings of the Brownsville Houses, through mid-rise blocks such as Howard and Marcus Garvey, to towers like the three 22-story Hughes Apartments, the 16-story buildings of the Tilden Houses and the 24-story tower at Glenmore Plaza. South of Pitkin Avenue the side streets are lined with semi-detached multi-family rowhouses. In the western half there are the Nehemiah houses that East Brooklyn Congregations began building with a groundbreaking in Brownsville in October 1982, and near Ocean Hill there are limestone and brownstone townhouses. On top of all that, the city's Brownsville Plan, designated in 2018, set out more than 2,500 new affordable homes, and its buildings are still opening. Each generation leaves a different wall behind the TV.

For anyone hanging a screen, that range is the whole story. Forum posters who live in public housing describe the walls in older NYCHA buildings as "heavy plaster," and one remembers "actual cinderblock walls separating the apartments"; another warns that "you will need hammer drill for most areas of the apartment." Those descriptions match what we expect in buildings of that age: hard plaster over masonry, concrete block between apartments and poured concrete in the towers. A Nehemiah house from the 1980s is a different animal, a two- or three-bedroom single-family house of about 1,000 to 1,200 square feet with a full basement. The semi-detached rowhouses below Pitkin can have plaster on framing on one side of the living room and masonry on the other. And the newest buildings, such as the nine-story Brownsville Arts Center and Apartments that broke ground at 366 Rockaway Avenue in September 2025, are more likely to have drywall with management rules attached. A stud finder that works in one Brownsville apartment can be useless three blocks away.

Then there is the TV signal itself, which in Brownsville has a local history. On September 19, 2022, the city announced the citywide rollout of Big Apple Connect at the Langston Hughes Houses in Brownsville, and ABC7 reported that the Brownsville Houses had been one of the pilot sites. The program gives NYCHA households free in-home internet "including a modem and router" and "basic cable TV service, including a cable box and remote control," through agreements with Altice's Optimum and Charter's Spectrum. In practice that means many Brownsville apartments already have a cable box, a router and a coax outlet in a fixed spot, and the TV wall has to work with where that equipment lives. NYCHA's house rules add one more limit: "Residents may only install TV antennas inside their apartments." None of this stops a good installation, but it decides where the screen, the box and the cables can sensibly go.

The commercial side matters too. Pitkin Avenue was once Brooklyn's great shopping street; Wikipedia records 372 stores along it in 1942, including 43 menswear shops. Today the Pitkin Avenue Business Improvement District, founded in October 1993, covers 32 block faces and 212 ground-floor businesses, according to the city's Small Business Services directory, and Belmont Avenue has its own corridor of food sellers and small shops around the new Osborn Street Plaza. Barbers, clothing stores, takeout counters, clinics, churches and youth programs all ask for screens, and almost none of them own their buildings. We plan those jobs around the landlord, the shop's hours and the people walking past the screen, which is why the commercial section further down has its own compliance notes.

Six Brownsville facts that shape a TV job before we choose a bracket

  • Most homes are rented. The Furman Center puts Brownsville's homeownership rate at 15.8 percent in 2024, less than half the citywide 32.7 percent. Permission is part of nearly every residential job here, so we ask about it when you book.
  • Public housing is a fifth of the rental market. The same profile counts 21.3 percent of rental units as public housing. NYCHA's 2024 handbook says residents may not add "any fixtures or fittings" without prior written consent from the property management office, and that residents are responsible for damage.
  • The towers are tall and the walls are hard. NYCHA buildings here run from three stories at Howard Avenue to 24 at Glenmore Plaza. Hammer drills, masonry bits and anchors made for block or concrete are the norm, not the exception.
  • Many homes already have a cable box and router in place. Big Apple Connect was launched citywide at the Langston Hughes Houses in 2022 and supplies NYCHA households with a cable box, modem and router. The TV wall has to reach that equipment without a cord inside a wall.
  • Homeowners are concentrated in a few housing types. East Brooklyn Congregations built about 4,500 Nehemiah houses across East Brooklyn, starting in Brownsville in 1982, each with a full basement and a parking pad. Owners can open walls for an in-wall kit; renters usually cannot.
  • New buildings keep arriving. The Brownsville Plan called for more than 2,500 affordable homes, and 366 Rockaway Avenue alone will add 283 rentals, with completion expected in December 2027. New tenants moving into these buildings are some of our most frequent callers.

Same-Day Repair in Brownsville

Need Repair in Brownsville? A Bracket Working Loose From Block, a Screen Sitting Crooked, or an Old TV That Has to Come Down

The urgent TV calls we get from buildings like Brownsville's fall into a few patterns. The most common is a bracket that was fixed to a concrete block wall with anchors meant for drywall or solid concrete. Hollow block has a thin face with an empty core behind it; a plastic plug or a short screw driven into that face can feel tight on the day and still pull through months later, especially on a full-motion arm that someone swings out every evening. The second pattern is a screen that sits lower on one side because one anchor landed in a mortar joint instead of the block itself. The third is the move: a family leaving one apartment for another, with a TV still on the old wall and a lease that says the place has to be handed back in good condition.

If a screen is tilting or the bracket has moved, do not push it back into place and do not keep using the arm. If two adults can lift it safely, take the TV off the bracket, unplug it and lay it flat on a blanket, screen up. Then call (347) 934-8335 or send photos on WhatsApp. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. Putting the same TV back up properly is priced as a new mount on that wall, so a 55-inch screen re-hung on plaster is $179.99 plus $45. If the old holes are too close to where the new fixings need to go, we move the bracket slightly rather than reuse damaged holes, and we tell you before we start. If the problem turns out to be the wall itself, a crack running through the plaster or block that crumbles when touched, we say so plainly; in a NYCHA apartment that is a repair for the development, and you can report it through NYCHA's normal channels before anything goes back on that wall.

We do not patch or paint walls ourselves. When a TV comes down at move-out, the holes it leaves are for a painter, a handyman or your landlord's maintenance staff to fill, and we tell you that up front so the take-down is booked with the patching already planned.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Brownsville's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every TV call we take from Brownsville. They differ in three ways that matter more than any brand of bracket: what the wall is made of, how the TV gets to the apartment, and who has to agree before a hole goes in.

The NYCHA tower

Elevator buildings from 13 to 25 stories, such as Hughes, Tilden, Seth Low and Glenmore Plaza. Concrete and block walls, long corridors and a development office that controls alterations.

The NYCHA mid-rise and walk-up

The six- and seven-story Brownsville Houses, the three-story buildings of Howard Avenue and Van Dyke, and the rehabbed four-story tenements on Tapscott Street. Stairs, plaster and masonry.

The Nehemiah house

Two- and three-bedroom single-family houses from the 1980s and later, mostly in the western half, with full basements, parking pads and owners who can decide for themselves.

The semi-detached rowhouse

The multi-unit rowhouses south of Pitkin Avenue, often with an owner on one floor and tenants on others, and walls that change from framing to masonry across one room.

The new affordable building

Brownsville Plan buildings and the newer towers on NYCHA campuses, such as Van Dyke III, with drywall interiors, lottery tenants and a management office with rules.

The older townhouse

Limestone and brownstone townhouses toward Ocean Hill, with plaster on lath or on brick, parlor rooms, and sometimes a decorative fireplace where the TV wants to go.

The NYCHA tower: hard walls, a long carry and a development office with a say

Brownsville's towers hold a large share of its apartments. Wikipedia's list of the developments includes the three 22-story buildings of the Hughes Apartments, the eight 16-story buildings of the Tilden Houses, the 17- and 18-story Seth Low Houses, the 13-story buildings at the Howard Houses, the 14-story buildings at Marcus Garvey and Van Dyke, and the 25-story tower at the Woodson Houses. For a TV installer these buildings share three traits. The walls between apartments and along the corridors are usually masonry, which holds a screen very well but needs a hammer drill and the right anchor. The carry depends on the elevator, so we ask which car serves your line and whether it has been reliable that week. And the development, not the resident, decides whether a fixture can be added, which is why the permission section further down matters most for people in these buildings.

Inside a tower apartment the living room usually has one long wall shared with the next apartment, one wall on the outside of the building with the windows, and one interior wall that may be plaster on block or, in renovated units, drywall. The long shared wall is usually the best TV wall: it faces the sofa, it is solid, and it is far from the window glare. The catch is cabling. A block or concrete wall gives no way to fish cables inside it, and the cable outlet and the Big Apple Connect box are often on a different wall entirely. So the plan in most tower apartments is a fixed or tilting bracket on the shared wall, the cable box on a console directly below the screen, and a short paintable cord cover from the bracket down to the console, painted to match. That arrangement adds nothing structural to the building and comes off cleanly at move-out.

The NYCHA mid-rise and walk-up: the stairs come first

Not every NYCHA building has an elevator. The Howard Avenue houses are three stories, the Van Dyke Houses mix three-story and 14-story buildings, and the Tapscott Street Rehab is a group of eight four-story tenements rebuilt for public housing. In walk-up buildings like these the first question is the stairs. A 65-inch TV in its box is long and awkward on a narrow stair with a turn at each landing, and a 75-inch box is longer still. The safest way to get a screen up is in its original packaging, so if your TV is still being delivered, ask the store to leave it boxed and keep the foam until we arrive.

The walls in these older buildings are usually plaster, and plaster here is the hard kind that sits over masonry rather than on wood lath. That is good news for holding a TV, because the fixing can go through the plaster into the block or brick behind it, but it means a hammer drill, a slow start so the plaster face does not chip, and an anchor sized for masonry. We price the wall by what the pilot hole shows behind the plaster. If it is plaster on framing, the add-on is $45; if it is brick, it is $65; and if it is block or poured concrete, we tell you the price from the photos before you book. In the six-story Brownsville Houses on Rockaway Avenue the elevator helps with the carry, but the walls follow the same pattern.

The Nehemiah house: an owner's decision and a basement full of options

The Nehemiah houses are the clearest example of homeownership in Brownsville. The Gotham Center's history of the program describes two- and three-bedroom single-family houses of about 1,000 and 1,200 square feet, sold for $30,000 and $35,000 to working families, with carpeted living rooms, full basements, double-glazed windows, natural gas furnaces and a parking pad for each house. Many are still owned by the families who bought them, or by their children. For a TV that changes everything. An owner does not need a landlord's consent, can choose to open a wall for an in-wall power and cable kit, and can decide whether the cable box lives in the living room or the basement.

Because these houses are modest in size, the living room TV is often seen from the dining area as well as the sofa, and the full basement is frequently a family room or a teenager's space with a second screen. We look at both. Upstairs, if the TV wall is drywall over wood studs, the job starts at the base price of $179.99 with nothing added for the wall, and an in-wall kit at $169 hides the power and cables completely. Downstairs, basement walls are usually masonry, so the cables run in a cord cover or the TV sits on a console. The carpet in the living room is the other thing we plan around: drop cloths go down before the drill does, and the dust from a pilot hole is vacuumed as we go, not at the end.

The semi-detached rowhouse: an owner downstairs and a wall that changes halfway across

Below Pitkin Avenue the streets are lined with semi-detached and attached multi-unit rowhouses. Many are owner-occupied on one floor and rented on the others, so the tenant who wants a TV upstairs may have the landlord living directly below. We suggest a short text to the owner before the visit: which wall, how many holes, and whether the wires will go inside the wall or in a cover. Owners who are asked first rarely object, and the answer protects the tenant's deposit when they move out.

The walls in these houses are less predictable than in a NYCHA building. The wall shared with the attached house is usually masonry under its plaster; the side wall facing the gap or driveway may be masonry too; and interior partitions are often framed, with plaster or later drywall over them. In one living room that can mean three different fixings and three different prices. We scan, knock and then drill one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it, because the dust tells the story: white powder for plaster, red for brick, grey grit for block, and a sudden drop into nothing for a hollow framed wall. If the cheapest wall to mount on is not the one you first chose, we tell you, and you choose.

The new affordable building: drywall, a lottery lease and an office that wants notice

The Brownsville Plan and the NYCHA campus infill have brought a generation of new buildings: Van Dyke III, a 12-story building with 180 affordable homes and a childcare center on the Van Dyke Houses campus; the senior and family buildings along Livonia Avenue; and the nine-story Brownsville Arts Center and Apartments rising at 366 Rockaway Avenue, with 283 rentals for households earning 30 to 70 percent of area median income. Buildings of this generation usually have drywall interiors, and the wall behind your TV may be on wood studs, on metal studs or, near the corridor and the shafts, on block.

The management offices in these buildings often have rules about contractors: working hours, insurance paperwork, and whether a wall can be opened. A paintable cord cover needs only a few small screws and is the option most offices accept without discussion. An in-wall kit needs two neat openings in the drywall, which some offices allow and some do not. We ask you to check before the visit and, if the office wants a certificate of insurance naming the building, we send it ahead. New tenants are often in the middle of a move when they call, so we also book these jobs for after the furniture arrives, when the sofa is where it will stay and the TV height can be judged from it.

The older townhouse: plaster, brick and the fireplace question

Toward Ocean Hill, at Brownsville's northern edge, there are limestone and brownstone townhouses older than almost everything else in the neighborhood. Their walls are plaster, sometimes on wood lath and sometimes directly on brick, and the party walls are solid masonry. Brownstoner's forum has a long thread on exactly this kind of house, where one poster explains that party walls in older Brooklyn houses often have no studs at all, just plaster over brick, and another reassures the owner: "First, you will be amazed at how light the TV actually is." Both points hold. A party wall of brick is an excellent place for a TV if the anchors go into the brick and not the mortar, and a modern flat screen is far lighter than the furniture these walls have carried for a century.

Many of these rooms have a fireplace, often decorative now, and the chimney breast above it is the obvious spot for a screen. Above-fireplace mounting adds $130 on our list, and if the breast is brick, the brick add-on of $65 applies as well. The bigger issue is height: a screen over a mantel sits high, and a tilting bracket brings the picture down toward the sofa. If the fireplace still works, we look at whether heat rises over the face of the breast, and if the cables cannot go inside the masonry, they come down the side of the breast in a painted cover or drop behind a console placed beside the hearth.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Brownsville Apartment

These notes describe how our technicians work in apartments and houses like Brownsville's. They are our practice, not an account of any particular address.

Ask about the paperwork at the door. In a NYCHA apartment or a managed building, the first thing the technician asks is whether the consent for the mount has been given, and by whom. If it has not, we can still set the TV up on its stand, connect the box and sort out the sound, and come back for the mount once you have the answer. Nobody drills a wall the resident is not allowed to drill.

Find the equipment first. Before choosing a wall, the technician finds the coax outlet, the cable box and the router. In many Big Apple Connect apartments they sit together by the entrance or in a corner of the living room, and the cable plan follows from that spot. A TV that is twelve feet from the box needs a longer HDMI run along the floor line or a box moved to a console below the screen.

Knock along the wall at screen height. A hollow drum points to drywall on studs; a dull, hard knock that hurts the knuckles points to plaster on masonry or bare block. On hard walls a scanner is mostly useful for finding wiring and pipes, not studs, so the knock and the pilot hole matter more than the beep.

One hidden pilot hole. The technician drills one small hole where the bracket plate will cover it. Grey grit and a sudden give mean hollow block, and the anchors have to land in the solid webs; steady grey dust means solid block or concrete. If the result changes the price you were quoted, you hear it before any more drilling.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen, and you sit where you watch. In a small tower living room the sofa is often closer to the wall than people think, so the screen usually goes lower than they expect. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket on concrete costs a wall.

Wall by Wall

Block, Concrete, Plaster, Brick or Drywall: What Holds a TV on a Brownsville Wall

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, assumes drywall over wood studs and a TV up to 55 inches, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. In Brownsville that wall is common in Nehemiah houses and some new buildings and rare almost everywhere else. Each wall below needs different bits, different anchors and a different amount of time, which is why the add-ons exist. They are listed so you can add up the total yourself before anyone visits.

Hollow concrete block. This is the wall between apartments in many NYCHA buildings and in some newer construction near the shafts and corridors. A concrete block is mostly air: two thin faces joined by webs, with open cores between them. An anchor driven into the thin face over a core grips almost nothing, and an expanding plug can crack the face and leave a loose hole. The fixings that last are anchors designed for hollow masonry, which spread behind the face, or fixings placed into the solid webs and the filled parts of the wall. On a full-motion arm, which levers the TV's weight onto the top anchors every time it is pulled out, we use every fixing point the wall plate offers. Because block walls vary so much, we confirm the price for block from your photos and tell you before you book.

Poured concrete. Some tower walls, columns and the edges around windows are solid poured concrete. It is the strongest wall you can mount on and the slowest to drill, and the one thing that stops a hole dead is steel reinforcing bar inside it. If the bit hits steel, we stop, move the hole a short distance and re-drill rather than force through, which is why the bracket's slotted holes and a little planning matter. Concrete also cannot be opened for cables at all, so the cable plan is always a cover or a console. Like block, poured concrete is priced from your photos before you book.

Plaster (+$45). Older walk-ups, rowhouses and townhouses have plaster walls. Plaster on wood lath is hard on the surface and crumbly underneath, so a plug that grips only the plaster slowly loosens; the fixing has to reach the stud or use a toggle rated for the load. Plaster over masonry is very strong once the anchor reaches the block or brick behind it. The add-on is $45 when the plaster sits on framing; when the pilot hole shows brick behind it, the brick price applies instead, and we say which before we continue.

Brick or stone (+$65). Party walls in the rowhouses below Pitkin and the townhouses toward Ocean Hill are often solid brick under their plaster, and a few living rooms have exposed brick or a stone feature wall. Brick holds a TV well when the anchor sits in the brick and not the mortar joint, which is softer and can crumble around a fixing. We use a hammer drill and sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, keep holes away from the edges of each brick, and on soft old brick spread the load across more fixings.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). The upper floors of many Nehemiah houses and some renovated apartments have ordinary drywall over wood studs. A lag bolt into the center of two studs is the textbook fixing, and the job is priced at the base of $179.99. Above a fireplace (+$130). In the townhouses near Ocean Hill and a few older houses elsewhere, the chimney breast is the most tempting TV wall. Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

After "put it on the wall," the most common request is "make the wires disappear." There is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to drill a hole behind the TV, push the TV's own power cord into the wall and pull it out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be run inside walls, because a cord there can be pinched by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and cannot be inspected. A question on the Mike Holt electrical forum about which in-wall TV power devices are acceptable in New York City shows how often installers have to check this for the city specifically.

The right way, on a hollow framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives you a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI and coax. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a hollow cavity between the two openings, so it suits drywall in a Nehemiah house or a new building, and some framed plaster walls, and it does not suit block, concrete or brick.

On masonry, which covers most of Brownsville's NYCHA apartments, the honest choices are a paintable cord cover or furniture. A cord cover is $59, runs neatly on the surface from the bracket to the console or the outlet, and is fixed with a few small screws or a strong adhesive strip, which matters in a building where the handbook frowns on alterations. Where the TV sits directly above a console, the cables can often drop straight behind the furniture and never be seen at all. In a rental, a cord cover leaves a handful of tiny holes or none, and an in-wall kit leaves two openings that will need patching when you leave, which is exactly the kind of difference your landlord may want to hear about first.

Renters, NYCHA and Owners

Permission First: Mounting a TV in a NYCHA Apartment or a Brownsville Rental

With roughly five in six Brownsville homes rented, permission comes up on most of our residential calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public rules say and what we have learned from doing this work, so you can ask the right question before you book.

In a NYCHA apartment, read the handbook's alteration rule. NYCHA's 2024 Resident Handbook says residents "are not allowed to alter their apartments or add any fixtures or fittings in their apartments without prior written consent from their property management office (and residents will be responsible for any damage that occurs)." The shorter "Highlights of House Rules" adds that residents "may not damage, deface or destroy the apartment or any NYCHA property." Neither document mentions TV brackets by name. A wall-mounted TV is fixed to the wall, so the cautious reading is that it falls under the consent rule, and the practical step is to ask the property management office at your development, in writing, before the visit, and keep the reply. We have no inside channel at NYCHA and cannot give consent on its behalf; only your development can.

What to put in the request. Keep it short: your apartment, the wall, the size of the TV, the number and size of the holes a bracket needs, the fact that the wires will run in a surface cover and nothing will be cut into the wall, and the name and license number of the installer. A request that answers the obvious questions is easier to approve than a one-line "can I mount my TV?" If the office says no, or does not answer by the day of the visit, the TV goes on its stand instead.

In a private rental, start with the lease. Brick Underground's "Realty Bites" column put the question to New York real estate professionals. An attorney, Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas, said a ban on hanging things has to be in the lease to be enforceable. A property manager, Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group, drew the line most landlords use: "If the hole is larger than the size of a nail, then it's considered damage." A TV bracket leaves four to six holes larger than a nail hole, so in practice you should tell your landlord, get a yes in writing, and plan to have the holes patched before you move out.

Protect the deposit. New York's security deposit law requires a landlord to give an itemized statement of anything kept from the deposit within 14 days of move-out, and does not allow deductions for ordinary wear and tear. Bracket holes that are not patched can be treated as damage. The simple protection is a dated photo of the wall before the mount, a written yes from the landlord, and the holes filled and painted before the final walk-through. We take the TV down at move-out for $85; the patching is for a painter or the landlord's maintenance staff.

Choosing not to drill. If your consent has not come through, your lease is strict, or you expect to move within the year, a TV on its own stand on a sturdy console is a legitimate choice, not a compromise. It is $119.99 on our Brooklyn list and includes connecting the cable box and devices; a soundbar placed on the console is $79. For a heavy TV on a stand in a home with small children, we recommend a tip-over strap fixed to the furniture rather than the wall.

If you own the house. Owners of Nehemiah houses and rowhouses decide for themselves, and owners who rent out a floor sometimes ask us to mount a TV in the rental unit between tenants. One well-placed bracket, a cord cover or an in-wall kit and a line in the lease saying tenants use the existing mount stops five tenants drilling five sets of holes over the years.

Signal and Setup

Cable Boxes, Big Apple Connect and Antennas: Getting a Picture on a Brownsville Wall

Mounting the screen is half the job. The other half is getting the picture to it, and in Brownsville that often starts with equipment the household did not choose. Big Apple Connect, the city program announced at the Langston Hughes Houses in 2022, provides NYCHA households with a modem and router and a basic cable package "including a cable box and remote control," through Optimum or Spectrum depending on the development. Private buildings and houses sign up with a provider themselves. Whichever it is, the cable box has to sit within reach of the coax outlet and within an HDMI cable's reach of the TV.

That shapes the layout more than people expect. If the coax comes out of the wall by the front door and the best TV wall is across the room, there are three honest options: move the TV to the wall with the coax, run the HDMI along the baseboard in a low-profile cover, or ask the provider to relocate the outlet, which is their job and their decision in a NYCHA building. What we will not do is drill through a NYCHA wall or floor to move a cable outlet ourselves. In a house you own, we can run a cable along the basement ceiling and up into the living room, and in a business with landlord approval we can route cable through a dropped ceiling.

Wi-Fi is the other half of setup. A smart TV streaming in high definition wants a steady signal, and block and concrete walls absorb Wi-Fi far more than drywall does. If the router sits in the hallway and the TV is in a back bedroom behind two masonry walls, buffering is likely. Sometimes moving the router to a central shelf solves it; sometimes the TV is better on a wired connection. In a NYCHA apartment the router belongs to the program provider, so we can reposition it within reach of its cable but not rewire the building.

Antennas are the last piece. NYCHA's rules say residents "may only install TV antennas inside their apartments" and "may not install antennas on the exterior of the building." A flat indoor antenna can be placed behind or beside a wall-mounted screen or near a window, and the TV can scan for free over-the-air channels. Reception in a tall tower depends heavily on which way the apartment faces. We set it up, run the scan with you, and if a channel you want does not come in, we tell you rather than leaving you to discover it after we have gone.

When the TV is already mounted and only the setup is a mess, a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour on our list, then 30-minute blocks. That covers the cable box, the apps, the Wi-Fi connection, the soundbar and the remote, with you there to enter your own passwords.

Compliance notes for Brownsville businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, wall-mounted objects with a leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor must not stick out more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a clinic corridor or a waiting room can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls or ceilings. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable, or the cord runs in a surface cover to a reachable outlet.
  • Insurance paperwork before the visit. Commercial landlords, churches with property committees and managed buildings often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner. We send it ahead so the job is not held at the door.
  • Exits, sprinklers and alarms stay clear. We do not mount screens where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and we keep cables off the floor in any route people use to leave.
  • Rated hardware with a margin. Every bracket we install is rated for more than the TV's weight, and on block or brick we use anchors rated for the load. A screen in a barbershop or a youth program will be leaned on and bumped; it needs that margin more than one in a living room.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Brownsville: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brownsville residents, owners and businesses describe when they call: consent from a development office, block walls that swallow anchors, a cable box in the wrong corner, carpet in a Nehemiah living room, a smoke detector on the wall they wanted, and shops with a landlord upstairs. Each is answered here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

NYCHA apartments and development rules

The smoke and carbon monoxide alarm in our NYCHA living room is mounted right where the TV should go. Can your technician move it?

No, and nobody but NYCHA should. Smoke and CO alarms in public housing are the development's equipment, the handbook asks residents to cooperate with NYCHA on them, and moving or covering one can leave the apartment without protection. Keep the screen clear of the alarm rather than the other way round: a TV a little lower, or shifted to one side of the same wall, usually works. If the only sensible TV position really does conflict with the alarm, ask the property management office whether they will relocate it, and book the mount for after they have.

We are in one of the Tilden Houses towers and our living room's long wall is the one we share with the next apartment. Will drilling it bother them, and is it strong enough?

It is usually the strongest wall in the apartment: masonry between units holds a TV very well when the anchors are right. Drilling it is loud for a few minutes on both sides, so a word to your neighbor beforehand helps, and we do all the drilling in one short burst. The holes go only as deep as the anchor needs, nowhere near through. The cable plan matters more than the noise: a shared masonry wall cannot hide cables, so they run in a painted cover or drop to a console directly below.

My development office has not answered our consent request yet, and the TV arrives this week. What can we do in the meantime?

Set it up on its stand. That is $119.99 on our Brooklyn list and includes connecting the cable box, the apps and the soundbar, so the TV is fully working the same day. When the written consent comes through, the mount is booked as a separate visit at the normal wall price, and because the cable box and devices are already connected, that second visit is quicker. What we will not do is mount it first and hope the answer is yes.

We got our keys to a new apartment on the Van Dyke campus for next month. What can we sort out before moving day?

Quite a lot. Ask the management office now whether TVs may be wall-mounted, whether openings for an in-wall kit are allowed, and whether contractors need a certificate of insurance or set hours. Find out where the coax outlet and router will be, because that decides the best TV wall. Measure the sofa wall and the distance to the window. Then book the visit for after the furniture is in, so the height can be judged from where you will actually sit.

Block, concrete and the walls that swallow anchors

A friend put our TV up on the block wall with the plastic plugs that came in the bracket box, and now the top corner is pulling away. Is the wall ruined?

Almost never. Plastic plugs in hollow concrete block grip only the thin face over an empty core, and a TV's weight slowly pulls them out. The block around them is usually fine. Take the TV down if it can be done safely, and we re-fix the bracket with anchors made for hollow block, set into the solid parts of the wall and shifted far enough from the old holes that the new fixings bite clean material. Take-down is $85 if we do it; the re-mount is priced as a new mount on that wall.

Our tower apartment has a column of poured concrete beside the window. Can the bracket go on that instead of the block wall?

Often, if the column is wide enough for the bracket plate and the screen does not end up facing straight into the window glare. Poured concrete is the strongest fixing in the building, but it is slow to drill and may hide reinforcing steel; if the bit meets steel we move the hole slightly rather than force it. The trade-off is cables: a concrete column can never hide them, and a column near the window is usually far from the coax outlet, so plan on a cover running down to a console.

Nehemiah houses and owner-occupied homes

Our Nehemiah house still has wall-to-wall carpet in the living room. Will the ladder and drilling mark it, and can the cables run under the carpet?

Drop cloths go down before anything else, the ladder stands on them, and the dust from the pilot hole and the bracket holes is vacuumed as we go. Carpet is usually left cleaner than bare wood, because nothing drops straight onto it. Cables should not run under carpet: they get crushed underfoot and a power cord under a rug is a fire risk. If the TV wall is drywall, an in-wall kit at $169 takes the cables inside the wall to a low outlet; if not, a cover runs down the wall to the console.

There is an old coax line coming up through the living room floor from our basement. Can the new TV use it?

Possibly. A line from the 1980s or 1990s may still carry a cable signal if it runs to where the provider's service comes in, but it may also be cut, spliced or connected to nothing. We trace it from the basement end and test it before relying on it. If it is good, the cable box connects there and the TV wall plan follows; if it is dead, the provider runs a new line or we route one along the basement ceiling in a house you own.

We rent the top floor of a semi-detached house below Pitkin Avenue, and the owner lives downstairs. Who should book the TV job?

Either can, but the owner has to agree to the holes. The smoothest version is that the tenant books, the owner says yes by text with the wall named, and both know the TV is coming down and the holes are being filled at move-out. Some owners prefer to book it themselves, choose the wall and keep the bracket with the apartment. We price it the same way either way.

Shops, clinics and programs

Our store on Pitkin Avenue wants a screen by the entrance that shows customers our own security cameras. Is that a TV job or a camera job?

Both, and we do both. A public-view monitor is a TV or monitor mounted where customers see themselves on camera as they walk in, fed from the store's recorder. Ask whoever manages the room first; in a NYCHA community room or a church hall that is the office or the property committee. If they will not allow a wall mount, a TV on its own stand on a locked cabinet or a sturdy table is $119.99 on our list and moves out with you. If they agree to a mount, we send them the certificate of insurance, mount it above head height and run the cable to a lockable shelf so the input and settings stay put between sessions.

The only free wall in our Belmont Avenue shop backs onto the store next door, and both belong to the same landlord. Can we mount on it?

Usually, with the landlord's yes. A wall between two stores is often masonry and holds a screen well. Brownsville TV Installation FAQ

What exactly does the $179.99 base price cover?
+

It covers the labor to mount a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs: reading the wall, fixing the bracket, hanging and leveling the screen and connecting it. The bracket is sold separately, and plaster, brick, a fireplace, cable concealment and soundbars are listed add-ons.

Do you bring the anchors for a block or concrete wall, or should I buy them?
+

Leave the anchors to us. The plastic plugs packed with many brackets are meant for drywall, and the right fixing for hollow block, poured concrete or brick depends on what the pilot hole finds, so the technician brings anchors for each kind of wall.

How far ahead should I ask my NYCHA development office for consent?
+

As soon as you know which wall you want. We cannot say how long a property management office will take, so ask in writing before booking the mount, and if the visit date arrives first, have the TV set up on its stand and add the mount later.

Can the drilling dust set off our smoke alarm?
+

Fine masonry dust near an alarm can occasionally trigger one. We vacuum at the bit while drilling, keep the drilling short, and never cover, unplug or disable a smoke or carbon monoxide alarm, which in a NYCHA apartment belongs to the development.

Is my TV too heavy for a hollow block wall?
+

Usually not. A modern flat screen on a fixed or tilting bracket is well within what hollow block holds with anchors made for it. We check the weight from the model number, and on weak or damaged block we may advise against a heavy full-motion arm.

Is it safe to hang a TV on the wall above a bed?
+

Yes, when the bracket is rated for the TV and anchored into the real wall material. Over a bed we prefer a fixed or tilting bracket rather than an arm that gets bumped, and the cables run in a cover so nothing hangs down behind the headboard.

Is there a charge for looking at my photos and telling me the price?
+

No. Send the wall, the TV's model sticker and the spot for the cable box, and we tell you the wall we expect, the add-ons and the total at no charge.

Will you connect a game console as well as the cable box?
+

Yes. We connect the console, the cable box and any streaming device to the TV and check each input before we leave. Signing in to game or streaming accounts needs your own passwords, so have them ready on the day.

Can a soundbar hang without drilling extra holes in a rental wall?
+

Often. Some soundbars can hang from a bracket that attaches to the TV mount rather than the wall, and the simplest option of all is placing it on the console for $79. A soundbar mounted under the screen is $129 on our list.

When I move out, do I keep the bracket?
+

Yes, a bracket you bought is yours. Taking the TV down is $85 on our list, and the bracket comes off the wall with it if you ask. The holes it leaves are for a painter or your landlord's maintenance staff to fill, since we do not patch or paint walls.

Can a store TV be secured so nobody can lift it off the bracket?
+

Many brackets accept a padlock or security screws that stop the screen being lifted off its wall plate. Tell us it is a retail or program screen when you book and we will check that your bracket supports it, or suggest one that does.

Can the same visit add a video doorbell or security camera?
+

We install both, quoted separately from the TV. In a NYCHA apartment the handbook allows a video doorbell only if it is wireless and fixed to the door with adhesive, with no holes, so ask us about that kind. Homeowners and businesses can choose wired cameras.
