Why Orange County Properties Need Dahua Cameras Built for Distance

Orange County is big, and most of it is not built up. The Census Bureau puts the county's land area at 812.32 square miles and its 2020 population base at 401,312, which works out to 494 people per square mile — a small fraction of the density of the boroughs our Bronx crews come from. Between the cities of Newburgh, Middletown and Port Jervis sit villages, subdivisions, horse and vegetable farms, orchards and a great deal of open ground. These are the questions Orange County owners and site managers actually put to us, grouped by the problem behind them. Each is answered in full here rather than in a one-line FAQ, because on a big property the details are the job.

Distance, Cable and Radio Links on Big Lots

How far from the recorder can a Dahua camera sit on ordinary network cable?

On standard Power over Ethernet, about 100 metres — roughly 328 feet — of cable between the camera and the switch or recorder port that powers it. That limit is set by the Ethernet standard, not by Dahua, and it counts every foot of cable: up the wall, along the soffit, down into the basement and across to the rack. On a farmhouse with a barn 100 yards away, the straight-line distance may be under the limit while the cable route is well over it.

Dahua's own answer for longer runs is extended PoE. Its published figures are 300 metres at 100 Mbps with up to 25.5 watts, or 800 metres at 10 Mbps with 13 watts, between an ePoE camera and an ePoE switch or recorder port. The 300-metre mode carries a high-resolution camera comfortably; the 800-metre mode suits a single modest camera at the end of a very long run. Both need Dahua ePoE hardware at each end, so we plan them per camera rather than assume every port can do it.

Beyond those numbers there are three options: put a small switch with its own power partway along the run, change to fiber, or change to radio. Which is right depends on whether there is already power at the far end, whether a trench is cheap or expensive on that ground, and how many cameras need to come back from the same place.

When does a point-to-point radio make more sense than trenching to a barn or gate?

When the ground between the two buildings is expensive or impossible to dig — a paved yard, a drainage ditch, a field in crop, rock, or a driveway that would have to be cut — and there is a clear line of sight between them. A pair of 5 GHz bridge radios, one at the house and one at the barn, behaves like an invisible network cable. Dahua's PFWB5 bridge pair is recommended for links of up to 3.11 miles (5 km), and its transmission range also includes long-range outdoor bridges, so the distances on a typical Orange County farm are well inside what the equipment is built for.

What decides success is the path, not the distance. Radios need a clear zone around the straight line between them, and that zone has to stay clear in summer: an orchard in full leaf, corn at its height or a new hay barn can block a link that worked perfectly in March. We survey the path, mount both radios high enough to clear what will grow, and test throughput with every camera streaming before the cameras are fixed in place.

A trench still wins when the barn needs power anyway — an electrician digging for a new circuit can lay our conduit in the same trench for very little extra — or when the link must never depend on weather or line of sight. Many properties end up with both: fiber or copper in the trench that was already being dug, and a radio to the one building that would have needed a second trench.

Is fiber worth it between a warehouse and its guard booth or a second building?

Usually yes. Fiber carries no electricity, so it cannot bring a lightning surge or a ground-potential difference from one building into the other, and its distance limits are measured in kilometres rather than metres. On a distribution site where the guard booth sits at the truck gate and the recorder sits in an interior server room, fiber is the cleanest way to join them, and the same strand pair can carry the booth's computer and phone as well as its cameras.

The trade-off is that fiber does not carry power. Every building at the far end of a fiber run needs its own outlet, its own PoE switch and its own battery backup. On a new warehouse that is rarely a problem, because the booth and the outbuildings are wired for power from the start; on an older farm it can be the deciding cost.

If you are already pulling fiber for the building's data network, tell us before the cable is ordered. Adding two strands for cameras to a fiber run that is going in anyway costs far less than a second run later, and it keeps the camera network separate from the office network.

How do you get power to a camera at a gate or field edge with no outlet nearby?

There are four honest answers, in order of preference. The best is power over the camera's own cable, from a switch in a building that already has power, which works whenever the cable route is within PoE or extended-PoE range. The second is a new outlet at the gate, installed by a licensed electrician, feeding a weatherproof enclosure with a small PoE switch, a surge protector and a battery. The third is a radio and camera at the gate powered from that same outlet.

The fourth is solar, and it is the one most often oversold. A camera, a radio and a modem running around the clock draw power through the long nights of December and January, when an Orange County panel collects the least. A properly sized solar setup is a large panel and a large battery, not the small kit sold for a trail camera. For temporary needs — a construction phase, a harvest season, a fence line under dispute — our solar surveillance trailer in Orange County is often the better answer.

Whichever route you choose, we put the camera where the vehicle has to slow down, not where the outlet happens to be. A gate camera that sees only taillights at 40 miles an hour is a camera in the wrong place.

Power Outages, Lightning and Hudson Valley Storms

How long can the cameras keep recording through an O&R outage?

As long as the batteries last, and that is a number we size rather than guess. The recorder, the PoE switch powering the cameras, any radios and the internet modem all go on battery backup together; if any one of them is left off, the system stops recording or stops reporting the moment the power drops. A rack UPS sized for a recorder and a modest switch typically carries a small farm or home system through the short outages that make up most interruptions, and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in a long one so the hard drives are not damaged.

The July 2026 storms that O&R reported affected more than 47,000 customers over several days, and some Warwick and Goshen customers were still waiting on the fifth day. No ordinary UPS bridges an outage that long. If the property has a standby generator, we connect the camera rack to a circuit the generator carries and size the UPS to cover only the seconds or minutes while the generator starts. If it does not, we tell you plainly how many hours your batteries give, and set the recorder to restart and resume recording by itself when power returns.

Every outbuilding with its own switch or radio needs its own battery. A barn camera fed from a barn switch with no UPS goes dark even if the house rack is still running, and that is the first place we look when a customer says "only some cameras came back."

What protects a long outdoor cable run from a lightning surge?

Bonding, grounding and surge protectors, in that order. Lightning rarely strikes a camera directly; the damage comes from the surge induced in a long copper cable by a nearby strike, or from the difference in ground voltage between two buildings during a strike. A cable that runs from a house to a barn connects two separate grounding systems, and that is exactly the path a surge takes. Severe thunderstorms are a summer fixture here — the National Weather Service recorded two across the Lower Hudson Valley in the first eight days of July 2025.

On every cable that leaves a building we fit a network surge protector where the cable enters, bonded to that building's grounding system. Surge-protection makers are specific that the protector's ground should come from the building's own grounding system rather than from a separate rod at the camera. Exposed runs go in conduit, shielded cable gets its shield bonded at one end, and cameras on poles are bonded to the pole's ground.

Between buildings, fiber or a radio link removes the copper path altogether, which is why we lean on them for barns and outbuildings in open country. No protection is absolute, and surge damage is listed among the exclusions in most equipment warranties, but a properly bonded system turns a lightning season from a string of dead ports into the occasional replaced camera.

Seeing Anything at Night Where There Are No Streetlights

Should a dark field or trailer yard get full-colour cameras or infrared?

It depends on how dark "dark" is. Dahua's full-colour cameras use a very wide aperture and a sensitive sensor to keep colour at night, and the warm-light versions add their own white LEDs for scenes with little light. That works well under a yard light, at a lit loading dock or on a porch. Out in a field with no light at all, the picture depends entirely on how far the camera's own LEDs reach, and a full-colour camera cannot see the infrared that an IR camera relies on.

Infrared cameras flood the scene with light you cannot see and record in black and white. In true darkness, as one security supplier puts it, an infrared camera is the safer choice, and its IR reach is usually longer than a white-light camera's useful range. The cost is colour: you will know a pickup came through the gate, but not whether it was red or silver.

Most Orange County sites get a mix. Colour cameras go where there is already light or where colour identifies something — the dock doors, the farm stand, the gate with a light on it. Infrared or long-range cameras go where nothing is lit and the job is to see movement across open ground. Where the owner can add a motion-activated floodlight, a colour camera becomes the better choice almost everywhere it can see.

Do the white lights on a full-colour camera have to stay on all night?

No. Many current Dahua full-colour models offer what Dahua calls Smart Dual Light: the camera watches in infrared, and only switches its white light on when it detects a person or a vehicle. The scene stays dark and quiet for the rest of the night, and the light coming on doubles as a warning to whoever triggered it.

That matters in the countryside more than in the city. A white light burning all night on a barn can draw insects into the lens, annoy a neighbour across an open field, and make the camera itself easy to spot from the road. Switching to light-on-detection solves all three, and it means the colour image you get is the one that matters.

We set the light behaviour per camera at the night visit: always off for a camera that looks toward a neighbour's house, detection-triggered for the gate and yard, and always on only where the owner wants the area lit anyway, such as a loading dock that runs a night shift.

Alerts and Deterrence on Sites Nobody Watches After Dark

Will a camera with a siren and red-and-blue strobes actually scare someone off a remote site?

It changes the odds, and that is the honest claim. Dahua's active-deterrence cameras, including the TiOC line, are built to warn off intruders with light and a siren, alert the owner in real time and provide video for verification. On a farm yard or a contractor lot where nobody would otherwise know anyone was there, a sudden siren and flashing light tell the person they have been seen, and many will leave rather than find out who is coming.

What makes deterrence useful rather than annoying is the trigger. These cameras fire their siren and lights when the analytics classify the moving object as a person or a vehicle, inside an area we draw, during hours we set. A siren that sounds for every deer or every gust in the cornstalks will be switched off within a week by the owner, and then it protects nothing.

Deterrence is also only the first half of the job. The second half is a person: a push alert to the owner's phone, a call to the manager on duty, or a monitoring service if the site warrants one. A siren that sounds in an empty valley at three in the morning with nobody told is a noise, not a response.

How do you keep deer, blowing crops and highway headlights from flooding the phone with alerts?

By making the camera, not the phone, decide what counts. Plain motion detection reacts to any change in pixels: a deer crossing the orchard, wet leaves, a spider on the lens, headlights sweeping across a barn wall from Route 17. Dahua's AI cameras classify what they see, and we set alerts for people and vehicles only, so the wildlife that is everywhere in Orange County never reaches your phone.

Next we draw the rules tightly. A line-crossing rule across the gate rather than a box around the whole field; an intrusion area that ends at the fence rather than at the road; a minimum size so a raccoon is not mistaken for a person; and schedules, so the dock cameras alert only after the last shift leaves. We tune these at night, at the property, with the owner's phone in hand.

Finally we decide who gets what. The farm owner may want every gate event; the warehouse manager may want only after-hours intrusions at the fence. Fewer, better alerts are the difference between a system people trust and one they mute.

Warehouses, Docks and Truck Gates

How do you plan cameras for a building with a hundred-plus dock doors?

By the job each camera does, not by a ratio of cameras to doors. A dock wall needs views that run along it, so that every trailer and every door is covered from at least one direction and a person walking the wall is never out of frame. We typically set cameras at the building corners looking down each wall and add mid-wall cameras where the wall is long enough that the far doors become too small to make out.

The trailer yard is a separate problem. Yards like the 167-space one planned on Route 17K are covered from light poles and building corners with wider views, and the question there is whether you can follow a truck from the gate to a door and back. The gate and guard booth get their own cameras for faces and plates, and the inside of the building gets views of the dock plates and the staging lanes, where most disputes about damaged or missing freight are settled.

We draw this on the site plan before anything is ordered, mark which runs exceed copper limits, and show where fiber and intermediate switches go. A large building usually ends up on several switches tied back to one recorder or a small server, with everything on battery backup.

Can a gate camera capture trailer numbers and plates at night?

Yes, if it is a dedicated camera in the right place. A general view of the gate shows that a truck arrived; reading a plate or a trailer number takes a narrow field of view aimed at the exact spot where vehicles slow or stop, with a fast shutter and its own infrared. We set it so the characters fill a meaningful part of the frame, and we test it after dark with a real vehicle, because a camera that reads plates at noon can still blur them at night.

Trailer numbers painted high on the box need a second camera at a different height from the one aimed at the tractor's plate. Dahua also makes cameras that read plates automatically and keep a searchable list; those make sense where you need to look up "every visit by this truck," and are overkill where you only need a clear picture when something goes wrong.

For lots and gates outside a warehouse context, our parking lot cameras in Orange County page covers the same plate-camera thinking for retail and office lots.

Farms, Orchards and Farm Markets

On a farm with a limited budget, which locations get cameras first?

The ways in and the things that are expensive and portable. First the farm's entrances — the driveway and any field gate a truck can use — because every vehicle that takes something has to come and go that way. Second the places where valuable, movable things sit: the equipment yard, the fuel tanks, the tool and chemical storage, the irrigation pump. Third the buildings people work in, and last the open fields.

This order often means fewer cameras than owners expect. A farm in the Black Dirt with one road in and a cluster of buildings may need four or five well-placed cameras and one radio link, not a camera on every corner. We would rather spend the budget on a camera that reads a plate at the gate than on three that show empty fields.

Plan the trench now even if the camera comes later. If an electrician is already trenching to the pump house this season, a spare conduit in the same trench lets a camera go there next year without digging again.

Can the farm stand's register camera and the barn cameras share one recorder?

Yes, and on most farms they should. One Dahua recorder can take cameras from the market, the barns and the gate, with each building linked back by cable, fiber or radio. One recorder means one place to search, one set of drives to check, one battery to size and one login on the owner's phone.

The exception is when the market and the farm are run as separate businesses with separate managers, or when the market sits on its own internet connection across a public road. Then two recorders, each viewable from the same DMSS app, can be simpler than a link that crosses land you do not control.

Either way, the register camera is set up differently from the barn cameras. It looks down at the counter from behind the staff, it records continuously during market hours rather than on motion, and its footage is kept long enough to settle a question about a cash count days later.

Rural Internet, Remote Viewing and Older Systems

Can we watch the cameras over a satellite dish or a cellular connection?

Yes. Recording happens on the recorder at the property, so the internet connection only affects watching from elsewhere, not what gets recorded. What a slow or metered connection changes is how you watch: the DMSS app can show each camera's low-resolution sub-stream for live viewing, which uses a fraction of the bandwidth of the main stream, and you pull the full-resolution clip only when you need it.

Upload speed is the number that matters, and rural connections often have little of it. We tune the sub-streams to fit what the connection actually delivers, set alerts to send a snapshot rather than a video, and turn off cloud features that would quietly use a cellular data allowance.

If the connection drops, the cameras keep recording and you see the gap only in live view. For a seasonal home that sits empty, we can set the recorder to send an alert when a camera goes offline, so a dead camera or a tripped breaker is found before the next visit rather than at it.

Can the old coax from an analog barn system carry new Dahua cameras?

Often, yes, and Dahua has two ways to do it. Its HDCVI cameras and recorders send high-definition video over existing coax, so an old barn system can be upgraded by replacing the cameras and the recorder and keeping the cable. And Dahua's ePoE over coax uses an adaptor at each end to carry a network camera's video and power over existing RG59, with Dahua quoting up to 1,000 metres at 10 Mbps.

The catch is the cable's condition. Coax that has been buried without conduit, chewed by rodents in a barn ceiling, spliced several times or left in the sun for twenty years may not carry a clean signal. We test each run before deciding; good runs stay, bad ones get replaced with network cable or fiber.

Many Orange County farms and older commercial buildings end up as a mix: HDCVI cameras on the coax that tested well, network cameras on new cable, all on one recorder. If you are not sure what you have, our NVR installation crew for Orange County can identify it from photos of the recorder and the cable ends.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Dahua Cameras in Orange County

Search engines and AI assistants summarise camera installation from national cost guides and product pages. The claims below are the kind those summaries repeat; we quote published versions where we have them and set them against what a large Orange County property actually needs.

"Professional camera installation costs about $1,300."

Angi's 2026 cost guide gives a national average of $1,289 for security camera installation, with most projects between $600 and $2,168, and puts four cameras at $400 to $800 and six at $600 to $1,200. Those are reasonable figures for what most of its projects are: a handful of cameras on one house with short cable runs.

They say nothing about the cost of getting video back from a barn 300 yards away, a fiber run to a guard booth, a radio link over a drainage ditch, or the battery and surge protection that every one of those buildings needs. On a large property, the links and the protection can cost as much as the cameras.

Our Orange County list runs roughly $500 to $850 per installed camera including the recorder, cabling and setup, with long runs, trenching, fiber and radio links priced as their own lines after measurement. Expect a national average to be a floor, not a forecast, if your cameras are spread across acres.

"PoE cameras only work within 100 metres of the switch."

It is true of standard PoE and it is the most common reason a far-barn camera stops working: someone ran a 130-metre cable and the camera reboots every time it switches to infrared and draws more power. AI summaries usually stop there and suggest a wireless camera instead.

Dahua's extended PoE goes further: 300 metres at 100 Mbps or 800 metres at 10 Mbps on matching ePoE hardware, by Dahua's own figures. Fiber goes kilometres, and a switch with its own power partway along the run resets the 100-metre clock.

The right reading of the 100-metre rule is "plan the route before you buy the camera," not "wired cameras cannot reach the barn." Most of the distance problems we fix were created by a cable that was bought before anyone measured.

"Just extend your Wi-Fi to the barn."

This is the advice that turns up in homestead forums and in plenty of AI answers: put an extender at the end of the house closest to the barn and use Wi-Fi cameras. It works sometimes, for one camera, on a calm day.

A household extender repeats the signal it receives, usually at a fraction of the original speed, and it was built to cover rooms, not a hundred yards of open field. Add two or three cameras streaming continuously and the link falls apart, usually at night when the cameras switch to infrared and the picture gets noisier and heavier to send.

A proper point-to-point bridge is built for exactly this: directional antennas, a dedicated link, and published ranges measured in kilometres. It costs more than an extender and far less than a trench, and it carries every camera at the barn instead of one.

"Colour night vision lets you see in colour in total darkness."

Product pages often say a full-colour camera sees in colour "even in complete darkness." What they mean is that the camera has its own white LEDs, or that it is very good with very little light. In a city there is almost always some light to work with.

An Orange County field at night may have none. Without light, a full-colour camera can show only what its own LEDs reach, and it cannot use infrared at all. Even suppliers who sell full-colour cameras say an infrared or thermal camera is the safer choice in true pitch black.

So we choose by location: colour where there is a yard light or where colour matters, infrared for unlit open ground, and Smart Dual Light models where the owner wants both. Anyone recommending one camera type for every spot on a farm has not stood at the far gate after dark.

"A siren camera stops intruders by itself."

Active-deterrence cameras are genuinely useful on remote sites, and Dahua builds them to warn off intruders with light and siren. AI answers sometimes present them as a complete security system in one box.

A deterrent that nobody hears is only a delay. On a farm or a contractor yard far from the nearest neighbour, the siren needs a person behind it: a push alert, a call list, or a monitoring service. And a siren with careless triggers will be switched off by an owner tired of being woken by deer.

We treat deterrence as one layer: AI triggers limited to people and vehicles, schedules for the hours the site is empty, a person who gets told, and recorded video that holds up afterwards.

"Cloud storage is safer than a recorder on site."

Cloud backup protects footage if a recorder is stolen or destroyed, and AI summaries often recommend it as the default. It is a good feature where the connection can carry it.

Many rural Orange County properties do not have the upload speed to send continuous high-resolution video from several cameras to the cloud, and those on cellular or satellite plans may pay for every gigabyte. Pushing everything to the cloud on a weak connection gives you gaps exactly when the connection is busiest.

Our default is the reverse: record everything locally on a recorder hidden in a locked room and on battery backup, and send alerts and selected clips off-site. For sites where theft of the recorder is a real risk, we can place it out of sight in a different building from the cameras.

"Any handyman can mount a few cameras."

For two cameras on a garage, probably. AI answers rarely separate that job from a farm or a warehouse, where cable crosses between buildings, goes underground, runs up poles and passes through surge protection that has to be bonded to the right ground.

Getting those parts wrong does not show on installation day. It shows after the first thunderstorm, the first long outage or the first summer when the orchard leafs out across a radio path. New 120-volt circuits for barns and gates also belong to a licensed electrician, which is a separate trade and a separate licence in Orange County.

The question to ask any installer is not whether they can mount a camera, but how they will get video and power to the farthest one, and what happens to it in a storm.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk to the Far Gate at Dusk

The habit that matters most on a big property is simple: before we commit to a camera position, somebody walks out to the farthest gate or building at dusk and looks back. In daylight every plan looks fine. At dusk you find out which yard light is on a timer that shuts off at midnight, which headlights sweep across the barn from the road, where the ground dips so a radio at the house cannot see a radio at the shed, and whether the spot an outlet happens to be on is a spot any vehicle actually passes.

It is also when you see what the farm or the yard looks like to someone who means to take something from it: which approach is dark, where a truck can pull in without being seen from the house, how far the nearest neighbour really is. That walk usually moves at least one camera, and it often removes one, because the gate camera that reads a plate makes the two cameras watching empty field corners unnecessary.

We write what we find on the plan — the light, the line of sight, the outlet, the approach — so the owner can see why each camera is where it is.

FAQ

Orange County Dahua Camera FAQ

Do we need a town permit or the utility's approval to put a camera pole at our farm gate?
+

Usually not for a camera on a post on your own land, but it depends on the town, so we ask the town building department before setting anything tall or near the road. What always needs approval is attaching anything to a utility pole: those belong to Orange & Rockland or the phone company, and we never mount cameras or radios on them without the pole owner's written permission. If the gate needs a new outlet, the electrician who installs it handles the electrical permit and inspection for that circuit.

Can a monitoring company or our out-of-state security office watch the warehouse cameras?
+

Yes. A Dahua recorder can give a central monitoring station or a corporate security team their own login with live view, playback or both, limited to the cameras they need. We create a separate account for each party rather than sharing the administrator password, so access can be removed later without changing everyone's login. Monitoring companies often want specific event types forwarded, such as fence-line intrusion after hours, and we set those up with the monitoring provider during commissioning.

Will field dust, spray and spider webs blind the lenses, and how are they cleaned?
+

Dust from the fields and spray around wash bays will film a lens over a season, and spiders love the warmth of an infrared camera at night, which is the most common cause of false night-time alerts in the country. We mount cameras under eaves where possible, angle them away from prevailing spray, and leave the owner a soft brush and lens cloth with instructions. A wipe in spring and autumn keeps most cameras clear; cameras over a packing or wash area may need it monthly in season.

What does a yearly service visit on a spread-out system include, and what does it cost?
+

We clean every lens and housing, check each camera still points where it was aimed, read drive health and confirm how many days are really being kept, update firmware, test every battery backup by cutting mains power, check radio link strength and surge protectors, and export a test clip. You get a written list of anything that needs attention. Service visits after the original installation are billed at $195 an hour with a 3-hour minimum ($585), and on a large property the visit is planned so the 3 hours cover every building.

Can seasonal farm-stand staff be limited to seeing only the register camera?
+

Yes. Each person can have their own login on the recorder or in the DMSS app, and each login can be limited to particular cameras and to live view only, with no playback or export. Seasonal staff typically get the register and the market entrance; the owner keeps every camera and the right to export. When the season ends, we or the owner disable those accounts in a minute without touching anyone else's access.

Who fixes a camera that fails at a far barn after installation, and what is covered?
+

Call us and we come out; you do not deal with Dahua directly. A camera that fails from a defect within its warranty period is replaced, and Dahua cameras and recorders carry the manufacturer's warranty. Full terms — including 50% deposit, one-year parts-only warranty on AESS-supplied products, and the late-fee schedule — are set out in the service agreement. Damage from surges, lightning or physical impact, and adjustments made after completion, are billed as service work.

How do we get trailer-yard footage to the State Police or the Sheriff's Office quickly?
+

The site manager exports the clip from the recorder or from SmartPSS on the office computer to a USB stick or a download link, following export steps we leave at the rack and show at handover. Every recorder's clock is set to synchronise automatically, so the time on the clip matches the time on the report, and cameras are named after real places such as north dock wall or truck gate inbound. Export a generous window around the event rather than a few seconds, so the vehicle's arrival and departure are both on the clip.

Can you leave spare conduit so future field cameras don't need another trench?
+

Yes, and on a farm it is one of the best investments in the job. When we or an electrician have a trench open, we lay an extra empty conduit with a pull string to each building or gate that might want a camera later, and cap both ends. A camera added next year then needs only a cable pulled through the conduit, not a second trench across the field or the driveway.

Can you install in winter when the ground is frozen?
+

Most of the work, yes: cameras, recorders, radio links and any cable that runs on or inside buildings go in year-round. Trenching is the exception, because frozen ground and snow cover make digging slow and expensive. For a winter install we often put a radio link in place of the trench so the system works now, then lay conduit in spring if the owner still wants a cabled connection.

What is due up front, and when is the rest paid?
+

A 50% deposit is due when you accept the written quote, which lets us order the cameras, recorder, radios and batteries for your property; the balance is due when the installation is complete and handed over. Any change you ask for once work starts, such as an extra building or a longer run, is quoted in writing before we do it. The full payment and warranty terms are in the service agreement we send with the quote.
