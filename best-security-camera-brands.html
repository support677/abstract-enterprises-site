How We Judged

How an Installer Ranks Camera Brands Differently From a Reviewer

The pages that rank for this search today, from security.org and SafeHome.org to Wirecutter, CNET and Consumer Reports, mostly test consumer cameras one at a time. That is useful for picking a doorbell. It is less useful for a co-op lobby, a bodega with three aisles and a basement stockroom, or a two-family house where the owner upstairs and the tenant downstairs both want to see the front door. Our ranking leans on five questions that only show up after the cameras are on the wall.

1. Where does the video live?

A recorder in the building keeps working when the internet is down, keeps weeks of footage without a monthly bill, and can hand a clip to a detective on a USB drive. Cloud storage survives a stolen recorder but stops recording useful history the day a payment lapses. Most of the professional brands and the four big DIY kit brands put the video on a local box first. Ring, Arlo and Google Nest put it in the cloud first.

2. How does the camera get power and network?

Power over Ethernet sends power and data down one cable, which is why every professional brand in the table, and the Lorex, Reolink, Swann and eufy NVR lines, are built around it. A single cable per camera is also what lets us run a camera to the third floor of a walk-up through an existing riser rather than hanging a battery camera that someone has to charge on a ladder. Battery and Wi-Fi cameras earn their place where a cable genuinely cannot go.

3. What happens when the app changes?

Every brand eventually changes its app. Swann's Google Home integration ended in August 2025, according to Swann's own support page, and anyone who built a routine around it lost it. Google now sells its camera history under Google Home Premium rather than the older Nest Aware name. We weigh how much of the system depends on one company's cloud, because a recorder that speaks ONVIF can usually be moved to another brand's software, and a cloud-only camera cannot.

4. Who can service it in five years?

Axis says on its own site that it never sells directly to customers, and Hikvision, Dahua, Uniview and Hanwha recorders are the ones we find most often when we answer service calls in older installations. Firmware, replacement parts and technicians who know the menus are easy to find. That is less true of a one-off kit brand bought online, and it matters most for landlords and managing agents who change contractors.

5. What does the whole system cost to own?

Installed price plus any monthly plan over the life of the system. A cheaper camera with a required plan can cost more over several years than a professional camera with none. We publish installed prices for each brand on our own NYC price pages, and the section near the end of this page lists them together.

Professional Brands

Professional Camera Brands: Wired to a Recorder, No Required Plan

These are the brands we install when the job calls for cable, a recorder in a closet and cameras that stay put for years. They are rarely sold in a box at a big-box store, they expect a person who knows how to configure a recorder, and in return they give you more control over where the video lives, who can see it and what it costs to keep.

UniFi Protect (Ubiquiti): the easiest professional system to live with

UniFi Protect is the brand we recommend most often to homeowners and small offices who want professional cameras without professional complexity. Ubiquiti's physical security page is unusually direct about money: it says UniFi Protect "handles all AI and recording on your local UNVR or Enterprise NVR, eliminating cloud fees, subscriptions, and paid AI add-ons," and in its own question-and-answer section, to the question of whether there are fees, it answers "Absolutely not." The recording host can be a dedicated UniFi NVR, one of Ubiquiti's Cloud Gateways or a CloudKey+, which matters because many of our customers already have a UniFi router or Wi-Fi in the building and can add cameras to the same console.

On the camera side, Ubiquiti lists the whole G6 line, including the Dome, Turret, Pro Bullet, Instant and PTZ, as 4K, and it gives the G6 Dome a 1/1.8-inch 8MP sensor. The G6 Pro Bullet reaches up to 60 meters (197 feet) of infrared with what Ubiquiti calls Vision Enhancer, which is more reach than a typical Brooklyn backyard needs but useful along a long Staten Island driveway or the fence line of a Queens yard. Wired cameras need Power over Ethernet, and Ubiquiti says ONVIF compatibility lets an owner add some third-party cameras to the same system.

The honest drawbacks: UniFi is a whole ecosystem, so the recorder, the switch and the app all come from the same company, and if you leave you leave all of it. The app is the best-designed one in the professional half of this table, which is why owners who have never touched a recorder menu like it, but it is still a network product, and a building with a messy internet setup will feel it. We also see owners buy UniFi cameras online and then discover they need a console to record anything, which is exactly the kind of mismatch a short call before purchase avoids.

Our call: the default pick for a one- or two-family house, a professional office or a small retail shop where the owner wants to watch on a phone, pay nothing monthly and never think about the recorder. See our UniFi camera installation in NYC page for how we install it, and the UniFi installed price list, where a 4-camera system starts from $2,111.

Hikvision: the color-night-video workhorse, with a federal footnote

Hikvision says it was founded in 2001, and its cameras and recorders are among the most common professional equipment we find in New York houses, shops and apartment buildings. Its ColorVu line is built around showing color at night: the DS-2CD2187G2-LSU, which Hikvision calls a 4K ColorVu fixed dome network camera, records at 8MP, 3840 by 2160, and Hikvision describes ColorVu as 24/7 color imaging using white light. The same product page lists microSD storage up to 512 GB in the camera, ONVIF Profile S, G and T, and Power over Ethernet, and names the Hik-Connect app along with the iVMS-4200 and iVMS-5200 software.

In practice, Hikvision earns its place on a lot of residential jobs because the color image after dark is easy for a family to understand, the recorders are mature, and almost every technician in the city knows the menus. Hik-Connect is not the slickest app in the table, and we spend real time on service calls helping owners who lost their login or changed phones, which is why we run a separate Hik-Connect app support service. Hikvision's own product page did not confirm whether any subscription is needed, so we leave that out of the table; the systems we install record to a local recorder.

The footnote every buyer should know: the FCC added video surveillance and telecommunications equipment produced by Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company to its Covered List on March 12, 2021, "to the extent it is used for the purpose of public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes." For a private house or most private businesses, that language describes uses that do not apply. For a building with a government tenant, a contractor bound by federal procurement rules, or an owner whose insurer asks, it is a reason to choose another brand. We explain this in plain terms in the FCC section below rather than in a scare headline.

Our call: a strong value for private homes and businesses that want bright color night video and a recorder any technician can service; not our pick for government-linked buildings. Our Hikvision vs Dahua comparison sets the two side by side and covers the FCC's June 2026 rule on importing and marketing previously authorized covered equipment. See Hikvision camera installation in NYC, where a small 4-camera residential system usually lands between $1,800 and $3,000 installed.

Dahua: deterrence features and a deep product range

Dahua's own site gives its legal name as Zhejiang Dahua Technology Co., Ltd. and calls the company a "video-centric AIoT solution and service provider." Its range is wide, from small fixed cameras to the kind of pan-tilt-zoom dome in the photo, and its WizColor and TiOC lines are where most of our Dahua work happens. The IPC-HFW3849T1-AS-PV-PRO, which Dahua names an 8MP WizColor TiOC PRO bullet, records 8MP at 3840 by 2160, takes an SD card up to 512 GB, supports ONVIF Profile S, G and T, runs on PoE+, and is managed through the DMSS app, SmartPSS Lite or DSS, with DoLynk Cloud also listed. Dahua's TiOC 2.0 page describes "smooth 4K real-time images at 30 fps even when AI is enabled" with an F1.0 lens.

The TiOC idea, short for three-in-one camera, combines a color image with active deterrence: a flashing light and a speaker that can sound when the camera sees a person where nobody should be. Store owners on commercial strips like that, because a lit, beeping camera at the back door of a closed shop is often enough to move someone along before anything happens. Homeowners are more mixed, because the same feature has to be tuned carefully so it does not go off for every delivery person.

Dahua shares Hikvision's federal footnote: the FCC Covered List has included equipment produced by Dahua Technology Company since March 12, 2021, with the same scoping language. The same advice applies. For most private owners it is background; for government-linked work it rules the brand out. Dahua's site did not confirm whether a subscription is ever required, and our installations record to a local recorder.

Our call: pick Dahua when a storefront, a yard or a parking area benefits from lights-and-siren deterrence, or when a PTZ is needed on a budget. If you already own Dahua and are deciding whether to keep it, read our Hikvision vs Dahua comparison. See Dahua camera installation in NYC; a small 4-camera system usually lands between $1,800 and $3,000 installed, and our SmartPSS support page covers the desktop software.

Uniview (UNV): professional features without the Covered List question

Uniview says it was founded in 2011 and calls itself a "global provider of AIoT products," and its contact page lists its headquarters in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. It is not on the FCC Covered List, which names Hikvision and Dahua among video surveillance makers but not Uniview. That combination, a professional camera line from the same region and price tier, without the Covered List entry, is why Uniview has become a common answer when a building owner asks for something like Hikvision but is worried about that list.

The IPC3528LE-ADF28K-WP2, which Uniview calls an 8MP ColorHunter dome, records at 3840 by 2160, takes microSD up to 512 GB, supports ONVIF Profile S, G and T and runs on PoE. Uniview's ColorHunter uses warm light for color at night. The mobile app is EZView, which Uniview says lets you "view live or recorded video, receive alarms and manage cloud devices," with EZStation on the PC and EZCloud for remote access. Uniview's site did not confirm anything about subscriptions, and on our installations the recorder does the recording.

The trade-off is familiarity. Fewer owners have heard of Uniview, and fewer technicians know its menus by heart than know Hikvision's. In our experience the hardware holds up, and the EZView app is straightforward once it is set up properly, but if you change installers later, ask whether they work with UNV before you sign.

Our call: our usual pick for co-ops, condos and mixed-use buildings that want a professional NVR system, color night video and no Covered List conversation with the board. See Uniview camera installation in NYC and the Uniview installed price list, where a 4-camera system starts from $2,334.

Compliance-Minded Brands

Hanwha, Axis and Luminys: When the Paperwork Matters as Much as the Picture

Some customers do not start with resolution at all. A managing agent wants a brand the building's insurer will not question. A school or nonprofit applying for a security grant needs equipment that fits the grant's rules. A contractor wants a system that will not have to be ripped out if the tenant changes. For those buyers, the three brands in this section come up first, each for a different reason.

Hanwha Vision (Wisenet): strong analytics with an NDAA badge on the product page

Hanwha Vision sells its cameras and recorders under the Wisenet name. Its XNO-A9014R, which Hanwha calls an 8MP AI IR bullet camera, records at 3840 by 2160, is built on the Wisenet 9 chip with what Hanwha describes as a dual NPU for on-camera analytics, reaches 25 meters (82 feet) of infrared, takes a microSD card up to 1 TB, runs on standard PoE and supports ONVIF Profile S, G, T and M. Hanwha's product page displays an NDAA certification badge for that model, which is exactly the kind of document a building's compliance person asks to see.

Recording runs either on a Wisenet recorder like the one in the photo or on Hanwha's Wisenet WAVE software, which Hanwha says comes with a 30-day free trial recording license for up to four channels. That trial language tells you WAVE recording is licensed, so on a WAVE build the license cost belongs in the quote from day one. The Wisenet Mobile app handles viewing on phones. Hanwha's pages did not confirm whether a plain recorder setup ever carries a subscription, and the ones we install do not.

Wisenet's strength in our work is the analytics: person and vehicle detection that cuts down false alerts at a loading dock or a parking lot gate, and image quality that holds up in mixed lighting like a lobby with glass doors facing a bright avenue. The trade-off is price and setup time, both somewhat above the Hikvision and Dahua tier.

Our call: pick Hanwha when an insurer, a lender, a grant or a corporate tenant asks about equipment origin, or when analytics need to be accurate enough to act on. Our Hanwha installed price list shows 4-camera systems from $1,688, and the NVR installation page covers the recorder side.

Axis Communications: the long-life enterprise choice

Axis says it is based in Lund, Sweden, was founded in 1984 and has been part of the Canon Group since 2015. It also says plainly, "We never sell directly to customers," which means every Axis system goes in through a partner like us. Its AXIS Q1808-LE bullet is a 10MP camera with what Axis calls Lightfinder 2.0 for low light and OptimizedIR for darkness, a memory card slot, PoE Class 4 power, and an Axis 5-year warranty listed on the product page. Axis's datasheets also describe a 41MP model in the same family for very wide scenes.

Recording runs on AXIS Camera Station Pro. Axis says its own recorders come preloaded with licenses valid for the lifetime of the recorder, while running the software on your own hardware needs one- or five-year licenses, and the AXIS Camera Station Pro Mobile app handles phones. The software also supports third-party cameras, which helps when a building has a few older cameras worth keeping.

Axis costs more, and it should. What you buy is a camera line designed to be maintained by professionals for a long time, with careful documentation and firmware support. For a brownstone, that is usually more camera than the job needs. For a school, a medical office, a multi-floor commercial building or a property that will keep the same system for a decade, it is often the cheapest choice over the system's life.

Our call: pick Axis for larger commercial sites, schools and institutions that value long warranty terms and enterprise software. Our Axis installed price list shows 4-camera systems from $3,461, and the school camera installation page explains how we plan those buildings.

Luminys: a California company with a Section 889 statement

Luminys Systems Corporation lists its address in Irvine, California, and its compliance page says it is "fully committed to compliance with Section 889" of the National Defense Authorization Act, the federal rule behind most NDAA questions about cameras. The same page says its products are made in the supply chain of its parent company, Foxlink. For owners who want a professional camera line from a US-based company with that statement on record, Luminys has become a frequent request.

Luminys sells several camera lines, which it names LumiColor, LumiNightKing, LumiLuxSmart, LumiDeterrent and LumiSearch PTZ, with recorders in its R3 4K PoE NVR series and R5 8K LumiSearch NVR series and a remote service called LumiCloud. Its LumiNightKing page describes "full color images… in near total darkness without using IR or white-light LEDs," which, if it performs as described on a given site, solves a common complaint about color cameras: the bright light that neighbors dislike. Its LumiCenter software supports ONVIF G, M, S and T.

What Luminys's site did not confirm for us is a single flagship model with its resolution, the name of its mobile app, or whether any feature needs a subscription, so those cells are blank in the table rather than filled in from a reseller listing. We would rather tell you we checked and could not confirm than print a number we cannot source.

Our call: a good fit for owners who specifically want a US-headquartered professional brand with a published NDAA Section 889 commitment. Our Luminys installed price list shows 4-camera systems from $2,591.

Plain Answers

The FCC Covered List and NDAA, Explained Without the Scare Headlines

Almost every comparison of camera brands now touches on whether some brands are "banned." The honest answer is narrower than most headlines. The Federal Communications Commission keeps a list of equipment it has found poses a national security risk, called the Covered List, and its page was last updated on October 23, 2026 when we checked it. On March 12, 2021, the FCC added video surveillance and telecommunications equipment produced by Hangzhou Hikvision Digital Technology Company and by Dahua Technology Company, and it scoped both entries to equipment used "for the purpose of public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes."

Uniview, UniFi, Hanwha, Axis, Luminys and the consumer brands in this comparison do not appear on that list. Separately, Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act restricts federal agencies, and companies that contract with them, from using certain equipment. That is why Hanwha shows an NDAA badge on its product page and Luminys publishes a Section 889 compliance statement.

What this means for a New York property owner

  • A private house or apartment: the Covered List language describes uses that do not apply to you. The choice of brand comes down to picture, app, fees and service.
  • A private business: usually the same, unless a lease, a corporate parent, an insurer or a customer contract asks about equipment origin. Ask before you buy, not after.
  • A building with a government tenant, a federal contractor, a school or nonprofit using a security grant, or anything near critical infrastructure: choose a brand that is not on the Covered List, and keep the manufacturer's compliance documentation with the system records.
  • An existing Hikvision or Dahua system: nothing on the FCC page tells a private owner to rip out working cameras. If your situation changes, we can plan a staged replacement, often reusing the cable.

We are installers, not lawyers, and rules change. If a contract or grant depends on this, ask the person who wrote the requirement which list or rule it refers to, and we will match the equipment to it.

DIY Kit Brands

Lorex, Reolink, Swann and eufy: Local Recording Without a Required Fee

These four brands sit in the middle of the market. They are sold as kits a handy owner can install, they all offer wired PoE recorders, and each of them says on its own site that you can record locally without paying monthly. Most of the time when one of them reaches us, it is because someone bought the kit, opened the box and realized the hard part is not the cameras. It is getting cable from the basement to the front of a 1920s brick house without leaving a mess on the facade.

Lorex: the strongest no-fee kit for houses

Lorex says in so many words that its "security cameras and systems do not require a subscription," and its recorders back that up. The Fusion 4K+ 12MP 16-channel NVR, model N910A62, supports both 12MP and 4K+ cameras, ships with a 2 TB drive and takes up to 16 TB, and lists 72 watts of total PoE power for the cameras plugged into it. The Lorex Home app handles viewing, and Lorex lists Amazon Alexa and Google Assistant voice control, including showing cameras on an Echo Show or a Google display. If you want cloud backup anyway, Lorex's Connect Cloud+ plan is listed at $5.99 a month for a single camera and $16.99 a month for multiple cameras.

Lorex is a common choice among homeowners who arrive already knowing they want 4K wired cameras and no monthly bill. The recorder interface is friendlier than most professional brands, the floodlight and smart-light cameras suit backyards and alleys, and the kits are complete enough that we often install what the customer already bought rather than selling them new equipment.

The limits are about scale and service. A Lorex recorder is built for a house or a small shop, not a 40-unit building with a super and a managing agent who all need separate access. And because the brand is sold direct, a few owners end up mixing camera generations that the recorder does not fully support. We check model numbers before the visit for exactly that reason.

Our call: the best do-it-yourself-brand system for a one- or two-family house, especially when the owner already has the kit. See Lorex camera installation in NYC and the Lorex installed price list, where 4 cameras start from $1,939.

Reolink: wide views and flexible storage

Reolink's headline products are about field of view. The Reolink Duo 3 PoE is a 16MP dual-lens camera that Reolink lists at 7680 by 2160 with a 180-degree view, and Reolink's RLN16-410 16-channel recorder supports cameras up to 16MP. Storage is flexible: a microSD card up to 512 GB in the camera, a Reolink recorder, or Reolink's optional cloud, and Reolink's own blog says no subscription is required. The Duo 3 PoE page lists Google Assistant support, and everything runs through the Reolink App.

A 180-degree camera is genuinely useful in New York in a few specific places: the back of a house where a yard spreads left and right, a driveway shared by two houses, a parking area behind a store, or the front of a corner building where one camera has to see two sidewalks. It is less useful in a narrow vestibule, where a wide lens just shows more wall. Reolink also sells Wi-Fi and battery models, and we install fewer of those because they bring the same Wi-Fi limits as any other brand in brick and stone buildings. Swann: a complete kit, with one integration gone

Swann's EliteX 6K/12MP NVR security system, model SWNVK-8EX412MPB with four bullet cameras, comes with a 2 TB drive that expands to 8 TB, and Swann describes it as having "zero ongoing fees" with cloud backup optional. Swann's Secure+ plans start with a free tier that needs no subscription, then an Extra plan at $4.99 a month for one device and an Unlimited plan at $12.99 a month. Our call: fine for a house that wants a packaged no-fee NVR kit and does not care about voice assistants. See the Swann installed price list, where 4 cameras start from $1,779.

eufy: the Apple-friendly local-storage pick

eufy, Anker's security brand, built its reputation on local storage, and its site still leads with it: eufy says its local security systems carry no monthly fee. Its top PoE system, the eufy PoE NVR Security System S4 Max, ships with 2 TB of storage expandable to 16 TB, and eufy also stores video on a HomeBase or on the device itself depending on the product. Cloud-First Brands

Ring, Arlo and Google Nest: Easy Apps, Monthly Plans

These are the brands most people already know, and for good reason. Their apps are polished, their doorbells are everywhere, and they connect to the voice assistants and displays already sitting in many New York kitchens. The catch is that the video history, the part you need after something happens, usually lives in the company's cloud and needs a paid plan. That is not a reason to avoid them. It is a cost to put in the comparison alongside the installed price.

Ring: the doorbell standard for Alexa homes

Ring's current top wired models carry 4K labels: the Wired Doorbell 4K Pro, which Ring describes with a "Retinal 4K" image, and the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro. For a building that wants a doorbell on the network cable rather than on old bell wire, the Wired Doorbell Elite uses Power over Ethernet. Ring's product pages say the doorbell works without a subscription, but saved video history needs a plan, and Ring's plans page listed plans starting at $4.99 a month when we checked. Local video storage exists only through the Ring Alarm Pro base station. Ring is built around Alexa: live view on an Echo Show or a Fire TV is one of its biggest selling points.

In New York, Ring's real advantage is the doorbell. Most households buy one before any other camera, and it has to work with the bell wire, the chime and sometimes the old intercom already in the building. A common call starts with a Ring doorbell that will not stay powered on a weak transformer, or a tenant who wants one on an apartment door where the building's rules or the door itself make it tricky. The installation is where those problems get solved, not the brand.

Where we steer people away from Ring is as a whole-property recording system for a building. A super who needs to pull last Tuesday's hallway footage should not depend on whose credit card is on a cloud plan, and four cloud cameras on Wi-Fi in a brick walk-up are a support headache. For those jobs, a recorder-based brand from the tables above does the recording, and a Ring doorbell can still answer the door.

Our call: the pick for an Alexa household's front door and a few exterior cameras, with a plan budgeted. Arlo: the brand for places a cable cannot reach

Arlo's flagship, the Arlo Ultra 3, is a 4K camera, and it runs on a battery. Arlo's wired option, the Essential Plug-In, is a 2K camera that runs from an outlet. Storage is in the cloud, with local storage available through an Arlo SmartHub or base station with a microSD card. Arlo's Secure plans start at $9.99 a month for a single camera on its Smart Aware tier and climb to $49.99 a month for Ultra Secure, according to Arlo's own plans page. That profile makes Arlo the right tool for a specific set of New York problems: a landmarked facade where the owner is not allowed or does not want to drill, a rental where the tenant will take the cameras when they move, a detached garage across a yard with no conduit, or a temporary job such as a renovation or a storefront that is between tenants. In those spots, a camera that needs nothing but a mounting screw is worth the battery changes and the plan.

Feature by Feature

Night Video, Apps and Storage: Where the Brands Really Differ

Once you get past the logos, three features decide whether owners are happy with a camera system a year later: what the picture looks like at two in the morning, how the app behaves when you are in a hurry, and how much video is still there when you finally need it. Each brand handles these differently, and each brand's own names for its features make comparing them harder than it should be. Here is a plain translation.

Color at night: four names for a similar goal

Hikvision calls its approach ColorVu and describes it as 24/7 color imaging with white light. Dahua's WizColor and TiOC lines aim at the same result and add active deterrence on some models. Uniview's ColorHunter uses warm light. Luminys says its LumiNightKing line produces full color images in near total darkness without using infrared or white-light LEDs at all. The traditional alternative is infrared, which Hanwha's XNO-A9014R uses out to 25 meters and Ubiquiti's G6 Pro Bullet out to 60 meters with its Vision Enhancer, giving a sharp black-and-white picture without any visible light.

Which is better depends on the block. On a quiet residential side street in Queens, a camera that switches on a white or warm light when someone walks up can annoy the neighbors across the street and the people sleeping in the front bedroom. On a commercial strip, the same light is a deterrent and makes clothing colors readable for a police report. In a backyard bounded by other people's windows, infrared is often the considerate choice. We usually mix: color-at-night cameras where the light is welcome, infrared where it is not.

Apps: the part you touch every day

The professional brands each have their own app: Hik-Connect for Hikvision, DMSS for Dahua, EZView for Uniview, UniFi Protect for Ubiquiti, Wisenet Mobile for Hanwha and AXIS Camera Station Pro Mobile for Axis. The kit brands use the Lorex Home app, the Reolink App, the Swann Security app and the eufy Security app. The cloud brands use the Ring app, the Arlo Secure app and the Google Home app. In our experience, the consumer apps and UniFi Protect are the easiest for a family to learn, and the professional apps reward a proper setup: separate user accounts, saved views for each floor or area, and notifications limited to the cameras that matter at night.

Two app questions to ask before you choose any brand. First, can each person get their own login with only the cameras they should see? In a two-family house or a building with a super, that matters more than any image setting. Second, what happens when someone changes phones or forgets the password? A surprising share of service calls on every brand are login problems, not camera problems, and a system set up with the owner's own account and recovery details avoids most of them.

Storage: how many days are actually there

Recording time depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, the frame rate, whether they record all the time or only on motion, and the size of the drive. That is why we never promise "30 days" from a brand name alone. The capacities the manufacturers publish give a sense of the ceiling: Lorex's Fusion recorder ships with 2 TB and takes up to 16 TB, Swann's EliteX kit goes from 2 TB to 8 TB, eufy's S4 Max goes from 2 TB to 16 TB, and many professional cameras add a memory card slot of up to 512 GB, or up to 1 TB on Hanwha's XNO-A9014R, as a backup if the recorder goes offline.

For cloud-first brands, storage is set by the plan rather than the drive. Google says its cameras keep 6 hours of event video previews without a plan, and longer history comes with Google Home Premium. Ring and Arlo tie saved video to their plans in the same way. When we set up any system, we write down the retention it actually achieves at the settings chosen, so a landlord or manager knows how far back they can look before an incident report is due.

Doorbells and intercoms: where camera brands meet the front door

In New York the front door is rarely just a door. A house may have a gate at the sidewalk, a stoop, a vestibule and an inner door; an apartment building has a lobby panel wired to every unit. The doorbell camera brands in this comparison, Ring, eufy, Arlo and the Lorex doorbell many kit owners add, are designed for a single household at a single door. They work well on a one-family house or the private entrance of a garden apartment. On a shared entrance, they create a problem: only the person whose account owns the doorbell sees the visitor.

For buildings with more than one household, the right answer is usually a video intercom at the shared door and cameras from the recorder-based system covering the lobby and the street, with each household answering its own calls. That keeps the recording on the building's system, where the owner or super controls it, and keeps each tenant's door answering in their own hands. If a tenant wants a doorbell camera on their own apartment door as well, we check the lease and the door first, then pick a model that matches the existing chime wiring or the door's construction.

The brand question also runs the other way. Many owners pick their whole camera system to match the doorbell they already love. That is a reasonable instinct for a single-family house, and it is part of why Ring and eufy owners often stay with those brands. For anything larger, we suggest choosing the recording system on its merits and letting the doorbell be the one piece that matches the family's phones and speakers.

Power and wiring: the quiet deciding factor

Every professional brand here, and the PoE lines from Lorex, Reolink, Swann and eufy, runs on Power over Ethernet: one cable from the recorder or a PoE switch carries power and video to each camera. Ring offers PoE on its Wired Doorbell Elite. Arlo sells a PoE adapter rather than native PoE cameras, and we found no PoE model from Google. In practice, the brands built for PoE are the ones that keep recording through a Wi-Fi outage, a power blink that resets the router, or a January cold snap that drains a battery. That is the single biggest reason our recommendations lean toward recorder-based brands for anything permanent.

Nothing drilled that the landlord will object to, and cameras that leave with the tenant. Arlo battery cameras or plug-in Nest and Ring cameras, with a plan budgeted.

Three questions that settle most brand decisions in one phone call

Will you pay monthly? If the answer is no, the cloud-first brands drop off and the rest of the table stays. Who else needs to see the video? If the answer includes a super, a manager, a tenant or a board, a recorder-based brand with proper user accounts is easier to run than a shared consumer login. Does anyone ever ask where the equipment comes from? If yes, start with brands outside the FCC Covered List and keep the documentation.

Two answers that surprise people. First, the camera brand often matters less than the cable route and the recorder settings; a good mid-priced camera on a properly planned PoE run beats a premium camera on weak Wi-Fi every time. Second, you do not have to pick one brand forever. A recorder that supports ONVIF can often take cameras from more than one maker, which is how an owner can add a better camera at the busiest door without replacing the whole system.

Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Security Camera Brands

"The best brand is the one with the highest resolution"

Resolution is one line in a spec sheet. A 16MP camera pointed into the glare of an avenue at sunset, or recording to a drive set to keep three days, is worth less than an 8MP camera aimed well and recording for a month. The useful questions are where the video is stored, for how long, and whether you can get a clip off it quickly when the precinct asks.

"Hikvision and Dahua are banned, so you have to remove them"

The FCC Covered List entries for both companies are scoped to public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and national security uses. That is a serious matter for those buyers, and it is why we steer government-linked buildings elsewhere, but it is not an instruction to private homeowners to tear out working systems.

"No-subscription means no remote viewing"

Every recorder-based brand on this page offers a phone app that views live and recorded video from the local recorder. Ubiquiti, Lorex, Reolink, Swann and eufy all say on their own sites that you can run their systems without a monthly fee. What you give up without a cloud plan is an off-site copy, which a well-hidden recorder or a second drive can partly replace.

"Wireless cameras are easier, so they are better"

Easier to install, yes. In New York's brick, stone and plaster buildings, Wi-Fi signal drops fast between floors and through party walls, and battery cameras need charging. For anything permanent we run PoE cable, which carries power and video together and does not care how thick the walls are.

"All professional brands are the same hardware with different logos"

The professional brands share standards like ONVIF and PoE, which is a good thing for owners, but their apps, analytics, firmware support, licensing and supply-chain status differ in ways that matter. The comparison table above exists precisely because those differences decide which one fits your building.

FAQ

Best Security Camera Brands FAQ

What is the best security camera brand for a house or small building in New York City?
+

For most houses and small buildings we recommend a wired system that records to a recorder in the building: UniFi Protect for the simplest app and no fees, Uniview or Hikvision for color video at night, or Lorex if you want a do-it-yourself brand with no required subscription. Ring, Arlo and Google Nest make sense when smart-home integration matters more than local recording and you are comfortable paying for a plan.

Which security camera brands work without a monthly subscription?
+

Ubiquiti says UniFi Protect has no fees, Lorex says its systems do not require a subscription, Reolink says no subscription is required, Swann describes its EliteX kit as having zero ongoing fees, and eufy says its local systems carry no monthly fee. Professional recorder brands such as Hikvision, Dahua, Uniview and Hanwha record to a local recorder in the systems we install. Ring and Google Nest need a plan for saved video history.

Are Hikvision and Dahua cameras banned in the United States?
+

The FCC added both companies' video surveillance equipment to its Covered List on March 12, 2021, scoped to equipment used for public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and other national security purposes. For a private home or most private businesses, those uses do not apply. For government-linked buildings, federal contractors and some grant-funded sites, choose a different brand.

Which camera brands should I look at if a contract or grant asks for NDAA compliance?
+

Start with brands outside the FCC Covered List that publish compliance documentation. Hanwha Vision shows an NDAA certification badge on its XNO-A9014R product page, and Luminys publishes a statement of commitment to Section 889 of the NDAA. Axis, Uniview and UniFi are also outside the Covered List. Always confirm the exact model against the rule your contract or grant names.

What camera brands do professional installers actually use?
+

Most of our professional work uses recorder-based brands sold through installers and distributors: UniFi Protect, Hikvision, Dahua, Uniview, Hanwha Vision and Axis, with Luminys on request. Axis states that it never sells directly to customers. We also install Lorex, Reolink, Swann, eufy, Ring, Arlo and Google Nest, often equipment the customer already bought.

Can I mix camera brands on one recorder?
+

Often, yes. Hikvision, Dahua, Uniview and Hanwha cameras list ONVIF support, Ubiquiti says UniFi Protect can add third-party ONVIF cameras, and Axis Camera Station Pro supports third-party cameras. Features such as smart detection may only work fully with the recorder's own brand, so we check the exact models before promising a mixed system.

Which camera brands work with Alexa, Google Assistant or Apple HomeKit?
+

Ring is built around Alexa. Google Nest works natively with Google Assistant and can be added to Alexa through Google's skill. Arlo lists Alexa, Google Home and SmartThings. Is a wired PoE camera system better than Wi-Fi cameras in a brick or brownstone building?
+

For anything permanent, yes. A PoE cable carries power and video together, so the camera keeps recording when Wi-Fi is weak and never needs a battery change. Thick masonry and plaster walls cut Wi-Fi signal between floors and to the backyard. Wi-Fi and battery cameras still have a place where cable truly cannot go, such as a rental or a landmarked facade.

Does a 4K or 12MP camera need a matching recorder?
+

Yes. The recorder has to accept the camera's resolution and the brand's video format. Lorex lists its Fusion 4K+ 12MP NVR as supporting 12MP and 4K+ cameras, and Reolink lists its RLN16-410 recorder as supporting cameras up to 16MP. An older recorder may show a newer camera at reduced quality or not at all, so send us the recorder model before you buy cameras.
