The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Wikipedia describes Carroll Gardens as about 40 city blocks in northwestern Brooklyn, bounded by Degraw and Warren Streets on the north, Hoyt and Smith Streets on the east, Ninth Street or the Gowanus Expressway on the south, and the Brooklyn–Queens and Gowanus Expressways on the west, with Cobble Hill, Boerum Hill, Gowanus, Red Hook and the Columbia Street Waterfront District around it. It is part of Brooklyn Community District 6, with ZIP code 11231, and took its name in the 1960s from Carroll Street, Carroll Park and the deep front gardens that surveyor Richard Butt laid out in 1846.

The city's own neighborhood tabulation area is no help for counting buildings here, because it lumps Carroll Gardens together with Cobble Hill, Gowanus and Red Hook. So we drew a study area of our own for this page: every tax lot in the city's PLUTO property file that sits south of Degraw Street, west of Hoyt Street, east of Hicks Street and the expressway trench, and north of Hamilton Avenue and the Gowanus Expressway. That gives 2,156 lots on 62 tax blocks, holding 7,641 homes. Its edges are approximate, but it is close to the neighborhood residents mean.

What the PLUTO file shows inside those lines is a neighborhood of small buildings. Of 2,058 lots with at least one home, 545 hold two homes, 581 hold three and 426 hold four. Together that is 1,552 lots, three in every four. Only 173 lots hold eight homes or more. By building class there are 493 three-family houses and 349 four-family walk-ups, against 113 walk-ups with more than six apartments. Add 105 small condominiums in two- to ten-unit buildings, 66 walk-up co-ops and 243 buildings that combine a store with apartments, and the pattern is plain: almost every street door in Carroll Gardens is shared by a handful of households, and almost every building is governed by a handful of people.

That shapes what a buzzer repair is here. The city's records, which in other neighborhoods push owners to act, are nearly silent in these blocks. HPD has written 96 violations mentioning an intercom on the 62 blocks since 1990, at only 34 buildings, and 42 of those 96 were written at a single building on Luquer Street. The city's 311 system has logged 100 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints from the study area since 2020, and they came from just 30 addresses. The buzzers age like anyone else's; what differs is who notices and who acts. In a three-family where the owner lives on the parlor floor, a tenant is unlikely to call the city on a neighbor; in a four-unit condo, the person with the dead button is also one of the owners who has to pay.

Why It Waits

In Carroll Gardens, Nobody Is Going to Order This Repair

In much of Brooklyn the city's housing records act as a second alarm. A tenant calls 311, HPD inspects, a violation is written, and the owner has a deadline. Our earlier research for Bedford-Stuyvesant found 2,040 HPD intercom violations in its community board and 1,956 buzzer complaints to 311 since 2020. Carroll Gardens is far smaller, so the totals cannot be compared directly, but even measured per residential lot its record runs at roughly a third of Bed-Stuy's rate on both counts: about 47 intercom violations per thousand residential lots here against about 134 there, and about 49 buzzer complaints per thousand lots against about 129.

The Carroll Gardens record is also lumpy. Of the 96 intercom violations HPD has written on the study-area blocks since 1990, 42 were written at one building on Luquer Street between April 2020 and May 2024, and nine at one building on Fourth Place over more than three decades. Take those two buildings out and the rest of the neighborhood has 45 intercom violations across 32 buildings in 37 years. Before 2020 the annual count never went above five. Most of the violations are written at an apartment, not at the street door: 82 of the 96 descriptions name an apartment, in wording such as a defective intercom system "from building entrance to panel at west wall in the kitchen", or the "return buzzer" in a kitchen. Every one cites section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, the owner's general duty to keep a building in repair.

The 311 record says the same thing in a different way. The 100 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints since 2020 came from just 30 addresses, so a few buildings complained again and again while most never complained at all. Over the same period 311 logged 174 complaints in the study area about doors. In a neighborhood of 2,058 residential lots, those are small numbers.

None of this means the buzzers work. It means that in buildings with two, three or four homes, often with the owner living in one of them, broken buzzers are lived with, worked around and fixed when somebody gets round to it. A dead top-floor button becomes a habit of texting from the stoop, and a door left on a hook on a hot afternoon becomes normal. HPD's own records show the door side of that drift: the study-area blocks hold 722 violations mentioning a self-closing door, 455 of them Class C, written at more than a hundred buildings, and every year since 2020 has topped 40 such violations, a level the blocks reached only once before, in 1986.

So the work starts earlier than the repair. Somebody in the building has to decide that the buzzer is worth fixing, find out who has the authority to approve it, and get a price that the other owners can read and accept. We can make that easier: a diagnosis that says which part failed and why, a written price item by item from our published Brooklyn list, a copy of the findings for every owner who needs to see them, and a note of what was done that the next owner can find.

Who Decides

The Owner Upstairs, a Board of Three or a Managing Agent: Who Can Approve a Carroll Gardens Buzzer Repair

Before a technician touches anything, it helps to know which of four situations your building is in, because each one answers the questions of authority, duty and payment differently. What follows is general information from the statutes named, not legal advice; your own documents can change the answer.

An owner who rents out the other floors

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building with three or more households living independently as a multiple dwelling, and a one- or two-family house as a private dwelling. Most of Carroll Gardens' three- and four-family houses are therefore multiple dwellings, even when the owner lives in one of the apartments, and HPD requires the owner of any building with three or more units to register it. The Housing Maintenance Code puts the duty to keep the building in good repair on the owner, and that is the section HPD cites for a broken intercom. The Multiple Dwelling Law's specific intercom rule, section 50-a, applies in full only to buildings put up or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments; for older buildings, it lets tenants require an intercom on a written request from a majority of the apartments.

For an owner-occupant, the practical answer is simple: you can approve the repair yourself, the bill is yours, and if a tenant has complained, the fix is what clears the record. We need your go-ahead, not the tenant's, before we open the system or order parts.

A small condominium

Carroll Gardens has more than a hundred small condominiums, many of them rowhouses divided into three or four units, and a few larger ones in former churches. New York's Condominium Act, Article 9-B of the Real Property Law, divides every condominium into units and common elements. Its definition of common elements includes, among other things, "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" and any facilities the declaration designates as common. A street panel, the riser up the stair hall, the transformer in the cellar and the strike in the street door are used by everyone, which usually makes them common elements, maintained by the board of managers and paid for as a common expense. Whether the handset on your kitchen wall belongs to you or to the condominium is set by your own declaration and by-laws, and they differ from building to building.

The Act also says who answers to the city. For the purposes of the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Housing Maintenance Code, each unit owner is treated as the person in control of the unit and the board of managers as the person in control of the common elements. In a four-unit condo where the board is simply the four owners, that means the board decides on a street-door repair, even if one owner books the visit.

A walk-up co-op

The 66 walk-up co-ops in the study area are corporations that own their buildings, with each shareholder holding a proprietary lease for an apartment. No statute spells out who maintains a buzzer in a co-op; the proprietary lease and house rules do. A common pattern is that the corporation maintains the building's systems and the shareholder maintains the inside of the apartment, which raises the same question as in a condo about the station on the wall and the wire behind it. Read the lease clause on repairs before the visit; we will price the work so that the corporation's share and the shareholder's share, if there is one, are separate lines.

A rented apartment

If you rent, the repair is the owner's to approve and pay for. Tell the owner in writing, keep a copy, and if nothing happens, the city's 311 system accepts complaints about broken or missing doorbells, buzzers and intercoms. We will not bill a tenant for a building system or open it without the owner's agreement.

What We Install

Door Buzzer Repair Services in Carroll Gardens

Every job starts with the $250 service call and the same order of work: the street door first, then each floor's button or handset, then the wire and the transformer. What we repair after that depends on which part failed.

Floor-by-floor station repair

In a house with one apartment on each floor, each station is its own small circuit. We test every floor, replace a handset or button that has worn out, and confirm it can both hear the street and release the door. Station replacement runs $250 to $525 on our Brooklyn list.

Street panel and button repair

Cracked buttons, corroded contacts and faceplates that have weathered on a stoop for decades. We repair or replace the panel to suit the number of homes the building has today, not the number it had when the panel went in. Panel and faceplate work is $650 to $1,800.

Strike and release repair

A strike that hums but does not let the latch go, or a release that works from one floor and not another. We check the voltage at the strike with it energized, the keeper, and the way the door sits in the frame. A new strike with alignment is $450 to $950.

Transformer and power

When every button goes weak at once, the transformer in the cellar is the usual suspect, often an undersized replacement fitted years ago. A 24-volt transformer replacement is $325 to $650; anything on the line-voltage side is for a licensed electrician.

Riser and wiring repair

Breaks and crossed pairs in the wire that runs up the stair hall, often left by renovations on one floor. We tone each pair, repair it with an accessible junction and label both ends. Riser repair and re-termination is $850 to $2,400.

Door closing and latching

A buzzer is only as good as the door that locks behind the visitor. We adjust closers, reset latches and keepers and correct a door that stops short of the frame, the faults HPD writes as Class C when they stop a door from self-closing.

Rewiring after combined floors

When two floors become a duplex or a unit is split, the panel and stations stop matching the building. We retire the extra station, move or relabel buttons and make sure the remaining handsets release the door.

Shared street doors in store buildings

On Court and Smith Streets a shop and the apartments above it often share one street door, or sit side by side with separate doors that share a transformer. We repair the residential circuit without disturbing the shop's own doorbell or lock.

Written findings for owners and boards

After the visit you get a written note of what failed, what was done and which pair serves which floor, in a form a condo board, co-op or out-of-town owner can file and pass on.

Larger walk-ups and a few elevator buildings

How the repair changes with the building

One apartment per floor. In three- and four-family houses the wire to each floor usually runs up the stair hall in a single bundle, with a short branch into each apartment near its entrance door. A floor that goes dead after work in the hall points to the bundle; a floor that goes dead after work inside one apartment points to the branch. We tone from the street panel to each floor before opening any wall, so the repair is made at the break rather than by pulling a new run through finished plaster.

Garden-floor units. The lowest apartment in many houses has its own door under the stoop and sometimes no connection to the main buzzer at all. Where it shares the panel, its pair often leaves the main run in the cellar, which is also where the transformer sits. Access to the cellar, which in some houses means access through that apartment, is part of planning the visit.

Condo and co-op buildings. The circuit is the same as in a rental house; the difference is the paperwork. Shared and in-unit parts are priced as separate lines, and findings go to whoever the board names.

Store buildings. The residential street door and the shop often share a transformer or a cellar, and sometimes the same door. We trace which circuit belongs to which before replacing anything, so a repair upstairs does not silence the shop's bell.

The Front Gardens

Thirty-Three Feet Between the Gate and the Bell

The gardens that gave Carroll Gardens its name change the buzzer problem on the blocks that have them. Wikipedia records that an 1846 law required buildings between Henry and Smith Streets to stand 33 feet 5¼ inches back from the street, and that the deep gardens survive from First to Fourth Place between Henry and Smith, and on President, Carroll and Second Streets between Smith and Hoyt. The Department of City Planning noted in 2008 that the mapped width of those streets includes the front yards, so the garden in front of a house on those blocks is, on the City Map, part of the street. On those blocks, a visitor who presses a button at the parlor door has walked the length of a garden from the sidewalk, and a visitor who stops at a locked gate is more than thirty feet from the nearest bell.

For a repair, that distance matters in three ways. First, a buzzer button that was moved to the gate post at some point has a long run back to the house, usually buried under the garden path or fixed along the fence, and that run is exposed to digging, roots, water and the shovels of whoever replants the beds. Second, a release that opens the gate as well as the street door needs its own strike in an iron gate, which works and fails differently from a strike in a wooden door. Third, on the Carroll and President Street blocks inside the Carroll Gardens Historic District, anything new that is mounted at the entrance falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Its Permit Guidebook allows staff-level approval for intercom panels in or beside a door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, limits them to one panel per opening, and says ordinary maintenance and repair needs no permit.

When we repair a buzzer on one of the garden blocks, we test the run from the gate separately from the run inside the house, so that a cut in the garden is not mistaken for a fault in the riser. If the wire has been cut more than once, we will say so and suggest how the next repair can be made without digging up the same bed again. We do not trench gardens ourselves; where a new run has to be buried, we tell you what has to be laid and the gardener or mason lays it.

Rules that apply to Carroll Gardens entrances

  • Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a: class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments a two-way voice intercom with a door release; older buildings must provide one when a majority of apartments ask in writing. In our study area 1,950 of 2,046 residential lots with a recorded year predate 1968.

  • Egress and access: a magnetic lock on an exit door needs a release that lets people out on a power failure, and the 2010 ADA standards set 48 inches as the highest forward reach for controls in places they cover.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the questions behind Carroll Gardens calls, grouped by what kind of decision they are about. Most of them are less about wire than about who owns it.

Owners, boards and who pays

Our four-unit condo has no managing agent. What can one owner set in motion before the other three have signed off?

Anyone can book the diagnosis, and that is usually the sensible first step, because the board cannot vote on a repair until somebody knows what failed. The $250 service call covers the visit and the diagnosis. What one owner usually cannot do alone is commit the condominium to paying for work on the street panel, the riser or the strike, because under New York's Condominium Act those shared parts are normally common elements controlled by the board of managers.

In a four-unit building the board is often all four owners, and its by-laws say how it can act: at a meeting, by written consent, or through one officer for repairs under a set amount. We send a written, item-by-item price to the address the board gives us, keep the street door locking on its key and latch while the owners decide, and start work when we have the go-ahead in writing from whoever the by-laws authorize.

In our condo, is the handset on my kitchen wall my own property or part of the building's system?

Your declaration answers that, not the hardware. The Condominium Act defines common elements to include apparatus and installations that exist for common use, and lets the declaration designate others. A street panel and the riser serve everyone; the station in your kitchen serves only you, but it is connected to the shared circuit and is useless without it. Some declarations treat everything from the wall plate inward as part of the unit, others treat the whole system as common.

On the quote we split the two: the station line, $250 to $525 on our Brooklyn list, is shown separately from anything on the shared side. That lets the board and the owner apply their own documents without our having to decide who owns what, and it avoids the common mistake of a board paying for an upgrade in one unit that the other owners did not choose.

Two of our three condo owners want to split the buzzer bill equally and one wants it split by unit size. What does the Condominium Act say about dividing common expenses?

For the shared parts it sets a default. The Condominium Act provides that common expenses are charged to unit owners according to their respective common interests, the percentages set out in the declaration, with some exceptions the Act and the declaration allow. Those percentages were fixed when the condominium was created, often by floor area or value, so in a rowhouse condo the owner of the large garden duplex may carry a bigger share than the owner of a top-floor studio.

That rule covers the shared parts. If the fault is in one owner's station and the declaration makes the station part of that unit, it may be that owner's cost alone. Because our written price separates shared and in-unit lines, the owners can apply their percentages to the right numbers.

One owner in our condo will not let anyone in to reach the wiring. Does the board have a right of access for a repair?

The Condominium Act gives the unit owners, exercised through the board of managers, an irrevocable right of access to each unit during reasonable hours to the extent necessary to operate the property, or to make emergency repairs needed to prevent damage to the common elements or to another unit. By-laws can add reasonable rules on how that access is arranged. How it applies to a particular buzzer repair is a question for the board and, if it comes to that, its lawyer.

In practice we try to make access unnecessary. We tone the pairs from the street panel and the cellar first, which often locates a break in the hall or the basement without entering any apartment. If the fault really is inside one unit, we tell the board exactly where, how long we would need, and that the rest of the building can be repaired around it in the meantime.

Our co-op's proprietary lease says shareholders maintain their own apartments. How does a board decide whether a break in the wire inside my walls falls under that clause?

Your lease decides, and co-op leases vary. Many leave the inside of the apartment to the shareholder and the building's systems to the corporation, which leaves the wire inside your walls in a grey zone: it sits in your apartment, but it carries the building's intercom.

What we can supply is the evidence the board needs to decide. Our written findings say where the break is, measured from the panel, whether it is in the riser that serves other floors or in the branch that serves only your apartment, and what each repair costs on our list. A break in the shared riser is almost always the corporation's; a station that has simply worn out is more often the shareholder's. The board makes the call with the facts in front of it.

Rentals, complaints and the city

Our three-family's buzzer has been unreliable for years and nobody has complained. If we fix it now, does anything have to be filed with the city?

Usually not. Repairing an existing low-voltage buzzer, whether that means a new station, transformer, strike or a repaired riser pair, is ordinary repair work: there is no HPD form to file when no violation exists, and our work stays on the low-voltage side, so no electrical permit is involved unless an electrician has to add or change a line-voltage circuit for the transformer. In the Carroll Gardens Historic District, the LPC's Permit Guidebook says ordinary maintenance and repair needs no permit either.

Filing matters only if HPD has already written a violation, in which case the correction has to be certified by the date on the notice. It is still worth doing the repair before anyone complains. A three-family is a multiple dwelling, the Housing Maintenance Code makes the owner responsible for keeping it in repair, and the HPD intercom violations in these blocks all cite that duty. Keep our written findings with the building's papers; they show when the system was last repaired and tested, which is useful if a complaint ever does arrive.

A tenant in our owner-occupied three-family filed a 311 complaint about the buzzer. Does the fact that we live here change how HPD treats the building?

No. The Multiple Dwelling Law counts a building with three or more households living independently as a multiple dwelling whoever lives in it, and HPD requires the owner of any building with three or more units to register it. An owner on the parlor floor is the landlord of the other two floors for these purposes, and a complaint is handled the same way as in any rental building: HPD may inspect and, if the intercom is broken, issue a notice of violation with a class and a correction date. Intercom violations in Carroll Gardens have been written as Class B, which HPD gives 30 days, and Class A, which it gives 90.

The difference for a two-family house is real: there the Multiple Dwelling Law treats the building as a private dwelling. For a three- or four-family, the practical path is to repair the buzzer promptly, certify the correction if a violation is issued, and keep our written note that every floor was tested at the street door. HPD tells tenants when a correction is certified, and they can challenge it.

HPD wrote an intercom violation at our building. What is the certification deadline, and what happens if we miss it?

The deadline is printed on the notice of violation: HPD gives 90 days to correct a Class A violation and 30 days for Class B, and each notice shows the date by which the correction must be certified. HPD describes certification of correction as free, filed on paper or through its eCertification system. After it is filed, HPD may audit it with a reinspection, and tenants are notified and can challenge it, which also triggers an audit. A certification found to be untrue goes into false-certification status.

Miss the date and the route changes: HPD may require a dismissal request reinspection before the violation is cleared, which the owner applies for and pays a fee for. Either way, keep our written findings with the notice. They show the date of the repair, what was replaced and that every apartment was tested at the street door, which is what an auditing inspector will check.

Changed buildings, changed buttons

We combined two floors into a duplex. Which handset should stay, and what happens to the other one?

Keep the station on the floor where the household actually answers the door, usually the main living floor, and disconnect the other one at the riser rather than simply leaving it dead on the wall. A station that is still wired but no longer used can be the cause of a fault later, for example a handset left off its hook that holds the line open for the whole building.

If the household wants to answer from both floors of the duplex, a second station can usually be wired in parallel on the same pair, so that either one rings and either one releases the door. Combining floors also changes the street panel: one button now serves a unit that used to have two. We relabel the panel so that visitors and couriers see the building as it is now, and note the change in the written findings.

Our panel still has a button wired to an apartment that was merged away. Can a leftover live wire cause trouble for the other floors?

It can. A button whose pair was cut back at the riser is harmless. A button still connected to a pair that now runs to nothing, or to a station left inside a wall during a renovation, can short or pick up interference and make the other buttons behave oddly, especially in wet weather when the outdoor panel takes on moisture. A handset abandoned off its hook inside a wall can hold the line open for everyone.

We check what the extra button is connected to before deciding. If its pair is live, we disconnect it at the panel and at the riser and mark it spare, which keeps it available if the building is ever divided again, and we note the change in the written findings so the next owner knows the pair is retired rather than broken.

The owner of one floor wants their new video doorbell to open the street door too. Can it be tied into the shared strike, and whose decision is that?

Technically it often can; the decision is usually not that owner's alone. Tying a video doorbell into the street-door strike means connecting a private device to a shared part, and in a condo or co-op the strike, the transformer and the riser are normally the building's. That makes it a board decision, and a sensible board will want to know what happens to the other floors if the device fails or is removed.

The technical risk is power. Many video doorbells draw more than a bell circuit was designed for, and wiring one onto the shared transformer can leave every other floor's buzzer weak. We can tell the board whether the existing transformer has the capacity, price a separate supply if it does not, and wire the release so that removing the doorbell later leaves the building's circuit intact.

We live in one of the converted church buildings, and the panel is newer than the doors. Which usually fails first, the electronics or the door hardware?

In our experience the door hardware. Wikipedia records that the South Congregational Church complex on Court and President Streets and the former Westminster Presbyterian Church on Clinton Street were both converted to condominium apartments. Conversions like these typically put a modern panel and wiring into a building whose entrance doors, or replicas of them, are large and heavy. A strike sized for an ordinary door struggles to hold a heavy leaf that has dropped, and a closer that is too light never quite brings the door home.

So we test the door before the panel: let it close on its own, push it shut by hand and press the release, and look at the keeper and hinges. If the door is the problem, the repair is adjustment, a heavier closer or a strike suited to the door. Anything new on a landmark entrance is a matter for the LPC and the board; like-for-like repair is not.

Storefronts and garden blocks

Our store on Court Street shares the street door with the apartments upstairs. Can the shop's hours and the tenants' buzzer work on one door?

They can, but the door has to be set up for both. The usual arrangement is that the shop uses its own entrance and the shared door stays locked, with tenants buzzing visitors in. Where the shop also uses the shared door, for deliveries or a back office, the risk is that it is left unlocked all day for convenience, which defeats the buzzer and, in a building with three or more apartments, runs into the self-closing and self-locking door rules.

Options include a separate release button for the shop inside the hallway, a keypad or fob for staff, or a strike set to stay locked with the buzzer working as normal. Which one suits depends on the lease between shop and building owner. We repair the residential circuit first, then price whichever shop arrangement the owner chooses, as a separate line.

Our buzzer cable runs under the front-garden path. What happens to it when the garden is replanted or the path is relaid?

Often it gets cut, and nobody notices until the gate button stops working. On the blocks with Carroll Gardens' deep front gardens, a button or release at the gate depends on a wire that crosses thirty feet or more of garden, and unless it was laid in conduit at a known depth, a spade or a new edging can sever it. Water in a damaged cable causes the stranger symptom of a button that works in dry weather and fails after rain.

Before the garden work starts, tell us or your gardener where the run goes; we can trace it so it can be marked. If it has already been cut, we test from the gate and from the house to find the break, splice it in a sealed connection if the cable is sound, and suggest conduit if the path is open anyway. We do not dig gardens ourselves; we tell you what needs to go in the trench.

Types of Door Buzzer Equipment We Repair in Carroll Gardens

A Carroll Gardens rowhouse can hold parts from four or five decades, installed by whichever owner happened to be doing the work.

Street button panels

From single brass buttons set in a door frame to four- and six-button plates with name cards. Weathering, cracked buttons and moisture behind the plate are the usual faults on a stoop that faces the street.

Apartment handsets and stations

Wall handsets with a talk button and a door button, plain bell-and-release buttons, and newer stations with a screen. Each floor's station is the part most often replaced by one owner without telling the others.

Bell transformers

The small transformer, usually in the cellar near the electrical panel, that powers the buttons, the ringers and the strike. An undersized replacement is a common reason why every button is weak.

Electric strikes

The part in the door frame that lets the latch go. Fail-secure strikes stay locked when power is lost, which suits most street doors; Allegion's guidance notes that with a strike, people inside can always leave by turning the lever.

Closers, latches and keepers

The hardware that brings the street door back into the frame and holds it there. With four households using one door, this is the part that wears out first.

Gate strikes and gate buttons

On the garden blocks, a release in the iron gate and a button on the gate post, with a long run back to the house that is easy to cut during garden work.

Video doorbells and video stations

Single-unit video doorbells added by one owner, and multi-button video stations on newer conversions. We check they are powered separately from the shared bell circuit.

App and IP door panels

Panels that call residents' phones over the internet, found in some newer condos. When they stop calling, the fault is usually power, network or an administrator account that left with the developer.

Repair or replace in a building with a few owners

In a large building, the decision to replace a whole intercom is made by a managing agent with a budget. "Every landlord is required by law to have a working intercom"

Answers built from national pages often state the rule as if it were universal. The source of New York's specific rule, Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, is narrower: it requires an intercom with a door release in multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments, and in older buildings only when a majority of the apartments ask in writing.

In Carroll Gardens that distinction matters, because 1,950 of 2,046 residential lots with a recorded year predate 1968 and three in four residential lots hold two to four homes. For most buildings here, the intercom question is not whether the law demands one, but whether the one already in place is being kept in repair under the Housing Maintenance Code.

"If the landlord will not fix it, the city will make them"

The city can, through 311 complaints, HPD inspections and violations. AI answers tend to present that as the normal route to a repair.

The record in Carroll Gardens shows how rarely it is used. On the 62 blocks of our study area HPD has written intercom violations at 34 buildings since 1990, 42 of them at a single building, and 311 has received buzzer complaints from 30 addresses since 2020. In small owner-occupied buildings, tenants and owners mostly sort it out between themselves, or do not.

"A buzzer repair costs $150 to $400"

Vendor guides do quote figures like that; Swiftlane, for example, puts minor apartment intercom repairs at $150 to $400. AI answers repeat such ranges without saying which repair or which city.

Our Brooklyn list starts with a $250 service call that includes the diagnosis and covers simple fixes; a station is $250 to $525, a transformer $325 to $650, a strike with alignment $450 to $950 and riser repair $850 to $2,400. A national range cannot tell you whether your fault is a loose terminal or a cut riser behind plaster.

In a Carroll Gardens condo the more useful number is often the split: what falls on the shared side and what falls inside one unit. That is why our quotes separate the two.

"In a condo, the board fixes everything"

Answers that treat a condominium like a rental building, with the board as landlord, miss how New York's Condominium Act divides the property into units and common elements.

The Act defines common elements to include apparatus and installations that exist for common use, and lets each declaration designate others. The shared panel and riser are usually common; the station inside a unit may or may not be, depending on the declaration. The Act also makes each unit owner the person in control of the unit for Housing Maintenance Code purposes.

For the 105 small condominiums in our study area, the answer to who fixes what is in a document the owners already have. Our itemized quotes are designed to be read against it.

"Just replace the old buzzer with a wireless or app system"

Some vendor articles present cellular or app-based call boxes as needing no building wiring, and AI answers repeat that as a reason to skip repair. ButterflyMX, for example, describes its intercom as requiring no building wiring.

A panel that calls phones can replace the button-to-apartment wiring, but the street door still has to open, which means a strike in the door, power to it and a door that closes and latches. In a rowhouse those parts are often what failed in the first place.

For a four-unit building, an app panel also brings a subscription and an administrator account; that is a decision for all the owners, not a shortcut around a repair.

"Brownstones in historic districts cannot change anything without a permit"

This one is half right. Work on the exterior of buildings in the Carroll Gardens Historic District does need Landmarks Preservation Commission review.

But the LPC's Permit Guidebook says ordinary maintenance and repair does not need a permit, and it allows staff-level approval of intercom panels in or beside a door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, with one panel per opening. Repairing a working layout is not the same as installing something new.

Only 140 of the 2,156 lots in our study area are inside the district. For most Carroll Gardens buildings, landmarks rules are not a factor in a buzzer repair at all.

"An intercom system costs about $2,500"

HomeAdvisor puts the national average cost of installing an intercom at about $2,500, and that figure travels widely in AI answers about intercoms and buzzers.

It describes a new installation, not a repair. Most Carroll Gardens calls end with one part replaced on a system that otherwise works, at the lower end of our Brooklyn list. A full replacement is a different project with a different price.
