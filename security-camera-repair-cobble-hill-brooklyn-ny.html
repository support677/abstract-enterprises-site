The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Cobble Hill is small: about 40 blocks bounded by Atlantic Avenue to the north, Court Street to the east, Degraw Street to the south and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway to the west. Its streets are mostly brownstones and brick row houses from the middle of the nineteenth century, with Greek Revival, Italianate and later styles side by side, low apartment buildings that went up before the 1920s, a few large courtyard complexes, churches that have been turned into homes, and newer buildings on the land where Long Island College Hospital stood until it closed in 2014. Court and Smith Streets carry most of the shops, and Atlantic Avenue the rest. That is the setting. For a camera repair, three features of it matter more than anything else.

The first is the historic district. The Cobble Hill Historic District was designated in December 1969, extended in June 1988 and listed on the National Register in 1976, and it covers about 22 blocks. Inside it, the Landmarks Preservation Commission treats a camera on a facade as a fixture it cares about. Its rules say cameras on landmarked buildings "will be located to avoid a cluttered or haphazard appearance" and attached in a way that minimizes their visible impact, and staff can approve one only if the installation "will not call undue attention to itself or detract from any significant architectural feature(s)" of the building or its neighbors. The 2018 amendments go further for the most visible wall: they bar many kinds of equipment from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative. None of that stops a repair. It does change how a repair should be done, because a replacement camera with a bigger housing, a new bracket in a new place or a fresh run of conduit up the brick is no longer the same fixture.

The second is the walls themselves. Old brick and brownstone are thick, interior walls are often plaster on wood lath with moldings nobody wants touched, and vestibules are small boxes of tile, wood and glass. That is hard on cameras in specific ways. Infrared light from a camera in a tight vestibule bounces off glazed tile and the glass of the inner door and turns the night picture to white haze. A Wi-Fi camera on the garden wall often sits two masonry walls and a full house depth away from a router in the front parlor, and its signal comes and goes with the weather and the neighbors' networks.

The third is the gardens. Behind most rows, the back yards touch one another along low walls and fences, and some houses look onto shared courtyards or private walks rather than a street. Garden cameras are useful for the rear doors and the garden-floor apartments that many of these houses have, but they also see over the wall. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives the owner or tenant of a home a right to sue someone who records their back yard from neighboring property without written consent, where the recording is meant to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. That is a high bar and it is aimed at bad behavior, not at an ordinary camera watching its own garden. But when a dead garden camera is replaced with a modern one whose lens is far wider than the old model's, the new picture can take in two or three neighbors' patios that the old one never saw. Getting the view right, and masking out what is not yours, is part of the repair.

Footage still matters in a neighborhood this quiet. CBS New York reported a robbery in Cobble Hill in which building surveillance video showed the suspect waiting outside for an hour and a half before following a woman into the courtyard. The 76th Precinct, which covers Cobble Hill along with Carroll Gardens, Red Hook and parts of Gowanus and the Columbia Street Waterfront from its station at 191 Union Street, has asked the public for help with video in other cases over the years: a break-in at a Japanese restaurant, a group of suspected thieves on Atlantic Avenue, a shoplifting arrest at a Court Street store, and a package taken from an apartment building's vestibule on Amity Street near Henry Street in the early afternoon. Most of those stories are several years old, and we found no neighborhood-level news from 2025 or 2026 about camera failures. The point is not that Cobble Hill is dangerous. It is that when something does happen, people go looking for footage, and the camera they look to is often the one that quietly stopped recording months ago.

So a camera repair in Cobble Hill is rarely just "make the dark camera work again." It is making it work on the same footprint, with a picture that is usable at the door, in the vestibule and in the garden, recording at a quality you can actually use, and warning you the next time it stops. Many owners pair the repair with a look at the door buzzer or video intercom at the same entrance, since on these houses both systems share the same stoop and the same vestibule.

Brownstone and brick row houses

Three- and four-story houses from the 1840s onward with stoops, vestibules, parlor floors and garden floors. Cameras over the door, under the cornice and on the rear wall, wired down to a recorder in the cellar.

Houses on courtyards and mews

Cottages and townhouses that face a private walk or courtyard rather than a street, such as the 34 cottages of Warren Place. Cameras that look across a shared path at neighbors' doors.

Courtyard apartment complexes

The Home and Tower Buildings on Hicks Street, with outdoor stairs, open balconies and large interior courtyards instead of hallways. Many cameras, all of them outdoors.

Pre-war walk-ups

Low apartment buildings built before the 1920s on Henry, Clinton and Court Streets, often with a shop at street level and a recorder in a cellar shared with the meters and the boiler.

Church and school conversions

Former churches and parish buildings turned into apartments or condos, such as St. Peter's on Hicks Street and the old Strong Place Baptist Church. Tall ceilings, thick stone, cameras placed wherever a crew could reach.

Brownstone and brick row houses: four floors of cable in an old wall

The typical Cobble Hill row house has a camera over the front door or under the stoop, sometimes one under the cornice looking down the block, and one or two on the rear wall facing the garden. The recorder is in the cellar or a garden-floor closet. The cameras on the upper floors are the problem when they fail, because their cables did not go up the outside of the house; on most of the systems we see, the installer fished them inside a wall cavity, through a closet or up beside a chimney breast, to keep the facade clean. That was the right decision for the house, and it means the repair has to respect it. We test a dead upper-floor camera from both ends of its cable before anything else, because on runs like these the fault is far more often a connector at the camera or at the recorder than the cable itself, and a connector can be replaced from the end without touching the wall.

When the cable really has failed inside the wall, there are usually three choices: reuse the old cable as a pull string to bring a new one through the same path, find a different hidden route such as an existing chase or a closet, or run a short, painted surface raceway on the rear wall where nobody sees it. We price each one so you can choose. What we do not do is drill a new hole through the front wall because it is quicker. On a landmarked house that creates a new visible change, and on any old brick wall it is one more place for water to get in.

Houses on courtyards and mews: the camera that sees across the walk

Some Cobble Hill houses do not face a street at all. Warren Place, a gated street of 34 brick cottages built in the 1870s, faces a private courtyard between Warren and Baltic Streets, and other houses in the neighborhood look onto shared courts and private walks. A door camera on a house like that often looks straight across a narrow path at a neighbor's front door and window. When it fails and is replaced with a newer model, the wider lens and better night picture can turn a camera that used to show your own step into one that records the comings and goings of the household opposite. On these houses, part of the repair is agreeing on the view: aiming the replacement at your own door and step, and using the recorder's privacy masks to black out the parts of the frame that belong to someone else. If a shared association or management group controls the walk, we check with whoever speaks for it before a camera that covers common ground is changed.

Courtyard apartment complexes: every camera is an outdoor camera

The Home Buildings of 1876 to 1877 and the Tower Buildings and cottages of 1878 to 1879 on Hicks Street are reached by exterior staircases and open balconies around large interior courtyards; after their restoration they held 188 apartments, and condominium conversion began in 2008. In a complex built like that, the cameras that would sit in a dry interior hallway anywhere else are on outdoor landings, under balcony slabs and on courtyard walls. They take wind-driven rain on the lens, snow on the housing, and summer sun on the dome, and their connectors live in the weather. The failures we expect there are water at a connector, a fogged lens where a seal has given up after a few winters, and domes clouded by sun. The repair is outdoor-rated hardware, sealed connections and drip loops, even when the camera is "inside" the complex.

Pre-war walk-ups: the cellar everybody shares

The low apartment buildings on Henry, Clinton and Court Streets were mostly built before the 1920s, and many have a shop on the ground floor. Their recorder is usually in the cellar, often on a shelf near the meters, the boiler and the shop's stock. That location causes most of their camera repairs. Damp air and dust shorten the life of the recorder and its drive; the outlet it plugs into may belong to whichever circuit was closest, sometimes the shop's; and anyone with a cellar key can unplug it to free a socket. Part of a walk-up repair is putting the recorder somewhere dry and secure, on the owner's own power, and labeling what it is so the next person in the cellar leaves it alone. Many of these buildings also have original risers and conduit with little room left in them, so a failed vertical run is a planning job rather than a quick pull.

Church and school conversions: high ceilings and thick stone

St. Peter's Church on Hicks Street, built in 1860, and its school became condominiums, and the Strong Place Baptist Church of 1851 to 1852 was converted to apartments in 2010. Buildings like these hand a camera system unusual problems. Interior ceilings are far higher than a row house's, so a camera mounted at the top of a converted nave or stair hall shows faces at the bottom as a few pixels each. Exterior walls are thick stone or brick that the developer's crew drilled wherever they could reach. When a camera fails in a building like this, the repair is often a different lens on the same mount, a varifocal camera that can be zoomed to the doorway, or a second camera lower down on the same cable path, rather than a new system.

New buildings on the hospital campus: sophisticated systems, unsettled ownership

Long Island College Hospital closed in August 2014, and its land has been redeveloped in phases. 6sqft reported the Polhemus Residences at 100 Amity Street and the Polhemus Townhouses at 88 to 98 Amity Street as the first phase, with 5 River Park at 347 Henry Street holding 25 units, and listed amenities including an attended lobby and a secured package room. CityRealty describes 354 Hicks Street, bought from the original developer in 2022, as a 20-story building with 34 condos over five floors of medical space and an attended garage, with completion expected in 2027. Buildings like these usually run network cameras on a managed switch with a video intercom, sometimes tied to a cloud account. The hardware is current; the trouble is that logins, warranties and service contracts can sit with the developer, the sponsor's installer or a management company. A repair there starts with finding out who is allowed to change what, then fixing the fault.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

In New York, companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for others must be licensed by the Department of State; we work under NYS license #12000287431. Low-voltage work does not always need a Department of Buildings permit, but installers note that larger cable work, new conduit through fire-rated construction, or work in a building already under a DOB renovation can bring one in, and condo and co-op common areas have their own approval rules. Employers who watch staff by camera should know that New York Labor Law §203-c bars video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms. On landmarked buildings, including shopfronts inside the historic district, a facade camera replaced in kind is ordinary repair, while a new or moved one is not. Businesses can also register their camera locations with the NYPD through the city's Connect New York program at no cost; Gothamist reported owners had added more than 5,500 cameras through that initiative. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

The historic district: what a repair can and cannot change

Our house is on one of the blocks added to the Cobble Hill Historic District in 1988, not the original 1969 area. Does that change how a camera repair is handled?

No. The June 1988 extension made those blocks part of the same historic district, so the same Landmarks rules apply to a house there as to one on the blocks designated in December 1969. What matters is whether your own lot is inside the district line, which you can confirm by address on the Landmarks Preservation Commission's online map. If it is, replacing a dead camera in kind, on the same mounts and at about the same size, is ordinary repair of safety equipment, which the LPC's permit guidebook does not require a permit for. Moving the camera, adding one, or changing its look enough to draw attention is a different kind of job, and we tell you before the visit if your repair is heading that way.

The replacement camera another company quoted is about twice the size of our old one and needs a new length of conduit up the brick. Is that still a repair?

Probably not, as far as the Landmarks rules are concerned. A much larger housing and new exposed conduit are visible changes to the facade, and the LPC's guidebook lets staff approve only about ten inches of visible conduit, while its 2018 amendments discourage new equipment on a primary facade where there is a feasible alternative. In most cases there is one: a current camera of similar size and color that fits the old base or an adapter plate, with its cable kept on the old hidden path. We price that option first, and if a bigger camera really is needed for what you want to see, we explain what an application would involve so you can decide.

We would rather take the camera off the front of our house and watch the stoop from inside the vestibule or from under the stoop. Does moving it off the facade avoid the Landmarks question?

Often it is the simplest route. Installers who work in Brooklyn's districts point out that interior or less visible mounting frequently avoids Landmarks review while still covering the door, and the 2018 rules are built around keeping the primary facade clear. The trade-offs are technical. A camera inside the vestibule looks through or past glass and has to be set up so it does not light up the tile and the door with its own infrared, and a camera under the stoop sees people at knee height unless it is placed carefully. Taking the old camera down also leaves anchor holes in the facade; we remove the camera and its bracket and seal the cable entry, but filling mortar or stone is work for your mason.

Behind the row: gardens, courtyards and neighbors

We heard New York lets a neighbor sue over a camera that records their back yard. Does that matter when the dead camera on our garden wall is replaced?

It is worth knowing about, though it is aimed at bad behavior rather than ordinary cameras. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a gives the owner or tenant of a home a right to sue a person who mounts a camera on neighboring property to record their back yard without written consent, where the recording is meant to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten; the law defines the back yard as the part of the lot behind the rear wall of the house. A camera watching your own garden and rear door is not that. But modern replacements often have much wider lenses than the cameras they replace, so we aim the new one at your own garden, set privacy masks over the neighbors' yards and windows, and check what the infrared lights up at night. We are not your lawyer, and if a neighbor has already complained, that is a conversation for one.

Our cottage faces a narrow shared walk, and the door camera looks straight at the houses opposite. When it is replaced, how do we keep it to our own doorstep?

Three settings and one choice of hardware do most of the work. The camera is aimed down and toward your own step rather than straight out, so the opposite houses fall out of the bottom of the frame; privacy masks on the recorder or camera black out any part of a neighbor's door or window that is still visible, in the recording as well as the live view; and motion detection is limited to your side of the walk, so people going to the house opposite do not trigger alerts. A camera with a narrower or zoomable lens makes that much easier than a very wide one. If an association or management group looks after the walk and its gate, we check with them before changing a camera that covers common ground.

Vestibules, parlor floors and old walls

Our brownstone's vestibule is barely deeper than the two doors, with a tiled floor and glass in the inner door. Which kind of camera actually works in a space that small?

A small camera with its infrared switched off and a wide but not fisheye lens, mounted high in a corner so it looks across the space rather than at the inner door's glass, is usually the answer. The tile and glass are what ruin night pictures in vestibules like yours, because the camera's own infrared bounces straight back into the lens. Give it light instead: a small warm fixture on a motion sensor, mounted away from the camera, or a camera built to record in color in low light. If the vestibule must also show who is on the stoop outside, that is often better done by a second camera outside the street door, because one camera cannot see both well through glass. On our Brooklyn list, this kind of fix runs from a settings change within the service call to $350 to $700 for a consultation and replacement.

The cable to our camera over the front door was fished down inside a wall with its original plaster moldings, and that camera is now dark. Will the wall have to be opened to fix it?

Usually not. On hidden runs like that, the fault is most often at one of the two ends, at the connector behind the camera or at the recorder, and both can be fixed without touching the wall. We test the camera on a short known-good cable, test the run end to end, and check for splices in the cellar or a closet before concluding the cable itself has failed. If it has, the old cable can often pull a new one through the same path, or a different hidden route can be found. Opening the wall is the last option, it is written into the price before we start, and the patching and painting afterwards are for your plasterer or painter, not us.

Courtyard complexes, conversions and condos

Our condo has outdoor stairs and open walkways instead of interior hallways, and its cameras face the weather all year. Do they need a different camera from an indoor hall?

Yes. A camera on an open landing or under a balcony slab gets rain blown sideways onto its lens, snow on its housing and full summer sun, even though it looks as if it is inside the building. It needs an outdoor weather rating, connectors sealed against water, a drip loop on the cable, and ideally a hood or a position under the slab that keeps the lens dry. Indoor domes in those spots fog and fail after a few winters. When we repair a system in a courtyard complex, we check the rating of every camera on the open stairs and balconies, not only the one that died, and price outdoor-rated replacements where indoor models were used.

Our condo is in a converted church, and the cameras hang from a ceiling more than two stories up. Faces in the lobby below come out tiny. Can that be fixed without rewiring?

In most cases, yes. A wide camera at that height spreads its pixels over the whole floor, so a face at the entrance is only a small part of the frame. The usual repair is a varifocal camera on the same mount and cable, zoomed to the doorway and the mailboxes, or a second camera lower down, such as on a column or above the inner door, fed from the same cable path. Before choosing, we check the recorder's free channels and the camera's power budget on the switch and confirm with the board how a lift or tall ladder gets in.

Our building was a rental for decades before it became a condo, and the camera recorder is still registered to the old management company's cloud account. Can it be moved into the condo's name without losing the cameras?

Usually, yes. The cameras themselves are rarely tied to an account; it is the recorder's cloud registration and its administrator login that need to change hands. The cleanest route is for the old management company to release the device from its account, which many will do once the board asks in writing. If they will not or cannot, most recorders can be reset with physical access and proof that the condo now owns the equipment, then registered to a new account in the condo's name with its own administrator and separate logins for the managing agent and any board members who need them. We copy any footage the board wants to keep before a reset.

Our co-op board wants to use the repaired hallway cameras to keep an eye on one shareholder it suspects of subletting. Is that a reasonable use?

That is a question for the board's lawyer rather than a camera company, but here is what we can say from our side. People ask whether landlords can use outdoor cameras to monitor tenants in New York co-op apartments often enough that the question appears on legal Q&A sites, and an attorney answering it on Justia noted that cameras in common areas such as hallways and stairwells are lawful as long as they do not record sound. Pointing a camera at one door, or into a unit, to watch a particular person is different in kind from ordinary building security, and it can create problems for the board. When we repair hallway cameras, we restore their normal coverage of the common areas, switch off audio recording, and leave decisions about who may view the footage, and why, to the board's written policy.

Waiting, following and the shared street door

In one Cobble Hill robbery, building video showed the suspect waiting outside for an hour and a half before following a woman into the courtyard. Would our cameras have kept the waiting, or only the moment he moved?

It depends on how your recorder is set. Many systems are set to record only on motion, and a person standing still near a gate can fall below the motion threshold, so the recording jumps from an empty frame to the moment someone moves. For entrance and gate cameras, we usually set continuous recording at full quality, which most drives can hold for a week or more, with motion or person detection used only for alerts. Some cameras can also flag a person who stays in one area for a set time. On a repair visit we check exactly this: whether the camera covering the approach to your door or gate keeps every minute, and how far back the recorder can go.

Our shop on Court Street shares the street door with the apartments upstairs, and the landlord's old camera over that door has died. Can our shop's own system take over watching the door?

Technically, yes: a camera covering the shared door can be connected to your shop's recorder in place of the landlord's dead one. The question is permission. The door, the wall and the stair belong to the landlord, and the residents use that entrance every day, so we would want the landlord's written agreement before mounting or connecting anything there, and a clear understanding of who can see the footage and who maintains the camera. Often the simplest arrangement is that the landlord repairs the building camera and gives the shop a viewing login, or that the shop's camera is aimed at the shop's own door only. We can price either and explain the options to the landlord with your permission.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair systems from any manufacturer and any installer. A company that services this part of Brooklyn lists Invid, Hikvision, Uniview and Provision among the brands it works on, and we see those alongside Dahua, Lorex, Hanwha, Axis, UniFi and the consumer brands. Here is what we meet most in Cobble Hill and what usually fails.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Older systems in many brownstones, wired with coaxial cable to a DVR in the cellar. Connectors corrode at the wall and a shared power supply often fails first. Many can be upgraded to sharper cameras on the same hidden cable, which matters when that cable is inside a wall with moldings.

Network (IP) cameras

Standard in the new hospital-campus buildings and in newer shop systems, powered and connected by one cable from a PoE switch or NVR. Faults are usually a switch port, a connector, a password or an address change after the network was updated.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the video, usually in a damp cellar or a closet. Drives wear out, settings vanish after outages, firmware updates break phone viewing. Hybrid recorders let a house keep its coax cameras while adding network ones.

Low-profile facade cameras

Small turrets, mini domes and compact bullets in dark or stone colors, the usual like-for-like replacements on landmarked fronts where a bigger housing would draw attention.

Color low-light cameras

Cameras that record in color using existing light instead of their own infrared. The common fix for tiled vestibules and glass doors where infrared bounces back into the lens.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras whose lens can be zoomed after mounting, for converted churches with tall ceilings, long narrow shops on Atlantic Avenue and garden cameras that should see your yard and not the neighbors'.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Motorized or fisheye cameras in courtyards, store floors and rooftop corners. Motors, saved positions and dewy domes are what we fix most often.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Consumer cameras on garden walls, in apartments and in vestibules. Signal through brick, battery life in winter and app accounts belonging to someone who moved away are the usual problems.

Video door stations

Intercom stations with built-in cameras at courtyard gates, condo lobbies and brownstone doors, sometimes recorded by the main system and sometimes not. We check which before promising footage from them.

National brands versus a local licensed repair

Owners with Ring, Arlo, Nest, Wyze or SimpliSafe cameras often ask whether to call the brand or us. For a sealed consumer camera that has simply died, the brand's warranty or a replacement is usually the cheaper path, because there is nothing inside to repair; one industry write-up puts it bluntly, that a $60 consumer camera usually costs less to replace than to diagnose. What the brands cannot do is come to the house and see that the garden camera is two brick walls from the router, that the vestibule camera is reflecting its own light off the door glass, or that the new doorbell is bigger than the opening the old one filled on a landmarked frame. That is where a visit pays for itself. For wired systems with a recorder, a licensed local repair is the normal route; the companies that make them generally sell through installers rather than sending technicians. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Cobble Hill

Search summaries, chat assistants and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general advice about fixing security cameras. Much of it is fine for a detached house in the suburbs. Some of it goes wrong in a landmarked neighborhood of brownstones, tiled vestibules and shared gardens. Here are the claims we see repeated most often, and what we find on Cobble Hill repairs.

"When a camera dies, replace it with the newest, highest-resolution model you can get"

On a modern building with plain walls, that is often good advice. On a Cobble Hill row house inside the historic district, the newest model can be the wrong one. Higher resolution often comes in a bigger housing, many current cameras come only in white, and some need a different base that will not sit on the old anchors.

The Landmarks rules ask that cameras be placed to avoid a "cluttered or haphazard appearance" and that they not "call undue attention" to themselves. A camera that is twice the size and a different color in the same spot can do exactly that, and turn a simple repair into a question for the Commission.

The better replacement is the one that matches the old footprint and still gives a usable picture. A small current camera with a good sensor almost always beats the old one on picture quality while looking the same from the sidewalk.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for an older house"

Wireless cameras avoid cable runs, which sounds ideal for a house with plaster walls. The catch is the signal. In a typical row house the router is near the front, and a garden camera is a full house depth away through brick partitions, plaster and a chimney stack.

Installers who work in Brooklyn brownstones report Wi-Fi signals in back yards and basement units that are too weak for any wireless camera to stay connected. A camera that tested fine on installation day can drop out every rainy evening.

Wireless can work here, but only with the network fixed first, usually a wired access point near the back of the house. Otherwise the "easy" camera becomes the one we are called about every few months.

"If the night picture is too dark, the camera needs more infrared"

In an open yard, more infrared can help. In a small Cobble Hill vestibule, the camera's own infrared is usually the problem. Its light hits the glazed tile, the varnished wood and the glass of the inner door a few inches away and floods the lens.

Adding an infrared illuminator next to the camera makes that worse. What helps is switching the camera's infrared off, adding visible light mounted away from the lens, or using a camera that records in color with low light.

We test this with the vestibule as dark as it really gets at night, not with the street door open on a sunny afternoon, because the difference between the two is the whole problem.

"On your own property, you can record whatever your camera sees"

Mostly true, but not completely, and Cobble Hill's shared gardens and courtyards are where the exceptions live. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a homeowner or tenant a right to sue someone who records their back yard from neighboring property without written consent, where the purpose is to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten.

An ordinary camera watching its own garden is not that, and the law sets a high bar. But a replacement camera with a much wider lens than the old one, pointed over a low garden wall, can start recording neighbors' patios and windows that the old camera never saw.

The fix is simple and belongs in every garden-camera repair: aim at your own yard, set privacy masks over what is not yours, and check where the infrared lands at night.

"A cable fault inside a finished wall means opening the wall"

Sometimes it does, but far less often than people fear. On hidden runs, the failure is most often at an end: the connector behind the camera, a crimp at the recorder or a splice in the cellar, all of which can be reached without touching plaster.

Even when the cable itself has failed, the old run can often be used to pull a new one through the same path, or the camera can be fed by a different hidden route. Opening a wall with original moldings is the last option, not the first.

The right order is to test the camera, the port, both connectors and the run itself, then decide. Skipping straight to new cable is how owners end up paying for a wall repair they did not need.

"If you can see the live picture, the system is recording"

Not necessarily. Many recorders keep showing live video from every camera while their hard drive has stopped saving, or is saving only some cameras, or only in pieces. The live view comes straight from the cameras; recording depends on a drive that may be worn out.

In Cobble Hill the recorder is usually in a cellar nobody visits, so a warning light or a beep goes unnoticed. The first sign is often the day someone needs a clip and finds weeks missing.

The check takes two minutes: open playback, pick a random hour from last week on each camera, and make sure it plays. On every repair we also turn on the recorder's health alerts so a stopped drive reaches your phone.

"Repairing an old camera system is rarely worth it compared with replacing it"

For a single cheap consumer camera that is often right. For a wired system it usually is not. One industry analysis puts the average camera repair at about $350 against an average replacement price of $1,227, and HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416.

On our Brooklyn list, a connector reterminate or PoE port swap runs $150 to $350, a single camera replacement on a working system $250 to $600, and a DVR or NVR replacement with a drive $700 to $1,500. Replacing a whole system in a Cobble Hill brownstone also means new cable through old walls, which is where cost and disruption pile up.

Repair also keeps the existing hidden cable and the existing mounts, which on a landmarked facade is worth a lot by itself. When a system really is past saving, we say so and price the alternative.
