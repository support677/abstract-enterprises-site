The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Ulster County is a place where the person who bought the soundbar is often not the person who will press play. The county comptroller counted 2,013 active short-term rentals in 2024, up from 942 in 2015, and found that by May 2025 they made up 3.28 percent of every housing unit in the county — above the 2 percent ceiling the county's own Housing Smart Communities Initiative recommends. In the Town of Shandaken the share is close to one home in ten. Add the weekend houses owned by people who live in the city, the long-term rentals around SUNY New Paltz and in Kingston, and the family homes where grown children come back for holidays, and you get a county full of televisions that strangers, guests and relatives have to figure out without help.

That changes what a good soundbar installation means here. In an owner-occupied apartment the job ends when the bar is level and the TV remote controls it. In an Ulster cabin that is listed for rent, the job also has to survive check-in at 9 PM on a Friday, a guest who presses the input button four times, a power cut on a windy night and an owner who is three hours away in Brooklyn. Ulster's comptroller found that 36 percent of short-term-rental owners use a mailing address outside the county, with New York City the most common. When the owner is that far away, a soundbar that falls back to the wrong input is not a small annoyance; it is a review, a refund request or a long drive.

Then there are the walls. Ulster's housing is old in several different ways at once. Stacker's reading of Census data ranks Ulster among New York's counties with the oldest homes, with 25.3 percent — 21,829 houses — built in 1939 or earlier. In Uptown Kingston that means the colonial stone houses of the Stockade District, where John and Crown Streets are described by Friends of Historic Kingston as the only intersection in the country with 18th-century stone houses on all four corners. Down by the Rondout it means brick row houses from the canal era. Around New Paltz, Hurley and Marbletown it means thick stone walls laid by Dutch and Huguenot families. In Saugerties and along Kingston's older streets it means Victorians with plaster on wood lath. West along Route 28, past the Ashokan Reservoir toward Phoenicia and Pine Hill, it means log homes, knotty-pine cabins and timber-framed barns turned into living rooms. Very little of that takes a soundbar bracket the way a modern drywall wall does.

Weather and wiring complete the picture. Central Hudson serves 93,209 customers in Ulster County, and during the nor'easter at the end of September 2026 the Daily Freeman reported more than 14,000 of them without power at the peak. Internet service is uneven, too: the county says all 1,293 of its remaining unserved locations will get fiber under the federal BEAD program, with Verizon building it, which tells you how many homes are still waiting. On the r/catskills forum, owners compare satellite, cable and fixed-wireless service, and one describes a house that came with a satellite plan that was "incredibly slow". A soundbar that relies on Wi-Fi for its app, its updates or its music streaming needs that context planned in, not discovered after the installer has left.

So an Ulster County soundbar job is planned around three questions before anything is drilled. What is the wall made of, and what does the bracket bite into? Who will be using this TV, and how much do they know about it? And what happens when the power, the internet or the owner is away? The rest of this page answers those questions for the houses, cabins, barns, apartments and businesses we see between Saugerties and Ellenville, and between the Hudson at Highland and the high peaks beyond Big Indian.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Ulster County's Architecture

No two Ulster valleys were built the same way, and the TV wall tells you which era you are in within a minute. These are the building types we meet most often, from the Hudson shore to the high Catskills, and what each one asks of a soundbar installation.

Colonial stone houses

Uptown Kingston's Stockade District, Hurley, Marbletown, Stone Ridge and the stone houses of New Paltz. Walls of field or quarried stone, often two feet thick, frequently plastered inside, with deep window reveals that leave little straight wall for a TV. Anchors go into sound stone faces, never into the plaster skin or the mortar joint.

Rondout brick row houses

The 1850s-era brick rows down by Kingston's waterfront, built when the Delaware and Hudson Canal made Rondout a coal port. Solid brick party walls and exterior walls, sometimes exposed inside after renovation; sleeve or wedge anchors set in the brick itself, not the mortar.

Victorians on wood lath

Saugerties, Kingston's older avenues, Rosendale and Highland. Plaster over wood lath, irregular stud spacing and decades of repairs layered on top of one another. A plywood backer ($85) across two studs often gives the bar a centred, solid fixing.

Catskill cabins and log homes

Along Route 28 through Boiceville, Phoenicia, Mount Tremper, Shandaken and Pine Hill, and up the side roads to Big Indian and Oliverea. Knotty-pine board walls, full-log walls, wood stoves and bluestone hearths. The bracket goes into solid timber, and every cable route is planned on the surface.

Converted barns and timber frames

Post-and-beam barns in Gardiner, Accord, Kerhonkson and the Rondout Valley, reborn as living rooms and event spaces. Exposed posts, board infill and high ceilings; the bar is fixed to a post or a framed TV wall built for the purpose, and the sub is placed for a room that is mostly air.

Newer apartments and condos

Midtown Kingston, the 9W corridor, New Paltz near the college and Lake Katrine. Drywall on wood or metal studs, association rules about drilling, and lots of shared walls. Straightforward fixings, with attention to neighbours and to the building's paperwork.

Methods we use on Ulster walls

Stone. We locate a solid face of stone large enough to take the anchor with margin all round, mark the bracket to suit it and drill with a hammer drill and masonry bit, slowly, to avoid spalling the face. Where the stone is plastered over, we find the stone behind the plaster before we commit to a hole. Bluestone deserves a particular word: it is a layered sandstone, quarried by the thousands of tons in the 1800s — Opus 40's history says at least 10,000 Ulster County people made a living from the bluestone trade by 1898 — and it can split along its bedding planes if an anchor is set too close to an edge. On bluestone hearths and veneers we keep anchors well back from edges and joints, and we tell you before drilling if a piece looks thin or loose. Brick and stone carry the published $65 surcharge.

Brick. In the Rondout and in Kingston's older blocks, brick walls are usually sound but the mortar between them may be soft lime mortar more than a century old. Anchors go in the brick, not the joint. Exposed brick that has been sandblasted or repointed is often harder than it looks, and the hole is drilled in stages. We vacuum as we go, because brick dust travels.

Plaster on lath. Victorian plaster sits on wood strips nailed to studs that were rarely laid out at modern spacing. We find framing with a magnet or a small pilot hole, never trust plaster alone to carry a bar and its bracket, and use a painted plywood backer when the studs and the TV's centre line do not agree. Old plaster can crack whatever care is taken; you hear that from us before the drill comes out, and the decision stays yours.

Pine boards and logs. Tongue-and-groove pine is thin, and it is often nailed to strapping with an air gap behind, so a short screw holds nothing. We find the framing or strapping behind the boards and fix into that, or we use a longer fixing into a solid log. Full-log walls are a different case: logs keep drying and settling for years — InterNACHI's log-home guidance puts early settling at about three-quarters of an inch per foot of wall height — so we keep the bar's bracket on one log course where possible rather than bridging two.

Barns and timber frames. A barn post is the strongest fixing in the room and also the most visible, so we talk through appearance first. Often the cleanest answer is a TV-attached bracket on a full-motion mount fixed to a post, or a framed media wall built by your carpenter with blocking where the TV and bar will go. Cables run along beams in matching raceway or drop inside a hollow post cover.

Drywall and metal studs. Newer Kingston and New Paltz apartments, and the additions on many older houses, are ordinary framed walls. These are the only walls in the county where wires can routinely be hidden inside with the $169 in-wall kit. Metal studs add $30 for fixings rated for steel framing.

Most Ulster owners who have us fix a bar to a stone or log wall also want a better picture of what happens at the house while they are away, and pair the visit with wireless camera installation in Ulster County so the rental can be checked between guests.

Compliance and paperwork for Ulster businesses

  • Electrical work stays with electricians. A new or moved outlet behind a TV is electrical work. Ulster County's Electrical Licensing Board licenses master electricians and publishes the licensee list; we work alongside your electrician and never add outlets ourselves.
  • Short-term-rental registration. Ulster County requires short-term rentals to register, and individual towns layer their own rules on top — Woodstock's non-owner-occupied rentals are limited to 180 days and 26 weekends a year, and Shandaken asks the owner or manager to respond to an emergency within 30 minutes and be on site within an hour. A dependable TV setup is part of meeting those expectations without a phone call.
  • Kingston's permit cap. Kingston limits full short-term-rental permits to 1 percent of the city's housing units, a rule adopted in August 2023 according to Hudson Valley One. Permitted hosts tend to treat their units as businesses, and their AV setup accordingly.
  • Protruding objects. In public corridors, the 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2) limit wall-mounted objects between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to a 4-inch projection. A bar under a TV in a lobby or hallway is placed with that in mind.
  • Certificates of insurance. Landlords, condo boards and commercial managers in Kingston and New Paltz routinely ask for one. We issue it at no charge, naming whoever needs to be named.

Ulster restaurant and inn owners who are adding soundbars are often also looking at the rest of the building's low-voltage systems; low-voltage installation in Ulster County covers data runs, ceiling speakers and the wiring a busy room needs.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ulster owners, hosts and renters put to us that the rest of the silo does not already answer. They are grouped by the situation they come from, and each answer starts with the short version.

When the TV belongs to a rental

Should a rental's soundbar sit on the guest Wi-Fi or on a network of its own?

On a network of its own where the router allows it, and never on a network whose password changes between guests without the bar being updated. Most Wi-Fi soundbars only need the network for their app, music streaming and software updates; the TV sound itself travels over the HDMI cable. If the bar shares the guest network and the host changes that password every stay, the bar quietly drops off and its app stops working, which is when guests start messaging.

Many routers can run a separate owner or device network alongside the guest network. We put the bar, any Sonos speakers and the streaming box on that, so guests can use the Wi-Fi without being able to see or regroup the speakers. Where the router has no second network, we leave the bar on HDMI only and turn off features that depend on the app, so nothing breaks when the password rotates.

What should the house guide say so guests can run the TV sound without phoning the host?

One page, three steps, and a photo. Guests read a TV page under poor light at night, so the guide should show the single remote they need, say which button turns everything on, and say what to do if there is picture but no sound — usually pressing the TV's power button off and on, because that re-runs the HDMI handshake between TV and bar.

We write that page for you at the end of a rental setup, based on how we actually configured the system, and we tape a small label to the back of the bar showing which cable goes in which port. Shandaken's licensing rules expect a host or manager to respond to an emergency within 30 minutes; a TV with no sound is not an emergency, but a clear guide keeps it from becoming a 10 PM phone call that feels like one.

Guests bring their own streaming sticks — will plugging one in upset the soundbar setup?

Usually not, as long as they use the HDMI input we leave free for them. Trouble starts when a guest unplugs the soundbar's own cable from the TV's eARC or ARC socket to make room for a stick; the TV then plays through its speakers, the bar goes silent and the next guest reports it broken.

So on a rental we leave one TV input visibly free and labelled for guest devices, mark the soundbar's HDMI cable and socket so nobody mistakes it for a spare, and mention the free input in the house guide. Where the TV has only one or two HDMI inputs, a powered streaming device of your own on the second input leaves guests less reason to unplug anything.

Cabins, barns and houses that change hands

We bought a furnished cabin and the soundbar is still registered to the seller's app account — can you move it to ours?

Usually, yes. Wi-Fi soundbars such as Sonos are tied to the account that set them up, and a bar still linked to a previous owner's account cannot be added to yours in the app until it is released. The seller can remove it from their system; if that is not possible, a factory reset clears it so it can be set up as new.

We do the reset and the fresh setup on your network as part of a Sonos setup ($125) or, for other brands, within an Ulster setup-only visit, and check that the TV still controls the bar afterwards. Ask the seller for the remotes and any rear speakers or sub that belong to the system before closing — they are easy to leave behind in a move.

Our converted barn has no hollow walls anywhere — how are the soundbar's power and HDMI kept tidy?

By planning the route around the timber rather than through it. Most barn conversions leave posts, beams and board walls exposed, so there is nowhere to hide a cable inside. The tidy options are: bring the sources to the TV so only one power cable drops to an outlet; run the cables along the underside of a beam or the edge of a post in raceway that matches the wood; or have your carpenter frame a shallow media wall with a chase inside it.

If the barn is mid-renovation, the third option is by far the best and costs us nothing extra to plan — we can tell your carpenter where blocking and a cable chase should go before the boards are hung. If it is finished, a TV-attached soundbar bracket keeps the bar and screen as one unit and leaves just one cable bundle to manage.

Our rental has a soundbar in the living room and another in the bedroom — will guests' phones connect to the wrong one?

They can, if both bars show up under the same Bluetooth or casting name. A guest who picks “Soundbar” from a list of two may end up playing music in an empty bedroom and decide the living-room bar is broken.

We rename each bar after its room in its app and, where the model allows, switch off Bluetooth discovery on the one guests do not need for music. The guest guide then says which name belongs to which room, so nobody has to guess.

The air in our cabin gets very dry with the wood stove going — can static hurt the soundbar?

It is worth taking seriously, though it is rarely dramatic. Industry guidance on humidity and electronics notes that very dry air, below about 30 percent relative humidity, encourages static discharge, and a stove-heated cabin in January can get that dry. The usual victim is not the bar's speakers but a port: someone touches an HDMI or USB connector after shuffling across a rug.

We route cables so connectors sit tucked behind the TV rather than within easy reach, and suggest touching something metal before plugging guest devices in. A humidifier helps the room, the woodwork and the people as much as the electronics.

Seasons, sound and access

Rental guests lose remotes — what is the backup plan for the soundbar?

Make the TV's own remote the only one that matters. With HDMI-CEC set up, the TV remote turns the bar on and off and changes its volume, so a missing soundbar remote stops being a problem. Keep the bar's own remote in the owner's closet for settings, not on the coffee table.

For TVs whose remotes also go missing, a programmed universal remote ($99) makes a sensible spare, and most TVs and bars also have buttons or an app to fall back on. The guest guide says which remote to use, and the spare goes where your cleaner can find it.

We already own good stereo speakers — can the TV switch between them and the soundbar?

Yes, with one decision made up front: which system the TV sends sound to by default. Plenty of owners keep a proper stereo, or a pair of studio monitors, for music, and add a soundbar for films and TV rather than replacing what they already like.

The cleanest arrangements are either an A/V receiver that drives the stereo speakers and takes over from the bar ($129 receiver line), or keeping the bar as the TV's default and connecting the stereo to the TV's optical or headphone output for when you want it. We set it so the switch is one menu change or one input button, write that down for you, and make sure the two systems never play at once.

Our driveway is steep and sometimes unplowed — what happens to a winter appointment?

Tell us when you book, and we plan around it. Arrival windows are two hours wide, between 8 AM and 8 PM, seven days a week, and you can move a booking free of charge up to 24 hours ahead — which is usually enough notice to see a storm coming.

If a technician arrives and cannot reach the house, or there is no access when they get there, the published $150 return-trip fee applies, the same as anywhere else. A photo of the driveway and a note on where to park when you book helps us bring the right vehicle and footwear, and a caretaker or neighbour with a key and a shovel saves everybody a second trip.

Types of Soundbars and Mounts We Install

We install the bar you choose, or help you choose one; the brand matters less than how it fits the room and who will be using it. These are the categories we fit across Ulster, with the situations where each one earns its place.

Compact all-in-one bars

Bars around two feet wide, such as the Sonos Beam (Gen 2), which Sonos lists at 25.63 inches and 6.35 lb with HDMI eARC. Right for cabin bedrooms, small rental living rooms and TVs under 55 inches.

Full-width Atmos bars

Flagships like the Sonos Arc Ultra (46.38 inches wide, 13.01 lb per Sonos) or Samsung's HW-Q990 series. Sonos recommends at least 4 inches between bar and TV and a foot of clearance at each end; up-firing channels need a flat ceiling, which many cabins and barns do not have.

Bars with wireless subs and rears

Systems whose rear speakers are wireless for audio but, as Samsung's own specifications note for the Q990 series, each need an AC outlet. In timber rooms, placement of those outlets often decides the layout.

Optical-only and budget bars

Bars like the Sonos Ray connect by optical rather than HDMI. Useful for older TVs in guest rooms, though they need a programmed universal remote ($99) if guests are to manage with one handset.

Brackets

Four Seasons

Weather, Power and a Soundbar in the Hills

Ulster weather reaches indoors more than people expect. A house that is heated to 50 degrees between weekends, a cabin that sits damp through a wet spring, a nor'easter that takes the power for a day: none of these will ruin a soundbar on their own, but each one is worth planning for.

Power cuts and restarts. When the power drops and returns, TVs and soundbars do not always find each other again; the HDMI handshake that links them can need a fresh start. That is the most common reason for a "no sound" message after a storm. The September 2026 nor'easter left more than 14,000 Ulster County Central Hudson customers without power at its peak, according to the Daily Freeman, and in July 2026 storms the paper reported nearly 50,000 customers across the Central Hudson area in the dark. We show you, your caretaker and your guide-writer the one-minute restart routine that fixes most cases: TV off at the wall, bar off at the wall, bar on first, then TV.

Cold and damp. Electronics prefer the conditions people do. Industry guidance on humidity and electronics puts the comfortable range at roughly 40 to 60 percent relative humidity and warns that condensation risk rises when warm, moist air meets cold surfaces. For a cabin that is closed up between visits, the practical advice is simple: keep some heat on, let the room warm up before switching the TV and bar on after a cold spell, and avoid mounting the bar on an uninsulated exterior wall that sweats in spring if another wall will do.

Wood heat. A wood stove is the heart of many Ulster living rooms in winter. Keep the bar, the sub and their cables out of the stove's heat path, keep cables away from stovepipes entirely, and never run a cord cover along a wall that gets hot to the touch.

Internet that comes and goes. When the connection drops, the TV sound over HDMI keeps working; app features and streaming do not. We set the bar so the parts that matter work offline, and if fiber arrives on your road under the county's BEAD buildout, a short setup-only visit can re-join every device to the new router at once.

Ulster owners who want the network itself to be more reliable before winter often book network installation in Ulster County alongside the soundbar, so the streaming box and the bar are wired rather than relying on a router two floors away.

Field Notes: Reading an Ulster Wall Before the Drill Comes Out

How our technicians size up a wall in this county, written as general guidance — no client's home is being described.

Knock first. A hollow knock on a smooth wall is drywall or plaster with a cavity; a dull, dead knock is stone, brick or a log; a lively, slightly drummy knock on boards usually means paneling over strapping with air behind. That first knock decides which bit comes out of the case.

Look along the wall from one end with light behind you. Stone houses that were plastered long ago show their waves, and so do old pine boards. A long soundbar will show those waves too, so shims are planned before the bracket goes up, not after.

Find the heat and the cold. Note where the stove is and where its warmth rises, feel the outside wall for chill and damp, and keep the bar away from both. On bluestone or brick, pick a solid face well back from any edge or joint and drill one pilot hole where the bracket will cover it; read the dust before committing. Then think about the next person to use the TV, who may never have seen it before, and set it up for them.

FAQ

Ulster County Soundbar Installation FAQ

Can you tell us whether a soundbar will suit our TV before we buy it?
+

Yes. Send us the TV's model number and the bar you are considering, and we will check the connections (HDMI eARC, ARC or only optical), the width against the TV, and whether the bar's height will block the bottom of the screen or the TV's remote sensor. If a different model would suit the room or the guests better, we will say so.

Can the TV send sound to the soundbar without an HDMI cable?
+

Some can. Certain TVs and soundbars from the same maker can link over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, and nearly every TV can pair with a Bluetooth speaker. Wireless links can add delay or drop out, so on most installs we use HDMI eARC or ARC, and use a wireless link only where a cable truly cannot reach.

Will automatic software updates undo the settings you made?
+

Usually not, but occasionally an update resets a sound mode or a TV setting. We write down the key settings we chose, such as the TV's audio output, CEC and the bar's dialogue mode, so you or a caretaker can put them back in a minute if an update changes something.

Can the soundbar's lights and front display be dimmed for a bedroom?
+

On most models, yes. Many bars let you dim or switch off the status light and the front display in their settings or app, and some TVs let you turn off their own standby light. We set those for bedrooms and sleeping lofts and tell you where the setting lives.

Is there a minimum charge for a single soundbar in Ulster County?
+

No separate minimum. A bar placed on a console is the $79 line and a bar mounted on the wall is $129, plus any wall surcharge, and that is the bill. The three-hour minimum on our $195 specialty rate applies only to specialty work outside the menu, not to a standard soundbar install.

Can you label the cables so a caretaker can reconnect things later?
+

Yes, and we recommend it for any house that is not lived in full time. We label each cable at both ends with what it connects to, and the written note we leave describes which TV port the bar uses, so a caretaker or cleaner can put a pulled cable back without a call.

Should the soundbar's built-in voice assistant be switched off in a rental?
+

Many hosts choose to. If the bar has a microphone and a voice assistant signed in to your account, guests can use it and may see personal information. We can sign the assistant out or switch off the microphone where the model allows it, and list in the guest guide what is and is not enabled.

Can the setup note include model and serial numbers for our rental inventory?
+

Yes. Tell us before the visit, and the written note we leave will list the soundbar, sub, rear speakers and remotes by model and serial number as printed on the equipment. Hosts often keep it with their insurance and inventory records.
