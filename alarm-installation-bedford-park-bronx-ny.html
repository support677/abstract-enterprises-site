A good share of our Bedford Park calls are about a system that already exists. A keypad in a third-floor apartment beeps every minute because its backup battery is spent. A shop panel on the Boulevard shows a communication trouble because the phone line it used to dial out on was cancelled years ago. A siren in a building cellar keeps tripping at night and the super has unplugged it, which means the cellar is now unguarded. Call (646) 490-0629, tell us what the keypad says and whether the siren is sounding right now, and you get an arrival window on the call.

Until we arrive, do not cut wires or pull the panel’s transformer out of the wall to stop a noise; that usually leaves the battery to run the siren again later, and it can erase settings we would otherwise reuse. The Neighborhood

Why Bedford Park Properties Need Alarm Systems

On a map Bedford Park is a small, tidy shape. Wikipedia bounds it by Mosholu Parkway on the north, Webster Avenue on the east, East 196th Street on the south and Jerome Avenue on the west, gives its area as 0.455 square miles, and places it in Bronx Community District 7. The Grand Concourse cuts through the middle and splits its ZIP codes: 10468 to the west of the Concourse, 10458 to the east. Patrol belongs to the 52nd Precinct, whose station house is on the neighborhood’s own eastern edge at 3016 Webster Avenue. The New York Botanical Garden lies just across Webster, and Lehman College and the high schools of what Wikipedia calls the Educational Mile sit just to the west, along Goulden Avenue and the Jerome Park Reservoir.

What fills the space between those edges is housing, most of it older than anyone living in it. Wikipedia’s summary is five- or six-story apartment buildings and three-story Victorian houses, with taller apartment houses on the Concourse. CityNeighborhoods.NYC describes prewar brick apartment buildings with decorative lobbies and courtyards along Villa Avenue, Bedford Park Boulevard and Decatur Avenue, and Art Deco apartment houses along the Concourse. When the city proposed rezoning Webster Avenue and the side streets around it in early 2011, CityLand summarized the planners’ description of Bedford Park as a mix of one- and two-family detached houses, attached and semi-detached houses, and five- to seven-story apartment buildings. The large majority of households rent: the Furman Center reports a 4.0 percent homeownership rate for the Kingsbridge Heights/Bedford district in 2024 and a rental vacancy rate of 1.1 percent.

Those two facts, old multi-unit buildings and a population of renters, decide what an alarm looks like here. A tenant cannot rewire a landlord’s building, and in a district with that vacancy rate a tenant who moves usually moves somewhere else nearby, so the right apartment system is one that installs without damage and leaves with its owner. An owner or managing agent has a different problem: the building’s weak spots are the places nobody watches, the cellar door, the boiler room, the roof bulkhead and the mail area, and any alarm on them has to make itself heard by the right person without waking forty households at four in the morning. And a shopkeeper on the Boulevard has an alarm that lives under other people’s bedrooms.

The local reports we found while preparing this page fit that picture. Norwood News reported in September 2025 that police were looking for a man who, at about 4 a.m. on September 10, entered a residential building near Perry Avenue and Mosholu Parkway South through the front door as a tenant was leaving. In October 2020 the same paper reported that two people forced their way through a secured front entrance at 325 East 201st Street before dawn and then forced open the mailboxes and took packages. In February 2024 it reported a house burglary near Bedford Park Boulevard and Bainbridge Avenue in which the intruder forced open a window. And in July 2025 it reported that two people entered the gym at 3000 Jerome Avenue through an unlocked front door just after 4 a.m. Four different openings: a held door, a forced entrance, a window and a door nobody locked. Each is a different alarm problem, and this page treats them separately.

The bigger picture is calmer than single incidents suggest. Wikipedia, drawing on NYPD figures, says the 52nd Precinct ranked 49th safest of 69 patrol areas for per-capita crime in 2010, recorded 192 burglaries in 2022, and saw crime across all categories fall 76.1 percent between 1990 and 2022. An alarm is not a response to a crime wave. It is a way of knowing within seconds that a specific door or window has opened when it should not have, and of getting that message to someone who can act on it, which in a building with a front door shared by dozens of people is worth having in any year.

Two more local facts shape the work. First, Bedford Park’s housing is under strain: the Furman Center counts 283.6 serious housing code violations per 1,000 privately owned rental units in the district in 2025, up from 152.1 in 2006. That tells us to expect old wiring, painted-over junction boxes and doors that do not close well, and it is why we check a door’s fit before we put a contact on it. Second, the neighborhood’s fire risk is shared. A burglary alarm in one apartment concerns one household; smoke in a stairwell concerns everyone. We keep those two kinds of signal on clearly separate paths, and we explain the difference to every client, because a confused alarm in a multi-unit building is worse than none.

Building Specialists

Alarm Systems for Bedford Park’s Architecture

The building types below are the ones the sources above describe for Bedford Park. Each one changes where sensors go, how cable or radio signals travel, and who has the right to say yes.

Prewar walk-ups, five and six stories

Brick buildings on the side streets with one stair, apartments off a hallway on each floor, and often a fire escape at the rear or on the street front. No elevator means a tenant cannot easily go down to check a noise in the lobby. Inside the apartment we rely on wireless sensors, because tenants cannot open walls; for the building, the stair, cellar and roof door are the zones that matter.

Elevator apartment houses on the Concourse

Taller buildings with larger lobbies, an elevator machine room at the top, and in some buildings a trash room on each floor or a staffed front desk. Radio signals from a unit alarm travel well inside one apartment but rarely reach a lobby panel through several floors of masonry, so building zones get their own panel near the super’s office.

Courtyard buildings

CityNeighborhoods notes decorative lobbies and courtyards on Villa Avenue, Bedford Park Boulevard and Decatur Avenue. A courtyard adds a gate and a second set of windows facing inward; ground-floor apartments facing a courtyard need the same glass and window protection as those facing the street, because a courtyard gate is easy to leave open.

Three-story Victorian and attached houses

Owner-occupied or split into two or three apartments, often with a basement entrance under the stoop and a rear yard. These are the buildings where a hardwired system is practical, because the owner can approve cable runs in the basement and closets, and where one panel can cover the owner’s floor while tenants keep their own.

Mitchell-Lama and subsidized towers

The Furman Center counts ten Mitchell-Lama properties in the district. Large cooperatives like these usually have management, security staff and building systems of their own, so the realistic alarm job is inside individual apartments, installed in a way the house rules allow, or common-area work bid through management.

Mixed-use buildings on the Boulevard

Shops on the ground floor and apartments above, sharing walls, a cellar and sometimes a street door. The shop’s alarm and the building’s alarm have to be designed so neither one sets the other off and so the siren does not sit under a bedroom window.

Materials and methods in older buildings

Masonry and radio. Prewar walls are brick, terra cotta block and plaster, and the floors between apartments are often heavy. That is good for privacy and bad for wireless range. A sensor and its panel in the same apartment almost never have a problem; a sensor in the cellar trying to reach a panel on the second floor often does. We test signal strength from every sensor location with the panel in its final spot before we fix anything to a wall, and where a building zone is too far away we add a wired zone expander or a second panel rather than hoping.

Door frames. Apartment doors in older buildings are steel or heavy wood in steel frames that have been painted many times. A surface-mount contact with adhesive will fall off a heavily painted, uneven frame in the first humid summer. On rentals we use small screws into the door and frame only where the lease or landlord allows, and otherwise a contact sized for a recessed edge or a bracket that clamps without drilling. We say in writing which method we used so the tenant knows what to patch on move-out.

Windows and fire escapes. Many apartments have one window onto a fire escape. That window is a way out in a fire and must stay free to open, so we never add a lock, a bar or anything that slows it. A contact on the sash or a glass-break sensor in the room tells you if it opens; it does not change how quickly it opens.

Cable in common areas. Where an owner wants hardwired zones on the cellar and roof doors, we run cable in the cellar ceiling and the stair bulkhead in raceway or existing chases, never through occupied apartments without permission. Old cable from long-dead alarm or intercom systems is common in these buildings; we tag what we reuse and leave the rest alone unless the owner asks for it removed.

Power. A panel needs a permanent outlet or a low-voltage transformer on a circuit that will not be switched off. In an apartment that is usually an outlet inside a closet; in a cellar it is a dedicated outlet the super will not borrow for a space heater. Every panel we install carries a backup battery, and we tell you roughly how long it should last on your system.

Commercial Alarm Systems

Business Alarm Systems in Bedford Park

Bedford Park Boulevard. Wikipedia calls the Boulevard the neighborhood’s main business district, and CityNeighborhoods describes the shopping streets as delis, Dominican cafés, bodegas, bookstores and restaurants serving Caribbean and South Asian food. Physically these are narrow ground-floor stores in apartment buildings, with a glass front, a roll-down gate, a back room and often a cellar reached by a hatch or an inside stair. The alarm problem is the back and the bottom. The front faces a lit street and a gate; the rear door, the roof hatch and the cellar face nobody. As a matter of AESS practice we protect the glass, but most of the sensor budget goes on the back door, the cellar and the cash area.

Webster Avenue. When the city planned the 2011 rezoning, CityLand reported the corridor between East 193rd and East 211th Streets as auto repair shops, low-scale office uses, parking lots, vacant lots and minimal foot traffic, a legacy of the Third Avenue El that ran above it until 1973. Garages, body shops and small offices of the kind CityLand described have wide overhead doors, yards and cars left overnight, and they are quiet streets after closing. For them we combine heavy-duty overhead door contacts, motion coverage of the office and parts room, and outdoor beams or a camera along the yard.

Jerome Avenue and the Grand Concourse. Jerome Avenue runs under the elevated 4 line on the western edge, with corner stores, gyms and services; the July 2025 Norwood News report of an entry through an unlocked front door at 3000 Jerome Avenue is a reminder that the best-placed sensor does nothing if the system is not armed at closing. On the Concourse, where the apartment houses are larger, a ground-floor office or store may share a lobby or a cellar with residents. Our answer at closing time is a keypad by the staff exit, an auto-arm schedule as a backstop, and a phone notification when the last person leaves without arming.

Institutions and their neighbors. Lehman College at 250 Bedford Park Boulevard West, the high schools along the reservoir, the Botanical Garden across Webster, and the churches and community groups around Bainbridge Avenue all have their own security staff and systems, and we do not describe them as clients. What they do create is a neighborhood of small businesses that serve students, staff and visitors on short hours, with tutoring centers, copy shops, takeout and day programs that are empty at night and on breaks. Those rooms need alarms that arm on schedule and alert the owner, not a pricey guard contract.

Daycares and programs inside apartment buildings. News 12 reported in August 2025 that a daycare operated inside 2850 Grand Concourse, a building whose residents had petitioned management about an unlocked entry. A program inside an apartment building has a duty to its families that a residential tenant does not, and its alarm should cover its own doors and windows, include panic buttons for staff, and be planned with the building owner so the two systems do not fight.

Rules a Bedford Park business alarm has to respect

  • Fifteen-minute siren limit. NYC Administrative Code §24-238 says an owner may not operate an audible burglar alarm on a building unless it is capable of and does automatically stop sounding within fifteen minutes of being activated. Every siren we program here times out well inside that.
  • State-licensed installers. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under General Business Law Article 6-D; applicants must be at least 18, be fingerprinted, complete at least 81 hours of approved education and pass an exam. Ask anyone who quotes you for their license number. Ours is #12000287431.
  • Building fire alarm systems are a separate scope. FDNY guidance says fire alarm plans go to the FDNY for approval, the work is done by a New York City licensed electrician holding an FDNY fire alarm company certificate, and the finished system is acceptance-tested and inspected by the FDNY. A shop’s burglary panel with a monitored smoke detector is not a substitute for a required building fire alarm system, and we say so on the quote.
  • Egress. An alarm never locks a door. Exit doors in a shop must still open from inside with one motion; a sensor on them only reports that they opened.

Shops that want to see as well as hear usually add cameras; see security cameras in Bedford Park or our commercial camera page for the Bronx.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Systems in Bedford Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bedford Park tenants, owners, supers and shopkeepers put to us, grouped by what they are really about. Each one is answered where it is asked, and none of them is repeated in the FAQ further down.

Inside your own apartment

I rent on the fourth floor of a six-story building off Bedford Park Boulevard. Can I have an alarm without drilling into the landlord’s door frame?

Yes. A wireless system is designed for exactly this. The panel or hub sits on a shelf or a closet wall and plugs into an outlet; the door contact, window contacts and motion sensor are battery powered and talk to the panel by radio. Contacts can be mounted with a removable adhesive suited to painted steel, or with a clamp bracket, so nothing is screwed into the frame. On a heavily painted, uneven frame adhesive is not reliable in the long term, and in that case we will suggest two small screws and tell you exactly what would need filling when you move out; it is your choice and, if your lease requires it, your landlord’s.

A fourth-floor apartment changes the plan in a useful way: the windows that matter are the ones a person can reach from outside, which usually means the fire escape window and any window next to it, not every window in the unit. That keeps the sensor count, and the price, down. The apartment door is the main entry point to protect, followed by the fire escape window.

Before you buy anything, read the alterations clause in your lease. Most only restrict permanent changes. A system that comes off the wall without damage normally falls outside them, but if your lease is unusual, ask your landlord in writing and keep the reply.

If my apartment alarm trips while I’m at work across town, who actually hears it, and does anyone come?

That depends on how you set it up, and it is the most important choice you make. With self-monitoring, the alarm sends a notification to your phone and to anyone else you add, such as a relative who lives nearby; you look at the alert, check a camera if you have one, and decide whether to call 911. With professional monitoring, a central station receives the signal, tries to reach you and your contacts, and requests a police response if it cannot confirm a false alarm.

The siren is for the people inside the building and for the intruder. In a prewar building with thick walls, neighbors across the hall may hear it; neighbors two floors up may not. A siren alone is not a plan, and it must stop within fifteen minutes under the city’s noise code, so the notification is what really matters.

For a tenant, the honest answer is often self-monitoring plus one trusted contact in the neighborhood. If you travel, or your contact list is thin, monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract.

Our bedroom window opens onto the fire escape. How do you protect it without making it harder to climb out in a fire?

By only adding things that report, never things that hold. A slim contact on the sash tells the panel when the window opens; a glass-break sensor in the room hears the glass if someone breaks it. Neither one changes how quickly or easily the window opens from inside. We will not install a lock, a pin or a bar on a fire escape window as part of an alarm job, and we will tell you if something already there looks like it blocks the way out.

At night, when the system is armed with you at home, the panel can be set so that opening this window sounds the alarm immediately, but you can always open it to escape; the alarm simply reports it. If a fire is the reason, the noise is the least of your concerns.

Fire escape windows are also where air conditioners and window fans go in summer. If you plan to put a unit in that window, tell us; we will put the contact on the part of the sash that still moves, or rely on the glass-break and motion sensors for that season.

My cat has the run of the apartment all day. Will the motion sensor keep setting the alarm off?

It does not have to. Pet-immune motion sensors are made to ignore animals below a stated weight, and they work best when mounted at the height and angle the manufacturer specifies, aimed away from furniture a cat can climb. Cats are harder than dogs because they jump onto shelves and radiator covers, which put them in the sensor’s view at human height; we look for the climbing routes during the visit and place the sensor so they are out of its field.

In small apartments there is a simpler option: leave the motion sensor out of the armed-away setting entirely and rely on the door and window contacts plus a glass-break sensor. The perimeter tells you someone is coming in before they reach the room a motion sensor would see.

We test with your pet at home before we leave, if the pet is willing, and we come back to adjust at no extra charge within the first weeks if the sensor still catches the cat.

Four of us share an apartment near Lehman College. Can each roommate have a separate code, and can we see who disarmed it?

Yes. Modern panels support many user codes, and the app keeps a log of which code or which phone armed and disarmed the system and when. Each roommate gets a personal code and app login; when someone moves out mid-lease, you delete their code without changing anyone else’s. A guest code can be set to work only on certain days or hours, for a friend who waters the plants over winter break.

Agree on the rules before installation, not after: who is the main account holder, who is a notification contact, and whether the log is visible to everyone. In shared apartments the log is often what prevents arguments about who left the door unarmed.

When the lease ends, the equipment goes with whoever paid for it. We note on the invoice who owns the panel, so there is no dispute later.

The building’s shared spaces

Can a building alarm cover the cellar, boiler room and roof door without a siren waking forty apartments at 3 a.m.?

Yes, and in most Bedford Park walk-ups that is the right design. Those zones are set to report silently: when the cellar door or the roof bulkhead door opens outside the hours the super expects, the panel sends a notification to the super, the managing agent or a monitoring center, and optionally starts a camera clip. There is no siren in the stair at all, or a short local chirp only at the door itself.

A loud siren has its place: on a roof door that should never open, the noise itself may drive someone off. That choice is made door by door with the owner, and every siren we program stops well inside the fifteen-minute limit in the city’s noise code.

The roof door has a special constraint. It is part of the building’s fire exit route in many buildings and must not be locked against people leaving. A contact and an alarm on it are fine; anything that keeps it from opening is not.

Someone forced our lobby mailboxes after getting through the street door. Can an alarm protect the mail area when the entrance itself was the weak spot?

An alarm cannot stop a determined person at a door, but it can make the mail area expensive to linger in. Norwood News reported a 2020 case at 325 East 201st Street in which two people forced their way through a secured front entrance before dawn and then forced the mailboxes. In a case like that, a forced-door contact on the street door that sends an instant alert outside normal hours, plus a motion sensor or camera covering the mailbox wall, gives the super or a monitoring center a signal at the moment of entry rather than when tenants find empty boxes in the morning.

The street door itself is often the real fix. A door that does not close and latch is the first thing we check, and if it needs a new closer, a stronger strike or a better entry system, we say so and refer that part to our access control and intercom teams.

The lobby, the street door and the mail area are the owner’s responsibility, so this is an owner’s project. Tenants who want it should ask the owner or managing agent in writing, and can mention that the zones we would add report silently to the super rather than sounding in the lobby.

A stranger followed a neighbor in through the front door. Which alarm sensor would actually have noticed?

None of them, on its own, and it is better to say so plainly. The case Norwood News reported near Perry Avenue and Mosholu Parkway South in September 2025, a man entering as a tenant left, is exactly the kind of entry a door contact cannot distinguish from a resident coming home. The door opened normally; no alarm will call that suspicious.

What helps is layered. In each apartment, a unit alarm protects the place that matters most to that household. In the building, a camera on the vestibule records who came in, and motion sensors on the cellar and roof zones report anyone who wanders where residents do not usually go at night. An intercom with video lets residents see who is at the door before they buzz.

And the cheapest layer is a habit: residents not holding the door for people they do not know. We are happy to give a building a short notice for the lobby that explains why.

Who should hold the codes and receive the alerts for a building alarm: the super, the managing agent or the tenant association?

The owner decides, because the owner is responsible for the building’s common areas and pays for the system. In practice we recommend a short chain: the super first, because the super lives on site or nearby and can look; the managing agent second; and a monitoring center third if the owner pays for one. Each person gets a personal code so the log shows who disarmed the cellar at what time.

A tenant association can receive notifications if the owner agrees, which some owners like because it gives residents confidence the system works. Tenants should not hold codes that disarm building zones unless the owner specifically allows it.

Write the chain down. We leave a one-page sheet in the super’s office listing each zone, who is called, and the steps when a zone trips, and we update it when staff change.

Fire signals and burglary signals in a shared building

Is the smoke detector the landlord put in my apartment connected to any alarm, and will the fire department come if it goes off?

Usually it is a stand-alone device that only makes noise in your apartment. In New York City multiple dwellings the owner must provide and install approved smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in each apartment, and HPD’s posted notice puts maintenance and replacement of lost or broken detectors during the tenancy on the tenant, with an owner able to charge a tenant up to $25 for a battery-operated smoke detector. None of that connects the detector to a monitoring center. If it sounds and nobody is home, nobody is told.

If you want to be told, a monitored smoke or heat detector or a listener device that hears the existing detector can be added to an apartment alarm, so a sounding detector sends an alert to your phone or to a monitoring center. That is a convenience on top of the required detector, not a replacement for it; keep the landlord’s detector in place and working.

If you smell smoke or the detector sounds while you are home, leave and call 911. Monitoring is for when you are not there.

Can one panel handle burglary for my unit and also smoke and carbon monoxide, or should they be kept apart in a six-story building?

For a single apartment, one panel can do both: the same hub reports a door opening and a monitored smoke detector, and the monitoring center treats them as different signal types, fire signals being handled ahead of burglary. That is normal and allowed for your own unit.

What must stay separate is your unit system and any building fire alarm system. If the building has a fire alarm system with pull stations, horns and strobes in the halls, that system is designed, approved and inspected under FDNY rules by licensed electricians, and a tenant’s alarm must never be wired into it or labeled in a way that suggests it is part of it.

We label every device we install, and we tell you in writing which devices report fire and which report burglary, so nobody confuses a burglary siren with an evacuation signal.

There’s an old bell and a red pull box in our hallway. Is that a fire alarm system you can add burglary sensors to?

No, and please do not let anyone try. If it is a fire alarm system, it belongs to the building, it is regulated, and changes to it go through FDNY plan approval and a licensed electrician holding an FDNY fire alarm company certificate. Adding burglary devices to it would be wrong in design and in law.

Sometimes what looks like a fire alarm is the remains of an old system nobody maintains. That is a question for the owner and an electrician, not for an alarm installer, and an old, dead fire alarm system is not something we would ever describe to a tenant as working.

What we can do is add a separate burglary system for the owner’s common areas, on its own panel and its own wiring, and our fire alarm team can quote work on the building’s fire system through the proper process.

Shops on the Boulevard and Webster Avenue

My store on Bedford Park Boulevard has a roll-down gate. Do the sensors go on the gate, the glass or the back door?

All three can, but the gate is usually the last priority. A gate contact tells you the gate went up; useful, but the gate is in full view of the street and most of the risk is elsewhere. The glass behind the gate gets glass-break or shock sensors, the back door gets a heavy-duty contact, the cellar hatch or stair gets a contact, and the register area and stock room get motion coverage.

The common mistake is placing a motion sensor that looks through the glass at the gate. Gates rattle in the wind and pass light from headlights; a sensor that can see them is a false alarm waiting to happen. We aim interior motion sensors away from the storefront.

If the gate motor is electric, we can monitor it too, and an alert when the gate opens outside business hours is a cheap addition.

We run a repair bay on Webster Avenue whose overhead doors open twenty times a day. How do you alarm a door like that?

With a contact made for overhead doors, mounted on the floor or the track where it will not be crushed by the door or hit by a forklift, and with a schedule. During business hours the bay doors are in a zone that is not armed; at closing the panel arms them automatically, and if someone forgets to close a door, the panel tells you that the zone cannot arm instead of silently skipping it.

Inside, motion sensors cover the office and parts room, and in a bay where cars are left overnight a camera adds the evidence an insurer will ask for. Outside, a beam across the yard gate or a camera with line-crossing detection can warn before anyone reaches a door.

Webster Avenue is quiet at night, which is good and bad. Good, because any motion is unusual. Bad, because nobody nearby will hear a siren, which is why these alarms should always report to a phone or a monitoring center.

Our shop siren ran for an hour on a Sunday and the tenants upstairs complained. What does city law say about how long it can sound?

NYC Administrative Code §24-238 says no owner of a building may operate an audible burglar alarm on it unless the alarm is capable of and does automatically stop within fifteen minutes of being activated. A siren that runs for an hour breaks that rule, whatever caused it.

Usually the cause is a panel with no siren timeout set, an old outdoor bell with its own battery that keeps ringing, or a fault that re-triggers the alarm after each timeout. We check the panel’s bell cutoff, replace bells that cannot be timed, and fix or bypass the zone that keeps re-triggering.

Then look at why it went off. A siren that sounds every few weeks is a sensor in the wrong place, a loose door or a battery problem, and fixing it keeps the neighbors on your side.

Our office shares the street door and hallway with the apartments upstairs. Can we arm our space while tenants come and go?

Yes, by protecting your own door and rooms, not the shared hallway. Your alarm covers the door from the hall into your space, your windows and your interior; the hallway stays outside your system because residents use it at all hours and you have no right to alarm it.

If the building owner also wants the shared street door watched, that is a building zone on the owner’s panel, not yours. Two separate systems in one building are normal; we label each one and make sure neither siren sits where it will startle residents for the other one’s alarm.

Ask your landlord before you install anything in a shared space, and tell the super where your panel is, so an alarm from your office is not mistaken for a building problem.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Systems in Bedford Park

We could not open Google’s AI Overview for these searches while writing this page, so we do not quote it. The claims below are ones we hear from tenants, owners and shopkeepers who looked things up first, in chat answers, on national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and on product pages. Each is followed by what applies in Bedford Park.

“A home alarm costs about $750 installed”

National averages describe a typical American house. HomeAdvisor quotes an average of $747 with a range from $55 to $2,500, which is a reasonable picture of the whole country and a poor picture of one Bedford Park apartment or one Webster Avenue garage.

An apartment on the fourth floor may need four sensors; a three-story Victorian with a basement entrance may need twenty; a storefront needs glass and back-door protection a house never does. Averages hide all of that.

Our basic wireless apartment package starts at $600 at the Bronx rate, a building’s common-area zones are priced door by door, and every quote is a written list of sensors you can compare line by line with anyone else’s.

“Renters can’t have a real alarm system”

This comes from the idea that a real alarm means wires in the walls. Wireless panels with cellular communicators, monitored or self-monitored, are full systems: contacts, motion, glass-break, smoke and CO, sirens and app control.

In a district where the Furman Center puts homeownership at 4.0 percent, a removable system is not a compromise; it is the normal case, and it is how most of our apartment work in neighborhoods like this is done.

The real limits for renters are the lease and the building: no drilling where the lease forbids it, nothing on the building’s common areas without the owner, and nothing that interferes with a fire escape window.

“Any alarm will call the police when it goes off”

Only a professionally monitored system has someone receiving the signal and requesting a response. A self-monitored system sends a notification to your phone and leaves the decision to you.

Many people buy a kit, skip the monitoring subscription, and assume the siren is enough. In a solid prewar building a siren may not be heard by anyone who will act on it.

Decide in advance who receives the alert. That choice, not the brand of the panel, decides whether an alarm in Bedford Park does anything useful when you are away.

“Wireless sensors work anywhere in the building”

Range figures on boxes are measured in open air. Brick walls, plaster, steel doors and heavy floors cut range sharply, and a cellar sensor three floors below a panel may drop out.

Inside one apartment this is rarely a problem. Across a building it often is, which is why building zones in older Bedford Park walk-ups get their own panel near the cellar or the super’s office.

We measure signal strength at each sensor location before fixing it, and we write the readings on the job sheet.

“A smoke detector on your alarm replaces the landlord’s detector”

It does not. HPD notices make clear that owners must provide and install approved smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in each apartment, and tenants must keep them working. A monitored detector on your own alarm is an extra.

Removing the landlord’s detector because you have one on your alarm can leave the apartment out of compliance and you with a violation to answer.

Keep both: the required detector where it is, and a monitored device or a listener that tells your phone when it sounds.

“Louder sirens are better”

In an apartment building, the people a loud siren wakes are mostly neighbors, and the city’s noise code requires a building burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes in any case.

An alarm that wakes forty households for nothing a few times a year is one the owner will eventually unplug. A silent notification that reaches the right phone is more effective.

We use sirens where the noise itself is the deterrent, inside an apartment or at a door that should never open, and silent reporting where neighbors would bear the cost.

“Anyone with a ladder can install alarms”

Homeowners may install systems on their own property, but anyone in the business of installing security or fire alarm systems in New York State needs a Department of State license under Article 6-D, with fingerprinting, 81 hours of education and an exam.

Unlicensed installers turn up most often in cheap online ads and in buildings, where a handyman is asked to put sensors on a cellar door.

Ask for the license number before anyone starts work, and ask for proof of insurance in an occupied building.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking the Building Before Pricing the Apartment

This is a note on how we work, not a story about any particular building or tenant. When a Bedford Park tenant asks for an apartment alarm, we ask to see the route an intruder would take, not just the apartment. That means a look at the street door, the stair, the roof door if it is reachable, and the fire escape from outside if we can see it from the sidewalk or yard.

The reason is practical. A fourth-floor apartment with a fire escape that runs past a reachable roof needs a different window plan from one whose fire escape ends high above a locked yard. A ground-floor apartment beside a courtyard gate that never closes needs glass-break sensors that one on the third floor can skip. And sometimes the walk turns up a building problem, a roof door wedged open or a cellar door that does not latch, which no apartment alarm can fix and which the tenant can report to the owner in writing.

We do not go anywhere we are not allowed, and we never enter common areas without the tenant or the super. But ten minutes outside the apartment usually saves the tenant from paying for sensors in the wrong places.

FAQ

Bedford Park Alarm System FAQ

How long does it take to install an alarm in a Bedford Park prewar apartment?
+

A typical wireless apartment system with a door contact, three or four window sensors, a motion sensor and a keypad takes about two to three hours on site, including signal testing at each sensor and a walkthrough of the app. A building’s common-area zones take longer, usually a day, and longer again if any zone needs cable from the cellar.

Does the alarm still send alerts if the building’s internet or my Wi-Fi goes down?
+

Yes, if it has a cellular communicator, which every panel we install for monitoring does. The panel sends signals over the cellular network, so a dead router, a cut cable line or an unpaid internet bill does not stop it reaching your phone or the monitoring center.

Do you need to get into other tenants’ apartments to wire a common-area alarm?
+

Almost never. Building zones are wired through the cellar ceiling, the stair and the roof bulkhead, or they are wireless. If a run would need to pass through an occupied apartment, we look for another route first and only go in with the owner’s arrangement and the tenant’s agreement.

What happens to my alarm and my monitoring if I move out of my Bedford Park apartment?
+

A system you paid for is yours. We can take it down, patch any screw holes we made and reinstall it at your new address at our service rate, and monitoring moves with you without a new contract. If you leave it behind, delete your codes and app access first.

Will the alarm keep working during a building-wide power outage?
+

For a while. Every panel we install has a backup battery, and the sensors run on their own batteries, so the system keeps protecting and reporting through an ordinary outage. How long depends on the panel and what is connected to it, and we tell you the expected time for your system.

Can I arm my alarm from my phone after I’ve already left for the train?
+

Yes. The app shows whether the system is armed and which doors or windows are open, and lets you arm or disarm from anywhere you have a signal. You can also set a reminder that notifies you if nobody has armed the system by a certain time.

Do you install emergency buttons for an older relative who lives alone in the neighborhood?
+

Yes. A wireless pendant or a fixed button by the bed or in the bathroom connects to the same panel as the burglary sensors and can report to a monitoring center or to family members’ phones. We set it up and show the resident and the family how it works.

If I stop paying for monitoring later, does the alarm still do anything?
+

Yes. Without monitoring the panel still sounds its siren within the city’s time limit and, with app service, still sends notifications to your phone. You lose the central station that calls you and requests a police response, so make sure someone on your contact list is close by.

The previous tenant left a keypad and sensors behind and nobody knows the code. Can it be reset for me?
+

Often, yes. Many panels can be reset to factory codes on site, after which we test the sensors, replace weak batteries and set up your own codes and app. If the panel is locked to a company that will not release it, or is too old to talk to a cellular communicator, we quote a replacement panel that reuses the working sensors.

Are glass-break sensors worth it for a ground-floor apartment facing the street or a courtyard?
+

Usually, yes. A window contact only reports that the window opened; someone can break the glass and climb through without opening it. One glass-break sensor can cover several windows in the same room, which makes it good value on a ground floor.
