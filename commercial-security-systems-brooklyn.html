TERMINOLOGY

Brooklyn Commercial Security Terms, Translated

The vocabulary that decides what a Brooklyn quote actually contains.

PoE / PoE+. Data and power on one network cable per camera, reader or intercom — one battery-backup point in the closet instead of a wall-wart behind every device.

NVR vs DVR vs VMS. The recorder generations: DVR for analog coax, NVR for IP, VMS as the software layer above recorders and sites. A 2026 Brooklyn quote built around a DVR deserves a follow-up question.

ONVIF. The interoperability standard keeping cameras and recorders brand-independent — the difference between owning a system and renting a vendor.

OSDP. The encrypted reader-to-panel protocol replacing legacy Wiegand wiring, which can be skimmed at the door with a pocket device. Ask for it on every new opening.

Fail-safe vs fail-secure. What each locked door does when power drops — release or hold. Egress and fire code decide per opening; preference does not.

Electric strike vs maglock. Two ways to electrify a door: the strike cooperates with the latch; the maglock holds by force, suits storefront glass, and carries strict egress rules.

REX. Request-to-exit — the sensor or bar letting people leave freely while the street side stays locked. Skipped, the door either alarms all day or traps someone.

Door position switch. The contact reporting whether a door is actually shut — the cure for the yard door propped since the morning delivery.

Mobile credential. The phone as key: revoked in seconds, rarely lent out — which across three shifts of staff is exactly the point.

Dual-path communicator. Alarm reporting over cellular and internet together, so one cut line or ISP outage never silences monitoring.

Video verification. A clip rides with the alarm signal so the central station dispatches on evidence — the single best false-alarm reducer a business can buy.

Retention. Days of footage held before overwrite. Claims timelines and insurers set the number; the drives just have to obey it.

Edge analytics. Person, vehicle, line-cross and loiter detection on the camera itself, alerting live instead of after a scrub session.

Roll-down contact. The gate’s own alarm point — because in this borough the gate, not the door, is the first thing tested at 3 a.m.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Real Questions from Brooklyn Business Owners

What does a commercial security system cost in Brooklyn?

Working borough numbers: a storefront package of cameras plus a monitored alarm lands in the low four figures; a small office or studio with cameras and a door or two on credentials runs mid four figures to low five; an integrated mid-size build — sixteen to twenty-four cameras, four to six controlled doors, monitored intrusion — commonly falls in the $15,000–$45,000 class; warehouse and portfolio work starts around $50,000. The spread between two quotes for the same address is rarely the cameras — it is doors, gates, yard cabling and what got quietly excluded. A fair quote itemizes all of it, and ours are priced from a walkthrough that costs us eight minutes of driving.

Is a cloud camera plan worth it for a Brooklyn business?

Run the math over years, not months. Per-camera and per-door licensing compounds forever, and a twenty-four-camera cloud stack can quietly cost more than the cameras did. Cloud earns its fee for multi-location operators — a brand with storefronts on three avenues — and teams with no IT staff. A single-location business usually comes out ahead owning an on-premises recorder with off-site backup. We price both ways at a three-to-five-year horizon in every proposal; hybrid wins most often.

What should access control run per door in Brooklyn?

Most standard commercial doors land between roughly $1,500 and $3,000 installed — reader, electrified hardware, contact, request-to-exit and programming. Easy openings with existing pathway can come in near $500–$1,000; storefront glass leaves, fire-rated doors and enterprise openings reach $6,000–$8,000; new cable runs add $250–$600 per door, and gates and roll-downs carry their own hardware lines. A per-door price quoted before anyone has seen your doors is a guess wearing a suit.

How do I check a Brooklyn security company's license?

Two layers, both checkable in minutes. Statewide, security and fire alarm installation is licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D — verify any company with the Department of State; our number is 12000287431. Inside the city, electrical scope runs through DOB-licensed electricians and permits where the work requires them, and fire-alarm tie-ins bring FDNY requirements. Ask for the license numbers and the COI written to your landlord’s limits before you compare a single price.

Do I need a big national company, or is a Brooklyn installer enough?

Read the contract term before the brochure. The recurring complaint about the national chains is multi-year monitoring agreements with automatic renewal, equipment you never quite own, and a truck dispatched from a regional branch. A local licensed integrator gives you hardware you own outright, month-to-month monitoring, and — in our case — a shop that is physically in the borough, which is what same-day actually requires. For most single-location businesses: local install, optional monitoring, no agreement that needs a lawyer to exit.

Is a 100% deposit before install ever legitimate?

No. Deposit-and-balance is the professional structure — ours is fifty percent down, balance at completion — and full payment before installation removes every ounce of your leverage on the punch list. It is also the common thread in the installed-and-vanished stories behind half our takeover calls. A written scope, a deposit structure and a named warranty are the floor of a professional proposal.

Can I install my own store cameras in Brooklyn?

On property you own, legally yes — Article 6-D licenses businesses installing for others, and owners on their own premises sit outside it. In practice the lane narrows fast: most Brooklyn storefronts are leased, which puts the landlord’s alteration language in play; monitored certificates need a carrier-recognized installation; electrified hardware on egress and fire-rated doors is code territory; and anything near a building fire alarm is off the table. A camera over your own register, in your own building, is a fine Saturday project. A leased storefront is where DIY gets expensive quietly.

Will you reuse the wiring the last company left in our building?

When it earns its keep, yes — and an hour on site answers it. Existing coax frequently carries IP video over converters, sound Cat5e regularly survives for cameras, and pathway is reusable even when its cable is not. Access control is the usual exception: door hardware wants dedicated home runs, and legacy reader wiring is often the skimmable old protocol worth retiring. The proposal says keep, convert or replace, line by line, because billing you to rip out working cable is billing you twice.

Should our Brooklyn building be wired or wireless?

Wired PoE is the commercial default — one cable per device, centralized backup, no battery fleet, no gambling on radio through brick and joist construction. Wireless earns specific Brooklyn spots: a yard camera where trenching is absurd, a detached garage across the lot, a no-drill lease clause. The honest answer for almost every building is a wired backbone with wireless as a rare, documented exception, decided position by position at the survey.

How many days of video should a Brooklyn business store?

Thirty days is the working floor. Slip-and-fall claims, insurance investigations and police follow-ups reach back weeks, and discovering the recorder overwrote the incident is the most expensive storage lesson there is. Cash-heavy, regulated and higher-liability operations — nightlife, dispensaries, medical — commonly hold sixty to ninety. Retention is drive math against resolution and camera count, so we spec the number in writing and size the recorder to deliver it.

When the internet goes out at our shop, what stops working?

Almost nothing, if the system was specified for it. Cameras keep recording to a battery-backed local recorder, door controllers keep enforcing cached credentials and schedules, and the alarm fails over to cellular so the central station never goes deaf. What pauses is remote viewing and cloud sync, which catch up on reconnect. Cloud-only cameras and internet-only alarm reporting are the two specs we talk Brooklyn owners out of most often.

Who handles security in a Brooklyn mixed-use building — owner or storefront tenant?

The lease decides, and in mixed-use buildings it is worth reading twice. The usual split: the owner runs the residential entry, lobby and common areas; the storefront tenant secures the commercial space — its own alarm, interior cameras, its own door credentials. The contested zones are shared cellars, rear yards and any door that serves both sides. Bring the lease to the survey; we design to your side of the line, issue the COI the owner demands, and keep the two systems separate so neither party’s footage or credentials leak into the other’s.

Can our landlord make us remove cameras when the lease ends?

Usually yes for anything attached to the building — which is why the time to settle it is before installation, not at move-out. Standard commercial leases treat mounted equipment as alterations: the landlord can require removal and restoration, or can claim fixtures left behind. We put restore-on-exit terms in writing with the proposal, mount with removal in mind where the lease is short, and keep your recorder, credentials and data portable — so the system leaves with you instead of becoming a security deposit dispute.

The company that installed our system is gone — can you take it over?

Yes, and it is one of our most common Brooklyn jobs. Installers close, sell their accounts or stop answering, and owners inherit locked recorders, dead cameras and fobs nobody can add. Takeover is a bounded, normal project: we audit what exists, recover or factory-rebuild access, replace only what is actually dead, and put the system under health monitoring so silent failure stops being possible. Bring whatever paperwork survives; we have rebuilt from none.

How long does a commercial security installation take in Brooklyn?

From real jobs: storefront camera-and-alarm packages run one to two days on site; a suite or small office with a few controlled doors, two to four; integrated mid-size builds, one to two weeks; warehouse and multi-building projects phase over two to five. Brooklyn’s variables are stock and access — masonry drilling, occupied apartments above a retail job, landlord paperwork at managed buildings — and the pre-crew calendar (approvals, COIs) is where surprise delay actually lives. We start that paperwork at the survey so the install date holds.

Who pays for security in a Brooklyn commercial lease — landlord or tenant?

The lease answers it, and the standard split is: landlord carries base-building and common areas, tenant carries everything inside the demised space, with the alterations clause governing consent and COIs for your install. Negotiate two things now rather than at surrender: language confirming your security equipment is a removable trade fixture (many leases otherwise convert improvements to landlord property at move-out), and clarity on who maintains any landlord-provided systems your insurance assumes exist. A recently sold building adds urgency — new ownership inherits the lease, not the handshake.

We fired a manager who knows the alarm code — what changes today?

Today, in order: revoke every credential they held — fobs, mobile, PINs die in one click on a managed system; change the alarm code they knew, and if the business ran one shared code, replace the practice with per-person codes so the panel logs who armed what and one exit never forces a company-wide reset again; pull their remote camera and monitoring logins; then read the audit trail for the past few weeks with fresh eyes. Physical keys are the expensive remainder — every lock they could open gets rekeyed or stays a known hole, which is the recurring bill that converts Brooklyn businesses to credentials.

Why did our cloud cameras stop recording when the internet dropped?

Because cloud-only architecture records nowhere else — the cameras were healthy, the pipe was gone, and the footage for that window never existed. It is the single most common failure we diagnose on systems installed by someone else, and it has architectural fixes, not brand fixes: local recording on an NVR or on-camera edge storage as the source of truth, with cloud as the remote-access layer on top. The follow-on check while we are there: whether the subscription, the upload bandwidth and the retention setting actually match what the owner believes they bought. They frequently do not.

Can a badly designed access system make a building less safe?

Yes, and we inherit the examples: maglocks on egress doors without request-to-exit hardware, which trap people or violate code; readers on legacy wiring that a pocket device can skim at the door; unlock schedules that leave the rear door free all weekend because nobody audited them; shared PINs taped inside the counter; and orphaned admin accounts nobody can log into, so nothing can be revoked at all. Access control done wrong manufactures confidence without security — the doors click, so it feels handled. The audit is an hour with the door schedule and the panel; the redesign is usually cheaper than the assumption it replaces.

What is the average cost of a commercial security system in Brooklyn?

Most single-location Brooklyn businesses land between $5,000 and $22,000 installed for cameras, monitored intrusion and a door or two on credentials; storefront packages start near $3,000, and warehouse or multi-building work runs $18,000–$65,000+. Building stock moves the number — masonry, lofts, occupied floors above — which is why the binding figure comes from a free walkthrough, not a bracket.

Do I need a permit for commercial security cameras in Brooklyn?

Low-voltage camera work inside your own commercial space frequently proceeds without a standalone permit, but the scope decides: electrified door hardware, anything touching fire systems, exterior work on landmarked blocks and structural mounting can each trigger filings or consent. Add the landlord’s alteration clause at leased properties. We state which approvals apply to your exact scope in the proposal — and start them at the survey.

Do security systems lower business insurance premiums in Brooklyn?

Frequently — monitored intrusion alarms with certificates carriers recognize are the most consistently credited item, and camera coverage of named risks can add more. The sequence that works: broker first for the crediting requirements in writing, install to that spec second, so the certificate and the policy describe the same system. The credit compounds annually; the install is once.

Are security cameras a tax write-off for a Brooklyn business?

Generally deductible as business equipment, and installed systems commonly qualify for accelerated treatment such as Section 179 in the purchase year — your accountant confirms the specifics for your entity. It belongs in the quote comparison: after-tax cost is the real number, and it often closes most of the gap between the bid you want and the bid you fear.

Can my Brooklyn landlord make me take down security cameras?

Inside your leased space, the alterations clause governs: install with consent and the cameras are contractual, not discretionary. Exterior mounts on the building’s walls are where landlord permission genuinely rules, so get it in writing before drilling. The surrender clause is the quieter risk — paper your system as removable trade fixtures now, or lease language may convert it to landlord property at move-out.

Will my cameras record if the internet goes down in Brooklyn?

Only if the system records locally — an NVR or on-camera storage keeps writing through any outage while the alarm reports over cellular. Cloud-only systems record nothing for the duration, which is the most common failure we diagnose on inherited Brooklyn installs. One question filters every proposal: where does video live when the internet is down? “Nowhere” is a finding.

Should a Brooklyn business use a local security company or a national chain?

For one to a few locations, local usually wins where it hurts later: owned equipment, month-to-month monitoring, a published service rate, the same crew twice, and familiarity with the borough’s building stock. National brands fit large multi-state portfolios wanting one contract. Whichever way, read term, auto-renewal window and hardware ownership before comparing a single price.

How much is business alarm monitoring per month in Brooklyn?

Six Things Brooklyn Business Owners Learn the Hard Way

What a crew casing a Brooklyn storefront looks for

How access control quietly reaches end-of-life

Coverage beats camera count every time

The most common Brooklyn survey finding: four cameras staring at one corner, none on the rear yard where the losses happen, the sales floor filmed in triplicate while the register goes unwatched. Coverage is a map problem — entries, exits, points of value, approach lines — and eight placed cameras beat sixteen scattered ones every time, at lower cost, with footage someone can actually find.

The gap beside the roll-down gate

What keeps running when the internet quits

Built right: everything that matters. Cameras record locally on battery-backed equipment, door controllers enforce cached credentials, the alarm rides cellular, and the only casualties are remote viewing and cloud sync, which catch up on reconnect. Built wrong — cloud-only cameras, internet-only reporting — a routine ISP outage is a blackout. This is a design question, not a footnote.

Harden the recorder before someone else does

The forgotten DVR on factory credentials with a port punched open to the internet is how most real-world camera breaches happen — not Hollywood hacking, just housekeeping nobody did after the installer left. Hardening is unglamorous and decisive: default passwords purged, firmware maintained, security gear on its own VLAN, UPnP off, remote access through the platform instead of an open router.
