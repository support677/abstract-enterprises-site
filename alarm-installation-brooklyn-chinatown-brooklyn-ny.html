The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Chinatown Properties Need Alarm Installation

Brooklyn Chinatown grew on Eighth Avenue in Sunset Park, and its shape still follows that avenue. Wikipedia describes the original core as Eighth Avenue between 50th and 65th Streets, with Chinese settlement along the avenue stretching from about 42nd to 68th Streets since the 1980s and spreading onto Seventh and Ninth Avenues. The first businesses came early in that decade: Choi Yung Grocery at 5517 Fort Hamilton Parkway in 1983, Canton House on Eighth Avenue between 55th and 56th Streets in 1985, and Winley Supermarket at 5523 Eighth Avenue in 1986. CBS New York, telling the history of the Brooklyn Chinese-American Association in May 2026, recorded that Shiu Kan Leung opened the first Chinese-owned store on Eighth Avenue, an electronics shop, in 1980, and quoted the association’s Paul Mak: “In 1990 ... based on the survey, we had about 3,000 Chinese people in the neighborhood.” He now estimates more than 200,000.

What that growth produced, for an alarm installer, is a neighborhood of family businesses. The avenue is lined with restaurants, bakeries, supermarkets, seafood markets, phone shops, travel and service offices and small temples, most of them in two- and three-story buildings with the business on the ground floor and apartments or workshops above. The numbered side streets hold brick row houses and small multi-family houses, many of them shared by more than one household. And the families who run the businesses usually live within walking distance of them. That is the detail that shapes almost every alarm we design here. A restaurant on Eighth Avenue that opens at ten in the morning and closes at eleven at night is empty for about ten hours. The family’s home, a few blocks away, is empty for the other fourteen. Both are empty on a schedule that anyone paying attention can learn.

The break-ins that have been reported here fit those two empty periods closely. In June 2017, the Brooklyn Reporter listed a run of overnight burglaries by a two-person crew within a few blocks of each other: a basement door forced at Hot Space, 774 51st Street, at about 4:30 a.m.; the security gate broken at DCPLZZ Seafood, 770 59th Street, at about 4:25 a.m., with $2,000 taken from the register; the front gate broken at Hotpot, 811 53rd Street, at about 4 a.m., with $8,000 taken from the register while one of the pair kept watch; and the kitchen door broken at Mr. Q’s, 5302A Eighth Avenue, with $2,900, beer and an iPad mini taken. In July 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported that a man had forced his way through the front door of a home at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue on June 26 and taken $10,000 in cash. One set of incidents happened while the stores were shut; the other while a home was.

There is also a pattern from outside the neighborhood that families here ask us about. In June 2022 the FBI’s Newark Field Office warned that burglary rings were watching Asian-owned businesses to learn the owners’ routines, sometimes going through the owner’s car at work to find a home address or following the owner home, and then breaking into the home while the owner was at the business. In December 2024, ABC News reported similar burglaries in several states, including New York, and said suspects had reportedly used Wi-Fi jammers to interfere with alarm signals. We have no report of that ring working in Brooklyn Chinatown, and we will not pretend otherwise. But the method fits a neighborhood where the business and the home are both run on long, regular hours, and it is the reason this page spends as much time on the home as on the store, and on how an alarm’s signal leaves the building as on where its sensors go.

None of this means the neighborhood is dangerous, and we do not sell alarms with crime maps. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 72nd Precinct, which covers Sunset Park, shows 89 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 90 in the same period of 2025, and 107 in all of 2025 against 1,645 in 1990, a fall of 93.5 percent. A burglary that does happen, though, tends to fall on a building whose hours are predictable and whose weak point is out of sight, and an alarm is how the owner finds out in the first minute rather than at opening time the next morning.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Brooklyn Chinatown’s Architecture

Most of Brooklyn Chinatown was built long before it became Chinatown, as brick row houses and two- and three-story mixed buildings, in the era when Wikipedia says a stretch of Eighth Avenue bordering Bay Ridge and Sunset Park was nicknamed Lapskaus Boulevard for its Norwegian families, and it has been adapted ever since. Storefronts were reopened in the 1980s, houses were divided into more apartments, cellars became stockrooms, and upper floors became workshops. Six kinds of building cover almost everything we are asked to alarm here.

Shop downstairs, family upstairs

The typical avenue building: a store or restaurant at street level behind a roll-down gate, a cellar below reached by a stair or a sidewalk hatch, and one or two floors of apartments above with their own street door beside the gate.

The kitchen-to-yard restaurant

A long, narrow dining room with the kitchen at the back, a kitchen door opening onto a yard or a passage, a walk-in, and an office or storeroom wedged between them. The kitchen door is the one most likely to be propped open by day.

The brick row house on a numbered street

Two- and three-story attached brick houses on the side streets between Seventh, Eighth and Ninth Avenues, with a stoop or a few steps at the front, a basement door under them, a small back yard, and windows at the rear facing other yards.

The house shared by several households

A house that now holds more households than it was built for, sometimes relatives and sometimes tenants: more doorbells, a common hall, shared stairs, and several people who all need to come and go at different hours.

The walk-up with a fire escape

Four- and five-story apartment buildings with a locked street door, a common stair and a fire escape on the rear or the front. The building’s systems belong to the landlord; a resident’s alarm stays inside the apartment.

The upstairs workshop or office

Second and third floors over stores used for sewing workshops, offices, travel and service agencies, associations and small temples, reached by a separate stair from the street and often empty overnight and all day Sunday.

Shop downstairs, family upstairs: two alarms that never share a code

The avenue building is the one we alarm most often in Brooklyn Chinatown, and it is two jobs in one shell. The store needs protection from closing until opening; the apartments above are lived in all the time and belong to other people, even when the building owner runs the store. So the store gets its own panel, protecting the gate, the door behind it, the window glass, the rear door, the cellar, any roof hatch reached from the store, and the counter and office inside. The households upstairs can have their own alarms on their own panels if they want them, reporting to themselves. Nothing about the store alarm tells the owner when a tenant comes or goes, and no tenant has a code to the store.

Where the two meet is the street door to the stair, usually a few feet from the store’s gate, and the cellar. In many of these buildings the cellar is shared: the store keeps stock there, the boiler and the meters are there, and the building’s super or the tenants may need to get to them. We put the cellar on the store’s alarm only when the store has the only key and the only way in. Where the cellar is shared, we protect the store’s own door into it instead, and put a contact on the sidewalk hatch if the store controls it. That keeps the store alarm from going off when a tenant goes down to reset a breaker at midnight.

There is one rule we never bend in these buildings. A store siren under apartments has to stop quickly. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes of activation, and we set store sirens well inside that, because the families upstairs are the ones who listen to it, and a monitored alarm has already done its real work long before the siren stops.

The kitchen-to-yard restaurant: the back door is the front door after midnight

The 2017 break-in at Mr. Q’s on Eighth Avenue came through the kitchen door, not the gate, and that is exactly the door we worry about in a restaurant here. During service it is propped open for air, used for deliveries and garbage, and often fitted with a screen door or a light inner door as well as the steel door that is shut at night. After closing it opens onto a yard or passage that nobody on the avenue can see. So the kitchen door gets a heavy-duty contact on the steel door itself, set so the panel will not arm while it is open and will tell whoever is closing which door is the problem. If there is a window in the kitchen or the storeroom onto the same yard, it gets a contact or a glass-break detector, and the motion detector in the kitchen is aimed along the floor toward that door rather than across the cooking line, where hot air from the stoves and steamers keeps moving long after the gas is off.

Restaurants also keep their cash and their most valuable stock in odd places: a drawer in a tiny office between the kitchen and the dining room, a cellar storeroom for imported goods, a locked cabinet behind the counter. Each becomes its own zone, so an alert at 4 a.m. says which part of the building was entered, and the office can be protected by a shock sensor on the safe or cabinet as well as by the motion detector in the room.

The brick row house on a numbered street: front door, fire escape, yard

The side-street house is where the family lives, and it is empty when the business is busiest. The survey starts at the front, where the door at the top of the stoop and the basement door beneath it are the two entrances from the street. The June 2026 burglary at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue came through a front door that was forced, so the front door contact reports the instant the door opens, before any entry delay has run. Then we go to the back: the rear door to the yard, the ground-floor back windows, any window onto a fire escape, and anything near the back wall a person could stand on, such as a shed, a fence, or the flat roof of a one-story extension. Rear windows facing a yard are watched by nobody in the middle of a working day.

Inside, a single motion detector placed on the route from the back of the house to the bedrooms catches most of what the contacts miss, and in a house where several adults work different shifts, the system is set so that whoever leaves last does not have to remember to arm it. The panel and its cellular communicator go somewhere other than the first closet inside the front door, and the keypad by the door is a separate device, so damaging the keypad does not stop the alarm from reporting.

The house shared by several households: one building, several alarms, clear rules

Houses here are often shared by more than one household, and sometimes by more households than the building’s paperwork allows. We do not decide who may live where, and we do not wire anything that would make an unsafe arrangement more permanent. What we do is design to the building as it legally is and to the people who actually use it. The front door, the common hall, the cellar and the yard door belong to the owner, and the owner decides whether they are on an alarm and who has a code to that part. Each household can have its own small panel for its own rooms, on its own codes, reporting to itself. Bedrooms with windows to a fire escape keep those windows free to open from inside, always.

The practical benefit of separating the systems is that nobody has to share a code with somebody they do not know well, and nobody’s alarm is armed or disarmed by another household’s routine. The owner can see that the common doors are protected; a household can see its own rooms; and when a household moves out, its panel goes with it or is reset in minutes.

The walk-up with a fire escape: inside the apartment door only

In a walk-up, the street door, the intercom, the halls and the fire escape belong to the landlord, and none of them should be touched by a resident’s alarm. An apartment alarm here is small and removable: a panel with a cellular communicator in a closet, a contact on the apartment door, contacts or glass-break on any window onto the fire escape or a low roof, and one motion detector covering the hall inside the door. Everything is wireless and fixed with adhesive or small screws. The fire escape window is the one that matters most and the one with the strictest rule, which is covered in its own section further down: the window must stay openable from inside without a key, and the sensor must never get in the way.

The upstairs workshop or office: its own stair, its own hours

Second floors over Eighth Avenue stores hold a great deal of business that never appears on the sidewalk: sewing workshops, accountants, travel and immigration services, associations and small temples. Wikipedia’s history of Brooklyn’s Chinatowns notes that garment workshops moved here from Manhattan for lower costs. These spaces share three features. They are reached by a separate street door and a narrow stair, so the stair door is the first contact. Their windows often open onto a fire escape at the back. And they are empty at different times from the store below, often all day Sunday, when the store downstairs may be busiest. So an upstairs space gets its own panel and its own schedule, and we never tie it to the store’s alarm just because the two share a wall.

Store Hours, Home Hours

The Store Is Empty at Four in the Morning and the Home Is Empty at Four in the Afternoon

Most alarm advice is written for a household that leaves for work in the morning, comes home in the evening and sleeps at night, with an alarm armed “away” in the day and “stay” at night. That is not how a family that runs an Eighth Avenue business lives. A bakery may open before six and close at eight in the evening. A restaurant may take its first delivery at nine, open at eleven and close well after ten. A supermarket may be open every day of the year, with staff in the building from before dawn to late at night. The owners, and often several relatives, work those hours, and they go home a few blocks away when the business closes. So the business is empty in the small hours, and the home is empty through the day and the evening, every day, on the same clock.

That gives a burglar two easy windows, and the incidents reported here fall into both. The June 2017 store burglaries with reported times were all between about four and half past four in the morning: late enough that the last closer had long gone, early enough that the first baker or delivery driver had not arrived. The June 2026 home burglary at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue took place at a home that was entered by force through its front door; the report does not say what time, but cash was taken, and a home where the household is out at work is the home a burglar can search without hurry. The FBI’s 2022 warning described the same idea from the other side: crews watching a business to learn its owner’s hours, then going to the owner’s home while the owner was at work.

Designing for those two windows changes four things about the alarm. First, schedules. Most panels we install, including 2GIG panels on the Alarm.com service, can remind the owner when the system has not been armed by a set time, and the reminder for a store is set to its real closing time plus a margin, not to a generic midnight. The home system gets a reminder for the time the last adult normally leaves for the business. If nobody has armed the system half an hour after that time, the phones in the family get a message, and anyone can arm it from the app wherever they are. Second, reports. In a store, a report that the system was armed at 11:14 p.m. and disarmed at 6:02 a.m. is a quiet daily record that the right people closed and opened. In a home, the same report tells the family whether a relative who came home in the afternoon left the door unlocked when they went back to the restaurant.

Third, the moment of entry. On a home where nobody is expected during the day, there is no reason for a long entry delay on the front door, and the communicator can report the opening the instant it happens, so that by the time the person at the door has reached the keypad, the monitoring station or the family’s phones already know. Fourth, the people who get the call. The store’s alarm should reach the people who close and open the store; the home’s alarm should reach the people who live there, and both lists should include someone who is likely to answer the phone at the hour the building is empty. A call list made of three relatives who are all in the same kitchen at the same time is not much use.

Long closures matter too. Lunar New Year falls on Saturday, February 6, 2027, and many businesses here close for several days around it while families travel or gather at home. A closed store and an empty home in the same week is the situation where an alarm earns its keep, and it is worth spending ten minutes before the holiday to check that both systems arm, that the call lists name people who will be in New York, and that the monitoring station has a note of the dates the business will be shut.

Signal Path

Jammers, Torn-Out Cables and the First Few Seconds of a Break-In

An alarm’s sensors decide when it knows something is wrong. Its signal path decides whether anybody else ever finds out. Families here often ask us about the signal path, mostly because of the news reports about jammers, and they are right to.

Start with what has been reported. ABC News, in December 2024, said suspects in burglaries targeting Asian American homes and businesses in several states reportedly used Wi-Fi jammers to interfere with alarm signals, and put cameras outside victims’ homes to confirm nobody was in. DMP, a panel manufacturer, describes cellular jammers as illegal devices that block alarm signals so that they never reach the monitoring center, and says U.S. Customs and Border Protection has seen about an 830 percent increase in seizures of them since 2021. Brick Underground, writing about alarms in New York City, quoted Vincent Ingrisano of Alarms R Us on why a professional system should not depend on the home’s Wi-Fi: “The point is, you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.” Closer to home, ABC7 reported in January 2025 that burglars who came through the roof of a deli in Carroll Gardens tore out the alarm cables and the camera recorder before leaving.

Taken together, that describes three ways an alarm is defeated, and each has an answer. The first is a system that depends on the building’s Wi-Fi or internet, which a jammer or a cut cable outside the building silences. We install panels with their own cellular communicator, independent of the store’s or the family’s internet, with battery backup, and we use the internet only as a second path if the owner wants one. The second is jamming of the cellular path itself. A panel that loses contact with the cellular network can report that loss, and the monitoring service can treat a communicator that stops checking in as a trouble condition; where an owner wants more, jam detection devices of the kind DMP sells can tell the panel that its signal is being interfered with. No technology is a guarantee, and we say so, but a system that notices it is being jammed is very different from one that simply goes quiet.

The third is a system that waits too long to talk. Many alarms are set to report only when the entry delay runs out, which gives an intruder thirty or forty-five seconds to find the panel or keypad and smash it. We set the communicator to report an entry as soon as a protected door opens, so the event is already on its way before anybody reaches the keypad, and we mount the panel itself somewhere other than beside the door, out of sight, with the keypad as a separate device. In a store, the panel goes in a locked office or high in a back room, not under the counter where a camera recorder usually ends up, and its wiring is kept out of reach. In the Carroll Gardens deli, the owner estimated $1,000 to replace the alarm and the surveillance system after the burglars pulled them out; a system that had already reported the roof breach would at least have told someone while they were still inside.

Cameras complete the picture rather than replacing the alarm. The suspects ABC News described put their own cameras outside homes to watch for the family; the family’s own cameras, on the front and back of the home and on the store’s gate and rear door, show who was there and when, and appear in the same app as the alarm. The FBI’s list of advice for business owners included motion-sensor lighting on the front and back of the home, cameras on and around the home and business, and an alarm system. We install all three, and we explain what each one does, because the light makes the building less inviting, the camera makes a record, and only the alarm tells someone right away.

Fire Escapes

Bars, Fire Escapes and What a Sensor Can Do That a Gate Cannot

Walk down any side street between Seventh and Ninth Avenues and you will see window bars on ground floors and, on many houses and walk-ups, gates or bars over windows that lead onto a fire escape. Bars are an understandable answer to a fear of break-ins, and on a ground-floor window that is not a way out, they can be a reasonable one. On a window that leads to a fire escape, they are governed by strict rules, because that window is how people get out when the stair is full of smoke.

The rule, as Habitat Magazine summarized it for New York buildings, is that a gate or bars on a window facing a fire escape must be approved by the FDNY and must open from the inside without a key, with a quick-release latch; a key-operated lock is not acceptable. The FDNY publishes a list of fire escape window gates that hold its Certificate of Approval, and it notes that approvals once issued by the Board of Standards and Appeals were revoked as of July 1, 2008. NYC 311 lists bars or gates on fire escape windows among the conditions housing inspectors check. In the fire on 58th Street near Seventh Avenue on November 5, 2026, news reports said firefighters used a rear entrance to reach people trapped behind window bars. We mention that fire only because it shows, in our own neighborhood, why the rule exists.

An alarm cannot replace a sound gate, but on a fire escape window it can do something a gate cannot: it can protect the window without making it harder to open from inside. A contact on the sash reports the window opening; a glass-break detector in the room reports the glass breaking; a motion detector on the route inside reports anyone who gets past both. None of them has a key, none of them slows down a person climbing out, and none of them needs FDNY approval, because none of them blocks the window. For households that have bars on a fire escape window that do not meet the rule, the safe order is to have the bars replaced with an approved gate or removed by a qualified contractor, and to put the window on the alarm. We do not remove or alter gates ourselves, but we will plan the alarm around whichever gate you choose.

Two practical points follow. A contact on a fire escape window is placed so it never obstructs the sash or the gate’s release, and its magnet is fixed to the frame rather than to the gate. And on a home alarm, a fire escape window is set to report instantly, with no entry delay, because nobody in the family comes in that way. If a relative uses the fire escape to reach a neighbor or to take out laundry, we can set that one window to chime when the system is off, so the household learns to notice it.

Restaurants and bakeries: heat, steam and early starts

Kitchens are hard on alarms. A detector aimed at any of those will sooner or later trip the alarm at 2 a.m. for no reason. In a restaurant we put motion coverage in the dining room and the passage to the office, use contacts on the kitchen door and windows rather than a motion detector over the cooking line, and where the kitchen needs a detector, aim it low along the floor toward the back door. Bakeries add an early start: a baker who opens at five needs a code that works at five, a keypad by the door they come in, and a way to disarm the kitchen and front without disarming the office where the previous day’s cash is kept.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn Chinatown businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. On the avenue, where nearly every store has apartments above it, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • Licensing. Alarm installation businesses in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, and Brick Underground notes that anyone who installs or maintains alarm systems in the city must be licensed. Ours is #12000287431; ask any installer, including a relative or a friend of the family, for theirs.
  • Fire escape windows. Gates or bars on a window to a fire escape must be FDNY-approved and open from inside without a key. Alarm sensors on those windows are placed so they never obstruct the sash or the gate’s release.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm is not a code fire alarm system. Monitored smoke and heat detectors on a burglar alarm are useful in a store or a home, but they do not replace a fire alarm system where the building code requires one, and in kitchens they are placed and chosen to avoid cooking smoke.
  • Detectors in rental housing. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and sets carbon monoxide rules for owners and tenants. Adding monitored devices to a burglar alarm does not replace those duties.
  • No citywide alarm permit that we could find. We found no general New York City permit for a burglar alarm in a home or store. Avoiding false alarms still matters, because every unnecessary dispatch costs the police time and the owner credibility with the precinct.

The family business and the family home

Anyone who watches our restaurant can work out exactly when our apartment is empty. Can the alarm make those hours less useful to a burglar?

It cannot hide your hours, but it can take away the advantage they give. A burglar who knows a home is empty from ten in the morning to eleven at night is counting on two things: that nobody will notice the door being forced, and that there is plenty of time to search. An alarm removes both. The front door, the back door and any window onto the fire escape or the yard report the instant they open, with no entry delay on the doors nobody uses, and the communicator sends the event over its own cellular link before anybody reaches the keypad. Your phones, and the monitoring station if you choose it, know within seconds, at the restaurant, in the middle of service. Indoor lights on a timer and a video doorbell that you can answer from the restaurant make the home look less predictable from outside, and an indoor siren tells the intruder in the first minute that the house is not quiet after all. We also set the reminder for the hour the last person normally leaves for work, so a busy morning never leaves the home unarmed for the whole day.

The FBI has warned that burglary rings follow business owners home. If somebody watched our house for a few evenings, what would they learn about our alarm, and how do we give away less?

More than most families expect, and most of it is easy to change. Someone watching from the street can usually see whether a keypad glows through the glass of the front door, whether the family taps in a code at the door or arms from a phone, how long the person coming home stands at the keypad, and whether an alarm sticker is the only sign of a system. Someone who walks past the back can see whether there is a camera over the yard and whether the back windows have anything on them. We place keypads where they cannot be seen through glass from the stoop, give the family key fobs or the phone app so nobody is seen entering a code, and fit an exterior camera and motion light at the back, which the FBI’s Newark office specifically recommended for front and back. The FBI also advised business owners to keep their cars locked at work and their registration secured, because rings have used the car to find the home address. That part has nothing to do with the alarm, but it is free, and it closes the route by which a home was found in the first place.

We want alarms at the store and at home. Should they share one monitoring account and one list of people to call?

No. They should be two systems on two accounts, viewed together in one app. A monitoring operator who receives an alarm needs to know exactly which building it is, which door, and who to call, and the right answers are different for each. The store’s account carries the store’s address and cross street, a note about the gate, the cellar and the apartments above, and a call list of the people who open and close. The home’s account carries the home’s address, a note about the stoop and the basement door, and a call list of the people who live there and anyone nearby who can check. If both buildings were on one account, a 4 a.m. alarm at the restaurant might start with a call to a grandmother asleep at home, and a 4 p.m. alarm at home might start with a call to a cook in the middle of the dinner rush. With two accounts, the family still sees both buildings, both arming states and every alert on the same phone, and each building has its own codes, so a staff member with a code to the store never has one to your home.

After the gate comes down

In June 2017 two businesses on Seventh Avenue kept a burglary crew out at the gate and the front door. If nobody gets in, will we ever know they tried?

Not with a basic alarm, which only reports doors that open and spaces that someone enters. The Brooklyn Reporter’s account says the crew tried to force the door at the Wellcare adult day center at 5515 Seventh Avenue and tried to break the electronic front gate and front door at Happy Family Grocery at 5402B Seventh Avenue, and left both places with nothing. A gate that holds and a door that holds are good news, but an owner who learns about the attempt from a dented gate at opening time has lost the chance to have the attempt reported, recorded and passed to the police that night. Two additions change that. A shock or vibration sensor on the gate, its housing or the door frame reports someone working on it with a tool, even if nothing opens; we set its sensitivity so that a passing truck or a delivery against the gate in the day does not count, and arm it only when the store is closed. And a camera on the gate, in the same app as the alarm, saves a clip of that minute. The next time someone tries, you get an alert at 4 a.m. and a picture of who it was, not a mystery.

Our kitchen has a screen door in front of a steel back door, and the 2017 break-in at Mr. Q’s on Eighth Avenue came through a kitchen door. Which door gets the sensor?

The steel door, always, because it is the one that is locked at night. The screen door is there for air and flies during service and does nothing after closing. We fit a heavy-duty contact to the steel door and its frame, recessed or surface-mounted depending on the door, so that the panel will not arm while the steel door is open and the person closing sees on the keypad which door is the problem. If the kitchen door is used for deliveries and garbage all day, the contact can chime inside during business hours so the kitchen knows when it opens, and becomes a full alarm zone once the store is armed. Because the Brooklyn Reporter says the kitchen door at Mr. Q’s was broken, we also look hard at the frame and the lock. A contact reports that a door has opened; it does not make a weak frame strong, and if the frame is soft or the lock worn, we will tell you before we fit the sensor that the door itself needs work.

Several stores on our block still have a bell box that rings but never calls anyone. At four in the morning on the avenue, is ours worth keeping as it is?

As a deterrent, a little; as protection, not much. A local alarm makes noise, and at four in the morning on a commercial block the people who hear it are mostly the families sleeping upstairs. Nobody is told who can do anything, and the city’s fifteen-minute siren limit means the noise stops long before most owners would hear about it from a neighbor. The crews described in the 2017 report were in and out with the register cash in minutes, and a bell that nobody answers does not change that. The good news is that a bell-only system usually has wiring that can be reused. We fit a new panel with a cellular communicator on the existing door and gate wiring, add a glass-break and a motion detector if they are missing, keep or replace the siren with a shorter cutoff, and connect the system to your phones and, if you choose, to a monitoring station. That falls on our panel-replacement line, $800 to $1,500, rather than a new system.

Our seafood restaurant keeps live tanks with pumps and heaters running all night. Will they set off motion detectors, and can the alarm warn us if a tank leaks?

The tanks themselves rarely set off a well-placed detector, but the warm air and moving water around them can if the detector stares straight at them. Passive infrared detectors look for heat moving across their field of view, so we mount them to look along the aisle or toward the doors, with the tanks behind or beside them rather than in front. Bubbling water and pump noise do not trouble a motion detector, but they can confuse an acoustic glass-break detector placed right beside the tanks, so glass-break sensors go near the windows instead. On the second part, yes: water sensors on the floor under the tanks and beside the walk-in drain report a leak through the same panel and app as the burglar alarm, at any hour, armed or not. Many owners also want to know if the power to the tanks is lost overnight; the panel reports a mains power failure in its own room, and a separate power-loss sensor can be added on the circuit that feeds the tanks.

Households, workshops and temples

Our sewing workshop is on the second floor above a store, reached by its own stair from the street. What does an alarm for an upstairs workshop cover?

The stair door first, then everything a person could use to get in without it. A workshop upstairs is entered from the street door at the foot of the stair, which gets a contact, and from the door at the top into the workshop, which gets a second contact so the system knows the difference between someone in the stair and someone in the room. Inside, a motion detector covers the room from a corner that sees the stair door and the back windows together; sewing machines and steam irons switched off for the night do not trouble it, though a heater left running might, so we place it accordingly. The workshop has its own panel and its own schedule, because it is usually empty when the store downstairs is open and open when the store is shut, and nobody in the store needs a code to it.

Relatives come from China to stay with us for months at a time. How do we let a visitor come and go without sharing our own code?

Give the visitor a code of their own, with an end date. The panels we install hold a separate code for each person, and a code can be limited to certain days and hours or set to stop working on a chosen date, so a relative staying until the spring has a code that simply expires when they fly home. Their arming and disarming appear under their own name in the app’s history, so you know when they came in without asking. If a code is too much to remember, a key fob does the same job with one press, and a lost fob is deleted from the app in a minute. For an older visitor, we walk through arming and disarming with them on the day, at the actual door they will use, and leave the steps written down. What we advise against is the common solution of giving the visitor your own code; it is the one that is never changed afterwards.

Our small Buddhist temple on the avenue is open to visitors all day and keeps donation boxes near the altar. What should be armed at night, and how?

The building at night, and the donation boxes at all times. When the temple is closed, the street door, any rear or side door and any reachable window get contacts, and a motion detector covers the hall from a position that does not face the altar candles or lamps, which move warm air. During opening hours the hall is disarmed so visitors can come and go, but the donation boxes stay protected: a small shock sensor or contact inside each box, or on the cabinet that holds them, reports anyone forcing it open, and a camera over the boxes records it. Nothing needs to be fixed to the altar itself, and all the devices are small and white or can be painted to match. Where a temple is run by volunteers, we keep the codes simple, give each keyholder their own, and set the call list to people who live close enough to check without driving across the borough.

If the store alarm goes off at 4 a.m. and we live three blocks away, should we go and look?

Not alone, and not into the store. The 2017 crew worked with one person watching the street while the other was inside, and a store owner who walks up to a broken gate at four in the morning may be walking up to two people, not one. With a monitored alarm, the monitoring station contacts you and, where the signals indicate a real break-in, requests a police response; with phone alerts only, the right call is 911. If you want to see what is happening, open the camera app first. If you do go, wait outside, across the street, for the police, and let them go in first. What you can usefully do on arrival is meet the officers, unlock the gate for them and tell them what is normally in the building. Afterwards the alarm’s event history, with the exact time each door opened, and the camera clips from the same minutes, are what the precinct and your insurer will ask for.

“Burglars break into homes at night.”

What moves a Brooklyn Chinatown quote up or down

Field Notes

Field Notes: Gate First, Kitchen Door Second, Then the Walk to the House

This describes how our technicians survey a Brooklyn Chinatown business and home, not a story about one particular job. On the avenue the survey starts outside, ideally at closing time with the gate down. The technician looks at the gate, its bottom rail and its housing, the lock and the motor if it is electric, and asks how hard the gate is hit by deliveries during the day, because that decides where a contact and a shock sensor can live without being knocked off. Then he goes in and through to the back: the kitchen door and its frame, the screen door in front of it, the yard or passage behind, the cellar door or hatch, and the stair to the roof. He notes where the register, the office, the safe and the recorder are, and where the stoves, steamers, tanks and compressors are, because those decide where motion detectors cannot point.

Then, if the family wants the home done as well, he walks to the house. He stands on the sidewalk where a stranger would stand and looks at what can be seen: the keypad through the door glass, the alarm sticker, the cameras or the lack of them. He goes round to the back, looks at the yard, the rear door and every window onto a fire escape, checks whether any gate on those windows opens from inside without a key, and asks who lives in the house and what hours each person keeps. What would alarms for both our store and our home cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

Will incense burning at a home altar or in a temple set off the smoke detectors on the alarm?
+

It can if a smoke detector is mounted close above the altar, because smoke detectors respond to smoke particles whatever their source. We place monitored smoke detectors in halls and near bedrooms, away from the altar, and in a temple hall we use a heat detector near the altar where the building has no code requirement for smoke detection there. A burglar alarm’s motion and door sensors are not affected by incense at all.

Can mice in a food store set off the motion detectors at night?
+

Rarely, if the detector is chosen and placed well. A mouse is small and close to the floor, and the detectors we use in food stores are mounted high and set to ignore small warm bodies, the same way pet-tolerant models ignore a cat. What does cause trouble is a detector aimed at shelving where rodents climb near the lens, so we keep detectors clear of the top of the shelves and walk-test them before we leave.

Our phone-repair counter is a stall inside a larger store. Can a stall have its own alarm?
+

Yes, within limits. A stall that locks into a cabinet or cage at night can have a small panel of its own, with contacts on the cabinet doors and a shock sensor on the cage, reporting to the stall owner rather than the store. What it cannot have is sensors on the store’s gate or doors without the store owner’s agreement, and the store’s own alarm must not be set off by the stall’s devices, so we plan both together where possible.

We have to lift the gate before we can reach the store door. Where should the keypad go, and how long should the delay be?
+

The keypad goes just inside the door you unlock after the gate, out of sight of the street, and the entry delay starts at the door, not at the gate. Lifting a gate and unlocking a door takes most people well under a minute, so the gate contact is set to start the timer without sounding, and the door gives the rest. Many owners prefer to disarm from the phone app before lifting the gate, which avoids the delay entirely.

Can a glass-break detector hear a window break over refrigerated cases that hum all night?
+

Yes. Acoustic glass-break detectors listen for the particular pattern of sound that breaking framed glass makes, not simply for loud noise, so a steady hum from coolers and freezers does not mask it or trigger it. We still mount the detector with a clear line to the storefront glass and away from the compressors, and test it with a glass-break simulator before we leave.

Does an electric roll-down gate need a different sensor from a manual one?
+

The sensor is the same kind of heavy-duty contact at the bottom rail, but it is placed so the motor cannot tear it off and the gate’s own limit stops are not disturbed. On an electric gate we also add a shock sensor to the housing, because a crew that cannot lift the gate may attack the key switch or the motor box instead, as happened at the electronic gate on Seventh Avenue in 2017.

How is a supermarket with a loading door on the side street alarmed?
+

As several zones on one commercial panel: each front gate, the loading door, the cellar, the office and the sales floor. The loading door gets a heavy-duty contact and can chime during business hours, then becomes a full alarm zone at closing. Larger stores usually need more detectors than a standard small business, so they are priced line by line on the commercial or custom hardwired rows of the list after one visit.

Our travel agency keeps customers’ passports in the office overnight. What should be protected?
+

The cabinet or safe that holds them, on its own zone that stays armed even while the front office is open. A contact on the cabinet door and a shock sensor on its body report anyone forcing it, and the office door and window are on the alarm at night as usual. Passports were among the items reported stolen in burglaries of Asian American homes elsewhere, so we treat them like cash.

Our store is open from early morning until late at night every day. Is an alarm worth it for so few closed hours?
+

Usually yes, because the few closed hours are the ones that matter. The overnight burglaries reported here in 2017 happened between about four and half past four in the morning, inside that short window. An alarm also does useful work while the store is open: panic buttons at the register, an office zone that stays armed, and a chime on the back door so staff hear it open.

Do motion detectors work in a store with high shelving that blocks the view?
+

Wall-mounted detectors struggle in tall aisles, because the shelving hides most of the floor. In stores like that we use ceiling-mounted detectors that look straight down each aisle, or place detectors at the ends of the aisles looking along them, and rely on contacts and glass-break sensors for the doors and windows so the motion detectors are only a second layer.

Who keeps the alarm’s codes and app when the person who arranged it is working at the store all day?
+

Whoever the owner chooses as the master user, and we suggest at least two. The master user can add and delete codes from the app wherever they are, so a code for a new relative or employee can be created from the store counter. We leave a written list of every code holder and zone, and a second master user means the system never depends on one busy person.
