The Neighborhood

Why Bensonhurst Properties Need Access Control

Start with the houses. Bensonhurst’s housing is mostly attached or semi-detached, built through the twentieth century in brick, stucco and stone, some now wrapped in aluminum siding, with fully detached houses more common toward Dyker Heights and apartment buildings clustered along 86th Street and New Utrecht Avenue. A large share of those houses are two-family homes with a rentable unit, and real-estate listings for two-family houses in the area advertise finished basements with a separate entrance. That means the typical door we are asked about is not a lobby door with fifty fob holders behind it. It is a stoop door shared by an owner on one floor and a tenant or relative on the other, plus a side or under-stoop door to a lower unit, plus a gate or yard door that the whole family uses to take out the garbage.

The people behind those doors are unusually varied. The 2020 Census counted 104,934 residents in Bensonhurst, 43.9 percent of them Asian, 34.8 percent white and 17.2 percent Hispanic. The neighborhood was long known as Brooklyn’s Little Italy and is now home to the borough’s second Chinatown; together with Bath Beach it holds the city’s largest concentration of immigrants from Hong Kong, and in October 2026 the corner of Bay Parkway and 86th Street was co-named Canton Avenue to mark Bensonhurst as the city’s largest Cantonese cultural center. Russian, Ukrainian, Georgian, Uzbek, Arab, Pakistani, Mexican and Guatemalan households live on the same blocks. In practical terms, one house can hold a grandmother who reads only Cantonese or Italian, parents who work opposite shifts, teenagers who lose keys, and a tenant who speaks Russian. A lock that only makes sense to someone fluent in English and a smartphone app does not work for that house.

Households also change shape here more than the deed suggests. A study reported after the 2010 Census found that Asian families in Bensonhurst very often live in houses divided into studio apartments, which means more adults coming and going through one entrance than the building was designed for. Every new arrival gets a copy of the key; every departure takes one away. After a few years nobody knows how many copies exist. That is the core problem access control solves in a Bensonhurst house: each person gets their own code or fob, the owner can switch one off without changing the lock for everyone, and the log shows which credential opened the door.

Then there are the avenues. 18th Avenue, co-named Cristoforo Colombo Boulevard, is lined with small family-owned businesses, many run by the same family for generations. 86th Street runs under the elevated BMT West End Line, with D train stations at 18th Avenue, 20th Avenue, Bay Parkway and 25th Avenue, and since the 2010s most new businesses between 18th and 25th Avenues have been Chinese restaurants, groceries, bakeries and salons. Bay Parkway has its own cluster of Chinese businesses. These are shops with a front door customers use, a back or side door where deliveries arrive, a basement or rear stock room and often a small office where cash and paperwork are kept. Staff open early and close late, and the owner is not always the one holding the keys.

Burglary on those avenues is not hypothetical. In May 2024 the Brooklyn District Attorney announced a ten-year sentence for a Bensonhurst man whose crew, between August 2022 and January 2023, broke into a grocery on 17th Avenue and took a safe holding about $15,000, hit another grocery on New Utrecht Avenue, stole about $38,211 in merchandise from a vape shop on 18th Avenue, and burglarized private homes on 76th Street and, while he was out on bail in December 2023, on 72nd Street. The case was built by detectives from the 62nd Precinct using phone location records and enhanced video. A keypad does not stop a determined burglar on its own, but door logs, contacts that report a door left ajar, and a stock-room door that is locked by default make the difference between a quiet loss and a case that can be solved.

So the order in which we design a Bensonhurst system is simple. First the shared front door and who should open it, with a code or fob per person and a key for every household. Second, the secondary doors people forget: the basement unit’s side door, the yard gate, the shop’s back door and the stock room. Third, schedules, so a cleaner’s code works on Tuesday mornings and nowhere else.

Bensonhurst facts that shape an access control plan

  • 104,934 residents on about 2.95 square miles at the 2020 Census, 43.9% Asian, 34.8% white and 17.2% Hispanic — one of the most mixed populations in Brooklyn.
  • 77,700 foreign-born residents, the second-highest number of any New York City neighborhood when the city reported its record foreign-born population in 2013.
  • Mostly attached and semi-detached houses of brick, stucco and stone, many of them two-family homes with a rentable unit; about 1,200 new housing units were approved between 2002 and 2005, many in three-story brick buildings sometimes called “Fedders houses.”
  • Community District 11, which the Census Bureau groups as Bensonhurst & Bath Beach, served by Community Board 11 at 2214 Bath Avenue, ZIP codes 11204 north of Bay Ridge Parkway and 11214 south of it.
  • 62nd Precinct at 1925 Bath Avenue, which recorded 178 burglaries in 2018, the safest year in its records at the time, after targeted work on repeat offenders.
  • Seven subway stops on the elevated West End Line (62nd Street to 25th Avenue), four on the N’s Sea Beach Line and two on the F along McDonald Avenue.

What We Install

Access Control Services in Bensonhurst

Each service below starts from the same three questions: who needs to get in, through which door, and at what hours.

Shared front doors in two-family houses

One keypad or reader on the stoop door with a separate code or fob for each person in both households, a keyed cylinder for every family, and an app view only for the owner. The tenant’s code can be switched off on the day they move out without touching anyone else’s.

Basement and ground-floor unit entrances

A keypad lever or deadbolt on the side or under-stoop door to the lower unit, set up so the lower household and the owner can open it and nobody else. Where the lower unit has an inner door into the main house, we treat that door separately.

Gates, yards and alley doors

Keypad or fob control on the yard gate or the passage between attached houses, where garbage goes out and deliveries come in. Weather-rated hardware, sealed cable entries and a manual release on the inside.

Shop back doors and stock rooms

Electric strikes or keypad locks on the rear delivery door and the stock-room door of an 18th Avenue or 86th Street store, locked by default, opened by named staff codes or fobs, and logged so a shortage can be traced to a time.

After-hours staff entry

Schedules that let the opener in at 5:30 a.m. and the closer out at midnight, a cleaner’s code that works one morning a week, and a delivery code that expires at noon. The owner sees each entry without being woken by it.

Cash offices and records rooms

A second layer of control on the small office where cash, payroll or patient files are kept, so only two or three people can open it even if many people can open the front door.

Video door stations and intercoms

A camera-and-call station at the shared front door that rings both floors separately, so each household answers its own visitors and releases the door from its own phone or handset.

Takeovers, repairs and code clean-ups

Keypads, strikes and controllers installed by someone else, cleared of old codes and old installer accounts, re-aligned, re-sealed and handed back to the owner with a fresh list of who can open what.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Bensonhurst’s Architecture

Bensonhurst’s building stock comes in a handful of types. Each decides where a keypad can be mounted, how cable reaches it, and who has to agree to the work.

Attached brick two-family houses

The most common Bensonhurst door: a wood or steel stoop door in a brick front, often with a storm door or a decorative iron gate in front of it. Battery keypad deadbolts suit most of these doors without any wiring; a wired strike needs a cable path from the jamb back to a power supply in the vestibule or basement, which brick makes slower but not difficult.

Semi-detached houses with side driveways

Houses with a shared driveway and a side door to the lower unit or the kitchen. The side door is often the one used every day, and the one nobody thought to secure. A keypad lever there, plus a contact that reports when it is left open, closes the gap.

Stucco and aluminum-sided fronts

Stucco cracks if drilled carelessly and aluminum siding hides whatever is behind it. We locate studs and old wiring before drilling, use the smallest possible penetrations, and seal every one so water does not follow cable into the wall.

Three-story brick buildings from the 2000s

The newer brick multi-family buildings that replaced many older houses after rezoning, often with several units behind a small vestibule. Once a building holds three or more households it is a multiple dwelling under state law, and self-closing, self-locking entrance rules and the city’s smart-access privacy rules apply.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

A shop on the ground floor of 18th Avenue, 86th Street or Bay Parkway with apartments above, reached by a separate street door beside the storefront. The residential door and the shop door belong on separate credential lists, even when the same family owns both.

Apartment buildings along 86th Street and New Utrecht Avenue

Apartment buildings cluster along these two subway corridors. Each has at least a street door and usually a cellar or rear door as well. Here the entrance rules of the Multiple Dwelling Law come first and the convenience features second.

Brick, stucco and the cable path

On a brick two-family house the question is never whether a keypad can go on the door; it is where the power and data for a wired system will travel. Battery-powered keypad deadbolts avoid the question entirely, which is why we recommend them for most single-family-household front doors. When an owner wants a strike, a reader and a door contact — usually because several households share the door, or because remote release from upstairs is the point — we look for the shortest hidden path: through the hinge-side jamb into the vestibule, down into the basement along an existing pipe chase, or along the underside of the stoop. Only when there is no hidden route do we use a small painted raceway, and we show you its path before it goes up. Every penetration through brick or stucco is drilled with the right bit, sleeved where it passes through masonry and sealed afterwards.

Storm doors, iron gates and old frames

Many Bensonhurst front doors sit behind an aluminum storm door or an ornamental iron security gate. A keypad on the inner door is useless if the outer door is locked with an ordinary key, so the plan has to decide which door is the controlled one. Often the answer is to leave the storm door latched but unlocked and control the solid inner door; sometimes it is to move the keypad to the gate and use a weather-rated strike there. Old frames bring their own problems: oversized bores from earlier locks, rotted jambs behind the strike plate, and doors that have sagged so the bolt no longer meets the keeper. We fix the door so the lock can work, because an electronic lock on a door that does not close square fails within weeks.

Two households, one door: who controls what

In an owner-occupied two-family house, the owner is both neighbor and landlord. We set the system up so the owner holds the master account, each adult in both households has their own code or fob, and the tenant can be given the ability to issue a guest code for their own visitors without seeing the owner’s family list. If the tenant would rather not use an app at all, a code and a key are enough.

Code and law callouts for Bensonhurst doors

  • Exit doors must open from inside without a key. The NYC Building Code allows locks on doors from individual dwelling units only if they are openable from the inside without a key or tool, and sets strict conditions for electrically locked egress doors. A keypad deadbolt with an inside thumb-turn meets this; a double-cylinder deadbolt that needs a key on the inside does not belong on an exit door.
  • Magnetic locks on shop doors need a sensor and a manual release. Building Code §1010.1.9.8 sets the conditions for sensor-released electric locks on egress doors in business and mercantile spaces; we walk through them in the questions below.
  • Three units or more changes the rules. Multiple Dwelling Law §4 draws the line between a two-family private dwelling and a multiple dwelling at three families; entrance-door and intercom rules in §50-a apply only on the multiple-dwelling side of that line.
  • Every unit still needs a key lock. Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 requires the owner of a dwelling to provide a key lock in the entrance door to each dwelling unit and at least one key, so a code-only lock is not enough on its own for a tenant’s unit door.
  • Smart access in buildings of three or more units carries privacy duties. HPD’s guidance on Local Law 63 of 2021 says owners of multiple dwellings using keyless entry must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, collect only minimal data, obtain express consent, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and may not use the system to harass or evict a tenant.

This is a plain-language summary to help you plan, not legal advice. Where a door is part of a required exit or a building has three or more units, we confirm the specific requirements before the hardware is ordered.

Shared Entrances in Two-Family Houses

Our two-family house has one stoop door for both floors. Should every person get a separate code, or one code per household?

One per person, almost always. A household code feels simpler on day one, but the first time someone moves out, loses their phone or gives the code to a friend, the owner has to change it for everybody on that floor, and the log only says “second floor” rather than who actually opened the door. Most residential keypad deadbolts hold dozens of user codes, and connected models let the owner name each one. Naming them — “Nonna,” “Tony,” “Mrs. Chen upstairs” — costs nothing and makes the system useful later.

What changes in a two-family house is who manages the list. The owner keeps the master account. The tenant household can be given its own codes and, on many systems, the right to create short-lived guest codes for their own visitors without seeing the owner’s family list. Agree at the start of the lease who may add codes and how quickly the owner will remove a departing tenant’s codes, and put it in writing.

Can the basement or ground-floor unit’s side door get a keypad without touching the main front door?

Yes, and it is often the better first project. In many Bensonhurst houses the lower unit has its own entrance down a few steps beside the stoop or along the side driveway, and that door is used every day by the lower household and rarely thought about by anyone else. A battery keypad lever or deadbolt there gives the lower household their own codes, gives the owner a log, and leaves the main door alone. If the lower unit also has an inner door into the main house — common where the basement was finished for a relative — that inner door should be treated as an exit and kept openable from both sides according to how the house is actually used.

One caution. If the lower unit is not a legal apartment, adding a lock does not change its status, and an inspector will look at exits, ceiling height and windows, not hardware. The Department of Buildings treats an apartment added in a basement, attic or garage without approval as an illegal conversion and can issue a vacate order where conditions are dangerous. We install locks on the doors that exist; we do not advise on whether a unit can be rented.

We rent the lower floor of our two-family house to a tenant. What does the law expect of our door locks as landlords?

Less than many owners fear, but not nothing. The city’s Housing Maintenance Code requires the owner of a dwelling to put a key lock in the entrance door to each dwelling unit and to provide at least one key, and the Building Code allows locks on doors from dwelling units only if they open from the inside without a key or tool. So the tenant’s door needs a lock the tenant can use with a key, and nothing on any exit door should need a key to get out. A keypad deadbolt with a key cylinder outside and a thumb-turn inside satisfies both, which is one reason it is the default we fit on lower-unit doors.

Several heavier rules stop at the two-family line. Multiple Dwelling Law §4 treats a building occupied by three or more families living independently as a multiple dwelling and one designed for and occupied by two families as a private dwelling; the self-closing, self-locking entrance door and intercom duties of §50-a and the privacy policy duties HPD describes under Local Law 63 of 2021 are written for multiple dwellings. Keep the tenant informed anyway: tell them in writing which credentials they hold, that the owner can see entry times if the lock is connected, and how to reach you if the lock fails. If a third household ever moves in, revisit the whole arrangement before issuing the third set of codes.

Multigenerational and Multilingual Households

What kind of keypad works best for an elderly parent with poor eyesight or stiff fingers?

Raised, physical buttons that light up, on a lock with a lever rather than a round knob. Touchscreen keypads look neat but stay dark until tapped and give no feel under the finger, which is hard for someone with cataracts or arthritis; a backlit keypad with distinct keys can be worked by touch. A lever handle opens with an elbow or a closed fist, where a knob needs a firm grip and twist. Mount the keypad at a comfortable height, away from glare, and make sure the stoop or step in front of it is lit at night.

Then make the code easy for that person, not for the installer. Six digits that mean something to them beat four random ones they will write on the door frame. If remembering any code is a struggle, a fob on the key ring is one tap with nothing to recall. Auto-lock spares them going back to check the door, and a lock that chimes or flashes when it locks gives reassurance. We set all of this up with the parent standing at the door, and we test it with them before we leave.

Can the codes and instructions be set up for relatives who read Chinese, Italian or Russian rather than English?

The lock itself speaks in numbers, which is part of why keypads work so well in a house like this. What needs translating is the routine: how to lock from outside, what the low-battery warning looks like, who to call when the code does not work. At handover we write that routine on a short card in plain language, and the family can rewrite it in whichever language each relative reads; it lives inside the door, not on the outside where a stranger could read it.

For the app, most smart-lock apps follow the language set on the phone, but coverage varies by brand, so if a relative will manage codes we check the app on their own phone before ordering. In Bensonhurst, where the 2020 Census found an Asian majority alongside long-established Italian, Russian-speaking and Latino households, it is common for the person who manages codes to be a grown child, and for the person who uses the door most to be a parent who never opens the app at all.

When the upstairs tenant leaves, how do we make sure every code, fob and phone credential they held stops working that day?

Keep a written list of what each person was given, and work through it on move-out day: delete each code by name, deactivate each fob by its number, and remove the tenant’s phone account from the lock or reader app. Then test from the outside with the old code to prove it no longer works. On a connected system this takes minutes and can be done before the moving truck leaves; on a basic standalone keypad you may need to stand at the lock to do it.

Do not rely on the tenant handing fobs back. Collect them if you can, but deactivate them either way. Change any shared code — a delivery or garbage-gate code the tenant knew — at the same time. And give the incoming tenant fresh credentials rather than recycled ones; a fob that once belonged to someone else should be re-enrolled, not handed on. That is the whole advantage over a mechanical key: nobody needs to call a locksmith to change the cylinder every time someone moves.

Shops and Stock Rooms on the Avenues

How should a family-run shop on 18th Avenue control the back door where deliveries come in?

Lock it by default and open it on purpose. The most common weakness we see on avenue shops is a rear delivery door that is propped or left on the latch all day because unlocking it with a key every time a driver knocks is a nuisance. An electric strike or a keypad lock on that door, with a contact that reports when it is left open, changes the habit: staff open it with their own code when a delivery arrives, it closes and locks behind the driver, and the owner can see if it stayed open for twenty minutes.

If drivers come at fixed times, a delivery code can be scheduled to work only in that window. If they come unannounced, a simple doorbell at the back door tied to a chime at the counter is often better than giving out a code at all. Inside, the door must still open freely for anyone leaving — a lever or push bar, not a key — because a rear door in a shop is often a required exit. The 2022 burglary of a vape shop on 18th Avenue, where about $38,211 in stock was taken, is a reminder that stock rooms and back doors are where the value sits.

What hours should an opener’s and a closer’s code work, and how do we cover the day someone swaps a shift?

Set each schedule a little wider than the shift itself — an opener due at 6:00 a.m. can usually be allowed from 5:30, a closer until half an hour after closing — and leave the owner’s own credential unrestricted. Tight schedules that lock someone out at 5:58 cause more propped doors than they prevent. Review the schedules whenever store hours change, and remember the days the avenue changes: on 18th Avenue the Festa di Santa Rosalia fills the blocks between 66th Street and Bay Ridge Parkway at the end of summer, and shops on those blocks often open and close at unusual hours that week.

For a covered shift, issue a temporary credential rather than lending one. Most connected systems let the owner create a code that works for one day or one week and then expires on its own; the log then shows the person who actually opened the door, not the person whose code was borrowed. Before dawn under the elevated line, light and placement matter too, so mount the keypad where the opener can see the keys without a phone torch, or use a fob reader so there is nothing to read.

Who should be able to open a shop’s cash office, and what should that door record?

The owner and one or two trusted managers, and nobody else, even if a dozen people can open the shop. Run the office door on the same controller or app as the shop’s other doors so it is managed in one place, but give it its own short credential list. Add a door contact so every opening is logged with a time, including the ones where someone propped the door instead of using a code. That turns “who went in there?” into a question with an answer.

The grocery burglaries in the 2022 Bensonhurst series both ended with a cash box or a safe being carried out, one from a store on 17th Avenue and one from New Utrecht Avenue. Access control alone would not have stopped a break-in at night, but a locked office door with a log and an alarm contact makes a burglar work longer and tells the owner and police exactly when the door was forced. Pair it with a camera on the office door and you have the time and the face.

Does a magnetic lock on a shop’s side door need a push-to-exit button and a motion sensor?

If it is on a door people use to leave, yes. Magnetic locks hold the door with an electromagnet and release only when power is cut, so the city’s Building Code sets conditions on them in a means of egress: a sensor on the inside that unlocks the door when someone approaches, a signed “PUSH TO EXIT” device within five feet that cuts power to the lock directly and keeps it released for at least 30 seconds, automatic release if power is lost, and release on fire alarm activation where the building has an alarm. Skip any of those and the door can trap people.

That is one reason we usually recommend an electric strike rather than a magnetic lock on an avenue shop’s side or rear door. A strike lets the ordinary lever or panic bar open the door from inside at all times, mechanically, whatever the power is doing, and the electronics only govern entry from outside. Fail-secure strikes stay locked from outside in a power cut while still letting people out; fail-safe ones release. We choose door by door and write the choice on the handover sheet.

Offices, Practices and After-Hours Visitors

What should a small medical or dental office lock separately from its waiting room?

Three doors, usually. The street or waiting-room door, which stays unlocked during hours and on a schedule or reader after hours. The staff door from the waiting area to the treatment rooms, on a reader or keypad so patients do not wander through. And the room where medication, records or the server sit, on its own short list. Each of those doors needs to open freely from inside; the control is on entry, not exit.

Logging matters more in a practice than in most shops, because the question after an incident is often who entered a particular room and when. Choose a system where each staff member has a personal credential and where the log can be exported for a date range. We do not set up the practice’s privacy compliance, but we configure the hardware so the practice’s own policies can be followed, and we keep the access server or cloud account in the practice’s name, not ours.

Can the door log show which staff member let the cleaning crew in after hours?

It can show which credential was used and when, and that is usually enough. If the cleaner has their own code that works only on Tuesday and Friday evenings, the log shows the code used at 8:05 p.m. and the door contact shows when the door closed again. If instead a manager stays late to let the crew in, the log shows the manager’s fob at the time the crew arrived. Either way the owner sees it the next morning without having been called.

What a log cannot show is who walked through behind the person who opened the door. If that matters, a camera on the same door, set to record on the door contact, ties each entry to a picture. We set the camera clock and the access controller clock from the same time source so the two records line up to the second.

Wiring and Mounting on Bensonhurst Doors

How do you run wire to a reader on a brick or stucco house front without ugly surface conduit?

We look for a hidden route first. On most attached brick houses that means drilling through the hinge-side jamb into the vestibule and running the cable inside, where it can drop to the basement along an existing pipe or meter route to a power supply mounted indoors. Under a stoop there is often a void that leads to the cellar. On stucco we locate the framing behind it and drill once, cleanly, then seal. A small painted raceway is the last resort, and when we use one it follows the trim lines so it reads as part of the door surround.

Battery keypad deadbolts need no wire at all, which is why we suggest them first for a single-household front door. Wiring earns its cost when several households share a door, when the owner wants to release the door from upstairs, or when a door contact and a reader are needed together. Low-voltage access wiring does not need an electrical permit in the city when installed by a qualified person, but any new 120-volt outlet for the power supply is separate electrical work.

Will a keypad lock fit a door that already has an aluminum storm door or an iron security gate in front of it?

It depends on how much space the outer door leaves. Many keypad deadbolts stick out an inch or more from the face of the door, and a storm door that closes close to the inner door can strike the keypad or crush it. We measure the gap before ordering. If it is too tight, the options are a slimmer keypad, moving control to the outer door with a weather-rated strike, or rehanging the storm door slightly further out.

With an iron gate, the question is which door is the barrier. If the gate is always locked with a key, a keypad on the inner door does little for the family; if the gate is left latched but unlocked, the inner door is the one to control. Some owners prefer a keypad on the gate itself, which needs a weather-rated lock, a sealed cable route through the post and a clear plan for opening the gate from inside without a key.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Bensonhurst

Ask an AI assistant how to secure the door of a Brooklyn house or shop and you will get advice written for a suburban single-family home or a downtown office tower. Here is where that advice goes wrong on a Bensonhurst block.

“A smart lock costs about $300 installed.”

National cost guides such as Fixr put the average installed smart lock at around $300, in a $250–$400 range. For a battery keypad deadbolt dropped into an existing, square door, that can be close. It is not the price of access control for a shared Bensonhurst entrance.

Once several households share a door, or a shop wants a back door that locks by default and logs every opening, the job becomes a strike, a reader, a door contact, cable run through brick and a power supply indoors. That is why our published Brooklyn range for a single controlled door is $1,500 to $2,500, and why a keypad on a single shop door is $1,500 to $2,000.

Comparing a $300 consumer lock to a wired door is comparing different products. Ask any installer which one they are quoting, and whether the price includes fixing the door so the lock can work, because on old frames that is often where the hours go.

“Every apartment building must have self-locking doors and an intercom.”

State law does require self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings, and an intercom in those of eight or more apartments erected or converted after 1968. AI answers often apply that to any building with a tenant.

A Bensonhurst two-family house is not a multiple dwelling. The Multiple Dwelling Law draws that line at three families living independently, so §50-a does not apply to a two-family house, though the Housing Maintenance Code still requires a key lock and at least one key for each dwelling unit.

The practical upshot: a two-family owner has more freedom in choosing hardware, and fewer excuses. The right system is the one that fits the household, not the one copied from a fifty-unit building.

“Go keyless — you will never need a key again.”

Keyless is appealing, and for some doors it is right. But on a dwelling unit door in this city, the owner has to provide a key lock and at least one key, and a keypad that dies in a cold snap with no key override leaves a family on the stoop.

We install keypad deadbolts with a mechanical cylinder as standard, and we hand every household a key. Most families use the code every day and the key twice a year, which is exactly how it should be.

In buildings of three or more units, HPD’s guidance on Local Law 63 adds rules on consent, data collection and retention for keyless systems. Going keyless is a decision with legal edges there, not just a convenience.

“Magnetic locks are the strongest option for a shop door.”

Magnetic locks are popular because they are simple to fit on a glass door. On an egress door, though, the NYC Building Code requires a sensor that unlocks for anyone approaching from inside, a signed push-to-exit device within five feet that cuts power directly, release when power fails and release on fire alarm activation where one exists.

Get any of that wrong and the door can trap people. Get it right and the lock still releases every time the power blinks, which on a busy avenue may not be what the owner had in mind.

For most avenue shop back doors, an electric strike that lets the inside lever open the door mechanically is the safer and more predictable choice. We fit magnetic locks where the door genuinely needs one, wired to the code.

“Give each household one shared code to keep it simple.”

Shared codes are easy to set up and impossible to manage. In a house where relatives come and go and a tenant changes every few years, a shared code spreads to friends, drivers and former tenants within months.

Individual codes cost nothing extra on most locks, and they turn the log into something useful. The owner can cancel one person without retraining everyone else on the floor.

Where a shared code is genuinely needed — a garbage-gate code for a building, a delivery code for a shop — we schedule it to work only in the hours it is needed and change it on a calendar.

“Cloud access control is always better than a standalone lock.”

Cloud systems shine when several doors, several sites or several managers are involved. They also bring subscriptions, accounts and dependencies on the internet connection.

For a two-family house with one front door and one side door, a pair of good standalone or app-connected keypad locks usually does everything the family needs at a fraction of the running cost. For a practice that must export logs by date and staff member, the cloud earns its fee.

The question is not which is better in general, but who will manage the codes, how often people change, and whether anyone needs the log to stand up later. Answer those and the choice usually makes itself.

“Access control prevents burglaries.”

It reduces some and records many. The 2022 Bensonhurst series prosecuted by the Brooklyn District Attorney included safes and cash boxes carried out of grocery offices and stock taken from an 18th Avenue shop. A burglar who forces a door is not stopped by a keypad.

What access control changes is the everyday leakage — copied keys, propped back doors, ex-employees who still have a way in — and the evidence afterwards. A door contact that reports a forced door at 3:12 a.m. and a camera on the same door give detectives something to work with; the 2024 case itself was built in part on enhanced video.

So we design for both: fewer ways in for the wrong people, and a clear record when someone gets in anyway. Pair locks with an alarm or camera where the risk justifies it, and do not let anyone sell you a keypad as a burglar-proof door.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Write the List Before You Need It

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular client. The call we get most often from two-family houses is not about a broken lock. It is about a list nobody kept. A tenant has moved out, or a relative has gone back abroad, or a shop manager has quit, and the owner wants to know who can still open the door. On a mechanical lock the honest answer is that nobody knows. On an electronic one the answer should be on a single page — and too often it is not, because the codes were added one at a time over several years with names like “User 4.”

So the last thing we do on every job is write the list. Every door, every person, every credential, whether it is a code, a fob or a phone, and when it is allowed to work. We name every code after the person who holds it. We note which strikes are fail-secure and which fail-safe, where the mechanical keys are, and how to export a log. Then we test, from outside, that a code we were asked to delete really is dead.

None of this costs extra hardware. It costs twenty minutes at handover, and it is the difference between a lock that looked modern on installation day and a door the owner actually controls three years later.

FAQ

Bensonhurst Access Control FAQ

Do I need a Department of Buildings permit to add a keypad and electric strike to a Bensonhurst house or shop?
+

Usually not for the low-voltage part. Under NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to run below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when a licensed electrician or other qualified person does the work; wiring tied into life-safety systems is the exception and stays with a licensed electrician. A new 120-volt outlet for a power supply is ordinary electrical work and is handled separately. Changing a door, a frame or an exit path can raise building-code questions, which we flag at the walkthrough rather than after the hardware is up.

Our attached house shares a yard gate with the house next door. Can a keypad go on it?
+

Only with the neighbor’s agreement, and it is worth putting that agreement in writing. A shared gate between attached houses usually serves both yards or a common passage, so both families need codes, both need a way to open it from inside without a key, and both should agree who changes the codes and who pays for batteries or repairs. Where a neighbor will not agree, we put the control on your own side door instead and leave the shared gate on its existing latch.

If the batteries in a keypad lock die while we are at work, how does the family get back in?
+

Most residential keypad deadbolts warn for weeks before the cells run out, on the keypad and in the app. If they are ignored, there is still a mechanical key cylinder, and many models also accept a 9-volt battery held against contacts under the keypad to wake the lock long enough to enter a code. We show every adult in the household where the key is kept and how the emergency contacts work on the day we install.

Can the side door relock by itself if someone in the family forgets to lock it?
+

Yes. Most keypad deadbolts and keypad levers have an auto-lock setting that throws the bolt again a set time after the door is unlocked, commonly anywhere from about thirty seconds to a few minutes. Pair it with a door contact or a lock that senses whether the door is actually shut, so it does not throw the bolt into an open door. In a busy household we usually set the side door to relock and leave the main door on manual, so nobody is shut out while carrying groceries in.

Can we get a keypad that matches the black or brass hardware already on our door?
+

Usually. Residential keypad deadbolts and handlesets come in matte black, satin nickel, bronze and brass-tone finishes, and many brands sell the keypad and the matching lever or handleset as a set. If the existing handleset is in good shape and from a compatible brand, we can sometimes keep it and match only the deadbolt. +

Yes, by arrangement. Can I see from my phone who came through the side door while I was away visiting family?
+

Yes, with a connected lock or reader. Will a keypad on a Bensonhurst door that faces the street keep working through winter weather?
+

Choose one rated for outdoor use and mount it where it is partly sheltered — under a stoop overhang, inside a vestibule or on the hinge side of a deep jamb. Driving rain and freeze-thaw cycles get into cheap keypads through the button membrane. Backlit keys help in the dark months, and lithium batteries hold up better in cold than alkaline. We seal cable entries on brick and stucco so water does not follow the wire into the wall.

What stops a passer-by on the avenue from watching me type the code to my shop’s side door?
+

Several things together: a keypad that lets you type random digits before and after the real code, a reader that takes a fob or phone instead of a code, mounting the keypad so the body shields the keys, and changing codes on a schedule. For shared codes such as a delivery code, set them to work only during the hours they are needed. A fob or phone credential cannot be shoulder-surfed at all, which is one reason shops move to them.

If there is a break-in, can my door logs be shared with detectives from the 62nd Precinct?
+

Yes. The access system can export the entries for a date and time window, and if a camera covers the same door, the matching video clip makes the log far more useful. Export only the window that is asked for, keep a note of who received it, and leave the controller in place and recording. We set up the export at handover so an owner can do it in a few minutes.

What happens to the codes and the lock app account when we sell the house?
+

Hand the locks over clean. Before closing, delete every user code and fob, remove each household member from the app, and either transfer the owner account to the buyer or factory-reset the lock so the buyer can set it up from scratch. Leave the mechanical keys and the written list of doors and models. A connected lock that is still tied to the seller’s account is a common reason buyers call us in their first week.
