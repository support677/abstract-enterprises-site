The Neighborhood

Why Bay Ridge Properties Need Alarm Installation

Bay Ridge does not have a burglary problem in the way some neighborhoods once did, and we will start by saying so. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 68th Precinct, which covers Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, lists 65 burglaries reported in 2026 through November 8, against 64 over the same weeks of 2025. In the whole of 2025 the precinct recorded 75. In 1990 it recorded 1,694. That is a fall of more than ninety-five percent over a generation, and it means that most Bay Ridge households will never come home to a forced door. Nobody should buy an alarm here because a salesperson showed them a crime map.

What the low number hides is how the burglaries that remain actually happen, and that is the part an alarm can do something about. When the Brooklyn Eagle reported in September 2019 that police were looking for one man tied to fifteen summer burglaries of apartments and houses in Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, the detail that stood out was the way in: most of the entries were through unlocked windows or doors. One apartment near Shore Road and Harbor Court was entered through a fire-escape window at about 8:45 on a Wednesday morning; another, near Fourth Avenue and 82nd Street, through an unlocked kitchen window at about 7:30 on a Friday morning. Those are not the hours or the methods most people picture when they think of a break-in. They are the hours when a household is getting ready, leaving, or has just left, with a window still open from the night before.

The businesses face a different pattern. Between three and four-thirty on the morning of August 28, 2026, thieves broke into restaurants on and near Third Avenue, including Bar Chuzo, Cebu on 88th Street and Kettle Black on 87th Street, according to ABC7 New York. Camera footage from about 3:30 a.m. showed a hooded figure with a flashlight searching a restaurant's basement, and at Bar Chuzo the office door had been kicked in and the safe broken open. "They just took everything that was in the safe, and that was that," the manager, Kristina Koleva, told the station. Police urged restaurants nearby to take precautions, among them "activating alarms, inspecting entry points and considering additional lighting." An earlier string of six connected burglaries, reported by the New York Daily News, took about $28,000 from Bay Ridge businesses in roughly two weeks; one Third Avenue restaurant, Lobo Loco, was hit twice in five days.

And then there are the jewelers on Fifth Avenue. In December 2024, News 12 reported a Sunday-afternoon attempt with sledgehammers on the glass of Pyramids Jewelry at 6911 Fifth Avenue, with customers inside the shop next door. Early in 2025 three masked people swung sledgehammers at the front of Lamar Jewelry, also on Fifth Avenue, at around dinner time. In both cases the security glass held and the thieves left with nothing, but the owner of Lamar Jewelry told reporters the triple-layered glass would cost about $15,000 to replace. Neither report mentions an alarm. Our view is simple: good glass slowed the attack, and an alarm built for that shop would have reported the first blow to the glass to a monitoring station and the owner's phone, whether or not anything was taken.

Put together, Bay Ridge needs a quieter kind of alarm than the box-store kits advertise. In the apartments and houses it has to watch the windows people actually leave open, including the ones that face a fire escape or a back yard, and still be easy enough to use that the household arms it in the morning as well as at night. In the restaurants and shops it has to treat the cellar, the office and the safe as separate places, report the moment any of them is entered, and keep working when somebody cuts a wire outside. The rest of this page is about how we do that in the building types that make up the neighborhood.

A lot of the alarms we meet in Bay Ridge were installed decades ago and have outlived the arrangement they were built for. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Bay Ridge's Architecture

Bay Ridge was built largely in the first half of the twentieth century, after the subway made it reachable and the seaside estates gave way to streets. The houses on Doctors' Row went up between 1906 and 1913; the Shore Road apartment buildings mostly from the late 1920s onward. Between those two dates and the avenues that frame them, six kinds of property cover nearly every alarm we are asked to install here.

The limestone or brick row house

Two and three stories over a cellar, attached on both sides, with a stoop to the front door, a door under the stoop, a long back yard and a flat roof with a hatch. The 54 Renaissance Revival houses of Doctors' Row are the best-known examples, and the side streets hold many more.

The two- and three-family house

Brick or frame houses with an owner's floor and one or two rental floors, a shared front door and hall, a cellar with the boiler, and in many cases a separate entrance to the ground floor. The most common alarm customer we see outside the apartment buildings.

The Shore Road and Ridge Boulevard apartment building

Elevator buildings and walk-ups, many of them co-ops, built from the late 1920s on. A locked lobby, a super, sometimes a doorman, and apartments whose fire-escape and courtyard windows are the only way in that does not pass the front door.

The avenue walk-up over stores

Three- and four-story buildings on Third, Fourth and Fifth avenues with shops at street level and apartments above, reached through a street door beside the storefront. The store and the apartments share a wall, a cellar and sometimes a roof.

The detached house on the Crescent Hill blocks

Freestanding houses on the twelve blocks between 75th and 87th streets that the 1908 covenants kept for one-family homes, with lawns, side yards, porches and garages, and windows on four sides instead of two.

The storefront and restaurant

Ground-floor businesses behind a roll-down gate, with the dining room or sales floor at the front, a kitchen or stockroom behind, an office somewhere near the back, and a cellar reached by a stair inside or a hatch in the sidewalk.

Row houses: the stair is the spine of the system

A Bay Ridge row house has fewer ways in than its size suggests, because two of its four walls are shared with the neighbors. That leaves the street front and the back. At the front there is the stoop door at the top of the steps, the door under the stoop that leads to the ground floor or the cellar, and the windows of the ground floor, which on many of these houses sit only a few feet above the front areaway. At the back there is a door to the yard, the rear windows of each floor, and on many houses a rear extension whose roof sits just below a second-floor window. The roof hatch over the top-floor stair completes the list. A Brownstoner forum thread from 2010 describes a Brooklyn client whose alarmed house was burglarized through the roof and the rear, because, as the poster put it, police typically only check the front. That is the reason we never leave the hatch or the back off a row-house system.

Inside, every floor of a row house is connected by one staircase. Whoever comes in through the yard, the cellar or the roof has to use it. So the motion detector that matters most is not in the front room, where visitors and street-facing windows make it fussy, but on the stair landing, aimed along the run of the stairs. A second detector at the bottom of the cellar stair catches someone who comes in below. With contacts on the doors and the reachable windows and those two detectors, most of these houses are covered by our basic system or a wireless system in the $600 to $1,200 range, depending on how many rear windows count as reachable.

Two habits of row-house living matter. First, families sleep with the upper windows open in warm weather, and the panel can arm with a chosen window bypassed for the night, so the alarm is still on everywhere else. Second, a siren in one row house is a siren in three, so we fit interior sirens rather than loud exterior bells, set the cutoff well inside the city's fifteen-minute limit, and suggest telling the neighbors on both sides whose alarm it is.

Doctors' Row and the other landmarked fronts: everything goes inside

Because the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates exterior work in the Bay Ridge Parkway–Doctors' Row Historic District, we design alarms for those 54 houses so that nothing changes on the facade. The panel, the keypad, the siren and the cellular communicator all go inside. Contacts sit on the inside of the frames, and on original wood doors we use slim surface contacts or concealed ones set into the hinge side rather than drilling the face. A cellular panel needs no cable up the front of the house, which used to be the visible sign of an old landline alarm. If an owner wants a camera or a doorbell outside, we suggest checking with the Commission first and placing anything at the rear, where the rules are easier, before considering the front. The same approach suits any limestone front an owner wants to keep untouched, landmarked or not.

Two- and three-family houses: one panel, several households

The two-family is where a cheap kit most often fails, because it assumes one household. In Bay Ridge an owner usually lives on one floor and rents the other, or a parent lives on the ground floor and grown children above. A partitioned panel gives each floor its own area and its own codes, so one household can arm while the other is still awake. The cellar, the boiler room and the yard door are often best kept armed by the owner around the clock, and the shared front door and hall are set by agreement, usually as an entry door with a delay on whichever area is armed. The owner sees whether the shared doors were protected, but not when the tenant comes and goes, unless both sides want it that way.

Where a rental unit is involved, the city's detector rules apply alongside the alarm. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes that have fuel-burning appliances, placed within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide devices on the alarm are useful, because they report to a phone and a monitoring station when nobody is home, but they are an addition to the owner's legal duties, not a replacement for them.

Shore Road and Ridge Boulevard apartments: the windows the lobby does not see

The apartment buildings on Shore Road and Ridge Boulevard give their residents a locked street door, a lobby, a super and, in some buildings, a doorman. That is real protection for the front door of every apartment. It does nothing for a window onto a fire escape at the side or the back of the building, a ground-floor window over a courtyard, or a window next to a low roof over the lobby or a garage. The 2019 burglary near Shore Road and Harbor Court came in through exactly that kind of window, in the morning.

An apartment alarm here is therefore small and specific: a contact on the apartment door, a contact on each window that opens onto a fire escape, a courtyard or a roof, a glass-break detector where a window cannot take a contact, and one motion detector in the hall that anyone coming from those windows must cross. The panel sits in a closet with its own cellular link, so it needs nothing from the building's wiring or internet. Fire-escape windows are never blocked, locked shut or fitted with anything that slows someone climbing out; the contact only reports that the window has opened. Everything is wireless and comes off cleanly when you move.

Detached houses on the Crescent Hill blocks: four sides, one family

The freestanding houses between 75th and 87th streets were restricted in 1908 to one-family dwellings on lots at least sixty feet wide, according to Hey Ridge, and much of that area is still zoned for detached houses only. A detached house loses the protection of party walls. It has windows on all four sides, a side yard or driveway that cannot be seen from the street, and often a porch, a sunroom or a garage. The survey here starts by walking right round the house and deciding which ground-floor windows a person could reach unseen; the side facing a neighbor's driveway and the back facing a garden are usually the ones that count. We often add a glass-break detector in a room with several windows rather than a contact on each one, and a contact on the garage's interior door so that a car in the drive is not the only key to the house.

Apartments over the avenue stores: a shared building, separate alarms

The walk-ups over Third, Fourth and Fifth Avenue shops are the one place where the store's alarm and the apartment's alarm live under one roof. They should still be separate systems, with separate panels and separate monitoring accounts, because the store owner and the tenants upstairs have different hours and different people to call. What they share is the building. An apartment above a restaurant that is searched at 3:30 in the morning has a back window over the same yard and, sometimes, a roof the same people could reach. Tenants on those floors get the same treatment as a Shore Road apartment: door, fire-escape and rear windows, and the hall motion detector. Where one owner holds both the store and the building, we can put both on one partitioned panel with separate areas and separate call lists.

How Break-Ins Happen Here

The Open Window in the Morning and the Empty Cellar at 3 a.m.: Planning an Alarm Around How Bay Ridge Is Actually Burglarized

Most alarm advice is written for a house that is attacked at the front door at night. Bay Ridge's own record points somewhere else. For homes, the pattern in the reports we could find is entry through windows and doors that were not locked, often in daylight, often in the morning. For businesses, it is entry after closing into the parts of the building nobody can see from the street: the basement, the office, the safe. Planning an alarm for this neighborhood means taking those two patterns seriously and building the system around them, rather than around a picture of a burglar with a crowbar at the front door.

Start with homes. When most entries are through unlocked openings, the most valuable feature of an alarm is not the siren but the moment of arming. A modern panel checks every door and window as you arm it and tells you, by name, which one is still open: "kitchen window" or "fire escape bedroom". It can refuse to arm until you close it, or let you bypass it deliberately for the night. In a household that leaves the kitchen window up in summer, that one check does more than any extra sensor. We set the zone names in plain language for exactly this reason, and we set the "stay" mode, the one you use while you are home, so that it covers the windows and the cellar while leaving the rooms you are moving through free. A family that arms in stay mode while they shower and dress at 7:30 in the morning has covered the hour when two of the 2019 burglaries happened.

Next, the fire escape. Where an apartment has one, its window is the one opening in an apartment building that bypasses the lobby, the locked street door and the super. Police and residents have known this for decades; a precinct source in a Brooklyn Paper report on fire-escape burglaries elsewhere in the borough summed it up: "If your window's open, they go right in." The alarm cannot lock that window, and it must never make the window harder to open in a fire. What it can do is report the window opening at once, in every mode, day or night, and sound the interior siren so that whoever is climbing in knows the household has been warned.

Now businesses. The August 2026 break-ins on Third Avenue happened between three and four-thirty in the morning, in restaurants that were closed, and the footage showed somebody searching a basement with a flashlight before an office door was kicked in and a safe broken open. An alarm with one motion detector in the dining room and a contact on the front door would have reported that night eventually, but late, and without telling anyone where the intruders were. The sensible design treats the building as a set of rooms with different values. The street door and gate are one zone. The dining room or sales floor is another. The kitchen and back door are a third. The cellar is a fourth, with its stair or hatch protected and a motion detector at the bottom. The office is a fifth, with a contact on its door, and the safe gets a shock sensor that reports anyone working on it. When the alarm trips, the monitoring station and the owner learn which zone opened first and which followed, which is what police and insurers want to know.

Finally, glass. The Fifth Avenue jewelry attacks show both the value of good glass and its limit. Laminated and multi-layer glass can take repeated blows without letting anyone in, which buys time. But a sound-based glass-break detector listens for the particular frequencies of glass shattering, and glass that cracks without falling out may not produce them. For a shop that has invested in security glass, the right sensor is a shock or vibration sensor fixed to the frame or the glass itself, set to report a heavy blow, and kept on a zone that stays armed even while the shop is open. Then the first swing of a sledgehammer reaches the owner's phone and the monitoring station whether it is Sunday afternoon or three in the morning.

None of this calls for a large system. An apartment may need only a handful of sensors, a row house somewhat more, a restaurant more again. What it calls for is putting each one where the neighborhood's own record says it is needed, naming it so an alert makes sense, and setting the modes so that the household or the staff actually use them every day.

Restaurants on Third Avenue: protect the back of the house first

The dining room is where a restaurant makes its money, but it is rarely where a burglar spends his time. In the August 2026 break-ins, the footage ABC7 aired showed a figure with a flashlight in a basement, and at Bar Chuzo the target was the office and its safe. A restaurant alarm that only watches the front door and the dining room tells the owner that something happened, late. We start at the back instead. The rear door and any kitchen door to a yard or alley get heavy-duty contacts. The cellar is its own zone, with a contact on the stair door or the sidewalk hatch and a motion detector at the bottom aimed along the route a person would walk, not at the compressors and fans. The office door gets a contact, and the safe gets a shock sensor bolted to it or to the wall behind it, so that someone working on the safe is reported even if they came in a way nobody expected.

The front is still covered: a contact on the roll-down gate if the restaurant has one, a contact on the door behind it, glass-break detection over the front windows, and motion across the dining room and the bar. But the zones are named and ordered so the alert tells a story. "Cellar motion, 3:28 a.m., then office door, 3:31 a.m." is what a monitoring operator, an owner and the police can act on. The panel reports over its own cellular line with battery backup, so a cut phone or cable line outside makes no difference, and every arming and disarming is logged with the code that was used.

Jewelers and other glass-fronted shops on Fifth Avenue: report the first blow

Two attacks on Fifth Avenue jewelers in two months, both stopped by the glass, tell a shop owner two things. The first is that the investment in glass worked; the second is that the attacks were not timed for an empty shop at three in the morning. Pyramids Jewelry was hit on a Sunday afternoon, and a worker at the candy store next door told News 12 that "we had customers in the store." Lamar Jewelry was hit around dinner time on a Friday on a busy stretch of the avenue.

So the alarm for a Fifth Avenue jeweler has two layers. The first is a set of shock sensors on the display windows and the door glass, on a zone that stays armed around the clock, including while the shop is open and the rest of the system is off. The second is the ordinary burglary layer for after hours: gate, door, glass, motion over the floor and the showcases, the back room and the safe. Hold-up buttons at the counter and in the back room send a silent signal to the monitoring station without alerting anyone in the shop. A camera on the front, recording to a recorder kept somewhere locked, turns the first blow on the glass into evidence. None of that replaces the glass; it makes sure somebody is on the way while the glass is doing its job.

Early-opening food shops: a short night, a predictable morning

Bagel shops and bakeries have the opposite timetable to the restaurants. They close in the afternoon or early evening and open before dawn, which gives them a long night but a very predictable morning: the same person unlocks the door at about the same time every day, in the dark. That predictability is useful for the alarm. The app can send the owner a notification if the system is not disarmed by the usual time, or if it is disarmed much earlier than usual, and the opening and closing log shows who locked up and when. The first person in can carry a key fob with a panic button, and the system can be set so the entry door gives a short delay while every other zone, including the back door and the cellar, reports instantly. A business that has been broken into more than once in a year, as the Daily News reported of one Bay Ridge bagel shop, also gets a walk round with us to find what the earlier break-ins had in common before any new sensors are chosen.

Cellars, stockrooms and the hatch in the sidewalk

Many of the storefronts on the avenues have a cellar used for stock, kegs, dry goods, paperwork or a walk-in cooler, reached by a stair behind the counter or a steel hatch in the sidewalk. Cellars are dark, damp and full of equipment that hums, blows and changes temperature, which is why they cause more false alarms than any other room in a store when the detector is badly placed. We put cellar motion detectors high in a corner, looking along the stair and the walkway rather than at the compressor or the boiler, and walk-test them with everything running. A water sensor on the cellar floor is worth adding at the same time; it reports a burst pipe or a backed-up drain to the same phone, which in a restaurant can save a night's stock.

Compliance notes for Bay Ridge businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building's audible burglar alarm to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. On avenues where apartments sit over the stores, we set the cutoff well inside that limit. Neighbors can report a chronic alarm to 311, which passes it to the Department of Environmental Protection, and a DEP inspector must witness the problem in person before a summons is issued.
  • Licensing. Alarm installers in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431. Brick Underground's guide to New York alarms makes the same point: whoever installs or maintains your system must hold that license, and you can check it with the Department of State.
  • Roll-down gates. A 2009 city law required many storefront gates to be at least 70 percent transparent by July 1, 2026. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026, that the City Council had unanimously voted to repeal that deadline, so that a store need only install a compliant gate when it replaces its gate for other reasons; the bill still needed the mayor's approval, and the Department of Buildings said permit rules for security grilles were unchanged. Whatever gate a store has, a heavy-duty contact on it reports when it is lifted.
  • Kitchen fire systems are separate. A restaurant's hood fire-suppression system is inspected and serviced by specialist fire-protection companies. We do not service those systems, and a burglar alarm does not replace them or a code fire alarm system where one is required.
  • Detectors in rental housing. Where a business owner also owns the apartments above, HPD's smoke and carbon monoxide detector rules apply to those units. Monitored detectors on the store's or the building's alarm can be added, but they do not replace the owner's duties.
  • Landmarked fronts. Exterior work on the 54 houses of the Bay Ridge Parkway–Doctors' Row Historic District, including signs, cameras and sirens on the facade, falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Ground-floor offices in those houses keep every alarm device inside.

Restaurants and shops on the avenues

The thieves who hit Third Avenue restaurants in August 2026 searched one basement by flashlight and broke open an office safe in another. Should our cellar and our office be separate zones from the dining room?

Yes, and they should be the first zones on the list rather than the last. What ABC7 showed from that night was a person working through the parts of a closed restaurant that cannot be seen from the street, and what was taken came from the office and its safe. If the whole building is one or two zones, the alarm can only say "intrusion" and the monitoring operator cannot tell the police anything useful. If the cellar stair, the cellar itself, the office door and the safe each report separately, the alert reads as a sequence: which room was entered first, which next, and how many minutes apart. That matters for the response and for the insurance claim afterwards. It also lets you set the zones differently. The cellar and the office can stay armed while a manager does paperwork at the bar after closing, and the safe's shock sensor can be on a zone that never disarms at all, so somebody prying at it at four in the afternoon is reported as surely as somebody prying at it at four in the morning. In most Third Avenue restaurants this adds three or four devices to an ordinary commercial system, which usually stays inside our standard small-business range of $1,500 to $3,000.

A Bay Ridge restaurant owner told the Daily News he has "a ton of cameras" and they have not stopped anybody. We have cameras too. What does an alarm do that they don't?

It turns a recording into a phone call while the break-in is still happening. Louie Lykourezos of Louie's Gyros was describing what many owners find: cameras are excellent witnesses and poor guards. Most store camera systems record continuously and send motion alerts that owners learn to ignore, because the street, the headlights and the cleaning crew trigger them all night. When a break-in happens, the footage is found the next morning. An alarm works differently. Each sensor is either closed or open, and when the system is armed, an opened back door or a moving body in the cellar is not a maybe; it is a signal that goes straight to a monitoring station staffed to act on it, and to your phone, within seconds. The interior siren tells the intruder he has been noticed, which shortens the visit. The best arrangement is both working together: the alarm decides that something is wrong, and the camera, in the same app, shows you what it is. We can usually connect an alarm to the cameras a restaurant already has, and adding new cameras to a business alarm runs $1,000 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list.

The sledgehammer attack on Pyramids Jewelry came on a Sunday afternoon, with customers in a shop nearby. What part of an alarm still works when a store is open and the system is off?

Any zone you choose to keep armed around the clock, plus the hold-up buttons. Most of a store alarm is switched off when the shop opens, because the door, the floor and the showcases are in use. But some sensors make sense to leave live all day. On a jeweler's window or a glass showcase, a shock sensor fixed to the frame reports a heavy blow whether the store is open or shut, and ordinary customers leaning on the glass do not set it off. The rear door and the cellar can stay armed through business hours if staff do not use them, with a chime for anyone who opens them on purpose. Then there is the person at the counter. A fixed hold-up button under the counter and a portable one on a staff key ring send a silent signal to the monitoring station, which calls the police without sounding anything in the shop, so nobody in front of the counter knows it has been pressed. We set the hold-up signal so it is never confused with an ordinary burglary alarm, and we practise it with the staff before we leave, because a button nobody has pressed in training is a button nobody presses in an emergency.

Our bagel shop has been broken into three times this year. Does a third break-in mean our alarm is simply the wrong one?

It means the alarm, the building and the routine need to be looked at together, and the answer is usually more specific than "buy a bigger system". A shop that is hit repeatedly is usually being entered the same way, at the same time of night, by someone who has learned that the response is slow or missing. So before choosing equipment we go back over each break-in with you: where they got in, how long they were inside, what they took, whether the alarm sounded, who was called, and how long it was before anyone arrived. Very often one of a few things turns up. The alarm was not armed, because the closing staff were never shown how or the panel was fussy. It was armed but reported to nobody, because the monitoring had lapsed or the call list was out of date. Or it reported, but only after the intruder was already deep in the shop, because the first sensor he passed was the dining-room motion detector rather than the back door, the roof or the cellar hatch he came through. Each of those has a different fix, and most cost far less than a new system. Where the panel itself is the problem, a commercial replacement on our Brooklyn list runs $1,500 to $3,000, and a panel swap on existing wiring $800 to $1,500.

Apartments, co-ops and the morning routine

Two of the 2019 Bay Ridge burglaries happened at 7:30 and 8:45 in the morning, through a kitchen window and a fire-escape window. Can we keep the windows armed while we are still home getting ready?

Yes, and for a Bay Ridge apartment that is the most useful single setting on the panel. The Brooklyn Eagle's report described an apartment near Fourth Avenue and 82nd Street entered through an unlocked kitchen window at about 7:30 a.m., and one near Shore Road and Harbor Court entered through a fire-escape window at about 8:45. Both are times when people are usually home or just leaving. The answer is the "stay" mode, which arms the doors and windows while leaving the interior motion detector off, so the household can move around freely. We programme it so that the fire-escape and kitchen windows report instantly, with no delay, and the apartment door keeps a short entry delay for whoever comes home. You arm stay when you wake up or when the last person goes to bed; you switch to "away" when the last person leaves. The app can remind you at a set time if the system is still off, and when you arm, the panel names any window that is still open, so the morning check takes a second. If a window has to stay open for air, you can bypass that one window for the session and keep everything else armed.

Our longtime housekeeper has a key and a code, and she is leaving. How quickly can her code be removed and the monitoring password changed?

Her code can be deleted from the app in under a minute, and it stops working on the keypad as soon as the panel receives the change, which over the cellular link is normally immediate. That is one of the advantages of a panel connected to an app service rather than an old keypad that had to be reprogrammed by an installer. The monitoring password, the word the station asks for when it calls to check whether an alarm is real, is a separate thing. If she knew it, change it too, and update everyone on the call list; we can make that change with you on the phone. Brick Underground suggests asking any alarm company how long password changes take, and that is a fair test: the answer should be "now", not "within forty-eight hours". Two other steps are worth taking on the same day. Check whether she has a key fob as well as a code, and delete it from the system even if she returns it. And if the locks are being changed, tell us, so the door contacts are checked after the locksmith has refitted the strikes, since a contact that no longer lines up with a new strike will leave the door reading open.

Our Shore Road co-op would rather we avoided mounting anything on the walls. Can we skip the wall keypad and run the alarm from our phones?

You can, although we usually suggest keeping one small keypad, for practical reasons rather than technical ones. A cellular panel on the app service can be armed, disarmed and checked entirely from the phones of the people who live there, and the panel itself can sit on a closet shelf. Sensors are fixed with adhesive or a couple of small screws on the apartment side of the door frame and the window sashes, which most co-op house rules treat as decoration rather than alteration, though it is worth asking the managing agent if your building has unusual rules. The problem with an app-only system is the day the phone is dead, lost, or in the bedroom when the entry delay starts counting down. A key fob on the keyring solves most of that without anything on the wall. If you want a keypad but not on the wall, a wireless one can sit on a table by the door or inside a closet near it. Visitors who need to come in, a dog walker or a relative, can be given a fob or a code that works only at certain hours, and nobody needs your phone.

Older systems in row houses and two-families

One zone on the old alarm in our row house still shows trouble after we put a new battery in the sensor. What usually causes that?

On an older wireless system, the usual causes are the sensor itself, the signal path, or the panel's memory of the fault, in roughly that order. A wireless door or window contact that has been on a Bay Ridge back door for fifteen or twenty years may simply have failed; its reed switch or radio gives out, and a new battery does not bring it back. Some panels also hold a trouble condition until it is cleared from the keypad, so the fault may already be fixed and still be displayed. If the sensor is on the far side of the house from the panel, in the cellar or on the top floor of a row house with masonry walls, the signal may now be marginal because something new, a refrigerator, a metal cabinet or a mirror, has been put in the way. On a hard-wired system, a trouble message on one zone usually means a wire has been cut, pinched by a door or a picture hook, or corroded at a contact in a damp cellar. A Brownstoner forum poster described exactly this problem with a remote zone, and the answer is the same for anyone: read the exact message on the keypad, note the zone number, and have it tested. A service visit outside warranty is $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and replacing one or two failed sensors is often part of the same visit.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Bay Ridge

The claims below turn up again and again in generic online advice, cost guides and machine-written answers about burglar alarms. They are paraphrased rather than quoted, and each is set beside what we see on Bay Ridge jobs and what the local record shows.

"A home security system costs about $1,400, somewhere between $750 and $2,000."

Those are national figures; Fixr, for example, gives a range of $750 to $2,000 and an average of $1,400. They describe an average American house, and they tell a Bay Ridge household very little, because the cost here is set by the number of openings, not by a national average.

A Shore Road one-bedroom with a door and two fire-escape windows may need only a handful of sensors and fall at the bottom of our list. A limestone row house with a cellar, a door under the stoop, a long back yard and a roof hatch needs more. A restaurant with a cellar, an office and a safe needs more again.

"Most burglars come through the front door, so reinforce it first."

That may be true of the suburban houses much of this advice is written about. In Bay Ridge, the front door of an apartment is behind a locked street door and a lobby, and the front door of a row house is at the top of a stoop in full view of the street.

The Brooklyn Eagle's 2019 report on fifteen Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights burglaries said most entries were through unlocked windows or doors, and the two it described were a fire-escape window and a kitchen window, both in the morning.

So the first sensors go on the windows people actually leave open, and the most important thing we set up is the check that tells you, as you arm, that one of them still is.

"Security cameras are enough to deter a burglar."

Cameras are a deterrent for some people and a record for everyone, which is valuable. But "I have a ton of cameras and all that, but that's seeming not to stop anybody," as Louie Lykourezos of Louie's Gyros told the Daily News during the string of Bay Ridge business burglaries, is something we hear in different words from shop owners all the time.

The burglars in the August 2026 Third Avenue break-ins were filmed, and still took what was in a safe. Footage found the next morning helps police and insurers; it does not bring anyone to the door at 3:30 a.m.

An alarm does that, and the two together are better than either. That is why we put both in one app and set the cameras to back up the alarm rather than replace it.

"Security glass makes a storefront alarm unnecessary."

The glass at two Fifth Avenue jewelers did its job: sledgehammers did not get through it, and the thieves left empty-handed. That is a strong argument for good glass.

It is not an argument against an alarm. The owner of Lamar Jewelry still faced a replacement bill of about $15,000, according to News 12, and a determined crew at three in the morning has more time than one on a busy avenue at dinner time.

A shock sensor on that glass reports the first blow at once, open or closed, so the glass buys the minutes and the alarm makes sure somebody uses them.

"People in apartment buildings with secure lobbies don't really need an alarm."

Guides often say that apartment residents need alarms less than townhouse owners, because a doorman or a locked lobby protects them. For the apartment's front door that is fair.

It does not hold for a fire-escape window, a window over a courtyard or a window beside a low roof. The 2019 burglary near Shore Road and Harbor Court came in through a fire-escape window, past every protection the lobby offered.

A Bay Ridge apartment alarm is therefore small and inexpensive but not optional for anyone whose windows can be reached: a door contact, the reachable windows, and a motion detector in the hall.

"Any Wi-Fi alarm is fine; it just uses your home internet."

Many self-install systems do report over home Wi-Fi, and it works most of the time. But an alarm is for the bad night, and on the bad night the internet may be the first thing to go, whether because a router has failed or because someone has cut the cable outside.

Vincent Ingrisano, an installer quoted by Brick Underground in its guide to New York alarms, put it plainly: "you don't want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work."

The panels we fit report over their own cellular link with battery backup. The Wi-Fi is used for cameras and the app at home, never as the only path to the monitoring station.

"Your city will require an alarm permit and fine you for false alarms."

Many towns in New York State do run alarm permit and false-alarm fine programs, and generic advice assumes New York City does the same. We found no general city permit requirement for a burglar alarm in a home or store in New York City.

What the city does regulate is noise. Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building's audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes, and neighbors can report a chronic alarm to 311 for the Department of Environmental Protection to inspect.

So in Bay Ridge we set short siren cutoffs, place detectors to avoid false alarms in the first place, and make sure the people on your call list know how to cancel a mistake, because a false alarm is still a cost to the police and to your neighbors.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Fire Escape and the Cellar Stair, Finish at the Front Door

This is how our technicians survey a Bay Ridge property, not a story about any one job. In an apartment, the walk starts at the windows rather than the door. The technician opens each window in turn and looks out: at the fire escape, at the courtyard below, at any roof within a long step, at the neighbors' windows across the airshaft. A window that opens onto a fire escape always gets a contact; a window twenty feet above a courtyard with nothing below it usually does not. Then he asks the household how they live: which windows stay open at night, who leaves first in the morning and who comes home last, whether anyone works from home, whether a relative or a helper has a key. Only then does he look at the apartment door and decide where the motion detector goes.

In a row house the order is cellar, back, roof, front. He goes down the cellar stair first, tries the cellar door and any door under the stoop, and notes where the boiler and the water heater stand. He goes out into the yard and looks back at the house, because the rear windows and any extension roof below them only make sense from outside. He climbs to the top floor, opens the roof hatch if it can be opened, and checks the wireless signal from there back to where the panel will go. The front door comes last, because it is usually the easiest opening in the house. In a restaurant or shop he does the same thing in commercial terms: back door, cellar, office, safe, stockroom, and only then the gate, the glass and the dining room. The written price follows that walk, line by line.

FAQ

Bay Ridge Alarm Installation FAQ

Can we switch to a different monitoring company and keep the panel and sensors we already have?
+

Often, yes. If the panel is a standard model that is not locked to its original dealer, we can take it over, connect it to a cellular communicator and our monitoring partners, and keep the sensors that still test well. Some dealer-installed panels are locked or too old to support a cellular connection, and in that case a panel replacement on the existing wiring runs $800 to $1,500 on our Brooklyn list. Check the end date of any existing monitoring contract before you cancel it.

My father lives alone in Bay Ridge. Can the app tell me if his bedroom door has not opened by mid-morning?
+

Yes. The app service our panels use can send a notification when a chosen sensor has not been used by a time you set, such as the bedroom door or the kitchen motion detector by ten in the morning. It does not watch or record him; it only notices that a door has not opened. Many families pair it with a panic pendant he can press if he falls, which sends an emergency signal through the same panel.

Can our restaurant's alarm also look after the kitchen hood's fire-suppression system?
+

No. Kitchen hood fire-suppression systems are inspected and serviced by specialist fire-protection companies, and we do not service them. Your burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke and heat detectors in the cellar, the office and other rooms away from the cooking line, but the hood system stays with its own licensed provider.

The sensors came with our house and nobody knows how old they are. How do you tell which ones need replacing?
+

We test each one. Every sensor is opened, closed or walked past while we watch the panel, and the panel's own signal-strength and battery readings show which wireless devices are weak. Model numbers on the back of each device tell us roughly when it was made and whether it works with a newer panel. Sensors that pass stay; the ones that fail or cannot talk to a new panel are listed with their price before anything is replaced.

If one of us sets the alarm off by mistake, what stops the police from being sent?
+

Entering your code at once cancels the alarm, and the panel sends a cancel signal to the monitoring station. The station then calls the first number on your list and asks for the household passcode. Giving the right passcode stops the dispatch. If nobody answers, the station follows the instructions you gave it, which is why we set up the call list and the passcode with every person in the household before we leave.

We are changing internet providers next month. Does anything on the alarm need to be redone?
+

Not for the alarm itself, which reports over its own cellular link and does not use your internet at all. Anything that does use Wi-Fi, such as a video doorbell or cameras, will need to be connected to the new router, which takes a few minutes per device once you have the new network name and password. Keep the old service running until that is done.

How should we choose our alarm codes so they are easy to remember but hard to guess?
+

Avoid the obvious: birthdays, house numbers, the last four digits of a phone number, and repeated or sequential digits such as 1111 or 1234. Give each person a different code so the log shows who used it, and keep the master code to one or two adults. A code the person already uses for something unrelated, and can say without looking it up, works best.

Our row house is three floors with one staircase. Is one motion detector on the stairs enough?
+

In many row houses it covers most of what matters, because anyone moving between floors must use that stair. We usually mount it on a landing looking along the flight, and add a second detector at the foot of the cellar stair if the cellar has its own way in. Rooms with several reachable windows may also get a glass-break detector, but contacts on the doors and reachable windows do most of the work.

Will a water sensor in our co-op kitchen warn us before a leak reaches the apartment below?
+

It will warn you as soon as water reaches the floor where the sensor sits, which is usually long before it comes through the ceiling below. We place small water sensors under the kitchen sink, beside the dishwasher and the washing machine, and behind the toilet, and they report through the same app and, if you want, to the monitoring station.

Our toddler has learned to open the door to the back yard. Can the alarm chime when it opens, even when the system is off?
+

Yes. Any door contact can be set to chime or speak the door's name on the keypad and send a phone notification whenever it opens, whether the alarm is armed or not. Many parents set the yard door and the stoop door this way. It costs nothing extra if the door already has a contact.

Do motion detectors take pictures or record video inside our home?
+

The ordinary motion detectors we install do not. They sense the heat of a moving body and report only that something moved in their area. Cameras are separate devices, and inside a home we only put them where you ask for them. If you want no indoor cameras at all, the alarm works the same without them.

If the cellular network in our area goes down, does the alarm stop reporting?
+

A widespread cellular outage would interrupt a cellular-only panel's reports, and the panel notes the failure and keeps trying. The siren, the keypad and the local alarm still work. Where a household or business wants a second path, the panel can also report over the internet, so that either link can carry an alarm if the other is down.
