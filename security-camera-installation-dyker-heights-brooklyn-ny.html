The Neighborhood

Why Dyker Heights Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Dyker Heights was laid out as a suburb inside the city, and its houses still sit on their own ground, which is the single biggest thing that shapes a camera plan here. Walter L. Johnson began developing the hill in 1895, putting in gas, water, telephone and electric lines and planting sugar maples, according to the Brooklyn Eagle. Restrictions recorded in 1899 required large plots of at least 60 by 100 feet, with houses set back from the street, and those rules stayed in force until 1915. About half of the roughly 150 original houses still stand, Wikipedia notes; the rest gave way to Mediterranean-style villas, condos, and semi-detached and attached homes. When the City Council approved a rezoning of 159 blocks in 2007, it locked in that pattern: much of the area is now zoned for detached houses only, and most of the rest for detached and semi-detached houses, with rowhouse zoning limited to a few edges. The result, CityNeighborhoods says, is a neighborhood of houses "with yards and driveways instead of stoops."

For a camera installer, that changes the arithmetic. A Park Slope brownstone has a front and a back, with party walls on both sides. A Dyker Heights detached house has a front lawn, a driveway along one side that may run all the way to a garage behind the house, a narrow side yard on the other side, and a rear yard that can be deep by Brooklyn standards. Every one of those is a way onto the property that a person can walk without passing the front door. The 2019 burglary pattern that police tied to Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights shows why that matters: CBS New York reported entries through unlocked windows and doors, a ladder put up to a back window while residents slept, and an air conditioner pulled out of a window. None of that happens where a doorbell camera can see. A camera plan here starts with a walk around the whole lot.

The second thing that sets Dyker Heights apart is that, for about six weeks a year, the front of many houses becomes a public attraction. From Thanksgiving into early January, homeowners on the blocks in the 80s between 11th and 13th Avenues put up displays that Wikipedia says can cost from $1,000 to more than $20,000, and Brooklyn Paper describes hundreds of thousands of visitors each winter, with tour buses dropping off at 86th Street and 12th Avenue. Those lawns hold expensive things in plain view, and they have been targeted: the inflatable Santa cut loose on 75th Street in 2016, and display power cords cut for their copper at a handful of homes after the November 2018 snowstorm. A camera aimed at a lit lawn among crowds has to do two awkward things at once, which is to read a face against bright lights and to ignore thousands of people who are doing nothing wrong. We cover how in its own section below.

The third is quieter and more recent. In 2024 and 2025, residents on 67th Street near 12th Avenue described months of trouble with squatters in a house on their block. Yahoo News reported in February 2025 that a fire there was the fifth at the property in two years, that a squatter died in it, and that the Department of Buildings ordered the owner to demolish the building. Surveillance video from the summer before had shown one of the alleged squatters smashing a neighbor's security camera with a piece of plywood, and another resident told News 12 that squatters stole from his yard, including his electricity. Most blocks never see anything like that. But it shows that on a street of detached houses the problem is sometimes next door rather than on the sidewalk, and that a camera which can simply be knocked off the wall gives you nothing on the night you need it. Through all of this, the overhead Con Edison lines that serve much of the neighborhood go down often enough that the civic association called outages "a regular occurrence" in 2019, so the recorder has to ride through a power cut too.

Every Side of the Lot

Front Lawn, Side Driveway, Narrow Side Yard and Rear Garage: Planning Cameras Around a Dyker Heights Detached House

Most Brooklyn camera plans are drawn for a house with two exposed walls. In Dyker Heights we usually draw one for four, and the order in which those sides get cameras follows how people actually reach a house here. The burglary reports that name entry points describe the back and the sides: a ladder at a rear window, an air conditioner pulled out of a window, unlocked side and back doors, basement windows broken in the evening. The theft reports describe the front: a package taken outside a home near 12th Avenue and 76th Street in April 2020, and the 75th Street lawn Santa in 2016. The car reports describe the driveway and the curb. A plan that only covers the front door misses most of that.

We start every detached-house plan by asking a simple question at each corner of the lot: if someone walked in here, which camera would show their face? Not their outline at the far end of the yard, and not the top of their head from a camera under the eaves, but a face, large enough to recognize, at the moment they are closest. That usually means cameras placed so that people walk toward them rather than across them, mounted between eight and ten feet up rather than at the roofline, and zoomed in on site so the part of the frame that matters is not wasted on sky and street. Wide views still have their place, but as a record of where someone went, not as the camera that identifies them.

The frontage and the lawn

The houses here are set back from the street, a legacy of the 1899 restrictions, so the front camera has more ground to cover than on a stoop block. One camera on the porch or the front corner of the house, looking out across the lawn to the sidewalk, records anyone who steps onto the grass. A second, tighter view from the gate post or the fence line back toward the porch catches the face of someone walking away with something, which is the moment a doorbell camera mostly records a back. On blocks with big holiday displays, the lawn camera has extra work to do from late November, which we come to in the next section.

The driveway and the car

The driveway is where the car is, and it is often the longest open approach to the back of the house. We usually aim one camera from the house end of the driveway, out toward the street, so anyone coming up the drive walks straight at it, and set it to record continuously rather than only when motion trips it, because a person moving slowly along a car can slip under a motion setting. If the driveway runs past the house to a garage behind it, a second camera at the rear corner of the house, looking down the drive toward the street, covers the stretch alongside the house and the side door that often opens onto it. A plate-reading camera only makes sense where every car must pass a narrow point, such as a single-car drive between two houses; we set one only where the angle is right and say so when it is not.

The side yard on the other side

On a detached house the side without the driveway is often a narrow path between the house and the fence, sometimes with a gate at the front and a basement window or two along it. That path is a route to the back yard that nobody on the street can see. A turret camera at the front corner of the house, aimed along the path, records anyone who goes through the gate, and an infrared camera there needs its night light turned down, because the neighbor's wall a few feet away will otherwise bounce the light back and wash the picture out. Where basement windows sit along that path, the same camera usually sees them; if the path bends, a second small camera at the back corner covers the rest.

The garage and the rear yard

The rear yard is where a ladder goes up and where a back door, a cellar entrance or a set of glass doors usually sits. We put the rear camera high on the back wall looking down the yard toward the back fence and the garage, so anyone coming in from the back walks toward it, and stop the frame at your own fence line. A detached garage at the back of the lot often needs its own camera on the garage itself, looking back at the house, which covers the garage door, the back door of the house and the top of the driveway in one view. Getting cable to a separate garage is part of the planning: if there is an existing underground line or conduit we use it; if not, the options are a buried run, an overhead run, or a camera at the garage that sends its picture back by an outdoor wireless link, and the quote prices whichever fits.

Display Season

Bright Lights, Big Crowds and a Lawn Full of Decorations: Getting Footage You Can Use From Thanksgiving to New Year's in Dyker Heights

Nowhere else in the city does a camera installer have to think about Christmas lights as a design problem. The displays that made the neighborhood famous began in the 1980s, and Wikipedia credits Lou Singer's bus tours, which started in 1985, with spreading the word. The early displays were on 84th Street between 11th and 12th Avenues, and the bulk of the big ones are still in the mid-80s between 11th and 13th Avenues, according to Brooklyn Paper. In December 2025, CBS New York showed crowds packing the sidewalks outside Lucy Spata's house on 84th Street. Community Board 10 has mailed almost 1,000 residents on the main viewing streets asking them to turn their lights off at midnight, Fox 5 New York reported in 2018, and visitors are asked to keep off lawns and respect driveways. For the houses on those blocks, and for any house with a display worth stealing, the camera has three problems to solve in those weeks.

Problem one: a face against a wall of light

A camera adjusts its exposure to the brightest things in the frame. Point an ordinary camera at a lawn covered in LED figures and it darkens everything else to cope with the lights, so the person standing in front of them becomes a black shape. That is the opposite of what you want. We handle it three ways. First, the camera that is meant to identify people is placed so the display is behind it or beside it, not behind the person, which usually means looking out from the porch toward the sidewalk rather than in from the gate toward the lit house. Second, we use cameras with true wide dynamic range, which handle bright and dark parts of the same picture far better than the cheap kind. Third, we set the exposure and shutter by hand on site, at dusk, with the display switched on, rather than leaving it on automatic in the daytime and hoping. The 2016 theft on 75th Street happened at about 5:45 p.m., right at dusk, and that is the light we test in.

Problem two: thousands of people who are doing nothing wrong

With motion alerts left on the usual settings, a house on a viewing block will ring its owner's phone every few seconds from early evening until the street empties. People switch alerts off after the first night, and then they are off when something does happen. The answer is to stop asking the camera about motion and start asking it about lines and hours. We draw a line across the edge of the lawn, or around a particular display piece, and set an alert only when someone crosses into it; a person on the sidewalk is ignored, a person who steps over the low fence onto the grass is not. We also schedule the heavier alerts for the hours after the lights go off, when nobody should be on the lawn at all. Recording, meanwhile, never stops. Everything is kept on the recorder whether or not an alert fired, because the useful clip is often the one nobody was notified about.

Problem three: recording a crowd fairly

The people who come to see the lights are guests of the neighborhood, and a homeowner's camera is there to watch the property, not to film the public. We frame the lawn camera so it takes in the lawn, the walk and the near edge of the sidewalk, and no more, and use the camera's privacy masking to black out the houses across the street and any neighbor's windows that fall in the frame. Audio stays off. The result records anyone who touches the display or steps onto the property, while the hundreds walking past are a blur at the edge of the picture.

Why the neighbor's camera matters as much as yours

The clip that showed the 2016 Santa theft on 75th Street came from a camera on the house next door, Brooklyn Reporter reported, not from the victim's own house. That is common on a street of detached houses, where a camera on one house looks naturally across the neighbor's lawn and driveway. We will never aim your camera into a neighbor's yard or windows, but the front lawns and driveways of two adjacent houses are often seen from the same front corner, and neighbors who each cover their own frontage end up covering each other's approach. Some owners on the same block ask us to plan their cameras together for exactly that reason, while each household keeps its own recorder and decides for itself what to share.

The display's power and the camera's power

Display wiring is not our work, but where it meets the camera system we keep them apart. Camera power never comes from the same outdoor outlets or extension runs as the display, so a tripped display circuit does not take the cameras down with it, and the recorder sits on its own battery unit indoors. After the copper thefts of November 2018, some owners want to know if the display loses power in the middle of the night. A camera aimed at the lawn shows that plainly, because the lawn goes dark on the picture, and some cameras can send an alert when the scene changes sharply. We set that up where the camera supports it.

Tamper-Resistant Cameras

When the Trouble Is the House Next Door: Cameras That Still Record After Someone Tries to Take Them Out

The 67th Street case is the clearest local example of something installers see all over the city: the person you most need on camera is often the one who goes for the camera first. Yahoo News described surveillance video from the summer of 2024 showing one of the alleged squatters destroying a neighbor's camera with a piece of plywood. A resident told News 12 that a destroyed camera had cost him nearly $5,000 to replace. Residents also described squatters stealing from backyards and taking electricity. And because the house sat on a block of detached and semi-detached homes, the problem was a few feet from the neighbors' side yards and rear windows, not out on the street. In February 2025, after the fire that killed a squatter, the Department of Buildings ordered the owner to demolish the building and put up a construction fence. Most Dyker Heights owners will never live beside a house like that, but the lessons apply to any camera that someone has a reason to want gone.

Height and housing first

A camera you can touch from the ground, or from the top of a fence, can be swung, covered or knocked off with a board. We mount the cameras on exposed sides at least nine feet up wherever the wall allows, use metal-housed turrets or vandal-rated domes rather than plastic bullets where someone could reach with a pole, and fix the mounts into masonry or framing, not into siding or trim. On a side yard facing a problem house, the camera often goes under the eave, angled down, with its housing tight to the wall so there is no arm to grab.

A second camera that watches the first

The single best answer to a smashed camera is a picture of whoever smashed it. We place cameras so that each exposed one is inside another camera's view, ideally one mounted higher or further away. If someone walks up to the side-yard camera with a board, the rear-wall camera records them doing it. That costs one well-placed camera, and it turns the attack into evidence.

Cable they cannot reach and a recorder they cannot find

A camera can be defeated without being touched if its cable runs down the outside of the wall within reach. Ours go straight through the wall behind the camera into the house, or inside a metal cover where an outside run cannot be avoided. The recorder lives indoors in a closet or cabinet away from the entrances, never in a garage or on a porch, with its own battery unit. For owners who want an extra layer, alert clips from one or two key cameras can be copied off-site as they happen, so even a recorder taken in a break-in leaves the clips behind. A video-loss alert to your phone tells you the moment any camera stops sending a picture.

Your own property, your own power

Every camera goes on your own house, garage, fence or a post on your own lot, aimed at your own yard and the shared lot line, and it stops there. We do not aim into the neighboring house's windows, even when it is empty, both because the law protects people's privacy in their homes and because it is unnecessary: what you need is a record of anyone who comes onto your property. If you suspect someone is drawing power from an outdoor outlet on your house, a camera aimed at that outlet answers the question quickly; the fix for the outlet itself is an electrician's job.

The original houses: big eaves, deep porches and old wire

Johnson's houses were generous, and the Brooklyn Eagle's 1899 description, quoted by Wikipedia, lists ten-foot ceilings on the first floor, five rooms a floor and a cellar under the house. For cameras, that means good news and bad. The cellar gives us a clean path front to back, and the deep eaves give us sheltered mounting spots well above reach. But the porch that makes the house beautiful also hides the front door from the street, so the porch camera has to sit under the porch ceiling looking at the door, and a second camera covers the porch steps from the lawn side. Many of these houses have had several owners and several generations of alarm, phone and camera wire. We trace each old run before deciding to reuse, remove or abandon it, and on shingle or clapboard siding every camera goes on a mounting block fixed through to the framing, so the siding is not split and the hole behind the camera is sealed.

Interwar detached houses: the long run to the garage

The brick and stucco houses of the 1920s and 1930s often have the driveway on one side and a one- or two-car garage at the very back of the lot. Front, side and rear cameras are straightforward: cable along the cellar ceiling, up through a small hole at each wall, into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick. The garage is the job that needs a decision. Network cable runs reliably to about 100 meters, which covers almost any Dyker lot, but the cable still has to get there, and an unprotected run across a yard does not last. If there is already a conduit or a buried line to the garage, we use it. If not, we price a buried conduit, a run along the fence in a protective cover, or an outdoor wireless bridge, and say which we would choose. Stucco gets drilled slowly with the right bit and sealed anchors, because a hurried hole cracks it.

Semi-detached two-families: two households, one wall

The midcentury semi-detached houses are usually two-families sharing a wall with the house next door. The owner's cameras go on the owner's own walls, the shared wall stays untouched, and the side camera is framed so it covers the driveway or side yard on the owner's side without looking into the neighbor's. Inside, the owner and the tenant household often share the front door, the hall and sometimes the yard. One recorder, owned by the owner, carries every camera; the owner's account sees all of them, and the tenant's account sees the front door and the shared entrance only. The Dyker Heights Civic Association's 2014 list of signs that a house had been illegally cut up, which included steel doors onto a shared common area and multiple meters, is a reminder to ask how a house is actually occupied before deciding who gets which view.

Rebuilt villas: stone, glass and finished basements

The newer houses on the old lots are often faced in stone or stucco with cornice bands, columns and big windows, and their basements are finished rooms rather than open cellars. On these we plan cable routes with the owner before drilling anything, often through a utility room, a closet stack or the garage. Cameras go under soffits and into plain wall areas, kept off carved stone, and where a camera must clear a projecting band or cornice to see down the wall, it goes on a wall arm fixed into the masonry below. The glass doors to the rear yard get a camera from the yard side looking back at them, because from inside, glass reflects the room at night.

Small condo buildings: the board decides, the closet locks

The condos built here since the late 1980s are small, often three stories and fewer than a dozen units, from 4 units at 1231 65th Street to 12 at 8405 13th Avenue. Cameras on the entrance, the mailboxes, the stairs, the parking area and the trash area are the board's decision, and the footage belongs to the association. We put the recorder in a locked closet, give one or two board members full access, and set up a front-entrance view that every unit owner can see if the board agrees. Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Dyker Heights Lot Clockwise, Starting at the Sidewalk

This describes how we survey these houses, not a story about one job. We start on the sidewalk in front, because that is where a visitor, a delivery driver or a thief first sees the house, and we look at what can be reached from there: the lawn, the porch, the gate, the end of the driveway. Then we go clockwise. Up the driveway, standing beside where the car parks and looking back toward the street to find the spot from which a camera would see a person at the car door face-on. On along the drive to the back corner and the garage, checking whether there is a conduit or old line to the garage and how far it is from the house. Across the rear yard to the back fence, turning to look at the back of the house, which is where we decide how high the rear camera can go and which neighboring windows it must stop short of. Then down the side path on the far side, counting basement windows and checking how close the neighbor's wall is, because that decides the night setting. Back at the front we go inside: down to the cellar to find the clearest route along the ceiling, to the point where the internet line enters, and to a dry closet on a living floor for the recorder. In display season we come back at dusk to set the lawn camera with the lights on. By the end of the walk we know the camera count, every cable length, where the recorder lives, who gets which view, and what the written quote will say.

Displays and the holiday weeks

The clip of the inflatable Santa taken on 75th Street in 2016 came from the house next door. Should our camera be planned to see the neighbor's lawn as well as our own?

Plan it to cover your own lawn well, and let the overlap happen naturally rather than aiming at the neighbor's. On a street of detached houses, a camera on your front corner looking out to the sidewalk will usually take in the edge of the next lawn and the shared stretch of sidewalk, and that is often enough to show someone carrying a display away. What we will not do is aim a camera into a neighbor's yard or windows. If you and your neighbor both want better coverage, the better route is for each house to cover its own frontage, so each of you has a clip of the other's approach, and to agree in advance to share a clip when either of you asks.

Our display is lit from about 4:30 p.m. until midnight. Will the lights blind the camera when we need it most?

They will if the camera is set on automatic and placed with the display behind the people you want to see. We fix both. The identifying camera is placed so the display is beside or behind it, usually looking out from the porch toward the sidewalk. We use cameras with true wide dynamic range, then set exposure and shutter by hand at dusk with the display switched on, which is when the 2016 theft on 75th Street happened, at about 5:45 p.m. We check the result on your phone while standing on the lawn, and come back to adjust it if the display changes much from one year to the next.

Thousands of visitors walk past in December. Can the camera alert us only when someone steps onto the lawn itself?

Yes. Instead of a motion alert, we draw a line along the edge of your lawn or around a particular display piece and set the camera to alert only when a person crosses it. People on the sidewalk are ignored; someone who steps over the fence onto the grass is not. We usually add a schedule as well, so the lawn alert is fully active after the lights go off for the night, when nobody should be there. Recording stays continuous throughout, so a clip exists whether or not your phone rang.

Driveways, garages and cars

After the carjacking at 11th Avenue and Bay Ridge Parkway in November 2025, we want the car covered on the driveway. Where does that camera go?

At the house end of the driveway, looking out toward the street, so anyone walking up to the car walks toward the lens and their face fills the frame. ABC7 reported that the 2025 case, at about 12:34 a.m., involved three men who assaulted a driver and took his minivan, so we also set that camera for night: a faster shutter so movement does not blur, low-light color where the driveway has some light, and continuous recording rather than motion-only. If the car sometimes parks at the curb instead, a second camera on the front corner zoomed along your own frontage covers it there.

Our garage sits at the back of the lot, about 80 feet from the house, with no cable to it. How does a camera out there get its picture back?

There are three practical ways, and the quote prices the one that fits. If there is already a buried line or an empty conduit to the garage, we pull camera cable through it, which is the cleanest result. If not, a new buried conduit across the yard is the most durable choice, and 80 feet is well inside what network cable carries reliably. Where digging is not wanted, an outdoor wireless bridge, a pair of small radios on the house and the garage, carries the picture across the yard, with the camera powered at the garage. We do not recommend an exposed cable stapled along the fence, because it does not last.

We share a driveway with the house next door, like the 1925 three-family at 6915 12th Avenue that was listed with a community driveway. Can we put a camera on it?

You can put a camera on your own house covering your part of a shared driveway, and we frame it so it covers the drive and your own doors without looking into your neighbor's windows. A camera that covers the whole shared drive, or one mounted on a shared structure, is something to agree with your neighbor first, preferably in writing, since the drive is used by both households. Many neighbors in that position are glad to have it covered and simply ask for a view of the drive on their own phone, which we can set up as a separate account limited to that one camera.

Problem houses, power and old wiring

A squatter on 67th Street smashed a neighbor's camera with a board. What stops that from happening to ours?

Nothing stops a determined person from reaching a camera if it is low enough, so the plan is to make it hard and to make sure the attempt is recorded. We mount exposed cameras at least nine feet up where the wall allows, use metal-housed or vandal-rated cameras on the sides facing a problem property, take the cable straight through the wall behind the camera, and place a second camera so it sees the first. If someone walks up with a board, the second camera records them. A video-loss alert to your phone tells you the moment a camera stops sending a picture, and alert clips can be copied off-site so they survive even if the recorder does not.

Power on our block goes out several times a year because the lines are overhead. Will the cameras keep recording during an outage?

They can, for the length of time the battery is sized for. We put the recorder, the switch that powers the cameras and the internet modem on a battery backup unit, sized so the cameras keep recording through the kind of outage the neighborhood has seen, such as the roughly three-hour outage in December 2019 that the Brooklyn Eagle reported. Remote viewing depends on the internet service staying up, which is outside our control, but recording to the recorder continues locally. We also test a power cut at handover, so you see the system come back by itself.

Our house is one of the originals, and there is old alarm and camera wire everywhere from previous owners. Can any of it be reused?

Sometimes. Older coaxial camera cable can carry a picture again with the right converters, and old network cable can be reused if it tests clean along its whole length. We trace every run with a tone generator, test it, and then decide with you whether to reuse it, remove it or leave it abandoned and labeled. Wire that runs through finished rooms and still tests well is often worth keeping, because it saves opening walls. Wire that is spliced, nicked or of unknown route usually is not worth the risk on a camera you rely on.

FAQ

Dyker Heights Security Camera FAQ

How many cameras does a detached house in Dyker Heights usually need?
+

Usually five to eight, because a detached house has a lawn, a driveway, a side path and a rear yard, and often a garage at the back of the lot. A typical plan is a lawn or porch camera, a gate-post view back toward the porch, one or two driveway cameras, a side-path camera, a rear-yard camera and one on the garage. A semi-detached house shares a wall, so it often needs five. We would rather install five cameras that each do one job you can name than a dozen that overlap and record the street, and the quote lists each camera by its job so you can leave some for later.

Is it worth getting the cameras in before the decorators arrive in November?
+

Yes, if you can. Running cable across a lawn, drilling the porch and setting up mounts is far easier before the lawn is covered in figures and cords, and it lets us place the lawn camera knowing where the display will go. We then come back at dusk once the display is lit to set the exposure and draw the lawn-edge alert line, which takes far less time than the installation itself. If the display is already up, we can still install; we just work around it and avoid its wiring entirely.

Can a technician still get to our house in December, when the streets in the 80s are packed with visitors?
+

Yes, but we plan the visit around the crowds. Fox 5 New York reported that resident parking on some blocks has been limited in the evenings during the season and that 86th Street has tour-bus spaces, so on the viewing blocks we book daytime work, finish outside work before the lights come on, and come back for the dusk adjustment on a weeknight rather than a weekend. Off the main viewing blocks, December is much like any other month.

Do I need a building permit to put security cameras on my Dyker Heights house?
+

We found no source showing that ordinary low-voltage cameras on a private house need a building permit, and we do not file one for a standard house camera system. What does apply is the state installer license, which AESS holds, and an electrician for any new outlet, which appears as its own line on the quote. If the house is a city landmark, which in Dyker Heights we know to be the case only for the Angel Guardian Home, exterior work needs Landmarks Preservation Commission approval first.

Can a camera on our porch catch the person who takes a package, or only the delivery?
+

A single doorbell often catches the delivery well and the thief poorly, because the person taking the box turns away from the door. The Brooklyn Eagle reported a package stolen outside a home near 12th Avenue and 76th Street in 2020, which is the kind of case this matters for. Pair the doorbell with a second camera on the gate post or the fence corner looking back at the porch. That view records the face of whoever walks back down the path carrying something, which is the picture police can actually use.

Will a wired system work with the Ring or Nest doorbell we already have?
+

Usually alongside it rather than inside it. Many doorbells only record to their own app and cloud plan, and a few can record to a recorder. We check yours during the walk-through and tell you plainly which it is. Either way there is rarely a reason to remove a doorbell that works; the wired system adds the lawn, the driveway, the side path, the rear yard and the garage, all recording continuously to a recorder you own, and the doorbell keeps doing what it does well at the door.

How long will the recorder keep footage before it records over it?
+

That depends on the number of cameras, their resolution and the size of the drives, and we size it to what you ask for. Most house owners here want two to four weeks of continuous recording, which covers a trip away and the time it takes to notice that something happened. Stores and condos often want longer. The quote states the number of days we have sized the drives for, and at handover we show you how to export and lock a clip so it is never recorded over.

Can we watch the cameras on our phones when we are away for the winter?
+

Yes. We set up the manufacturer's app on each phone with its own account, so each person sees what they should and nothing else, and we test remote viewing from a phone on mobile data before we leave, not just on your home Wi-Fi. If the internet service at the house goes down, recording continues on the recorder and you can review it when the connection returns. A video-loss alert tells you if any camera stops sending a picture while you are away.

My elderly parents live on the ground floor. Can they see the front door without learning a complicated app?
+

Yes. The simplest option is a screen in their kitchen or living room that shows the front door and driveway all the time, with nothing to press. The screen prices are on our published list. If they prefer a phone or tablet, we set up an account that opens straight to the front-door and driveway views, with playback and settings hidden, while the owner keeps a full account. We walk them through it at handover until they are comfortable.

Should we register our cameras with the 68th Precinct?
+

That is your choice. NYC 311 explains that owners can register the locations of their cameras with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org so detectives know whom to ask for footage after an incident nearby. Registration is voluntary and does not give the police access to your recordings; you still decide whether to export and hand over a clip when someone asks. Owners on busy blocks often register because a clip of the street outside can help a neighbor.

Can the cameras be painted or chosen to match the trim on an original house?
+

We usually choose white or black housings to sit quietly against the trim and eaves, and on the original houses we tuck cameras under porch ceilings and deep eaves where they are barely visible from the street. We do not paint camera housings ourselves, because paint can block the infrared light and the ventilation and can void the manufacturer's coverage. Cable is taken through the wall directly behind each camera, so nothing runs down the face of the house.

What happens to the old dead cameras bolted to our house when you put in new ones?
+

We take them down, along with any old cable that is no longer needed, and deal with the holes. Where a new camera goes in the same spot, its base often covers the old anchors. Where a spot is not being reused, the anchors come out and the holes are filled and sealed so water does not get into the wall. On stucco or stone, a perfect cosmetic patch may need a mason, and the quote says so before any work starts.
