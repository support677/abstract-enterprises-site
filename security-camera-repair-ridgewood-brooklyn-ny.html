The Neighborhood

Why Ridgewood Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Ridgewood is a Queens neighborhood, part of Queens Community District 5, with ZIP code 11385, patrolled by the 104th Precinct from its station house at 64-02 Catalpa Avenue. It borders Bushwick and East Williamsburg in Brooklyn, and the name itself started on the Brooklyn side: the line between the two counties ran on a diagonal across the street grid until 1925, and until a new ZIP code took effect in January 1980 Ridgewood's mail came through a Brooklyn post office. We serve it from our Brooklyn office and price it from our Brooklyn list because the same buildings run straight across Wyckoff Avenue onto Bushwick blocks, and the camera problems do not change at the borough line. That is all the history this page needs. What matters for a camera repair is how these buildings were built, who lives in them and who looks after the cameras.

Most of Ridgewood went up in a single burst. After 1905, new buildings here had to be masonry, and most of the neighborhood's housing was built between about 1905 and 1915, so many blocks are rows of nearly identical brick buildings by the same few builders. The best known are the Mathews Model Flats. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2009 report on the Ridgewood North Historic District describes them as three-story buildings plus a basement on lots 27.5 feet wide, with two apartments on each floor, six in all, lit by light shafts between neighbors, and more than 300 of them were sold between 1909 and 1912. The report says the design made them "easier to manage by owner-occupants", and that is still how a great many of them work: the owner lives on one floor and is the landlord, the super and, when cameras go up, the person who has to keep them running.

That arrangement shapes every repair call we get from the neighborhood. A camera system on a six-family is bought by one person, paid for by one person and used by seven households counting the owner, but it is watched by nobody in particular. The recorder sits in the basement, usually reached through the door under the stoop that the LPC report lists among the original features of these buildings. The cameras cover the stoop, the double doors at the top of it, the door under it, the halls where two apartment doors open off every landing, and the yard at the back. When a hard drive quits or a camera goes dark, nothing in the building changes, and the owner finds out when a tenant asks for video.

Tenants do ask. In January 2024, QNS reported that police were looking for a man who they said broke the lock on the front door of a building complex on Cypress Avenue in the middle of a Monday afternoon and went through the packages inside. On the neighborhood's subreddit, residents have posted about parcels vanishing from stoops and from inside buildings after the courier's delivery photo. And in July 2026, QNS reported a string of June burglaries near 70th Street and Central Avenue in which apartments were entered through broken window screens and a home through a rear basement window after the burglar came in through a gate. The 104th Precinct had recorded 81 burglaries that year through July 5, against 85 at the same point a year earlier. Each is a moment when someone goes to the recorder expecting a clip.

Footage requests do not come only from crime. In January 2026, NY1 reported a five-alarm fire in a three-story building on Madison Street near Seneca and Onderdonk Avenues that started on the first floor, spread to the buildings beside it, collapsed a roof and left four buildings vacated and 26 households registered with the Red Cross. In rows of attached buildings, one basement recorder can hold the only video of the street or the yards from a night like that, and a system that quietly stopped recording answers nobody.

So a camera repair in Ridgewood has three parts. Get the system recording again. Make it report on itself, so the owner hears about the next failure on a phone and not from a tenant. And, on the blocks inside the city's historic districts, put every replacement part on the facade where the rules allow, so that a repair does not turn into a violation. Many owners pair the repair with a check of the door buzzer or video door station at the same double doors, because in these buildings the front entrance carries both.

Six-family flats: one recorder in the basement, six apartments upstairs

The Ridgewood North report describes the Mathews Model Flats in detail: three stories plus a basement, two apartments a floor, each originally a cold-water flat, on 27.5-foot lots, with heavy bluestone stoops, full stone surrounds at the entrance, glazed double doors, entrances under the stoop and areaways with cellar doors at the front. That description is also a map of a typical camera system. The recorder goes in the basement because that is where the installer found an outlet and where cables from the front and back could meet. The stoop camera goes over or beside the double doors. A second front camera, if there is one, watches the door under the stoop or the areaway. Inside, there is a camera on the ground-floor hall and sometimes one on each landing. At the back, one or two cameras watch the yard.

Because these buildings were cold-water flats when they were built, their heating, plumbing and electrical service came later and in pieces, and the camera system had to fit into whatever was there. In our experience that leaves recorders on shelves next to the boiler, sharing a power strip with a washing machine or a dehumidifier, and cameras powered by plug-in adapters in whichever hall or basement outlet was closest. When one of those outlets goes dead, a camera goes dark and the recorder carries on as if nothing happened. On a repair we trace each part of the system to the outlet and panel that feeds it, and where the owner's or the building's common circuit should be carrying it, we say so. We do not do electrical work ourselves, so if a new outlet is needed we tell you exactly what and where, and an electrician adds it before we move the equipment. Then the recorder goes up off the floor and away from the boiler, with a battery backup beside it.

Two-family and one-family houses: fewer people, the same basement

Deeper into the neighborhood, away from the Brooklyn border, the six-families give way to two-family and one-family houses: the houses of Paul Stier, who according to the Commission built more than 2,000 houses in what became the Central Ridgewood district, the Ring-Gibson houses, and rows of similar brick fronts. These systems are smaller, often four to eight cameras on a recorder in the basement or a closet, but they fail in the same ways. A drive wears out, a front camera takes in water through a connector that was never sealed, and the yard camera's picture fades at night as its infrared weakens with age.

Owners here have often tried a fix already, so we start by asking what changed since the system last worked, because that is often where the fault is.

Landmark rows: built alike, reviewed alike

In 2014 the Commission described Central Ridgewood as "one of the most harmonious and architecturally-distinguished enclaves of working-class dwellings", and that harmony is the point of the rules that apply to cameras there. Rows of identical fronts by the same builder are reviewed as rows. A camera bolted through the stone surround of one doorway stands out on a row where every other entrance is the same, so on these blocks the repair starts on the sidewalk, checking the old mounting point against the rules in the next section, before anyone climbs a ladder.

Storefront flats: the store's cameras and the building's cameras in one basement

The Ridgewood North report notes storefronts in the buildings along Fairview and Grandview Avenues and Forest Avenue, and the shopping streets, Myrtle Avenue above all, are lined with buildings that have a store at street level and flats above. In those buildings two camera systems usually meet in the same basement: the store's, which watches the counter, the door, the stockroom and the gate, and the building's, which watches the residents' door beside the store and the stairs. Sometimes they share a recorder because whoever put in the second system found the first one already there.

The repair starts by sorting out whose cameras are whose, who pays for which fix and who should be able to see what. Then we make the two systems independent where they need to be, so the store can change its internet or its recorder without blacking out the residents' door, and a landlord's work upstairs does not cut power to the store's cameras.

Older frame houses and later homes with garages

Ridgewood still has wood-frame houses from before the 1905 masonry rule, and toward Glendale and Middle Village there are later one-family homes with driveways and garages. Frame walls take a mount differently from brick, and their cables often run outside under the eaves, where sun cracks the jacket. Houses with garages add a camera at the end of a long run to the back of the lot, which is where most of their dark cameras turn out to be.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Working a Six-Family From the Basement Up

These notes describe the order our technicians follow on a typical Ridgewood six-family whose owner has just found out, usually from a tenant, that the cameras have not been saving. It is our working method, not a story about one address.

Start at the recorder, and save first. The technician goes down through the door under the stoop and finds the recorder before touching anything else. Whatever it still holds for the dates the owner or the tenant cares about is exported first, and the recorder's clock is noted against the real time.

Read the log before the ladder. The recorder's log of video loss, disk errors and power loss usually shows when the system really stopped, often weeks before anyone noticed.

Trace the power before blaming a camera. The technician follows each camera's power back to its adapter, power box or switch port, and each of those to its outlet. In buildings that were wired long after they were built, a dead hall outlet or a tripped basement circuit explains more dark cameras than failed cameras do.

Walk the building top to bottom, then the yard. At each landing, the camera is checked for picture, aim and recording, with both apartment doors and the stair in view. Outside, the stoop camera, the under-stoop camera and the yard cameras are tested for power and signal at the camera, and each connector and bracket is checked for water, rust and movement.

Price before parts, then make it report on itself. The owner hears the cause and gets a written price before anything is replaced. After the repair, every camera is checked in daylight and after dark, and the recorder is set to send the owner's phone an alert for video loss, disk errors and a full or missing drive. The owner leaves with the logins, a list of which camera is which and a short demonstration of exporting a clip.

Historic District Repairs

Repairing a Camera on a Landmark Front: Two Per 25 Feet, Mortar Joints, Ten Inches of Conduit and a Cut Sheet

Ridgewood has four city historic districts, and together they take in more than 1,300 buildings: Stockholm Street, designated in 2000, with 36 rowhouses on the neighborhood's only brick-paved street; Ridgewood North, designated in 2009, with 96 buildings; Ridgewood South, also called the Seneca–Onderdonk–Woodward district, designated in 2010, with 210 buildings by the Commission's count (the National Register lists 211 contributing buildings); and Central Ridgewood, designated in December 2014, with 990. If your building is on one of those blocks, the camera on its front is equipment on a landmark, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook has a chapter on exactly that. A repair has to fit inside it.

Repair and like-for-like replacement: the easy case

The guidebook says no permit is needed for "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment". In practice that covers most of what we do on a facade: cleaning a camera, resealing a connector, replacing a dead camera with the same kind of camera on the same mount, tightening a loose bracket. Where an in-kind replacement does need an application, the guidebook says it needs the manufacturer's cut sheets, the one-page product drawings with the camera's size and finish, rather than full elevation drawings. On every facade replacement in a historic district we leave the owner the cut sheet for the camera we fit, so the paperwork exists if it is ever asked for.

Moving or adding a camera: where the rules start to bite

A repair that means moving a camera to a new spot, or adding one, is different. The guidebook says "Security cameras must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance", and for small residential and commercial buildings it allows no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage. A Mathews flat sits on a 27.5-foot lot, so a front with a camera over the double doors and a second one watching the door under the stoop is already at the limit. If a repair visit turns into "while you are here, could we add one at the corner", we say so on the sidewalk, before anything is drilled.

What a camera may be fixed to

The same chapter says equipment "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork" and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces". On a Ridgewood front, the decorative surfaces are easy to spot: the full stone surround of the entrance the Ridgewood North report describes, carved lintels and cornices, and in Central Ridgewood the original details the Commission listed, from cut-glass and wood doors to iron fences. When an old camera was bolted straight through one of those, its replacement goes back on a mortar joint or plain brick nearby where the view allows, and the old holes are left for the owner's mason, because we do not patch or point masonry.

Cable on the front: about ten inches

The guidebook wants conduit concealed through plain masonry, and says staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit". That is a short distance on a three-story front. On many older Ridgewood systems the cable runs down the face of the building to the areaway or across the stone of the entrance in a plastic channel. When a repair needs that cable replaced, we look for the concealed route first: through the wall behind the camera into the vestibule or a front room, or down inside the stoop to the basement. If the only practical route is on the face of the building, we tell the owner before the work, so the choice is theirs and not a surprise.

Who applies, and what we never promise

The Commission's application pages say owners, or a team working for the owner, apply through its online system, and a commercial tenant applies with the building owner's permission and signature. We prepare the cut sheet and photos; the owner or the owner's architect files. We never promise approval, and where a permit is needed we do not install the moved or added camera until it is granted. The plain repair, the one that puts a working camera back where the old one was, does not have to wait.

Fault by Fault

Old Drives, Wet Areaways, Busy Halls and Borrowed Logins: Why Ridgewood Camera Systems Fail

Four causes, on top of the power and shared-basement problems described above, account for most of the camera failures we find on Ridgewood buildings. None of them announces itself, and each is tied to the way these buildings are built and used. Knowing which one you have is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Drives that have recorded every hour for years

A recorder in a six-family usually records around the clock, because the owner wants the whole day on the stoop and in the halls. Its hard drive does nothing but write, year after year, and it is the part that wears out first. A failing drive can simply stop saving while the live picture carries on. The repair is a drive made for continuous recording, sized for the number of days the building needs, with anything that matters copied off the old one first.

Areaways and under-stoop doors: water at the front of the building

The areaway in front of a Ridgewood six-family is a small sunken space with the cellar doors in it, and the door under the stoop opens onto the same low ground. Rain runs into both, and in winter snow and salt end up there too. A camera mounted low there, or a cable that enters the building through the areaway wall, lives in that water. The faults we find are corroded connectors, a picture that drops out after every storm and cameras that fog from the inside. The fix is a sealed connection above the splash line, a drip loop so water runs off the cable before it reaches the wall, and where the facade rules allow, a camera moved up and out of the wet.

Halls where everybody passes: cameras knocked off their aim

In a building with two apartments on each floor, the hall camera watches a short corridor that six households, their visitors and every courier walk through, and it is often mounted low enough to be bumped. Moving day is when most of them get knocked, by a mattress, a dresser or a ladder. A camera turned an inch shows half of one door and nobody notices until a clip is needed. We re-aim each hall camera to show both doors and the stair and fix it so it cannot drift.

Logins that belong to someone who left

Small owner-run buildings collect people with access: the tenant who helped set up the app, the relative who installed the recorder, the contractor who never handed over the password. When that person moves on, the owner is locked out and alerts go to a phone nobody watches. The repair is to recover the recorder for the owner, change the default and shared passwords, remove old phones from the account and give limited, view-only logins to anyone else the owner chooses.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or service security systems, and its rules cover closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. An owner can work on their own system, or have an employee do it, without a license; a company working on someone else's property needs one. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch staff. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get an acknowledgment. If a repair adds or moves a camera that sees a counter, a kitchen or a back office where staff work, update the notice.

Footage requests. Detectives from the 104th Precinct may ask for a copy of a clip, and a copy on USB with the original timestamps usually answers that without the recorder leaving the store. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Ridgewood: Questions Answered

These are situations Ridgewood owners, tenants, shopkeepers and managers describe when they call. Each answer is written for the buildings here.

Six-family flats with the owner in the building

The previous owner of our six-family put a camera in the light shaft between our building and the next one. It has been dead for years. Should it be repaired or taken down?

Look at what it sees before deciding. A light shaft exists to bring light and air to the side windows of the apartments, so a camera in one usually looks straight into kitchens and bedrooms, in your building and the one next door, and there is rarely anything in a shaft worth recording. In most cases we recommend taking it down rather than repairing it, and putting the money into the cameras that cover the doors. If it does watch something real, we repair it with privacy masks over every window in its view.

Our recorder sits just inside the door under the stoop, which is also the door a burglar would most likely use. If someone gets in, can they take the video with them?

Yes, and it happens: a recorder on an open shelf by the door is easy to unplug and carry out. The repair is to move it deeper into the basement, into a locked cabinet or a closed space with air around it, so that anyone coming in through that door is recorded long before they reach it. We can also set the recorder to send a copy of key clips, or a still picture of anyone at the door at night, to the owner's phone as it happens, so the evidence leaves the building even if the box does not.

A small monitor inside our glazed front doors shows every camera in the building, including the one outside my own apartment. Can it show only the street, or should it go?

It can be limited. Most recorders let you choose which cameras appear on each screen, so the vestibule monitor can show only the stoop and the sidewalk, which tells a visitor they are on camera without showing every hall and the owner's door to anyone who walks in. We change the layout, make it return to that view after a power cut and lock the menu; or, if you prefer, the screen comes down and recording carries on.

Rainwater pools in our front areaway, and the camera mounted low beside the cellar doors has died twice in three years. Is that spot the problem?

Almost certainly. A camera a foot or two above an areaway floor is splashed by every storm, sits in the cold air that collects in the well, and takes the salt that ends up there in winter, and its connector is usually the first thing to go. We move it higher on the areaway wall or onto the underside of the stoop where the view allows, seal the connection above the splash line and loop the cable so water drips off before reaching the wall. On a historic district block the new spot has to be plain masonry or a mortar joint, which we check before drilling.

Landmark rows and the front of the building

Landmarks staff asked us for a cut sheet for our replacement camera. What is that, and who provides it?

A cut sheet is the maker's one-page product sheet for the camera: a drawing with its dimensions, its finish and how it mounts. The Commission's guidebook says applications for in-kind replacement of a camera need manufacturer cut sheets rather than full elevation drawings. We provide it for the camera we propose, along with photos of the existing mount and where the new one will go, and you or your architect send it with the application. If the job is a plain repair of the existing camera, no application is usually needed at all.

The cable from our stoop camera runs across the stone surround of the entrance in a plastic channel. When the camera is repaired, does the cable have to move?

Not for a plain repair of the camera. If the cable itself has to be replaced, it is worth moving, because the guidebook wants conduit concealed and allows only about ten inches of it in view, and the stone surround is the kind of decorative surface equipment should not cross. The usual new route is through the wall right behind the camera into the vestibule, or down inside the stoop to the basement. We tell you which route the building allows and what it costs before we start.

Every house on our row has the same small camera in the same spot under the cornice, put up by one installer years ago. Ours has died. Does the replacement have to match the neighbors'?

It does not have to be the same model, but on a row reviewed as a whole it should look the same: similar size, the same color, on the same mounting point. That is what keeps a replacement inside ordinary maintenance rather than a change that stands out. We bring a camera of matching size and finish, use the existing mount where it is sound and leave the cut sheet with you.

Shops and storefront buildings

After a shoplifting incident last year someone locked a set of clips on our Myrtle Avenue store's recorder, and now it seems to keep only a few days of new video. Could the locked clips be the reason?

They can be. On many recorders a locked or protected clip is never recorded over, so it sits on the drive permanently and takes space that would otherwise hold new video. Lock enough of them, or lock whole days, and the recorder has only a few days of room left. We find the locked files, export anything you still need to a USB drive, unlock or delete the rest with your say-so, and then check how many days the drive holds. If it is still short, the drive or the recording settings change next.

Trucks back up to the loading door of our workshop near Metropolitan Avenue, and the camera above the door has been hit twice this year. Where can it go so it still sees the door?

Not straight above the opening, where a box truck's roof or a raised tailgate reaches. The usual repair is to move the camera to the wall beside the door, a few feet higher than the tallest truck that uses it and angled back across the opening, or onto the ceiling just inside the door looking out, so it sees who comes and goes without sitting in the path. We replace the damaged camera, run the new cable along a protected route rather than down the door frame, and check that a person standing at the truck's tail is still big enough in the picture to recognize.

When you need the video

The courier's delivery photo shows a tenant's parcel in our hall, but our hall camera recorded the courier leaving and then nothing until the tenant came home. Did the camera miss the theft?

Possibly, and there are three likely reasons. The camera may record only on motion with a sensitivity set too low to catch someone walking in quickly and bending down; it may be aimed so the spot where parcels are left is at the edge of the picture or out of it; or its clock may be off, so the minutes you are looking at are not the minutes on the courier's photo. We check all three against the photo's time, then set the hall camera so the parcel spot sits mid-picture and motion there is always saved.

Our yard camera recorded someone climbing the back gate, but the clip ends before he reaches the basement window. Why does it stop?

Motion recordings are saved in clips with a set length after the movement that started them, and if that length is short, or the person stops moving for a moment, the clip ends. A camera with its motion zone drawn only over the gate stops caring once the person is past it. We lengthen the time saved after motion, add a few seconds before it, extend the zone along the path to the basement windows, and if the night picture is too dark near the building, check the infrared.

Our building has a hyphenated Queens house number, but the recorder's screen and every exported clip still carry the Brooklyn-style address the old installer typed in. Does that matter when we hand a clip to the police?

The address label does not change what the video shows, but it can cause confusion, and on the Brooklyn–Queens line confusion costs time. Until 1980 Ridgewood's mail went through a Brooklyn post office, so an installer working from old paperwork can easily type the wrong address, and a clip labeled with the wrong borough or without the hyphen can end up attached to the wrong report or a building across the street. On a repair we set the recorder's name to the building's full current address, name every camera by what it watches, such as "Stoop", "Under-stoop door" or "2nd floor hall", and correct the clock, so the label, the camera name and the time on every exported clip all agree.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we find on Ridgewood six-families, houses, storefront buildings and workshops, and the faults each one tends to develop here.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC plugs, from old analog to HD models, still common on six-families wired more than a decade ago. Typical faults: corroded connectors where the cable enters the brick or the areaway, failed plug-in adapters and infrared that has dimmed with age.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected over one data cable, standard on newer systems and in many shops. Typical faults: dead switch ports after an outage, address conflicts after a router change and default passwords never changed.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the footage, in the basement, a vestibule closet or a store's back room. Typical faults: worn drives, full or locked disks, clogged fans near a boiler or an oven, and settings lost after a power cut.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with an adjustable zoom, common on landings, at counters and over loading doors. Typical faults: a lens set too wide to recognize anyone, focus that drifted after a knock, and covers scratched or clouded.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving cameras in shops and halls, and fisheye cameras on store ceilings. Typical faults: lost presets, sticking motors and tours that leave the door unwatched.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Plug-in and battery cameras in windows, on hall shelves and in yards. Typical faults: weak signal through brick party walls, router changes, cold-weather battery drain and accounts tied to a tenant who has moved.

Video door stations

Door stations with their own camera beside a six-family's double doors or a shop's side door. Typical faults: video that reaches one phone but not another, failed call buttons and pictures never saved to any recorder.

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, often on a basement shelf without backup. Typical faults: dead ports after an outage, overloaded power budgets and extenders on long runs to a yard or a garage.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, adapters in dead hall outlets and backups whose batteries quietly died years ago.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Ridgewood

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, help forums and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere, and each points Ridgewood owners toward the wrong repair, a lost clip or a problem with the facade.

"Any work on a camera on a landmarked building needs a permit first"

Answers about historic districts often treat every change to a protected front as something that needs the Commission's approval before anyone touches it.

The Commission's own Permit Guidebook says otherwise for repairs. It lists "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment" as work that needs no permit, and for in-kind replacement it asks for the maker's cut sheets rather than full drawings. On Ridgewood's four districts that covers most camera repairs.

Where the rules do apply is in moving a camera, adding one or running new cable on the face of the building. We tell you which kind of job yours is before the visit, so you neither wait for a permit you do not need nor skip one you do.

"It's your building, so you can put up as many cameras as you want"

General advice on home security tends to treat the front of a house as the owner's to cover as fully as they like.

On a historic district block, the guidebook allows small buildings no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage and asks that they be "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance". A Mathews flat on a 27.5-foot lot therefore has room for two front cameras, and many already have two.

That is why a good Ridgewood repair often improves the two cameras a front already has, with better aim, a varifocal lens or a stronger night picture, rather than adding a third. The limit is about the street front; cameras inside the vestibule and the halls are not on the facade at all.

"Landlords have to keep building cameras working and show tenants the footage"

Some answers put cameras in the same list as heat, hot water and intercoms, as things a tenant can make a landlord provide.

Brick Underground has reported, citing a New York real estate attorney, that city buildings are not required to have functioning cameras, and that landlords generally do not have to show tenants footage outside a legal process. Another attorney quoted in the same article, Catherine Grad, said of reviewing footage when thefts keep happening: "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord." The state's intercom rule, for its part, applies to buildings with eight or more apartments, which leaves a six-family below the line.

For a Ridgewood owner living in the building, the practical answer is that a working camera system is a choice, and a good one: it answers a tenant's question in five minutes instead of starting an argument. For tenants, it means asking the owner in writing.

"If you can see the live picture, it's recording"

Quick troubleshooting answers often stop once the cameras show a picture on the screen or the phone.

Live view and recording are separate jobs. A recorder with a failing drive, a drive set to stop when full, a recording schedule switched off, or a drive that was never set up after a replacement will show every camera live and save nothing.

Check playback, not live view. On every repair we play back yesterday from each camera in front of the owner, and set the recorder to send an alert when it stops saving.

"'Disk full' means you need a bigger hard drive"

Answers about storage errors usually go straight to buying more capacity.

A full drive is often a setting, not a size. Recorders can be set to stop when the drive is full instead of recording over the oldest video, and clips that have been locked or protected after an incident are never recorded over, so a few days of locked footage from a past theft can quietly eat the space. A bigger drive in those cases fills up the same way.

We check the overwrite setting, export and clear old locked clips with the owner's permission and work out how many days the drive holds at the cameras' current settings. Only then, if the building needs more days, does the drive change.

"With a cloud doorbell, the police can pull your video whenever they want"

Some answers describe doorbell and cloud cameras as an open door for police requests.

Ring ended its in-app "Request for Assistance" tool in January 2024, and News 12 reported at the time that "Law enforcement agencies can still access videos using a search warrant." A recorder in your basement holds its video locally, and a copy leaves the building when you export it. Registering a business's cameras with the NYPD tells police where they are; it does not give them access.

In practice, a detective from the 104th Precinct asks, and the owner decides. Our job on a repair is to make sure there is a clean clip with a correct timestamp to hand over if you choose to.

"A front camera covers the house"

Simple camera plans often start and end at the front door, on the idea that anyone getting in has to come through it.

The June 2026 burglaries QNS reported near 70th Street and Central Avenue involved broken window screens on first- and second-floor apartments and a rear basement window reached through a gate. In a row of attached buildings, the back is where nobody on the street can see.

So on a Ridgewood repair we check the back as carefully as the front: the yard camera's night picture, its motion zone along the path to the basement windows, and how long it keeps recording once someone is past the gate.
