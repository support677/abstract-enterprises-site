The Neighborhood

Why New Utrecht Properties Need Alarm Installation

New Utrecht is the oldest name in this corner of Brooklyn. The Dutch town laid out in 1652 once covered Bay Ridge, Bensonhurst, Bath Beach, Dyker Heights, Borough Park and Fort Hamilton, and its center sat on 84th Street between 16th and 18th Avenues, where the 1828 Reformed Church and the 1654 burial ground still stand. Today there is no official boundary, and most real estate listings on the avenue say Bensonhurst. When people tell us they live or run a business in New Utrecht, they almost always mean one of two things: the blocks around that old town center, or the long diagonal of New Utrecht Avenue itself, with the West End line running overhead on steel columns since 1916. That is the area this page covers, roughly from 60th Street down to 86th Street.

Those two halves ask different things of an alarm. Along the avenue, the typical building is a two-story brick row of storefronts with one or two apartments over each store, reached by a narrow street door squeezed between two shop fronts. The roofs run level from building to building, the cellars sit under the sidewalk, and the trains pass a few feet from the upstairs windows every few minutes. On the side streets and around the old town center, the buildings are mostly attached and semi-attached brick two-families, small walk-ups, and the row houses on 17th Avenue between 81st and 84th Streets with their oriel windows, stoops and ornate railings. Many of those houses have an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, a yard at the back, and a cellar with its own door.

What ties the neighborhood together, from an alarm installer’s point of view, is noise. Researchers who measured the subway system for the American Journal of Public Health in 2009 found average sound levels of 81.1 decibels on platforms, and anyone who has stood under the el at 71st Street when a train goes over knows the street below is not much quieter. Breaking glass, a forced gate or a siren inside a closed shop can disappear into that sound. A burglar alarm that only makes noise has less chance of being noticed here than almost anywhere in the borough. The part of an alarm that matters most in New Utrecht is the part nobody hears: the signal that reaches the owner’s phone and a monitoring station within seconds, over a cellular path that does not depend on the store’s router.

The second thread is the way burglars have actually gone in nearby. In the series of break-ins the Brooklyn District Attorney charged in 2023, a grocery store on New Utrecht Avenue lost a security box holding about $10,000 on October 6, 2022, and a grocery on 17th Avenue lost a safe with about $15,000 a month earlier. District Attorney Eric Gonzalez called the defendants “allegedly professional burglars whose crime spree violated the sanctity of local homeowners,” because the same case also covered private homes on 76th Street and in Gravesend. In September 2025, News 12 reported that a burglar entered the gym on 86th Street in Bensonhurst through the roof just before 10 p.m., took cash from the register and left the way he came, and that a business on 86th Street in Bay Ridge was broken into a few hours later. Cash rooms and roofs are where we start a commercial survey on this avenue, and the reasons are in those reports.

None of this calls for panic, and the numbers say so. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 62nd Precinct, which patrols Bensonhurst and the avenue south of about 60th Street, shows 102 burglaries in 2026 through November 1, against 141 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 27.7 percent, and burglary down 92.5 percent over 33 years. Robbery is up 8.5 percent for the year, from 106 to 115. Grand larceny, which counts thefts without a break-in, is flat this year at 488 but 35.9 percent higher than 16 years ago. In plain terms: forced entry is much rarer than it was, and the losses that remain are concentrated where cash and stock sit and where a building can be entered without anyone seeing. That is what a New Utrecht alarm should be designed around, and it is usually a smaller system than people expect.

Need Repair in New Utrecht?

A Store Alarm That Rings Into the Train Noise, a Keypad Stuck on “Trouble” or a Panel Still Dialing a Phone Line: Alarm Repair in New Utrecht

A good share of the alarm calls we get from this part of Brooklyn are not about new systems at all. Someone buys a two-family on a side street off 18th Avenue and finds a keypad by the door that nobody has a code for. A store owner on the avenue learns, after a break-in, that the panel in the back room had been trying to reach a monitoring company over a copper phone line that was cut years ago. A tenant upstairs complains that the siren went off at two in the morning and kept going until a neighbor called the landlord. Each of these is fixable, usually in one visit.

Many older panels in the neighborhood were installed with a cellular radio on a network that no longer exists; Verizon’s CDMA network, which many alarm communicators used, was shut down by the end of 2022, and an alarm on it “may be unable to communicate in the event of an emergency,” as the alarm retailer Alarm Grid warned at the time. A panel whose wiring tests sound can usually keep its wiring and its sensors with a new communicator or a new panel on the existing wires, which is the panel-replacement line on our price list rather than a whole new system. Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the name printed on the keypad and what the screen says, and send a photo if you can. If a siren will not stop, we will talk you through silencing it over the phone first, because in a row of attached buildings it is waking more than one household. Service visits outside warranty are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), whether the equipment is ours or another company’s.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for New Utrecht’s Architecture

Most buildings in New Utrecht are old, attached and small, and very few of them are owned and used by one person. A store owner rents from a landlord who lives in Staten Island; a family owns a two-family and rents the top floor; a church owns a parish house next to the sanctuary. The alarm plan depends far more on who uses which part of the building, and on what it shares with the building next door, than on square footage. These six building types cover nearly every request we get from the neighborhood.

The two-story shop row

Brick storefronts on New Utrecht Avenue and 86th Street with one or two apartments over each store, a narrow residents’ door between shop fronts, a cellar under the sidewalk, a flat roof joined to the neighbors’ and the el a few feet from the upstairs windows.

The attached brick two-family

The most common house on the side streets: two floors of apartments over a cellar, a stoop or a few steps at the front, a yard at the back, an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, and party walls on both sides.

The 17th Avenue oriel row

Attached brick dwellings between 81st and 84th Streets with projecting oriel windows over the stoops, ornate railings and a door under the stoop, many still one-family or owner-and-tenant houses.

The corner building on the diagonal

Because New Utrecht Avenue cuts across the street grid, some corners hold wedge-shaped or oddly angled buildings with three street fronts, a store at the point and doors on more than one street.

The early apartment house

Walk-up and elevator apartment buildings from the early twentieth century, such as the ones Forgotten New York describes at 17th Avenue and 78th Street, built for middle-class tenants and still mostly rentals or co-ops.

The church, school and parish house

Congregations, schools and community groups around the old town center and along 18th Avenue, with offices, collection rooms and storage in buildings that are busy a few days a week and empty the rest.

The two-story shop row: one building, three alarms that must not trip each other

A two-story building on the avenue is often three separate premises stacked together. The store has a tenant who opens at seven and closes at ten, the upstairs apartment has a family on a different schedule, and the owner may be somewhere else entirely, responsible for the roof, the cellar and the street door to the apartments. If one panel protects the whole building as a single area, someone will eventually disarm it for everyone or set it off for everyone, and within a month the alarm is left off. So the first decision on these buildings is how many areas the alarm needs and who controls each one.

The arrangement we use most often is one panel with two or three areas. The store’s area covers the gate, the shop door, the glass behind the gate, the back door and the stockroom, and it is armed and disarmed by the store’s staff with their own codes. The apartment’s area covers the apartment door and its windows, especially any rear window over a one-story extension, and it belongs to the residents. A shared area covers the roof hatch, the cellar door and, where the owner wants it, the residents’ street door, and it is set up so that it can be armed by whichever party leaves last at night. A tenant who forgets their code cannot disarm the store, and a store employee cannot see into the apartment’s history.

The attached brick two-family: two households, one cellar

The owner-and-tenant two-family is the bread and butter of residential alarm work here. The front door and stoop are shared or split, the cellar is shared for the boiler, the meters and storage, and the yard door is often in the owner’s apartment or the cellar. Burglaries in houses like these nearby, including the 76th Street and 72nd Street homes in the Brooklyn District Attorney’s cases, involved cash and jewelry taken from inside, so the plan is both perimeter and inside: contacts on every door and on the windows someone can reach from the yard, the stoop or a lower roof, and a motion detector placed on the path between the entry and the bedrooms.

The household split works the same way as in a shop row. Each apartment is its own area, with its own keypad or fob, its own codes and its own phone app. The cellar and the yard door are a shared area that either household can arm when leaving, and the call list for a shared-area alarm goes to both households, then to the owner if the owner lives elsewhere.

The 17th Avenue oriel row: the window over the stoop

The row houses on 17th Avenue between 81st and 84th Streets are some of the most handsome buildings in the neighborhood, and they come with two openings people tend to forget. The first is the door under the stoop, which leads into the ground floor or the cellar and is out of sight from most of the street once someone is down the steps. The second is the oriel itself: a bay window projecting over the front of the house, often just above the stoop and its railings. Whether an oriel can be reached depends on the house, the railing and the height, and we look at each one rather than assume; where it can be reached, its sash gets contacts like any other window.

The corner building on the diagonal avenue

New Utrecht Avenue runs at an angle to the streets around it, so its intersections are irregular, and NYC DOT has described the meeting of New Utrecht and 17th Avenues near 79th Street as a “bowtie” with more than thirty traffic movements. The buildings on those corners are often irregular too. A store at the point of a wedge may have display windows on two streets and a door on a third, and the apartments above may have windows on three sides. That means more openings at street level than an ordinary storefront, and windows that face busy corners on one side and quiet side streets on the other.

The early apartment house and the parish building

In the walk-up and elevator buildings around 17th Avenue and 78th Street, and in the apartment houses along the side streets, a resident’s alarm protects one apartment: the apartment door, any window on a fire escape or reachable from a yard or a lower roof, and a motion detector in the entry hall. The building’s doors, halls and systems belong to the landlord or the co-op, so everything we install for a tenant is wireless, mounted inside the apartment, and removable without damage. For churches, schools and community groups, the pattern is the reverse: the building is large, used by many people a few days a week, and the valuables are concentrated in an office, a collection room or a storage room, which become their own areas with their own codes.

Roofs and Ceilings

The Way In Is Often Overhead: Planning a New Utrecht Alarm Around Joined Roofs, Hatches and Shared Ceilings

Most alarm advice starts at the front door. In a row of two-story buildings under the el, the front door is the hardest way in. It faces the avenue, it has a gate in front of it at night, and it sits under the lights of the station and the traffic. The roof faces nobody. On most of these rows the roofs are flat, joined from one end of the block to the other, and reached by a hatch, a bulkhead door or a fire escape at the back. Anyone who gets onto one of them can walk to yours. The September 2025 break-in at the gym on 86th Street, where the burglar came in through the roof just before 10 p.m. and left the same way, is the local reminder, and ABC7 has reported burglars cutting through the roof of a Brooklyn bodega as well.

So the survey on a shop row goes up before it goes in. We look at every opening on the roof: the hatch or bulkhead, any skylight, the exhaust fan or duct over a kitchen, old vents, and any place where the roof surface has been patched over a former opening. Each one gets a decision. A hatch or bulkhead door gets a contact. A skylight that cannot be opened gets a glass-break detector or, more often, a motion detector below it, because someone who breaks a skylight still has to drop into the room. A fan or duct big enough for a person gets a shock sensor or a motion detector on the space under it.

The ceiling is the second layer. Many stores along the avenue have a drop ceiling hung well below the roof deck, and the space above it is often connected to the space above the next store, as the May 2022 fire showed when it spread through the ceilings of two adjoining buildings. Someone who comes in from the roof of a neighboring building, or through that building’s ceiling, can reach yours without touching your roof at all. The answer is not a sensor on every tile. It is a motion detector in the store placed high and aimed so that the ceiling plane and the space below it are in its view, plus a shock sensor on the top of a shared wall where it meets the roof if that wall is thin. Anyone coming down through the ceiling crosses the detector’s view before they reach the floor.

There is a timing point that matters on this avenue in particular. The 86th Street roof entry happened before ten at night, an hour when many stores on New Utrecht Avenue and 86th Street are still open. A store alarm that only arms at closing cannot protect the roof while the shop is busy. On the stores where it makes sense, we put the roof hatch, the skylight motion detector and the stockroom on an area of their own that stays armed whenever the roof should be closed, which for most shops is all day. The front of the store stays disarmed for customers, and the roof reports the moment it opens.

Under the El

Nobody Hears It Over the Next Train: Why an Alarm Under the West End Line Has to Report, Not Just Ring

On most Brooklyn streets, a siren going off inside a closed store at three in the morning will get someone to a window. Under the el on New Utrecht Avenue, it may not. Trains pass overhead through the night, the steel structure carries the noise along the block, and residents who live with it learn to tune it out. The AJPH researchers who measured subway noise in 2009 recorded average levels of 81.1 decibels on platforms. A siren inside a closed shop is meant to be unbearable to stand next to, but on the street outside, with a train passing, it is just one more noise.

That is why we put the budget of a New Utrecht store alarm into reporting first. The panel has a built-in cellular communicator, so the signal goes out over the cellular network the instant the first sensor trips, without relying on a phone line, the store’s internet or its router, which usually sits in the same shop a burglar is standing in. It reaches the owner’s phone at once, and, if the store has central station monitoring, the monitoring station too. Vincent Ingrisano, an alarm company sales manager, put the reason simply to Brick Underground: “you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.” A siren is still part of the system, set to stop on its own well within the fifteen minutes city law allows, but it is not what brings help here.

The steel overhead raises a second question, which is whether a cellular communicator will work under it at all. In our experience it usually does, but signal under and beside an elevated structure can be uneven from one end of a store to the other, and the back room where people want the panel is often the weakest spot. We measure signal strength at the planned panel location before mounting anything. If it is weak, we move the panel to a better spot, use a communicator on a different carrier, or add a second path over the store’s internet as a backup. We will not leave a panel where it reports only some of the time.

The MTA’s work on the structure adds one more practical point. Boro Park 24 reported in August 2025 that repairs to the D line’s elevated structure at the New Utrecht Avenue station, including steel repairs, lead paint removal, concrete base replacement and sidewalk restoration, are scheduled to continue through 2028, with sidewalk closures and no-parking zones in place for years. Work crews, barriers and changed sidewalks mean the route a responder takes to your door may change from month to month. We write the monitoring account’s dispatch notes to describe your entrance by what does not change, such as the store name, the color of the gate and the door’s position between two neighbors, and we update the notes when you tell us the sidewalk in front of your building has been closed or rebuilt.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in New Utrecht

The commercial side of New Utrecht is a chain of small businesses along a few long streets, almost all of them in older two- and three-story buildings with apartments overhead. Groceries, bakeries, pharmacies, phone and electronics stores, salons, bridal and formalwear shops, restaurants, catering halls, insurance and tax offices and medical practices all share the same basic building: a gate, a glass front, a back door, a cellar and a roof. These six settings cover most of the businesses we hear from.

New Utrecht Avenue at the 62nd Street station

The busiest stretch of the avenue, around the transfer between the West End el and the Sea Beach line in its open cut. Groceries, food shops and services that open early and close late, under the el, with heavy foot traffic at rush hour and empty sidewalks in the small hours.

New Utrecht Avenue in the 70s

From Lt. Joseph Petrosino Park at 70th Street down toward the 79th Street station, including the blocks near 76th and 77th Streets that Forgotten New York calls “Wedding Row” for their bridal and formalwear shops, with catering halls nearby according to its readers. Expensive stock on display and halls that sit empty for days between events.

18th Avenue near the old town center

The shopping street that crosses the avenue and runs past the New Utrecht Reformed Church between 83rd and 84th Streets, with family-run stores, cafés and offices in attached buildings, most of them with apartments upstairs.

86th Street under the el

Where the West End line turns onto 86th Street, a long commercial strip of stores, restaurants and services under the structure. The September 2025 roof burglary of a gym here and a door break-in on the Bay Ridge end of 86th Street the same night are the most recent reports we know of.

17th Avenue

A mix of corner groceries, small shops and offices at the ends of the residential blocks. The 2023 indictment included a grocery on 17th Avenue in Bensonhurst that lost a safe with about $15,000 in September 2022.

Churches, schools and offices

The Reformed Church and its parish house, New Utrecht High School at 16th Avenue and 80th Street, and the doctors’, lawyers’ and accountants’ offices on upper floors along the avenues, where the alarm protects files, equipment and records rather than stock.

Groceries and delis: protect the cash where it waits

The two grocery burglaries in the 2023 indictment tell a store owner exactly where the money was: a security box holding about $10,000 at the store on New Utrecht Avenue, and a safe with about $15,000 at the store on 17th Avenue, plus the petty cash in each register. Neither report described how the burglars got in, and we will not guess. What they show is that the target was the place where a day’s or a week’s cash waits for the bank, and that is where we put the alarm’s attention inside the store.

In practice, the cash room or office door gets its own contact and its own zone, the safe or cash box gets a shock sensor that reports if someone tries to pry, drill or move it, and a motion detector covers the office from inside. The counter gets a hold-up button for staff during opening hours, which sends a silent signal to the monitoring station rather than a siren. The rest of the store is covered the usual way: a contact on the gate, glass-break detection behind the front windows, a contact on the back door and the cellar door or hatch, and the roof openings. The office zone can be set to report even while the store is open, so a door that should stay shut during the day tells you when it is opened.

Small, valuable stock: phone stores, vape shops and pharmacies

A vape shop on 18th Avenue lost about $38,211 in merchandise in November 2022, according to the same indictment. Stock like that, along with phones, accessories, fragrances and medicines, is small, light and quick to bag, so the time between the first sensor and the burglar leaving can be very short. The alarm cannot stretch that time much, but it can make sure the first sensor trips as early as possible, at the gate or the roof rather than at the display case, and that the report reaches someone in seconds.

Pharmacies have an extra concern. The December 2024 break-in at a Dyker Heights pharmacy on 13th Avenue, where two men forced the door with a crowbar just before 6 a.m. and took narcotics, was part of a citywide pattern, News 12 reported. A 13th Avenue florist told the station his shop now closes at 5:30 instead of 8 “because that’s when they start.” For a pharmacy, the locked drug cabinet or room becomes its own area with codes limited to the pharmacist, and the alarm stays armed until the pharmacist arrives, not until the first clerk does.

Bridal shops and catering halls: empty for days, then full

The bridal shops and catering halls along the stretch of the avenue nicknamed Wedding Row work on a different rhythm from a grocery. A formalwear or bridal store keeps expensive garments on display and in fitting rooms, and a catering hall may be dark Monday to Thursday and packed with guests on a Saturday night. Both are empty for long stretches in which nobody would notice a problem for days. For them, the alarm’s opening and closing reports matter as much as its sensors: the owner sees the first disarm and the last arm every day, and a text arrives if the building has not been armed by a set hour on a night it should be closed.

Compliance notes for New Utrecht stores, homes and two-families

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes of going off. In a row of attached buildings with families upstairs, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, with 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431, and any installer’s license can be checked with the Department of State.
  • Rented two-families need the right detectors. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in rented one- and two-family homes with gas or fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Monitored detectors on an alarm panel can meet that requirement while also reporting to the owner.
  • Exits stay exits. Nothing we install may lock, slow or block a required exit, a roof door used as a way out, or a fire escape window. Contacts report open and closed; they do not lock anything.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a home or a store, but it does not replace a building fire alarm system that a catering hall, school or larger building may need, and it is never tied into one without the building owner’s fire alarm contractor. AESS does not service kitchen hood fire suppression systems.
  • Unnecessary alarms can cost money. Habitat magazine reported in 2012 that the Environmental Control Board upheld a $2,500 fine against an owner for failing to maintain a fire alarm system “by not preventing unnecessary alarms,” one of them caused by construction work. Placing smoke detectors away from kitchens and using test mode during work keeps a monitored panel off that list.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in New Utrecht: Questions Answered

These are the questions store owners, two-family owners and renters along New Utrecht Avenue and the old town center bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. General questions about cost, timing and monitoring are in the FAQ further down.

Shop rows under the el

Above our drop ceiling there is an open space that runs over the partition into the store next door. If someone got into their store, could they reach ours without our alarm noticing?

They could reach the space above your ceiling, but they should not be able to reach your floor unnoticed. The May 2022 fire on New Utrecht Avenue, which spread through the ceilings of two adjoining buildings, shows that these spaces really do connect in many rows. We mount a motion detector high in your store and aim it so the ceiling plane is in its view, which means anyone dropping through a tile is detected before reaching the floor. If the partition between the two ceiling spaces is thin, a shock sensor on it adds an earlier warning. A survey of your ceiling with a ladder and a flashlight tells us which of these you need.

We own the building and live upstairs, and the only stair down from our apartment ends in the back of our store. How do we come and go at night without setting off the store alarm?

The stair becomes its own small area, separate from the store. Your apartment and the stair are one area, armed and disarmed by the household; the store is another, armed by whoever closes. The door at the foot of the stair, where it opens into the stockroom, gets a contact that belongs to the store’s area, and the motion detectors in the store are placed so they do not see the stair. When you come down at night you disarm only the store from a keypad or your phone before opening that door. If you forget, the panel gives you an entry delay to enter the code, not an instant siren.

The store under our apartment has been empty for months since the last tenant left. We own the building. What should the alarm do about a vacant storefront below our home?

Treat the empty store as part of your home for now. An empty storefront with paper on the windows tells everyone nobody is inside, and its back door, cellar and roof hatch connect to the rest of your building. We put contacts on the store’s doors and any roof opening, and one motion detector inside, all on a separate area of your panel that stays armed around the clock until a new tenant moves in. When the space is leased, that area can be handed over with its own codes, or the new tenant can take the sensors over on their own system.

Groceries, halls and offices

Our grocery’s safe is bolted down in the cellar, two floors below where the cashiers work. If someone gets to it during business hours, who would know?

Nobody, unless the safe and the cellar are on a part of the alarm that stays armed while the store is open. We put the cellar door and a shock sensor on the safe in their own area, armed all day except when a manager goes down with a code. If that area trips during business hours, the panel sends a silent alert to the owner’s phone and, with monitoring, to the station, without sounding a siren in a store full of customers. The two grocery burglaries in the 2023 indictment were about a safe and a security box, so in a store like yours the cellar is the first place we protect.

We rent our catering hall to families who bring their own decorators, florists and DJs, and those vendors are often the last ones out after a party. Who should arm the building?

Your own manager, not a vendor. Vendors get temporary codes that open only the service door and the event floor, valid only on the day and hours of the event, and they cannot arm or disarm the office, the kitchen or the liquor room. At the end of the night your manager walks the building and arms everything with a code that is theirs alone. If nobody has armed the hall by a set hour, the panel texts you, and the history shows exactly which temporary codes were used that day and when.

Two-families and row houses

Our house on 17th Avenue has an oriel window that projects right over the stoop and its railing. Is it really something a burglar could reach?

Sometimes. It depends on the height of the oriel’s sill above the top step, the strength and shape of the railing, and whether anything nearby, such as a porch light or a neighbor’s railing, makes a foothold. On some houses the oriel is well out of reach; on others someone standing on the railing can reach the sash. We look at each one in person. If yours can be reached, the sashes that open get contacts, and the oriel room is covered by the motion detector we place on the front of the house anyway.

Our row house still has its old wooden windows with storm sashes on the outside. Does the sensor go on the storm sash or on the window behind it?

On the inner window, the one you actually open, because that is the one an intruder has to get past to come in. A storm sash can be lifted or removed from outside without anyone touching the inner window, so a contact on it would only tell you the storm moved. If the storm sashes are left open in summer, we mount the inner contact so it is not affected. On windows where the inner sash is painted shut and never opens, a glass-break detector covering the room is usually a better choice than a contact.

Renters

People waiting on the platform at 79th Street can see straight into our second-floor apartment. Should the keypad and the alarm decal be kept out of their view?

The keypad, yes; the decal is your choice. A keypad visible from the platform can be watched while you enter a code, so we mount it on a wall that cannot be seen from the windows, or use a fob or the phone app instead of a keypad by the door. The decal is meant to be seen, and some renters like it on the window facing the platform for that reason, while others would rather not advertise anything. The motion detectors we install do not record images, so nothing in the apartment is watching you or the riders.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in New Utrecht

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about alarms for a home or shop here and the answer is usually written for a detached house in a quiet suburb, with prices averaged from national lead sites such as HomeAdvisor and Angi. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, put the way they usually appear, and what we actually see on jobs along New Utrecht Avenue and the streets around the old town center.

“A loud siren scares burglars off and alerts the neighbors.”

On a quiet street, a siren may bring a neighbor to a window. Under the West End el, it competes with trains that pass every few minutes and a street that researchers have measured at subway-platform levels around 81 decibels. A siren inside a closed store is easily lost in that noise, and the neighbors who do hear it are often the family upstairs, who are woken but cannot do anything about it.

What brings help on this avenue is the report: a signal over cellular to the owner’s phone and a monitoring station within seconds of the first opening, with a call list of people who answer. We put the budget there first and add a siren sized to the building, with a cutoff well inside the fifteen minutes city law allows.

“Most burglars come through the front door, so start there.”

Door attacks are common, and the 86th Street business broken into through its door in September 2025 is an example. But the gym a few blocks away was entered through its roof the same night, and in a row of two-story buildings with flat roofs joined end to end, the roof is the side nobody watches. The front door on the avenue faces traffic, a gate and the lights of the station.

Every system we install covers the front door. The correction is about the rest of the building: the roof hatch, the skylight, the ceiling space shared with the next store, the cellar under the sidewalk and the back door to the yard. In this neighborhood, those are where the survey spends most of its time.

“Burglary is rising, so get the biggest package you can.”

The 62nd Precinct’s numbers say the opposite this year. CompStat shows 102 burglaries in 2026 through November 1, against 141 a year earlier, a 27.7 percent drop, and burglary down 92.5 percent over 33 years. Robbery is up 8.5 percent, and grand larceny, theft without a break-in, is flat for the year but up over sixteen years.

Neither trend sizes your alarm. What does is the building: how many ways in it has, who shares it, where the cash or the valuables sit, and how long it stands empty. A second-floor apartment with one door and one reachable window needs a small system. A grocery with a cellar safe, a roof hatch and a gate needs more.

We would rather install a modest system that gets armed every night than a big one that is switched off after a month of nuisance alarms from the el. If a request includes sensors the building does not need, we say so on the quote.

“Wi-Fi alarms are fine, everyone has internet.”

Many kits report over the home’s or the store’s internet. In a shop, the router usually sits behind the counter or in the back room, in the same space a burglar is standing in, and it can be unplugged in a second. In a two-family, the internet may belong to the tenant, or be switched off for weeks when a provider changes.

The panels we install report over cellular first and can use internet as a backup path. Vincent Ingrisano of Alarms R Us told Brick Underground the point in one line: “you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.” Because cellular signal near the el can vary from the front of a store to the back, we measure it at the panel location before mounting.

Old cellular alarms are a related trap. Panels built for the 3G and CDMA networks that shut down in 2022 may still light up and beep while reaching no one at all. If your panel predates that, we test it rather than assume.

“One alarm covers the whole building.”

Technically it can. In practice, a single-area alarm in a building with a store downstairs and a family upstairs, or with an owner and a tenant on different floors, is the alarm most likely to be left off. Somebody always has to disarm it for everyone, or sets it off for everyone, and after a few weeks nobody arms it.

Our panels are divided into areas: the store, each apartment, and a shared area for the roof, cellar and common doors. Each household or business arms its own space with its own codes, sees its own history and gets its own alerts, and the shared area can be armed by whoever leaves last.

How do you stop the el’s vibration from causing false alarms?
+

By choosing and placing sensors with the trains in mind.
