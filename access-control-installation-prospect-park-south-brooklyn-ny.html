The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Park South Properties Need Access Control

Prospect Park South was planned on paper before a single house went up. In 1899 the developer Dean Alvord bought farmland south of the park and laid out what he called a “rural park within the limitations of the conventional city block and city street,” or in his Latin, rus in urbe, the country in the city. Wikipedia’s account, drawn from the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s report, says he put in the utilities, marked the entrance of most streets with brick piers carrying cast plaques lettered “PPS,” and hired a Scottish landscape gardener, John Aitkin, to plant trees not only along the curb but at the building line too, so that each house would seem to stand alone on its block. Only then did he sell lots.

His rules made the houses what they are. Wikipedia reports that houses had to be substantial and freestanding, larger than 3,500 square feet and costing more than $5,000, and the Brooklyn Eagle’s 2025 history adds that each one had to sit 39 feet back from the sidewalk, with eight feet left between the street and the sidewalk for grass and trees. Alvord’s staff architect, John J. Petit, designed many of them, in Queen Anne, Colonial Revival, Tudor and other styles, and the neighborhood association counts two hundred and six homes in the original plan. The Eagle says building was virtually finished by 1905, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Prospect Park South Historic District on February 8, 1979; it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1983.

That plan explains the doors. A Prospect Park South house is not a rowhouse with one street door at the top of a stoop. It is a large wooden or masonry house with a front door at the back of a porch, a side door near the driveway, a kitchen or service door at the rear, a basement door and often French or porch doors as well, all of them far from the sidewalk and partly hidden by trees that were planted to hide them. There is usually no fence or gate in front; the lawn is open and the first thing a visitor meets is the porch. A door system here has to cover a whole house, not a single entrance.

The second thing that shapes the work is how often these houses are lent to other people. The Prospect Park South Association’s own filming page says the neighborhood has been the backdrop for blockbusters, television series, music videos and commercials, that some neighbors list their homes on location sites such as Peerspace and Splacer, and that “we are pretty used to location scouts dropping flyers in our mailboxes.” It asks productions for a donation of $750 per shoot day, which pays for mowing the curb strip, landscaping the mall and snow removal. The Flatbush Development Corporation brought back its Victorian Flatbush House and Garden Tour on June 1, 2025, starting at Albemarle and Westminster Roads, with tickets sold out. And a century-old house is rarely without a restoration in progress somewhere.

Every one of those days means strangers in the house: a crew of dozens moving equipment through the front door, tour visitors walking from room to room, painters and carpenters coming and going for months. The old answer was a key under a planter or a set of keys handed to whoever seemed in charge. The risks of that are not abstract here. In May 2018 Bklyner reported that a 66-year-old homeowner was found fatally injured in the basement of his house on Stratford Road after police were called to a burglary, and that officers found a suspect hiding in a closet. Nothing on a door makes a house immune to that kind of crime, and we do not pretend otherwise. What a planned system does is make it obvious who was given access, to which doors, for which hours, and when each door actually opened.

Prospect Park South facts we checked before writing this page

  • Boundaries. The historic district runs from Church Avenue on the north to Beverley Road on the south, with the BMT Brighton Line (the B and Q trains) on the east and a western edge between Stratford Road and Coney Island Avenue (Wikipedia, citing the LPC district map). The association describes it as two blocks long by four blocks wide, from Stratford Road to Buckingham Road.
  • Size. The National Register listing gives the district 47 acres. Sources disagree on how much land Alvord bought: about 60 acres (Wikipedia), about 50 (NewYorkitecture) or 40 acres bought from the Dutch Reformed Church of Flatbush and a private estate (Brooklyn Eagle, 2025).
  • Dates. Land bought 1899; houses built 1899 to about 1910 (CityNeighborhoods.NYC); city historic district February 8, 1979; State Register June 13, 1983; National Register July 21, 1983.
  • Street design. Brick entrance piers with “PPS” plaques at most streets; planted center malls; trees at both the curb and the building line; 39-foot front setbacks (Wikipedia; Brooklyn Eagle).
  • Transit. The association tells visitors to take the B or Q to Church Avenue or Beverley Road. Beverley Road station sits on the open-cut part of the Brighton Line, and its station house dates from 1907 (Wikipedia and Urban Archive, from search excerpts).
  • Police and board. The NYPD’s 70th Precinct covers Midwood, Fiske Terrace, Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South; the area is in Brooklyn Community Board 14, which lists City Planning’s South of Prospect study among its projects.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Prospect Park South’s Architecture

Alvord’s houses look wildly different from one another, but the Historic Districts Council notes that he built them to nearly uniform size and layout and got his variety from the details. For a door system that is useful: the floor plans repeat, so the door positions repeat, even when the porch columns and the roof lines do not. The six types below cover almost every building we are asked about between Church Avenue and Beverley Road.

Queen Anne and Shingle-style frame houses

Wood-framed houses with towers, bays and deep wraparound porches, clad in clapboard or shingle. The front door sits well back under the porch roof; there is usually a side door near the driveway and a kitchen door at the rear.

Colonial and Classical Revival houses

Symmetrical houses, some of brick with columned entry porches, some of painted frame. The front door is often a wide door with sidelights and a fanlight, and the entry porch is smaller than on a Queen Anne house.

Tudor and other masonry-and-timber houses

Houses with stucco or brick below and half-timbering above, like the 1904 Tudor Revival house on Argyle Road that Wikipedia lists. Heavy, often arched, wood doors and stone or brick around the openings.

The one-off showpieces

A Japanese-influenced house on Buckingham Road from 1902–03 and a Swiss-chalet-style house on Rugby Road from 1900, both listed among the district’s notable houses. Unusual doors, carved trim, and owners who want nothing visible added.

Pre-war apartment buildings on the edges

Larger buildings outside the house blocks, such as The Sovereign, a six-story co-op of 73 apartments built in 1927 on Parade Place facing the Parade Ground. Lobby doors, a vestibule, a service entrance and a board that decides.

Church Avenue shops and mixed-use buildings

Low commercial buildings along the northern edge, with shopfronts on the avenue, apartments above some of them, and staff and delivery doors at the side or rear.

What the materials mean for the install

Wood frames and wood walls. Most of these houses are framed in wood, and a good share are clad in wood too. That makes cable work easier than in a masonry rowhouse: there are stud bays and floor joists to follow, and a basement below most first-floor doors. It also means everything we fasten is going into wood that has moved for a hundred and twenty years, so we use longer screws into solid framing, not the trim, and we check that a door still closes on its own in August and in January before we put a strike in it.

Porches. A deep porch keeps rain off a door station and shade on a camera, which is good, but it also puts the front door forty feet or more from the sidewalk and in shadow at night. Visitors cannot see a small keypad, and a camera sees a dark silhouette against a bright lawn. We plan a light within the door opening, a station with a wide dynamic range, and a reader at a height a delivery driver will find.

Borrowed Days

When the House Becomes a Set: Shoots, Tours and Trades

The single thing that makes Prospect Park South different from the blocks around it is how often its houses are used by people who do not live in them. The Prospect Park South Association says the neighborhood’s best-known screen appearance is still the “Pink Palace,” the house used for the establishing shot of the boarding house in Sophie’s Choice, which still stands but is no longer pink. It also says plainly that “not everyone is thrilled to see the trucks pull up the block,” and asks productions to tell homeowners early about early and late shoot days, to respect property lines and to support local businesses such as Lark on Church Avenue. On Reddit, a thread titled “Filming on Flatbush (prospect park south area)” tells curious neighbors to look for the pink and orange flyers crews tape up; we saw that thread only as a search excerpt.

A shoot day has a shape, and the city sets part of it. The Mayor’s Office of Media and Entertainment’s code of conduct for permitted productions says production vehicles may not enter a residential location before 6:00 a.m.; that crews must keep noise to a minimum before 8:00 a.m. and after 10:00 p.m.; that every crew member must wear an ID badge; that cables must be covered with mats; that catered meals must be served indoors or from a food truck, never on the sidewalk; and that crews must not trespass on private property, including stoops, or block access to homes. Where a permit holds parking, notices must go up at least 48 hours ahead. Read the other way round, that tells a homeowner exactly when the doors will be busy, which doors the crew will use, and that the dining room or the basement is likely to become a lunch room for the day.

Our approach is to plan each booking as a set of door states. Before the first day, the owner, the location manager and we agree on four things: which outside door the crew uses (usually the side or kitchen door, so the front door and its glass stay out of the way), which rooms are open, which rooms are closed, and who holds the right to open a closed room in an emergency. The system then follows that plan by itself, and the log shows whether it was followed.

Codes with a start and an end

Every person or company who comes into the house gets a credential with a beginning and an expiry, not a key. For a crew that usually means one code or phone key for the location manager and one for the key grip or whoever opens up in the morning, valid from the call time on the first day to an hour after wrap on the last. A cleaner gets two mornings a week. A restoration carpenter gets weekdays from eight to four until the contract ends. When the dates pass, the credential stops working without anyone remembering to cancel it, and nobody has to collect keys.

Rooms that stay shut for the day

A crew needs the rooms in the script, a hallway, a bathroom and somewhere to eat. It does not need the bedrooms, the office or the family papers, and those rooms can be locked on the booking calendar; the Q&A below explains how.

Handing the house back

At wrap, the system does the checking that people forget at the end of a long day. Booking codes stop. Interior doors lock or unlock to the owner’s normal setting. Each outside door reports whether it is closed and locked. If one is not, the owner and the location manager both get a message, and the log shows which credential opened it last.

Restorations that last a season

Restoration work is the longest borrowed time a house sees. A slate roofer, a painter, a window restorer and a carpenter may each need the house on different weeks, and a general contractor may want a lockbox. Separate credentials by trade, each ending with that trade’s contract, keep the record clear, and they let the owner see at a glance that the painter was in the house on a Sunday when nobody expected it.

Landmark Rules

What the Landmarks Rules Allow Around a Prospect Park South Door

Because the whole district is a city landmark, anything visible that is fixed to the outside of a house can need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit. The commission’s permit guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment is the document we work from, and it is more practical than many owners expect. It says ordinary maintenance and repair of this kind of equipment needs no permit at all, and it sets out criteria that let LPC staff approve new installations without a public hearing.

For door hardware, three of those criteria matter most. Light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells must be installed within or next to the door opening, and an intercom may also go on a fence or wall in front of the entrance. No more than two light fixtures and one intercom panel are allowed per door opening. And equipment must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, with conduit concealed behind the installation; where that is impossible, staff may accept about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match. For cameras, small residential buildings may have no more than two for every 25 feet of street frontage, well spaced and finished to blend in.

On a freestanding house with a porch, those rules point to one combined station and a light or two within the front door opening, with the cable dropped into the basement rather than run along the face of the house. Side and rear doors that cannot be seen from the street allow more freedom, and that is often where the everyday hardware goes.

We are not the commission and we do not decide what it approves. We prepare a drawing, a photo set and the manufacturer’s cut sheets that the owner, or the owner’s architect or expediter, can file, and we install only what the permit allows.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Prospect Park South

Prospect Park South itself is almost entirely houses, but it is ringed by busier streets, and its owners and neighbors run businesses on them. The commercial doors we are asked about are small: a café’s staff door, a shop’s stock room, a professional office in a mixed-use building, a co-op’s service entrance. The edges are also where change is coming. City Planning’s South of Prospect study, launched with Council Members Rita Joseph, Shahana Hanif and Farah Louis, looks at Coney Island and McDonald Avenues south of the park, and News 12 reported in May 2026 that it could allow thousands of new homes on those corridors. The planning department held a visioning workshop on September 15, 2026, and has neighborhood walking tours scheduled for October 25 and November 14.

Church Avenue

The northern edge of the district, with cafés, shops and the Church Avenue station of the B and Q. The association singles out Lark on Church Avenue as a local business productions should support. Staff and delivery doors want codes that open early and log who used them.

Coney Island Avenue

The western corridor, which City Planning says the 1961 zoning set aside for auto- and truck-oriented commerce; parts of it and of McDonald Avenue, the department notes, became known as Little Pakistan and Little Bangladesh. Shopfronts, auto businesses and offices with rear doors onto lots.

Beverley Road

The southern edge, with the Beverley Road station house of 1907 at the Brighton Line cut and small businesses and apartment buildings nearby.

Parade Place and the co-op edge

Pre-war apartment buildings such as The Sovereign, run by boards and managing agents, where the lobby, service entrance and package handling are commercial-grade problems even though the building is home to 73 households.

Houses of worship

Wikipedia notes that New York’s Albanian community established a mosque on Albemarle Road in 1972. Institutions like this buy through their own channels; we list it only as part of the map.

A shop’s doors and the apartments over it never share an administrator with us; each gets its own account, even where a single cable route serves both. Rules that apply to Prospect Park South doors

Below are the rules that come up most on jobs in and around the district, boiled down for planning. None of it is legal advice: how a rule applies to your door is for your architect, engineer or attorney to confirm.

  • Landmarks. LPC Rules section 2-20 and the commission’s permit guidebook govern intercoms, lights, cameras and conduit on the outside of any building in the historic district, as described above.
  • Free exit. NYC Building Code sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9 cover sensor-release and request-to-exit doors: a person inside must always be able to leave, and electrically locked egress doors must release on loss of power and, where required, on fire alarm. In houses we prefer hardware whose inside handle always opens the door.
  • Apartment buildings. For the co-ops and rentals around the district, state Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a already demands an entrance door that shuts and locks itself and a way to call up to apartments; whatever we add has to keep both doing their jobs.
  • Tenant data. In a multiple dwelling, entry data from fobs, phone apps or biometrics falls under Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, which limits collection and retention and requires a written policy for residents. A one-family house is outside it.
  • Film permits. Productions working on public property follow the Mayor’s Office of Media and Entertainment code of conduct; it does not govern what a homeowner does inside the house, but it tells you when crews may arrive and what they may not do on your property.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Prospect Park South: Questions Answered

These questions come from the situations described on this page: houses lent to location crews and tours, big frame houses behind open lawns, Landmarks rules at the door, and restorations. Each answer starts with the short version.

Location shoots and other borrowed days

During a location shoot the crew needs the parlor rooms and the kitchen but not the bedrooms or the home office. Can interior doors be locked off for the shoot and released again at wrap?

Yes. Each door you want closed gets a lock that follows the booking calendar, and every one of them still opens freely from the inside.

In a finished house with original interior doors, the least invasive choice is usually a battery keypad or app lock that replaces only the existing knob or lever set. It is programmed to lock at the crew call time on the first booking day and to go back to normal an hour after wrap on the last. Nobody on the crew gets its code; the owner and, if you want, one named person on the production can open it from a phone if a room genuinely has to be entered.

If the house is being rewired anyway, a few interior doors can instead get wired strikes on the same controller as the outside doors, which puts every door on one schedule and one log. We fit the lock so the inside lever always opens the door; a locked room must never trap a person who wandered in before the lock engaged.

Camera and lighting cables run through our front door for the whole shoot day, so it can’t close. How is the house kept secure while that door stands open?

By putting that one door in a planned “held open” state for the booking hours, keeping alerts live on every other door, and moving the crew’s own coming and going to a different door.

The city’s code of conduct for permitted productions requires cables to be covered with mats, but it cannot make a door with cables through it close. If the door has a door-position switch, its held-open alarm would ring all day, and the usual reaction is to turn alerts off for the whole house. That is the wrong trade.

Instead, the system is told in advance that the front door will stand open from call to wrap. The other outside doors, especially the basement and kitchen doors at the back of the house where nobody is watching, stay locked and alarmed. The crew enters through the door assigned to them with their booking code, so the log still shows who came and went. An hour after wrap, if the front door has not closed and latched, the owner and the location manager both get a message.

The location manager asks to keep a key so the crew can collect gear the morning after wrap. What should we give them instead?

A one-time code that works for a short window the next morning, on one door, and then stops.

A key that leaves the house does not come back on its own, and copies are cheap. A pickup code can be set for, say, 7 to 9 a.m. the day after wrap, on the door nearest where the equipment is stacked, and nowhere else. If the crew is late, the owner extends it from a phone. If nobody comes, it expires by itself.

Because each code is separate, the log shows exactly when the pickup happened and which door was used. If something is missing afterward, that record is more useful than a guess about who had which key.

Can door alerts go to the production’s location manager on shoot days instead of to us?

Yes, for the booking dates, and they can go to both of you if you prefer.

Most access platforms let alerts be routed by schedule. For the days of a booking we add the location manager as a temporary contact for the doors the crew uses: door left open after wrap, a locked room forced, a code tried repeatedly. Alerts about the rest of the house still come to you. When the booking ends, the location manager’s contact access ends with it.

We recommend that owners keep basement and back-door alerts for themselves throughout.

A set designer wants our new keypad out of the shot. Is there door hardware that doesn’t show on screen?

Yes. A concealed electric strike in the frame, with the reader inside or a phone key instead of an outside keypad, leaves the outside of the door looking as it did.

On many doors we can put the electric part of the system inside the frame, where the existing strike plate is, and control it from a reader mounted indoors beside the door, a fob read through glass, or a phone app. From the street and on camera the door shows only its original knob and lock. That also happens to suit the Landmarks rules, which favor equipment that is small, discreet and within the door opening.

Where a keypad is already on the door, some models accept a decorative cover plate or can be swapped for a lock body that matches the existing hardware. Set designers can also simply be told which side door to dress; the front door does not have to be the one that changes.

Big frame houses behind long lawns

There is no fence or gate in front of our house, only the open lawn. If the front door is the first thing a stranger meets, what does it need that a door behind a gate wouldn’t?

A clear call point at the door, good light, a camera that can see the walk, and a door that latches every time, because there is no earlier barrier to slow anyone down.

Alvord’s plan put houses 39 feet back from the sidewalk with lawns in front, and most remain open. That means a visitor, a delivery driver or someone with worse intentions walks straight up to the porch. A gate would normally give you a warning; here the porch has to do that job.

So the front-door station should show the whole walk, not just a face at the glass, and it should ring your phones the moment someone steps onto the porch, not only when they press the button. A light within the door opening, switched by motion or by the call, helps the camera and tells visitors they have been seen. Most important, the door itself must close and latch on its own every time; on a big wooden house that has settled, that often means a closer adjustment or a strike moved a fraction of an inch before any electronics are added.

Our front door sits at the back of a deep wraparound porch, far from the sidewalk. Where on the porch should the call station, reader and camera go?

In the door opening itself, at shoulder height on the latch side, with the camera angled to see both the visitor and the porch steps.

The Landmarks rules allow intercom panels and doorbells within or adjacent to the door opening, so that is where we start. On the latch side the station is where people naturally stand, and a combined station puts the call button, camera and reader in one housing. We aim it so that the porch steps and part of the walk are in frame, because on a deep porch the face at the door is less useful than seeing how the person arrived.

If the porch is very deep or wraps around a corner, a second camera under the porch ceiling covering the steps can help, within the two-per-25-feet guideline for small residential buildings. We avoid mounting anything on porch columns, railings or carved trim, which are decorative surfaces the rules protect.

Our house is wood frame with shingle siding, not brick. Does a strike, reader or camera mount differently on wood?

Yes. Fasteners go into solid framing behind the siding, cable comes through the frame or floor rather than the face, and we seal every opening against water.

Shingles and clapboard are thin and move with the seasons. A reader screwed only into siding will loosen. We locate the stud or the door framing behind it and fasten into that, with a mounting block where the surface is uneven. On a porch, a station is usually best mounted to the door casing, which is solid wood and part of the opening.

For an electric strike, the wood jamb has to be cut accurately so the latch still drops fully into it; old jambs can be soft, so we sometimes glue in a hardwood block first. Wherever cable passes through the outside skin of the house, we slope the hole slightly downward to the outside and seal it, because water that gets into a wood wall finds the sill.

Landmarks staff rules allow one intercom panel per door opening. How do we fit a call button, a camera and a reader into that one panel?

Use a combined door station: one housing with the camera, the call button and a built-in card, fob or phone reader.

Several manufacturers make stations that combine all three, in finishes from brushed steel to black, and single-button models suit a one-family house well. Mounted within the opening on the casing, one station plus up to two light fixtures stays inside what LPC staff can approve under the published criteria.

If you want a keypad as well, choose a station that includes one, or use a keypad deadbolt on the door itself, which is door hardware rather than a separate wall panel. We send the cut sheet and a photo mock-up to the owner or the expediter for the filing before we drill anything.

Our walls are original plaster on wood lath and the side door is at the far end of a big house. Will a wireless lock stay connected, or does it need a cable?

Often it will, but we test the signal at the door before deciding, and plan a short cable to a small hub or a wired reader if the test is weak.

Plaster on wood lath is not usually a problem for radio, but these houses are large, many rooms are between the side door and the router, and some walls and ceilings were repaired over the years with metal lath or foil-backed board, which block signal badly. A battery lock with a weak link drains its batteries quickly and drops commands at exactly the wrong moment.

During the survey we measure the signal at each door. Where it is marginal, a small bridge or hub placed nearer the door often fixes it. Where the basement is directly underneath, a cable up through the floor to a wired reader is usually the cheapest permanent answer, and it takes away the battery changes.

The house is too big to hear a knock at the kitchen door from the third floor. How can someone at a side or back door reach whoever is home?

Put a call station at each door people really use and let every station ring indoor screens on each floor and the owners’ phones.

In a house this size the front door is often not the busiest door. Deliveries, cleaners, trades and family come to the side or kitchen door, and the old bell there was wired to a single chime in the back hall. A small station at each of those doors can ring the same indoor screens as the front door, so the call is heard and seen in the attic office, the kitchen and the bedroom.

Each call shows which door it came from, and the person answering can open that door alone. For older owners who do not carry a phone around the house, a wall screen on the floor where they spend the day, at a comfortable height, matters more than the app.

Restorations and open days

Our restoration contractor is taking the original front door off to strip and refinish it. Should the electric lock prep be done while the door is off its hinges?

Yes, if you have decided on the hardware. A door on a bench is the easiest time to cut a mortise or a hinge pocket cleanly, and the refinisher can finish over the new cut.

If the plan includes an electrified lock or a power-transfer hinge, the door needs a channel for the cable and a pocket for the hinge or the lock body. Done on a bench, that is a clean, precise job; done on a hanging door, it is slower and riskier for old veneer and glass. The refinisher then fills, stains and finishes around the new work so nothing looks added.

If you are not ready to choose hardware, we can still record the measurements and the door’s construction while it is off, so the later job is quicker. What we do not recommend is boring holes for hardware you have not chosen; an unused hole in an original door is permanent.

Our house is on the route of a spring house tour. How do we keep visitors to the rooms on the route while the front door stands open all afternoon?

Lock the rooms off the route and the other outside doors for the tour hours, and give the volunteer at the front door a phone that shows every door’s state.

Tours bring many more visitors than a crew, but for a shorter time and with less equipment. The same scheduled locks that close bedrooms during a shoot can close them for an afternoon. The side, kitchen and basement doors are set to locked with re-entry blocked, so nobody can slip out to the yard and come back in a different way.

The front door stands open on purpose, so its held-open alert is paused for the tour window only.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Prospect Park South

Google’s AI Overview could not be loaded for Prospect Park South queries in our build environment, so none of the claims below is a quotation from it. They are the generalizations that recur across fast online answers, consumer how-to articles and national price guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, each set against what a house in this district really asks for.

“A smart lock on the front door secures the house.”

Quick answers often treat the front door as the house. In Prospect Park South it is usually the least-used door. Families, cleaners and trades come in by the side or kitchen door, and the basement has its own entrance at the back.

A house with one smart lock and four ordinary keyed doors still depends on keys for most of its traffic, and the keyed doors are the ones nobody can see from the street.

We plan every outside door, even if only some get electronics at first. The others get at least a door contact, so you know when they open.

“Temporary access means sharing your code.”

Some consumer advice says to change your code after guests leave. For a house that hosts a three-day shoot or a six-month restoration, that means one code shared among dozens of people and a lot of resets.

Any decent system gives each person or company its own credential with its own start and end time. The crew’s code dies at wrap; yours never changes.

Separate credentials are also the only way the log means anything. A shared code tells you the door opened, not who opened it.

“Historic houses can’t have modern entry systems.”

Owners in landmark districts are sometimes told, or assume, that nothing electronic is allowed on the outside of the house.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s published criteria allow intercom panels, doorbells and light fixtures within or next to a door opening, set limits on cameras per 25 feet of frontage, and require discreet attachment and concealed conduit. Ordinary repair and maintenance needs no permit.

A combined station in the door opening, with cable dropped into the basement, usually fits those criteria. Side and rear doors that cannot be seen from the street give more freedom.

“Wireless is always simpler in an old house.”

Wireless avoids cutting into plaster, which is a real advantage in a finished period house, and consumer guides lean on it heavily.

But these are large houses with many walls between a back door and the router, and some walls have been patched with metal lath or foil-backed board. A battery lock on a weak link drains fast and drops commands.

Most of these houses also have a basement under the first-floor doors, which makes a short cable straightforward. We test the signal at each door and choose door by door.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National averages mix a keypad deadbolt bought online with commercial readers, and they rarely include the strike, power supply, cable, programming or the time to work around original doors.

On our published Brooklyn list, which applies here unchanged, a fully wired door with reader, strike, cable and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500. A battery keypad lock on an interior door costs far less than that; a combined station and strike on a landmarked front door is at the upper end.

The written price after the survey is the one that counts, and it lists every door separately so you can decide which to do first.

“A camera at the door is the same as access control.”

Many quick answers blur the two. A camera records who was there; it does not stop anyone, it does not expire, and it does not tell you whether a door was left unlocked.

Access control decides who can open which door at which time and keeps a record of it. In a house that hosts crews and tours, that decision is what protects the rooms that should stay closed.

The two work best together: the log tells you a door opened at 4:12, and the camera shows you who it was.

“Crime statistics tell you which system to buy.”

Automated advice sometimes starts from precinct numbers and recommends heavier systems for higher figures. The 70th Precinct covers several very different neighborhoods, from Midwood to Prospect Park South, and its totals say little about one house.

What decides the right system is the house: how many doors, who comes through them, how often it is lent out, and which rooms matter.

We start every quote from the doors and the people who use them, not from a statistic.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Doors From the Basement

This describes how we survey large old houses in general, not a particular customer. Owners nearly always undercount their doors. Asked how many outside doors the house has, most say two: the front and the back. Walking the house usually turns up five or six: the porch door, a side door by the driveway, a kitchen door, a door from a sunroom or porch, a basement door at the bottom of outside steps, and sometimes a second basement door into a former coal room.

So we start in the basement, not at the front porch. From below you can see every first-floor door as a set of joists and a threshold, every old bell wire that runs to a door, and where cable can come up without touching a finished wall. Then we go outside and walk the house clockwise, trying each door from outside and from inside: does it close on its own, does it latch, is the frame sound, could a crew prop it with a cable? Only then do we talk about readers.

FAQ

Prospect Park South Access Control FAQ

Some productions leave equipment in the house overnight with a security guard. How do we let a guard stay without giving them the run of the house?
+

Give the guard a credential of their own that works only overnight and only on the door to the room where the equipment is held, plus a bathroom, and keep every other door and closed room on its normal locked setting with alerts on. The guard can always leave, because every lock opens from the inside, and can be let back in by that one door. Ask the production for the guard’s name and the company’s contact before the night, and agree on the hour the credential ends; the log then shows the guard’s comings and goings next to the crew’s.

Could the production’s electricians, or a breaker they trip, knock out our door locks during a shoot?
+

They could if the locks share a circuit with outlets the crew uses. We put the access controller and lock power supply on their own circuit where possible, label it in the panel as one not to switch off, and back it with a battery that carries the locks through a short outage. Battery keypad locks are unaffected by the house’s power at all. Productions usually bring their own generators for lighting, but crews do sometimes plug into house outlets, so it is worth showing the location manager which breaker must stay on.

If something goes missing or gets damaged during a shoot, can the door log show who was in which part of the house?
+

It can show which credential opened which door and when, including interior room locks that were on the system, which narrows a question down a great deal. It cannot show who walked through a door that was standing open, and it does not identify a person beyond the credential they used. That is why we give each company or person their own code and keep a camera on the doors crews use. Keep the log export with the location agreement; most systems let you download it for the booking dates.

How many separate codes can one keypad lock hold, and what happens when it is full?
+

It depends on the model, but residential keypad locks commonly hold from a few dozen to a few hundred codes, and cloud-managed systems hold far more. In practice a house rarely needs more than a handful at once if expired codes are removed automatically, which is how we set them up. If a lock does fill up, it simply refuses to add another code until an old one is deleted; existing codes keep working. A booking template that issues codes with end dates keeps the list short without anyone tidying it by hand.

Our house is near the open subway cut on the east side of the district. Can train vibration knock a strike or a closer out of adjustment?
+

Over time, possibly, along with seasonal swelling and the ordinary settling of a large wooden house; we have no measurements specific to houses beside the cut, so we treat it as one more reason to check adjustment rather than as a known problem. The practical answer is the same either way: fit the strike so the latch drops fully into it with a little room to spare, use a closer with adjustable latch speed, and check both at the follow-up visit. A door that rattles in its frame when a train passes usually needs its strike or weatherstripping adjusted, not a new lock.

How many cameras can a landmarked freestanding house have on its front, and does a door station’s camera count toward that?
+

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s staff-level criteria allow no more than two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on small residential buildings, well spaced and finished to blend in. A door station is treated under the separate criteria for intercom panels, which allow one per door opening within or next to the opening. How staff count a particular combined station on a particular house is their decision, so we include the station’s cut sheet and a photo mock-up with the drawings and let the filing settle it. Cameras on sides and rear facades not visible from the street are generally less constrained.

Our parlor rooms are divided by sliding pocket doors. Can those be locked electronically?
+

Usually not neatly, and we rarely recommend it. Pocket doors slide into the wall, so a lock has to work on the edge of the door or a hook into the opposite jamb, and the old track and rollers often leave the door loose. If a parlor must be closed during a shoot, it is easier to lock the hinged doors into it from the hall and leave the pocket doors as they are, or to fit a simple mechanical edge lock that the owner sets by hand. We would rather say so than cut into a century-old pocket door for a lock that will not hold.

What should we tell a location crew about our doors before the first shoot day?
+

Give the location manager a one-page note: which outside door the crew uses and how its code works, which rooms are locked and must stay closed, which breaker the door system is on, which door will be held open for cables and for how long, what time codes start and stop, and whom to call if a lock misbehaves. Ask them to tell you before anyone props a door other than the agreed one. Most crews are glad of clear rules; problems usually come from nobody having said where the edges are.

Our house has decades of abandoned alarm and bell wiring from earlier owners. Do you remove it?
+

Where it is exposed and clearly dead, yes: in the basement, along baseboards and around door frames we trace each old wire, confirm nothing still uses it, and remove or cut it back and cap it. Wires buried inside finished plaster walls we usually leave in place, labeled at the ends, because pulling them would damage the wall. Old bell wire to a door can sometimes be reused as a pull string for new cable, which saves opening a wall. Anything we cannot identify stays until we know what it is.

Where sources disagree, we say so. Alvord’s purchase is given as about 60 acres by Wikipedia, about 50 by NewYorkitecture and 40 by the Brooklyn Eagle, while the National Register gives the district itself 47 acres. The Eagle says he bought from the Dutch Reformed Church and a private estate; CityNeighborhoods names a different estate. The association counts 206 homes in the original plan and “about two hundred” today. The western boundary is drawn between Stratford Road and Coney Island Avenue on the Landmarks map, while the association describes the neighborhood as running from Stratford Road to Buckingham Road.
