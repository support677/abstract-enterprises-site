How It Is Wired

Two Buttons, One Transformer: Inside a Bronxwood House Buzzer

Most of the buzzers we open in two-family houses east of Bronxwood Avenue were never sold as intercoms at all. They are doorbell circuits with one extra job. A transformer steps the house's 120-volt supply down to a low voltage, typically in the range of 10 to 24 volts. One pair of wires runs from it to the push buttons at the front door, and from there to a buzzer or chime in each apartment. A second pair runs from a release button inside each apartment back to the electric release in the frame of the shared front door. Press the upstairs button and the upstairs bell rings; press the release upstairs and the latch at the door lets go for as long as the button is held. Some houses have a talk feature added later, with a small speaker at the door and a handset on each floor, but the basic shape is the same.

Because the circuit is so simple, the faults are simple too, and they tend to sit in a handful of places. The first is the porch. The buttons are outdoors, often on a wooden door casing or a metal plate beside a storm door, and they take snow, rain, de-icing salt and thumbs. A button that sticks closed makes a bell ring constantly or not at all; one with a corroded contact works on dry days and fails in wet weather. The second is the basement ceiling. In older houses the low-voltage wire was stapled along the joists, and over decades it gets moved for a boiler, a water heater, a new electrical panel or a finished basement. A staple driven through both conductors, or a splice wrapped in tape that has dried out, is behind a surprising share of buzzers that ring in the wrong apartment or ring by themselves.

The third is the transformer itself. It is usually mounted on or near an electrical box, sometimes beside the panel, sometimes in a ceiling junction box in the basement, and it is fed from a household circuit. When the circuit is switched off for other work, or the transformer simply burns out after decades, the whole buzzer goes quiet in both apartments at once. The fourth is the door. An electric release, sometimes called a strike, sits in the wooden frame where the latch would normally catch. When the door swells in summer, sags on its hinges or is pulled shut hard by a new closer, the latch presses against the release and it cannot let go, even though you can hear it buzz. That looks like an electrical fault and is not one; the fix is in the hinges, the closer or the position of the release.

Then there is the storm door. Many houses of this age and type have an aluminum storm door in front of the main wooden door, and the storm door has its own handle and latch that the buzzer does not control. If the storm door is latched, a visitor cannot reach the main door no matter what the release does. If the buttons were moved behind the storm door when it went in, visitors cannot reach the buttons either. Neither is a buzzer fault, but both turn up as buzzer complaints, and we check the storm door before we open a single wire.

None of this needs to be expensive. Testing a two-family buzzer is a matter of measuring the transformer's output, checking each button, checking the wire between the porch and the basement for shorts and breaks, and pulling the door slack to see whether the release opens under no pressure. That sequence usually finds the fault inside the first hour of the $250 service call. What makes it slower is access: the basement belongs to whoever has the key, and the upstairs handset is in the tenant's apartment. If you are the owner, arrange both before the visit. If you are the tenant, tell your landlord that the technician needs the basement and your apartment for the same half hour, and it will go faster for everyone.

What we test, in order, in a two-family house

  • The storm door and the main door: is either latched, swollen or sagging, and does the release open with the door held slack?
  • Each push button at the porch: does it close, does it spring back, and is it wet, cracked or packed with ice?
  • The transformer: what voltage it gives with nothing pressed and with a button held, and whether its circuit breaker is on.
  • The basement run: staples, splices, wire chewed or crushed where it crosses a beam, and any place it was moved for a boiler or a panel.
  • Each apartment: the buzzer or chime, the release button, and the handset if there is one.
  • The release in the frame: its coil, its wiring, and where it sits against the latch.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Bronxwood's Architecture

The building types below are the ones the sources describe and the ones we expect to meet along Bronxwood Avenue. Each changes where the buzzer's parts are, who can reach them, and what usually fails.

Detached frame two-family with a porch

The house most people picture east of the avenue: two stories, siding over a wood frame, a porch or stoop, a storm door, and a separate stair or side door for the upper apartment in some houses. The buttons are outdoors on the porch or the door casing, the transformer is in the basement, and the wire runs up inside a wall to the second floor. Weather at the buttons and a swollen wooden door are the commonest faults. If the upstairs apartment has its own street door, it may have its own button and release on a separate pair of wires, which is why one floor can fail while the other works.

Attached or semi-attached brick two-family

Brick fronts with a shared party wall and a common entrance or two entrances side by side. The run from the basement often follows the party wall, and old splices can sit in a box that was plastered over. Brick is harder on surface wiring at the door, so buttons tend to be on metal plates screwed into a wooden frame, and that frame is where water gets in. A release in a heavy hardwood door with an added closer is the most common release-side fault.

Three-family houses

A house with three apartments is a multiple dwelling under the state Multiple Dwelling Law, which changes the owner's obligations, as the next section explains. Physically it is the two-family system with a third button, a third bell and usually a longer run to the top floor. A third apartment added to a buzzer designed for two is a frequent source of crossed wires and of a transformer too small for the load.

Bronxwood Park houses on the Oak Drives

Forgotten New York describes the enclave's curving drives, large porched houses, an old-fashioned gate and a hill on Wallace Avenue steep enough that no road was built down it, and WFUV's 2014 visit found gated driveways and big porches still in place. Houses set back behind a gate and a long front walk are where owners ask for a release at the gate as well as the door, and where the wire to the porch is longest and most exposed. We do not assume any particular house's layout; we look first.

Pre-war apartment buildings west of the avenue

Wikipedia places older tenements mostly within five blocks of the White Plains Road el, and openigloo lists hundreds of pre-war buildings under the Bronxwood name, on streets such as Wallace, Holland, Cruger, Barnes and Paulding Avenues. These have a lobby panel, a riser to each floor, a master power supply and often an amplifier, and a fault at any shared part silences the whole building. The diagnosis is the same discipline as in a house, at a larger scale.

NYCHA's Pelham Parkway Houses

Twenty-three six-story buildings managed by the New York City Housing Authority. Entry doors and intercoms there are NYCHA's to maintain and repair, so a tenant with a dead intercom should report it to NYCHA rather than hire a contractor. We mention them so nobody in those buildings waits on a call to us that we cannot accept as a private job.

Mixed-use buildings on Allerton Avenue and White Plains Road

Stores on the ground floor with apartments above, where the residential door is beside the shop front and the buzzer panel shares a wall with a gate or an awning. The common faults are vandalized or weathered panels and releases fed from a power supply in the shop's cellar that the residents cannot reach.

What changes by building type

In a frame house we start at the porch and the door, because that is where weather and wood movement do most of the damage. In a brick house we start at the basement, because the run along the party wall is where old splices hide. In a three-family house we start at the transformer, because a system stretched to a third apartment is often running at the edge of what it can supply. In an apartment building we start at the shared parts: the power supply, the amplifier if there is one, and the master release, because one of those failing explains every dead line at once.

The materials change too. A replacement button for a wooden door casing on a porch should be sealed against water at the back as well as the front; one for a brick front is better on a plate with a gasket than screwed straight into the masonry. A release in a hardwood frame needs a pocket cut cleanly enough that the latch meets it squarely, and a release that is replaced without fixing a sagging hinge will fail again. Where a new run of wire is needed in a frame house, we route it where the next renovation is least likely to disturb it and label both ends.

Owner and Tenant

Owner Downstairs, Tenant Upstairs: Who Is Responsible for a Bronxwood Buzzer

In an apartment building the answer is short: the owner must keep the intercom working. In a Bronxwood two-family house the answer starts with a definition. Section 4 of the New York State Multiple Dwelling Law calls a building designed and occupied exclusively for residence by not more than two families a private dwelling, and a two-family private dwelling when it holds exactly two. A multiple dwelling is one occupied as the home of three or more families living independently of each other. Most of the houses on the side streets east of the avenue are on the first side of that line.

The rule people quote about buzzers is Section 50-a of the same law. It requires every entrance to a class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors, and it requires those buildings with eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system at the main entrance that lets each apartment talk to a visitor and release the lock. Older multiple dwellings must install the same equipment if tenants of a majority of the apartments request it in writing. The New York Attorney General's Residential Tenants' Rights Guide summarizes the same rule. Section 50-a is written for multiple dwellings. A two-family house is not one, so that section is not what requires a Bronxwood owner to keep a two-button buzzer working.

That does not make the buzzer optional in practice. What an owner has promised a tenant usually lives in the lease and in the way the apartment was rented: if the apartment came with a working bell and door release, the tenant will reasonably expect it to keep working, and an owner who lives downstairs relies on the same front door. Many other housing rules do apply to rented one- and two-family homes, and HPD handles complaints about them; whether a particular buzzer problem is something HPD would cite is a question for HPD or a housing lawyer, not for us. One rule we can state plainly: HPD's property registration applies to buildings with three or more units and to one- and two-family homes where neither the owner nor the owner's immediate family lives on the premises. An owner-occupied two-family house does not have to register; an absentee-owned one does.

The picture changes when a house becomes three families. A house legally converted to three apartments after January 1, 1968 is a class A multiple dwelling converted after that date, so the self-closing, self-locking entrance door requirement of Section 50-a applies to it, and it must register with HPD every year. The intercom requirement in the same section starts at eight apartments, so a three-family house does not need a talk-and-release intercom by that section alone. If you own a house that has quietly gained a third household, the buzzer is the least of the questions it raises; talk to an architect or a lawyer about legalizing it before you talk to us about the bell.

The pre-war apartment buildings west of Bronxwood Avenue are a different matter. They are multiple dwellings, and where an intercom has been installed, owners must maintain it; tenants there can report a dead intercom to 311, and HPD can inspect. In the Pelham Parkway Houses, the intercom and the doors belong to NYCHA, and repairs go through NYCHA. What follows on this page about two-family houses is practical advice about fixing equipment. It is general information, not legal advice.

For an owner and a tenant who share a front door, the useful agreement is about access rather than law: who has the basement key, which half hour suits both of you, and who pays for which part. If the fault is in the upstairs handset, many leases treat it differently from a fault in the shared door, and it is worth reading yours before the visit.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Bronxwood: Questions Answered

These are the questions we hear from Bronxwood owners and tenants, grouped by what is going wrong. The short answer comes first under each one; the longer explanation follows.

When the buzzer behaves strangely

Why does the buzzer ring in both apartments when only one button is pressed?

Because somewhere between the porch and the bells, the two apartments' wires are touching. In a house the most common place is the basement ceiling, where a staple driven too hard has cut through the insulation of two conductors, or an old splice has loosened inside its tape.

Moisture inside the button plate at the porch can do the same thing, bridging the terminals of two buttons. We separate the run into sections and test each, which finds a short without opening every wall. Once found, the repair is usually a few feet of new wire or a new splice in a box where it can be inspected later.

When the release clicks and the latch stays put

It usually means the release is working and the door is pressing on it. A swollen wooden door, a sagging top hinge, a new closer or a storm door pulled tight all push the latch against the release so hard that it cannot let go.

The quick test is to hold the door slightly toward the hinges, taking the pressure off the latch, while someone presses the release. If it opens that way, the fix is in the door or the release position, not the wiring, and it is often cheaper than a new release. If it still will not open with the door slack, the release coil or its wire is the next thing to test.

Why did the buzzer die right after work on the boiler or the electrical panel?

Because the transformer that runs it is fed from a household circuit, and the low-voltage wire often runs along the same basement ceiling the other trade was working on. A breaker left off, a transformer disconnected when a box was moved, or a wire pulled loose behind a new water heater are all common.

Check the breaker labels first; if the circuit is on and the buzzer is still dead in both apartments, the transformer and the first few feet of wire from it are where we start. Tell us what work was done and where, because it narrows the search.

Can a buzzer that a previous owner disconnected be brought back into service?

Usually, if the wire is still in the walls. Owners who stopped using a buzzer often just took the transformer off its box or cut the pair at the basement ceiling, and the buttons, bells and release are still where they were. Reconnecting a supply and testing each part tells us within the diagnostic visit whether the old system can work again.

What we look for first is why it was disconnected. If it was ringing by itself or humming, the short that caused it is probably still there, and reconnecting without finding it only brings the problem back.

Weather, porches and gates

Is a metal button plate or a plastic one better on a porch that faces the street?

For a porch exposed to weather, salt and plenty of thumbs, a metal plate with sealed buttons usually outlasts a plastic one, which cracks with cold and sun and lets water reach the contacts. Stainless steel and plated brass both hold up; what matters as much is a gasket or sealant behind the plate so water cannot run into the terminals from above.

Mounting matters too. A plate under the porch roof or the door head lasts far longer than one on an open post, and a flush plate is harder to knock loose than raised buttons. Labels belong behind a clear cover, so the right apartment's name survives the winter.

Should the buzzer button be on the gate post or at the front door?

It depends on whether visitors can reach the door. If the gate at the sidewalk is kept locked, the button has to be at the gate, and so does a release if you want to let people in without walking out. If the gate is usually open, the button belongs at the door, where it is sheltered and the wire run is short.

A button at the gate means a wire under or along the front walk, which is the most exposed run in the whole system. We check whether an existing run can be reused, and if a new one is needed, we price it before anyone digs.

Owners, tenants and access

The owner lives out of state. How does a buzzer repair get done when nobody nearby has the basement key?

It gets done in two steps instead of one. On the first visit we can test the porch buttons, the door and each apartment's bell and release with the tenants, which usually narrows the fault to the basement or not. The owner then arranges a key, a neighbor or a managing agent for the basement half of the job, and approves the price in writing before parts go in.

An owner who does not live in a one- or two-family house must register it with HPD every year, and the registration names who to contact. Tenants can usually find the right person through it. We send the written diagnosis to whoever is paying, and a copy to the tenant if the owner agrees.

Could each household have its own code at the front door instead of a key?

Yes. A keypad door station or a keypad on the release lets each household use its own code, and the codes can be changed when a tenant moves out without changing the lock. The buzzer buttons keep working for visitors, and the key still works if the power goes out on a fail-secure release.

In a two-family house we set one code per household and none shared between them, so an owner who changes the tenant's code does not change their own. We also leave the key cylinder in place, because a keypad is a convenience, not the only way in.

Three checks a tenant can make first

Three things, none of which needs tools. Whether the downstairs apartment's bell works; if it does, the shared parts are probably fine and the fault is on your line. Whether the handset or button inside your apartment is loose on the wall or its cord is unplugged. And whether the door opens when someone holds it slack while you press the release.

Write down what you found and send it to your landlord with the date. It saves a visit's worth of guessing, and it gives both of you a record.

Repair or replace

When an old two-button buzzer is worth keeping

Often, yes. A two-button buzzer has very few parts, and a failed button, transformer or release can be replaced individually for much less than a new system. Where the wire itself has failed in several places, or where nobody can find the run inside finished walls, replacement starts to make sense.

The decision we put to owners is simple: if the fault is one part, fix it; if the wire is the problem, compare the cost of new wire with the cost of a two-apartment video intercom that would use a new run anyway. We write both numbers down.

Will a wireless doorbell kit fix a dead buzzer in a brick two-family house?

It can replace the bell, but it does not replace the door release. Most battery doorbell kits ring a chime and do nothing at the door, so the upstairs tenant would still have to walk down to let someone in, and brick and steel can shorten their range.

If the release is what you rely on, the repair should keep the release. If you only need a bell, a wireless kit is a reasonable stopgap, and we will tell you so rather than sell you a visit.

Apartment buildings and businesses

How is a buzzer in a pre-war building on Wallace or Holland Avenue different?

It is shared. A lobby panel, a riser to every floor, a master power supply and often an amplifier serve every apartment, so a failure in one shared part silences the whole building while a failure in one line affects one apartment.

That is why the first question we ask in an apartment building is whether the neighbors' buzzers work. If they do, the fault is your line or your handset. If nobody's does, it is the shared equipment, and the owner or managing agent needs to arrange the repair.

A shop door on White Plains Road wears out its electric release every year or so. Is there a sturdier option?

Usually, yes. A release made for a home front door is rated for far fewer openings than a commercial door under the el gets in a day, and continuous-duty or heavy-duty releases exist for exactly this. Matching the release to the door's frame, the system voltage and the traffic is most of the fix.

The other half is the door itself. A heavy commercial closer slamming the latch into the release, or a frame shaken loose, will wear out any release, so we adjust the closer and check the frame before fitting the new part. We still confirm that people can always get out without a key.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Bronxwood

We read what general search summaries, national cost sites and online forums say about buzzer repair and compared it with what we find in Bronxwood houses. Google's AI Overview itself could not be opened from our research environment, so the claims below are ones that appear in published guides and forum threads we could read, and in the kind of generic advice an AI summary tends to repeat.

It treats every buzzer as an apartment-building intercom

National guides about intercom repair are written for lobbies: entry panels, directories, risers, master stations. Swiftlane's 2026 guide on apartment intercom replacement, for example, starts its replacement estimates at a small building and treats minor repairs as a separate, cheaper category.

In Bronxwood that framing fits the pre-war buildings west of the avenue and the larger developments, but it misses the houses. A two-family buzzer has no directory and no riser in the apartment-building sense. It has two buttons, a transformer and a release, and it fails like a doorbell does.

The practical consequence is that an owner who reads a lobby guide expects a lobby price. The right first step in a house is a diagnostic visit that tests those few parts, priced on our list at $250, which goes toward the repair if more is needed.

It assumes the landlord is always legally required to fix it

Generic answers about tenants' rights often say that a landlord in New York City must provide a working intercom. That is true of the buildings Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law covers, which are class A multiple dwellings of three or more families, with the intercom requirement starting at eight apartments.

A two-family house is a private dwelling under Section 4 of the same law. Whatever a Bronxwood owner owes an upstairs tenant about the bell and the release comes from the lease, from other housing rules and from common sense about a shared front door, not from Section 50-a.

We do not give legal advice, and an AI summary should not either. What we can say is that the confident blanket answer is wrong for the most common building type east of Bronxwood Avenue, and that a three-family conversion changes it.

It quotes one national price for everything

Published figures for minor buzzer and intercom repairs cluster in the low hundreds of dollars; Swiftlane's guide puts minor repairs at $150 to $400, and contractors in New York publish flat rates in a similar range. Those numbers mean little until someone has found the failed part.

In a Bronxwood house the honest range is wide because the parts are different. On our Bronx list a button or relay repair is $250 to $475, a transformer $325 to $650, an electric release with alignment $450 to $950, and a run of wire repaired or re-terminated $850 to $2,400.

The difference between the bottom and the top of that list is usually the wire. That is why we test the run before quoting, and why a quote that skips the diagnostic is a guess.

It says a dead buzzer means a dead transformer

Do-it-yourself doorbell guides, including Home Depot's, rightly list the button, the chime, the transformer and the wiring as the places to look. A summary that compresses that list tends to lead with the transformer, because replacing it is easy to describe.

In two-family houses the transformer is only the first suspect when both apartments go quiet at once. When one apartment works and the other does not, the transformer is almost certainly fine, because it feeds both. When the bell rings but the door will not open, the transformer is irrelevant and the door is the problem.

A DIY Stack Exchange answer we read made the more useful point: staples along a basement run can short the two wires together and mimic a failed part. In older Bronx houses, that is where a lot of time is saved or lost.

It says three apartments are the same as a single-family house

In a Reddit thread on r/AskNYC about a small building with no buzzer and one mailbox for three apartments, one reply said that three units are in the same category as single-family homes. We could read only the search excerpt, but the claim is a common one.

It is not what the Multiple Dwelling Law says. Three families living independently make a multiple dwelling; one or two make a private dwelling. A three-family house converted after 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and every building with three or more units must register with HPD.

For a Bronxwood owner, the dividing line between two and three families matters more than the brand of buzzer. If you are not sure which side your house is on, find out before you spend money on the front door.

It recommends a wireless doorbell as the fix

Battery and plug-in doorbell kits are the most common product answer to a broken bell, and for a single-family house without a door release they can be fine.

In a two-family house with a shared front door, the bell is only half the system. The upstairs tenant uses the release, and most wireless kits do not include one. Replace the buzzer with one of those kits and the tenant is walking down a flight of stairs for every delivery.

Where a wireless or app-based answer makes sense, it is a video door station or doorbell that also drives the release, set up with an account for each apartment. That is a different product, and it still needs power and a wire to the release.

It forgets that Bronxwood is three neighborhoods on three maps

Search tools and listing sites disagree about the name. Wikipedia treats Bronxwood as part of Allerton; the Bronx Times reported in 2014 that city planners relabeled the area Allerton; StreetEasy labels some Bronxwood Avenue houses Williamsbridge or Wakefield and a Holland Avenue house Bronxwood.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Hold the Door Before You Open the Wall

This is how we approach a Bronxwood house buzzer; it describes our method, not the story of one job. Before the technician opens anything, he goes to the front door with the person who called and asks them to press the release while he holds the door gently toward the hinges. If the latch lets go with the door slack and not with the door shut normally, the buzzer is working and the door is the job. That takes a minute and decides the rest of the visit.

Then he goes to the basement and finds the transformer before touching the porch, because the transformer tells him whether the system has power at all. Only after that does he test the buttons and the run. In houses that have had a new boiler, a new panel or a finished basement, he asks when, because the wire was probably moved then. And before leaving he stands at the porch while each apartment presses its own release, so the owner and the tenant both see it work.

FAQ

Bronxwood Door Buzzer Repair FAQ

In our two-family house the release button opens the front door from the downstairs apartment but does nothing from upstairs. Where is the fault likely to be?
+

On the upstairs release line, not at the door. Because the same electric release and the same transformer serve both apartments, a door that opens from downstairs proves that the release, its power and the door itself are working. What is left is the upstairs release button, the pair of wires from it down through the wall to the basement, and the splice where that pair joins the release circuit. A worn button is the quickest thing to test; a pair that was cut or pinched when a wall or ceiling was opened is the next. Most of these repairs fall in the $250 to $475 range on our Bronx list.

We put an aluminum storm door in front of the main door, and since then the buzzer sounds but the inner door has to be pulled before it opens. Did the storm door cause that?
+

Quite possibly. A storm door can pull air and pressure against the main door when it closes, and if the installer moved the main door's stop or its closer at the same time, the latch may now press hard against the electric release. A release under pressure buzzes but cannot let go. Hold the main door slightly toward its hinges while someone presses the release; if it opens that way, the fix is adjusting the door or repositioning the release, not replacing the buzzer. Check too that visitors can still reach the buttons with the storm door shut.

A house in Bronxwood Park has a locked gate at the sidewalk and a long walk up to the porch. What decides the price of adding a buzzer release at the gate?
+

Often, yes, but it needs a release at the gate and a wire to it, which is the part that sets the price. The existing buzzer wire to the porch is usually too thin and too far from the gate to reuse, so a new run along or under the walk is common, and a gate in the weather needs a release and a button rated for outdoor use. We look at the gate, the post and the walk before quoting, and we price the run separately so you can decide whether the gate is worth it.

The city planning map calls our block Allerton, but the listing we bought from said Bronxwood. Does the name change anything about the repair or the rules that apply?
+

No. Neighborhood names in New York City are not official, as the Bronx Times noted in 2014 when City Planning relabeled its maps, and the same blocks appear as Allerton, Bronxwood, Williamsbridge or Wakefield depending on the source. What matters for a buzzer is how many families live in the house, when it was built or converted, and how it is wired. What matters for pricing is that it is in the Bronx, where our list applies at 1.00. When you call, give the cross street rather than the neighborhood name, and we will know where you are.

Our basement was finished years ago and the buzzer wire disappeared into the new ceiling. Can it be repaired without opening the whole ceiling?
+

Usually. We test the run from both ends to find which section has failed, then open only the ceiling at that spot, often at a light fixture, a vent or an access panel that is already there. Where the old wire cannot be reached at all, a new pair can often be fished through a different path, such as a closet, a chase beside a pipe or the outside wall, and the old one left in place. We show you the route and the price before cutting anything, and a run repair falls in the $850 to $2,400 range on our Bronx list depending on access.

Will a one-button video doorbell work for a house where two apartments share the front door?
+

Sometimes. A video doorbell needs steady power, and the old two-wire run to the porch may or may not carry enough; we measure it before you buy anything. The bigger question is the second apartment. A single-button doorbell rings one household, so the second needs either a two-button video station or its own way to be called. And the door release has to be driven by something: some door stations have a release output, many consumer doorbells do not. We plan both before recommending a model.

Our buzzer makes a constant low hum even when nobody presses it. Is that dangerous, and can we unplug it ourselves?
+

Do not leave it running for days. A constant hum usually means a button stuck closed or a short in the wire, so a bell or the transformer is powered continuously and gets warm. If you know which breaker feeds the transformer, switching it off is a safe way to stop it until the repair. Do not pull wires off the transformer with the power on, because its other side is connected to the house's 120-volt supply. Note whether the hum is at the bell or at the transformer; it tells us where to start.

The buzzer works, but it is so faint at the back of the house that we keep missing deliveries. Can it be made louder or moved?
+

Yes. Many older buzzers were mounted in a front hall, and a deep two-family house carries sound poorly to a kitchen at the back. A second sounder wired to the same button, a louder chime or a bell moved to the middle of the apartment all work on the existing transformer in most houses, and we check that it can carry the extra load. For people who are often out back or in the yard, a door station that also rings a phone is the other route. This is usually a small job inside the $250 to $525 range on our Bronx list.

The upstairs tenant has a baby who naps in the afternoon. Can the bell be kept quiet at certain hours without stopping the door release from working?
+

Yes. The bell and the release are separate circuits in most two-family buzzers, so the bell can be quietened without touching the release. The simplest fix is a chime with a volume control or a silence switch fitted in place of the old buzzer upstairs. With a video door station or a doorbell that rings a phone, each apartment can set its own quiet hours in the app, and the visitor still reaches the other household. We do not disconnect a bell entirely, because the person upstairs still needs some way to know someone is at the door.

Door Stuck Locked or Stuck Open? Call the Bronx Line

If the door is stuck open, the house is unlocked: use the deadbolt or a key lock until the release is fixed, and do not prop the inner door. If it is stuck locked and someone needs to get in or out, open it with the key and leave the buzzer alone. If the buzzer is humming or hot, switch off its circuit if you know which one it is. Then call (646) 490-0629 with the number of families in the house, what the buzzer is doing and the cross street. A technician will normally arrive with the likely part.
