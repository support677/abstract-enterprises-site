The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need AVYCON Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley was settled in small, scattered pieces, and its properties still are. Wikipedia records that the Dutch explorer Adriaen Block led voyages up the river between 1611 and 1614, and the Hudson River Valley Heritage Area describes Dutch homesteaders spreading out from Kingston into Ulster and Dutchess and from New York City into Westchester and Rockland. Esopus, the settlement that became Kingston, dates to 1653 according to a historical marker transcribed by HMDB, and the great land grants of the Livingston, Philipse and Van Cortlandt families gave whole stretches of the valley to a few owners. By 1750, the Heritage Area says, most of the region had been settled as a network of small local communities, and that network survives. For a camera system, the result is simple to state and hard to wire: houses that sit far apart, outbuildings that sit far from houses, and town centers built of stone and brick long before anyone thought about running a cable through a wall.

The housing is old, and in some counties very old. Stacker's analysis of Census Bureau data, published in September 2025, gives Westchester a median year built of 1958 and counts 28.0 percent of its homes, about 109,608, as built in 1939 or earlier. Putnam's median is 1968 with 17.9 percent built before 1940; Dutchess is 1971 with 18.0 percent; Rockland is 1970 with 11.5 percent. Those figures understate the problem in the river towns. A study published through Fordham's research library describes Hurley as having the oldest surviving group of colonial stone houses in the valley. Kingston's Stockade District is, in the words of a National Park Service publication, the largest remaining example of early Dutch settlement in New York State. Newburgh's East End Historic District contains 2,239 resources, which Wikipedia describes as the most of any federally recognized district in the state. A camera cable has to get through every one of those walls somehow, and how it gets through decides the price, the look and how long the system lasts.

Then there is the weather and the grid. In July 2026 the Daily Freeman reported nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers without power after storms crossed the region. In September 2026 Central Hudson's own newsroom described crews restoring power to more than 19,000 homes and businesses after a slow-moving nor'easter. The Times Union reported that thunderstorms in June 2024 affected a combined 60,000 Orange and Rockland and Central Hudson customers, and when Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, Daily Voice reported NYSEG at 34,150 of 38,693 Putnam customers out at one point. A camera that only works while the power and the internet both stay on is a camera that goes blind exactly when a house sits empty and dark. That is the strongest reason we install AVYCON as a recorder-based system: the footage is written to a hard drive in the building, and with a battery backup on the recorder and the switch, the system keeps watching through the kind of short outage the valley gets every summer.

Internet is the other gap. Ulster County announced in September 2025 that a state ConnectALL award would bring fiber to nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities in remote parts of the county, and Hudson Valley One reported in 2023 that 1,070 Ulster properties still had no broadband at all, with cell service struggling in places like Shandaken. Mid-Hudson News reported in 2025 that ConnectALL grants target every documented unserved and underserved location in sections of Putnam, Dutchess, Orange, Ulster and Westchester, with fixed wireless planned for some of them. Cloud-only cameras, as one consumer guide puts it plainly, cannot save new footage without an internet connection, even if the power is fine. An AVYCON NVR does not need the internet to record. It needs it only to send video to your phone, and when the line comes back, the recordings from the gap are still on the drive.

The valley is also arguing, loudly, about cameras. In October 2026 Mid-Hudson News reported that Ulster County Executive Jen Metzger issued an executive order barring county departments from buying, contracting for or deploying Flock cameras and similar AI-assisted surveillance tools. The Times Union reported that Dutchess County is removing its Flock cameras after a packed public meeting in Pleasant Valley, and earlier that Flock had marked out locations for 11 cameras and license plate readers in Pine Plains before residents pushed back. Hudson Valley One covered Kingston police defending the city's cameras in September 2026 while residents raised privacy concerns. None of that is about a homeowner's own system, and none of it makes private cameras illegal. It does tell us what people here care about: cameras that watch your own property, record to a box you own, and do not quietly feed a network you never agreed to. That is how we set up an AVYCON system, and it is why we ask, at the first visit, exactly what you want recorded and what you do not.

The crime stories in local news are mostly small and close to home. News 12 reported in July 2026 that neighbors in Port Chester shared Ring footage of car break-ins around 4 a.m. on and near Tower Hill Drive. Patch and WRRV reported Scarsdale homeowners finding a hidden camera in the bushes outside their house, with police saying theft rings use cameras to watch for chances to burglarize homes. New York State Police issued a public alert in January 2025 about residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam, and their tips included installing or activating cameras. In May 2026 News 12 covered an overnight string of smash-and-grab burglaries at businesses in Warwick, Florida and Goshen, where one owner said they had never once had to look at the camera and did not even know the password. That last detail is the one we think about most. A system nobody can log into is not a system. We leave every AVYCON install with the owner able to open the app, find last night's footage and export a clip, and we write it down.

We found no Patch or hyperlocal article about the brand in the region. We could not capture Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches, Bing, DuckDuckGo or AnswerThePublic results from our build environment, so the questions on this page come from AVYCON's own FAQ, legal and consumer guides, Yelp and our own past pages, and each is labeled by where it came from in our notes.   • Old walls: 28.0 percent of Westchester homes and 18.0 percent of Dutchess homes were built in 1939 or earlier, per Stacker's Census analysis, and the river towns hold far older stone and brick.
  • Frequent outages: nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers lost power after storms in July 2026, and more than 19,000 after a nor'easter that September.
  • Internet still arriving: Ulster's ConnectALL fiber build targets nearly 1,300 unserved locations; in 2023, 1,070 Ulster properties had no broadband.

  • Surveillance skepticism: Ulster barred county use of Flock and similar AI tools in October 2026, and Dutchess is removing its Flock cameras.
  • Wildlife on camera: NYS DEC places the southern black bear range down to southern Orange and Rockland at the New Jersey line, and Teatown reports Westchester bear sightings rising from 2 in 2019 to 16 in 2021.

What owners often add once the cameras are in. On farms, estates and second homes, the next request after an AVYCON camera system is usually a gate or door that can be opened and logged remotely, or an alarm that calls someone when the cameras see a person after dark. Both are separate jobs with their own quotes. If you think you might want them, say so at the first visit, so the cable we pull now has room for them later and nobody drills through a stone wall twice.

The same AVYCON recorder and the same cameras end up doing very different work as you drive north from the city line. In southern Westchester and Rockland, lots are tight, neighbors are close and the hard part is aiming cameras so they watch your property and not the house next door. Further north, in Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, the hard part is distance: the barn, the gate, the pool house or the long gravel drive may sit hundreds of feet from the house, and the grid and internet are less reliable. Westchester: cameras among close neighbors

Westchester is the oldest housing stock in the region by Stacker's count, and much of it sits on suburban lots where the house next door is a few yards away. Most of the AVYCON work we expect here is the familiar suburban plan: a camera on the front door, one on the driveway, one or two on the back of the house, and a recorder in a closet or basement. The stories in local news fit that plan exactly. News 12 described Port Chester neighbors trading footage of 4 a.m. car break-ins on Tower Hill Drive, and Westchester police and residents in Scarsdale dealt with burglars leaving camouflaged cameras in bushes to watch homes. What makes a Westchester job careful is aim. A wide lens on a side yard can easily see straight into a neighbor's window, so we set the field of view and, where the recorder allows it, a privacy mask, and we show you the result on the screen before we leave. Our own Hudson Valley NVR page lists an 8- or 16-channel system for a Westchester colonial as a typical job, and that is about the right size for most houses here.

Rockland: tight lots, busy strips and bears

Rockland is newer than Westchester on average, with a median year built of 1970, so more of its homes have drywall and attics we can work through. Its business life runs along Route 59, Route 304 and Route 202 and around the Palisades Center in West Nyack, which Wikipedia describes as an 875,000-square-foot mall as proposed. For homes, the Rockland detail we plan around is wildlife. NYS DEC says the southern black bear range extends to southern Orange and Rockland at the New Jersey border, and that forested land along I-87 is a primary corridor for bears arriving from New Jersey. A camera that alerts on any motion will wake you up for every bear at the trash cans. AVYCON's app sorts alerts into Event, AI and Abnormal and supports people and vehicle detection, so we set alerts to people and cars and let the recorder simply record the animals.

Orange: warehouses, farms and Newburgh's row houses

Orange County gives us the widest spread of jobs. Its east side holds Newburgh, whose East End design guidelines tell owners to integrate security features with the architecture, avoid hiding architectural features and avoid damaging historic building fabric when attaching them. Its corridors carry freight: Wikipedia calls I-84 a major commercial artery and a mainstay of the Hudson Valley economy, and NY 32 runs as New Windsor's main commercial strip into Newburgh's Lake Street. And its west side is farmland. Our own NVR page describes a 32-channel build for an Orange County warehouse as a typical large job. The May 2026 burglaries in Warwick, Florida and Goshen that News 12 covered are a reminder that businesses out here can sit unwatched overnight for long stretches.

Putnam: lake houses and long outages

Putnam is small, wooded and hilly, with lake communities around places like Lake Mahopac, which a listing site describes as 587 acres. Its homes are a mix of older cottages and later houses, with a median year built of 1968. The Putnam detail we plan around is outages. During Isaias in 2020, NYSEG had almost nine in ten of its Putnam customers out at one point, according to Daily Voice. For a lake house or a second home that sits empty during the week, that is exactly when you want the cameras running. We size a battery backup for the recorder, the PoE switch and the router, and if the house has a generator, we put the camera equipment on a circuit the generator carries.

Dutchess: horse farms, estates and Route 9

Dutchess was created in 1683 as one of New York's first twelve counties, and it still holds a long stretch of what writers call the Great Estates Region along the river. Inland are farms; our own NVR page lists a 32-channel system for a Dutchess horse farm among its typical jobs, the kind of build where cameras cover a barn aisle, paddock gates, the drive and the house. Along Route 9 in Wappingers Falls, near the Poughkeepsie Galleria, the county is a commercial corridor, and in Beacon, Main Street runs almost a mile. Dutchess is also where residents pushed the county to remove its Flock cameras. Owners here tend to ask us one question before any other: who can see the footage? With an AVYCON NVR the answer is you, and anyone you give an app login to.

Ulster: stone, second homes and thin internet

Ulster holds Kingston, with five historic districts including the Stockade, and Hurley, with the valley's oldest group of colonial stone houses. A Kingston real estate guide describes the city's stock as stone Stockade colonials, Victorian row houses, mid-century cottages and renovated multis, and warns buyers to expect questions about masonry and wiring. Out in the towns, Ulster has the region's clearest broadband gaps, and Hudson Valley One quotes a local official saying that in most of the county the available provider is Spectrum. For a second home in the Catskill foothills, the plan is local recording on the NVR, careful upload settings so the app works on a modest connection, and a recorder that keeps everything on its drive when the connection drops.

Barn to Back Porch: What to Check on a Hudson Valley Property Before We Visit

Most of the time lost on a camera job is lost before the first cable is pulled: a recorder location that has no power or no network, an outbuilding further away than anyone guessed, a historic district approval nobody asked about, or an internet plan that cannot carry video to a phone. A walk around your property with a phone camera and a few notes prevents nearly all of it. Please do not drill into stone, open walls or move existing cameras yourself; just look, pace out the distances and send us what you find.

Walk the route a visitor would take

Start at the road. Walk the way a car, a delivery driver or a stranger would come onto your property: the end of the drive, the turn toward the house, the porch, the side door, the path to the barn. At each point, ask what you would want to see in a recording afterward. Usually it is a face at the door, a plate or a vehicle shape at the drive, and a person at the outbuilding. Take a photo from each spot facing where the camera would look. That walk tells us more about the right number of cameras than any package does, and it is the same walk our technician will take with you on the day.

Think about what must not be recorded

Before we visit, decide what you do not want on camera. For a home, that is usually the neighbor's windows, their yard and the public sidewalk beyond what you need. For a business, New York's rules are firmer. Labor Law §203-c prohibits cameras in restrooms and locker rooms whatever the employer's policy says, and Civil Rights Law §52-c requires employers to post notice of electronic monitoring where employees can see it. If you rent the property out, think about guests too, and keep exterior cameras on exterior areas. Telling us these limits at the start lets us aim and mask the cameras properly rather than adjusting them after the fact.

Check the internet where the recorder will sit

Run a speed test on a phone or laptop standing next to where the router is, and send us the upload number, not just the download. Upload speed is what carries video from your house to your phone. In parts of Ulster, Putnam and Dutchess that are still waiting on fiber, upload can be modest, and that shapes how we set the app's streams. The recorder records the full-quality picture to its own drive either way; the app can be set to show a lighter stream when you are away, and the full recording is always there to play back on the recorder or download later.

Stone walls in Kingston and Hurley

A colonial stone wall can be well over a foot thick, built of fieldstone set in old mortar, with no cavity inside for a cable. Drilling straight through takes a long masonry bit, a slow hand and a plan for where the bit will come out on the inside, because you cannot see through two feet of rock. We avoid new holes wherever the house already offers a path: a cellar window, a gap under the eaves, an old service entry. When a hole is needed, we drill it once, sleeve it, and seal it on the outside against water. Camera brackets go into solid stone with anchors chosen for the stone we find, not into a mortar joint that may be soft from two centuries of weather. In the Stockade, where the HLPC reviews exterior work visible from the street before a building permit is issued, we also bring the camera's cut sheet and a photo of the planned position, and we expect the commission's view on how visible the camera and any surface cable may be. A 2025 HLPC approval asked an owner to paint externally mounted lines or line hides to match the facade's existing color, and we plan for that kind of condition from the start.

Brick row houses in Newburgh's East End

Farms, barns and estates

On a horse farm or an estate, the recorder lives in the house and the cameras live everywhere else. Ethernet has a channel limit of 100 meters, or 328 feet, so a barn that sits further than that from the recorder cannot simply be fed with one cable. Licensing and the law

Does the person putting in our AVYCON system need a state license?

In most cases, yes. New York's licensing rule, 19 NYCRR 195.2(b), as Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum explain it, covers a closed-circuit television system used full-time or part-time to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire. A home or business camera system recorded and watched for exactly those reasons falls inside it. The Department of State licenses installers under Article 6-D, and its application notes that even electricians who want to do this work must file. A trade association warns that unlicensed installers can face fines it puts at up to ten thousand dollars. We install under NYS license #12000287431 and will show it on request.

We own a shop in Poughkeepsie. What do we have to tell staff before the cameras go on?

Two things matter most. Under Civil Rights Law §52-c, employers must post notice of electronic monitoring in a conspicuous place visible to employees, and the law that took effect in 2022 also requires written notice to employees when they are hired. Under Labor Law §203-c, cameras in restrooms and locker rooms are prohibited no matter what the employer's policy says. We plan camera positions with both rules in mind, point corridor cameras away from restroom doors, and remind you to post the notice before the system goes live. For your exact obligations, check with your own lawyer; this is a summary, not legal advice.

Our house is inside the Kingston Stockade. Can we put cameras on the outside at all?

Very likely yes, but plan for review. Outages and internet

If the internet goes out at our place in the country, does the AVYCON recorder keep saving footage?

Yes. An AVYCON NVR records to its own hard drive inside the building, so footage keeps saving whether or not the internet is up. What you lose during an internet outage is the ability to watch live or receive alerts on your phone. When the connection returns, the recordings from the gap are on the drive and you can play them back in the app. That is the difference from cloud-only cameras, which, as one consumer guide puts it, cannot save new footage without an internet connection even when the power is fine. For properties in the parts of Ulster, Putnam, Dutchess and Orange still waiting on fiber, that difference is the whole reason to choose a recorder-based system.

What happens to the system during a Central Hudson or NYSEG outage, and is a battery backup worth it?

Without backup, the recorder and cameras stop when the power does, and they restart when it returns. With a battery backup on the recorder, the PoE switch and the router, the system keeps recording through short outages, and the recorder shuts down cleanly rather than losing power mid-write if the battery runs low. Outages here are not rare: the Daily Freeman reported nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers out after storms in July 2026. A battery sized for a few hours covers most summer storms. For outages that last days, the answer is a generator circuit that carries the camera equipment. We size the battery for your utility and your house, and test it by switching the power off before we leave.

Compatibility and existing equipment

We already have a few cameras from another brand. Can a new AVYCON recorder use them?

Often, yes. AVYCON's support FAQ says that if a third-party camera supports the ONVIF standard, you can add it to an AVYCON NVR using the ONVIF protocol. We check each camera's model and settings before relying on it, because ONVIF support varies by model and firmware, and some features, such as a camera's own smart detection, may not carry over. Older analog cameras on coax are a different case; AVYCON's app also works with its hybrid DVRs, which can be an option where coax is already in the walls.

Our AVYCON recorder has two network ports. How are cameras added to a dual-port model?

AVYCON's support FAQ lists this as a separate question, because dual-port recorders let the cameras sit on their own network, separate from the house or office network the recorder uses to reach the internet. In practice we connect the cameras, or the PoE switch that feeds them, to the camera-side port, set the addressing so the recorder finds them, and connect the other port to your router. Keeping cameras on their own network is good practice on a business system, because it keeps camera traffic off the office network and keeps the cameras themselves away from the internet. We set it up and label which port is which so nobody swaps them later.

Why the county matters

Travel time from our Bronx office and local labor conditions differ across the region, so Westchester and Ulster are not priced the same. Your county is the one on your tax bill, which is not always the one your mailing address suggests, since some postal towns cross county lines. If you are not sure, tell us the street address and we will confirm which county page applies before we quote.

How to read a quote for a valley property

Take a farmhouse with a detached barn. The quote lists the recorder and drive, each camera, each cable run, the link or switch that reaches the barn, the battery backup, and the labor for each part, all from the county list. Move the same plan to a stone house in a historic district and the masonry drilling and any painted line hides appear as their own lines, with nothing else changing. Nothing is added on the day unless you ask for it, and if we find something on site that the photos did not show, we stop, show you, and agree the change before continuing.

FAQ

Hudson Valley AVYCON Camera Installation FAQ

Is there an AVYCON office or factory dealer somewhere in the Hudson Valley?
+

Not that we could find. AVYCON lists its headquarters at 16682 Millikan Avenue in Irvine, California, with a second office in Korea, and sells its cameras and recorders through distributors such as ADI Global Distribution. We found no AVYCON office or certified dealer listed in the six counties. We buy through that distribution channel and install under our own NYS license, and we handle the setup and support ourselves.

How is a private AVYCON system different from the Flock cameras Ulster and Dutchess pulled back from?
+

Flock is a network of license plate reader cameras used by police and governments; Ulster's county executive barred county departments from using it in October 2026, and Dutchess is removing its cameras. A private AVYCON system is a recorder in your own building with cameras on your own property, and nobody sees the footage unless you give them a login or choose to share a clip. We aim cameras at your doors, drive and lot, not at the public road.

How long does a new AVYCON camera take to show up once it is plugged into the recorder?
+

About a minute, according to AVYCON's own FAQ, when the camera is connected directly to a PoE port on the back of the NVR. That quick start is why most of an install day is spent on cable, mounting and sealing rather than on the cameras themselves. Cameras on a separate PoE switch or a dual-port recorder need their network settings checked, which we do during commissioning.

Can AVYCON camera video feed into other software, like a home automation system?
+

Usually, yes. AVYCON cameras typically stream over the RTSP protocol on port 554, which many video and home automation programs can read. Whether a particular program works well depends on the program and the camera model. If you want the video somewhere other than the AVYCON recorder and app, tell us what system you use and we will check before you rely on it.

Do single AVYCON cameras without a recorder need anything extra for storage?
+

Yes. The AVYCON ONE app listing says standalone cameras require a microSD card for storage and playback. A standalone camera can make sense for a single shed or a spot no cable will reach, but a recorder-based system is more robust for a whole property, because the footage sits on a drive indoors rather than on a card in a camera someone could pull off the wall.

The AVYCON ONE app has mixed reviews. Will you make sure it works on our phones before you leave?
+

Yes, that is part of every install. The App Store listing shows a rating of 2.9 out of 5, and one reviewer said the app hung often and that their installer struggled to save the device in it. We install the app on each phone that needs it, add and save the recorder, test live view and playback on the phone's own data as well as on Wi-Fi, and show you how to export a clip.

Which AVYCON recorder do you usually put in a Hudson Valley house?
+

For many houses it is a 16-channel recorder such as the AVR-NSV16P16, a 4K UHD NVR with built-in PoE that our Hudson Valley NVR page lists among the models we install. Smaller homes can use fewer channels and farms or warehouses may need 32. We size the recorder so there are spare channels left over, which lets you add a camera on the garage or barn later without replacing the box.

Can a long-zoom camera cover the far end of a big lot or a waterfront?
+

Often, yes. AVYCON's NDAA-compliant product list includes long-zoom pan-tilt-zoom cameras, such as 5MP 40x and 8MP 25x models. A PTZ camera can sweep a field, a dock or a long drive and zoom in on a distant gate. It only records where it is pointed at any moment, so we usually pair it with fixed cameras on the doors and drive and set the PTZ on a patrol or to watch one key spot.

Neighbors found a hidden camera in the bushes casing their house. Can our system help spot that kind of thing?
+

It can help. Police in the Lower Hudson Valley told WRRV that burglars had placed camouflaged cameras in bushes outside homes in Scarsdale. A camera covering the yard and the approach to the house records anyone who walks into a garden bed to plant something. We aim cameras along the edges where someone would have to stand, and set people detection so a stranger lingering in a flower bed sends an alert.

What do Hudson Valley Yelp reviewers complain about most with camera installers, and how do you avoid it?
+

One Wappingers Falls reviewer complained that an installer did not connect a sensor and simply tucked it into the wall, while the praise we saw for Poughkeepsie-area installers was for clean installation and neatly concealed wiring. We test every camera on the recorder, label every cable at both ends, leave a camera map, and walk you through each view before we leave, so nothing is left unconnected or hidden.

Does the six-county area include Yonkers and the river towns right above the Bronx?
+

Yes. Wikipedia describes the Hudson Valley as running from the Capital District south to Yonkers at the city line, and our six-county coverage takes in all of Westchester, including Yonkers, along with Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster. Properties in Westchester use the Westchester price page; Bronx addresses use the Bronx page.

If the police ask for footage after a break-in, how do we get it to them?
+

You decide what to share. During handoff we show you how to find the time on the recorder or in the app, export the clip and save it to a phone or a USB drive. The recording stays on your recorder until the drive writes over it, so if something happens, export the clip quickly and note how many days your system keeps. Giving the police a copy is your choice; the system does not send anything on its own.

Is a Hudson Valley recorder safe from lightning, or should we expect to replace it?
+

No recorder is immune, and our Hudson Valley NVR page lists replacing lightning-killed recorders among the jobs we do. You can lower the risk. We put surge protection and a battery backup on the recorder, switch and router, ground outdoor cable runs where the building allows, and keep long runs to outbuildings protected. If a storm does take the recorder, the cameras often survive and only the box needs replacing.
