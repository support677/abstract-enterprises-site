TERMINOLOGY

Ulster Commercial Security Terms, Translated

The vocabulary that decides what an Ulster quote actually contains.

PoE / PoE+. Data and power on one network cable per camera, reader or intercom — one battery-backup point in the closet instead of a wall-wart behind every device.

NVR vs DVR vs VMS. The recorder generations: DVR for analog coax, NVR for IP, VMS as the software layer above recorders and sites. A 2026 Ulster quote built around a DVR deserves a follow-up question.

ONVIF. The interoperability standard keeping cameras and recorders brand-independent — the difference between owning a system and renting a vendor.

OSDP. The encrypted reader-to-panel protocol replacing legacy Wiegand wiring, which can be skimmed at the door with a pocket device. Ask for it on every new opening.

Fail-safe vs fail-secure. What each locked door does when power drops — release or hold. Egress and fire code decide per opening; preference does not.

Electric strike vs maglock. Two ways to electrify a door: the strike cooperates with the latch; the maglock holds by force, suits storefront glass, and carries strict egress rules.

REX. Request-to-exit — the sensor or bar letting people leave freely while the street side stays locked. Skipped, the door either alarms all day or traps someone.

Limited-access area. The regulated-retail term for spaces — vaults, storage, back-of-house — where entry must be restricted and logged. Credentials plus audit trails are how the requirement gets met in hardware.

Mobile credential. The phone as key: revoked in seconds, rarely lent out — and no locksmith mileage when seasonal staff move on.

Dual-path communicator. Alarm reporting over cellular and internet together — in a county where valley internet drops with the weather, the cellular leg is not optional.

Video verification. A clip rides with the alarm signal so the central station escalates on evidence — the difference-maker where response is measured up a valley.

Retention. Days of footage held before overwrite. Insurers set the floor for most businesses; for regulated retail the state sets it in writing, and the drives have to obey it.

Edge analytics. Person, vehicle, line-cross and loiter detection on the camera itself, alerting live instead of after a scrub session.

Chapter 185 license. Ulster licenses electrical work at the county level under Chapter 185 of the county code, with Kingston’s city department and town and village building offices layered on top. Which pieces reach a low-voltage security scope depends on the work and the address; we flag it at the survey, and your own municipality gets the final word.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Real Questions from Ulster County Business Owners

What's a realistic security-system number for an Ulster business?

County working numbers: a village or Stockade storefront package of cameras plus a monitored alarm lands in the low four figures; an inn, taproom or practice with cameras and a few doors on credentials runs mid four figures; an integrated build — twelve to twenty cameras, three to five controlled doors, monitored intrusion — commonly falls in the $12,000–$38,000 class; dispensary compliance builds and yard-and-outbuilding properties climb from there on required coverage, trenching and distance. The spread between two quotes is rarely the cameras — it is doors, driveways and what got quietly excluded. A fair quote itemizes all of it.

How do I vet a security installer's licensing for an Ulster job?

Layer by layer, all checkable. Statewide, security and fire alarm installation is licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D — verify any company with the Department of State; our number is 12000287431. Then the local layer: Ulster licenses electrical work at the county level under Chapter 185 of the county code, and Kingston’s city building department plus each town and village add their own rules. Ask which licenses the installer holds for your address, confirm with your municipality directly, and require a COI naming your business and landlord before anyone touches a wall.

Does Kingston or my town require registering a monitored business alarm?

Practice varies by jurisdiction, and it is worth a five-minute call before monitoring goes live: Kingston and several towns handle monitored systems through their own ordinances and police agencies, others informally through the sheriff, and false-alarm-fee rules change at municipal lines. An unregistered or false-alarm-prone system can mean fines or slower response at exactly the wrong moment. Because the answer is address-specific across a city and twenty towns, we identify it for your property at the survey, handle paperwork where it applies, and tell you plainly to confirm the current rule with your own clerk or police agency.

Is a full-payment-up-front security quote ever legitimate?

No. Deposit-and-balance is the professional structure — ours is fifty percent down, balance at completion — and paying everything up front removes your leverage on the punch list entirely. It is also the common thread in the installed-and-vanished stories behind our takeover calls, which in a county of village main streets travel fast. A written scope, a deposit structure and a named warranty are the floor of a professional proposal; anything less is a story you are being asked to trust.

Am I allowed to wire my own cameras at my Ulster shop?

On property you own, generally yes — Article 6-D licenses businesses installing for others, and owners on their own premises sit outside it. The lane narrows fast: county Chapter 185 and municipal rules can reach parts of the work, leases put alteration language and COIs in play, monitored certificates need a carrier-recognized installation, electrified hardware on egress doors is code territory, regulated retail must meet written state security minimums a weekend install will not satisfy, and anything near a building fire alarm is off the table. A camera over your own register in a building you own is a fine project; a leased Stockade unit or a licensed dispensary is not.

The building came with decades of old alarm and camera wire — keep any of it?

Often, and an hour on site answers it. Sound alarm wiring frequently survives for contacts and keypads, existing coax can carry IP video over converters, and pathway — the expensive part through plaster, stone and finished ceilings — is reusable even when its cable is not. Access control is the usual exception: door hardware wants dedicated home runs, and legacy reader wiring is often the skimmable old protocol worth retiring. The proposal says keep, convert or replace, line by line, because billing you to rip out working cable is billing you twice.

Wired or wireless across an Ulster property with outbuildings?

Wired PoE is the commercial default — one cable per device, centralized backup, no battery fleet. Ulster is also where wireless earns real exceptions: a barn past any sensible trench, a gate pylon at the road, a dock or event-field camera. The honest answer for most properties here is a wired backbone with engineered point-to-point links — proper bridges, not consumer Wi-Fi — decided position by position at the survey, with the driveway trench priced honestly when wire is worth it.

How long should footage survive at an Ulster business before overwriting?

Thirty days is the working floor — claims, carrier investigations and police follow-ups reach back weeks, and seasonal businesses often learn about an incident long after it happened. Cash-heavy and higher-liability operations commonly hold sixty to ninety, and regulated retail holds whatever the state writes, with the recorder sized to prove it. Seasonal properties deserve one more question: if the building goes dark for three months, retention and health alerts have to be designed so the system is provably recording the whole time.

A storm takes out power up the valley for two days — then what?

A properly built system keeps working in the ways that matter: cameras record locally on a UPS-backed recorder and PoE switch, door controllers enforce cached credentials, and the alarm rides its cellular path throughout — monitoring never goes silent. Remote viewing and cloud sync pause and catch up on reconnect. Pair the system with the generator half the county already owns and the runtime question disappears. If nobody can tell you what each door and the recorder do at hour six of an outage, that is the finding.

What separates a real business system from the kit at my house down the road?

Consequences and paperwork. A home system protects property; a commercial system also has to satisfy an insurance carrier, a lease, egress and fire code at controlled doors, sometimes a state regulator, and, at claim time, a lawyer asking for footage from a specific Tuesday. That means continuous-duty hardware, monitored certificates carriers recognize, per-person codes through seasonal turnover, and retention sized to claims — requirements a doorbell kit was never built to meet. Half this county’s businesses run house-grade gear; the survey is where that conversation gets honest.

The landlord wants to see insurance before we mount anything — is that the norm?

Completely standard: a certificate of insurance naming the landlord as additional insured, at their stated limits, before mobilization — sometimes with a note on where equipment can mount on a historic frontage. Any commercial installer should produce the COI within a day without drama; hesitation usually means thin coverage. We issue COIs before mobilization as a matter of routine and can walk your lease’s alteration clause at the survey so the design sits cleanly inside it.

Our installer stopped answering years ago and the system limps along — options?

Takeover is standard work for us, not a favor — and the vanished-installer version is half the pile in this county: retirements, acquisitions, one-truck shops that folded. The path out: we audit what exists, recover or factory-rebuild locked recorders and panels, replace only what is actually dead, migrate monitoring month-to-month, and put the system under our health monitoring and published rate. Check any old agreement for auto-renewal language before canceling — then bring whatever paperwork survives. We have rebuilt from none.

Is the integrated cameras-doors-alarm platform overkill for a small Ulster operation?

Not when the pieces already failed separately — and not where seasons and distance raise the stakes. On one platform the forced gate pulls its clip, the alarm arms off the last code-out, the lot camera logs the plate onto the same timeline, and one service agreement covers it all. On a storefront-plus-outbuilding or inn-plus-annex property, integration is usually cheaper than the pieces, because the survey, trenching and mobilization get paid for once.

What's the actual calendar for an Ulster install, start to finish?

On-site time from county work: one to two days for a storefront package; two to four for an inn or practice with controlled doors; three days to two weeks for integrated and yard builds, with trenching the usual long pole; dispensary compliance builds run on the license timeline, and we sequence to it. The calendar before the crew is short — municipal paperwork where it applies, a landlord COI, sometimes a utility locate — and we schedule around your season rather than through it.

My lease is silent on security systems — who's responsible for what?

Silence usually means yours, inside your own unit: base-building systems stay the landlord’s, everything within the demised premises defaults to the tenant, and the alterations clause still governs how your system goes in. Two things to paper now rather than at surrender: written consent for the install with the COI, and trade-fixture language confirming your equipment leaves with you. We read the alterations and surrender clauses at the survey when owners bring the lease, which is the cheap moment to settle both.

Half our summer staff just left — what should happen to their access?

The seasonal-turnover checklist, same day: revoke credentials everywhere — fobs, mobile, PINs die in one click on a managed system, logged; retire any shared alarm code for per-person codes so every future exit is administrative; pull remote camera and monitoring logins; then read the recent audit trail with fresh eyes. If the property still runs physical keys, every lock a departed hire could open gets rekeyed or stays a known hole — and out here the locksmith mileage alone is the business case for credentials.

Our whole camera setup lives in an app — where does that bite an Ulster business?

At the gap. When the building’s internet drops — and on valley connections it drops with the weather — a cloud-only system records nothing for the duration, discovered weeks later when footage from that window is needed. Add per-camera licensing forever and uploads throttled by whatever bandwidth the road offers, and the sticker was not the price. The fix is architecture: local recording as the source of truth, cloud as the access layer. One question filters any proposal: where does video live when the internet is down?

Which lines in a monitoring agreement deserve a hard no?

Four: a multi-year term when month-to-month exists industry-wide; auto-renewal for another full term unless cancelled in a narrow window — calendar it the day you sign or refuse it; hardware that stays the company’s property or runs locked firmware, which is how exit fees become ransom; and unstated mid-contract service rates. None of those protect you; all of them protect the seller’s revenue. Our structure is the counterexample — owned equipment, month-to-month monitoring, a published $195/hr rate — and asking any bidder for the same is the fastest read on their model.

Does a seasonal or farm-based Ulster business need anything different?

The subsystems are the same; the calendar and the paperwork shift. Off-season arming, freeze and power alerts and provable recording through dark months become the design core; farm and event operations add gate, fuel and cold-storage coverage; owner-occupied land sidesteps landlord consent, but the moment monitoring, employees or an insurance rider enters, the commercial logic returns — certificates, retention sized to claims, credentials over shared keys. The county runs on exactly this hybrid, and the survey is where the line gets drawn honestly.

What is a fair per-door price for access control in Ulster County?

Plan around $1,200–$2,800 installed for a standard commercial door — reader, electrified hardware, contact, request-to-exit and programming. Easy openings can come in near $500–$900; storefront glass and fire-rated doors reach $5,000–$7,000; new cable runs add $250–$600 per door, and gates and engineered wireless links price separately. Per-door quotes issued without a walkthrough are guesses.

Is cloud video storage a sound choice for an Ulster company?

Reputable commercial cloud platforms — encrypted in transit and at rest, centrally patched, multi-factor admin enforced — are typically safer than the forgotten on-premises recorder running factory credentials on an open port. In this county the honest caveat is bandwidth: cloud-first architectures lean on valley connections that throttle uploads and drop with the weather, so local recording stays the source of truth and cloud rides above it. Either architecture done properly is sound; either one neglected is a liability.

Do power and internet outages blind an Ulster security system?

Not one designed for this county. Cameras keep recording on a battery-backed recorder and PoE switch, door controllers enforce cached credentials, and a dual-path communicator keeps the alarm reporting over cellular through outages that here ride every storm off the mountains — pair with a generator and the runtime question disappears. Remote viewing and cloud sync pause and recover on reconnect. Each door’s outage behavior is a deliberate, code-driven choice made per opening — ask for it in writing.
