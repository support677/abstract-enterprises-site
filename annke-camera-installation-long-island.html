The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need ANNKE Camera Installation

On this page, "Long Island" means what most people on Long Island mean by it: Nassau and Suffolk. Wikipedia notes that the term often refers specifically to those two counties, leaving out Brooklyn and Queens because they are part of New York City. That distinction matters for an ANNKE install, because almost none of the city rules a Brooklyn customer worries about apply out here. There is no Landmarks Preservation Commission, no HPD, no Department of Buildings review of a camera on a brownstone. Instead there are two counties with very different shapes. The state's county profile describes Nassau as two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets packed into 453 square miles. Suffolk is divided into ten towns spread across 2,373 square miles. A camera job in a village near the city line and a camera job on the North Fork are both "Long Island," but the distances, the houses and sometimes the paperwork are different, which is why we keep separate price lists for Nassau County ANNKE installs and Suffolk County ANNKE installs.

What the two counties share is the detached house. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau, of which 364,529 are one-unit detached homes, and 579,790 units in Suffolk, of which 463,095 are detached. The median Nassau home was built in 1955 and the median Suffolk home in 1970. A Newsday analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026 found that more than 5,000 of the houses sold were built in the 1950s and 1960s. So the typical ANNKE call on Long Island is not a lobby, a hallway or a storefront under an apartment. It is a family home on its own lot, with a front door, a back door, a driveway, a side yard and very often a garage, and the owner wants to see all of them from a phone. That is exactly the job a four- or eight-camera ANNKE PoE kit is sold for, and exactly the job where the kit's assumptions start to run out.

The postwar houses are the reason. The Encyclopedia.com history of Levittown records that the first Levittown began in 1947 on 1,300 acres of potato fields and, by the time it was finished in 1951, covered more than 5,000 acres with some 17,500 single-family homes. The first 6,000 were Cape Cods offered for rent; in 1949 Levitt and Sons dropped the Cape Cod and introduced a ranch. Keri Murray Architecture describes those ranches as built on concrete slabs, with an expandable attic, no garage, and hot-water radiant heating. History.com adds that before Levittown, William Levitt built 1,080 homes in Westbury and 2,250 in Roslyn. Seventy years on, US History Scene notes that there has been so much remodeling that very few original Cape Cods are left in Levittown at all. For a camera installer that adds up to two practical facts: there is often no basement to run cable through, and no two remodeled houses on the same street have the same attic, the same soffits or the same wiring left behind by the last owner.

The North Shore is a different world again. Wikipedia's history of the Gold Coast mansions describes more than 500 lavish private estates built from the early 1890s along the North Shore, concentrated in about 70 square miles, and the Gold Coast Mansions guide places them from Great Neck east to Huntington. After the Second World War many were demolished and their estates subdivided into suburban developments. Some survive as public places: the Vanderbilt estate is a museum run by Suffolk County, Oheka was restored after being repurchased in the mid-1980s, and Old Westbury Gardens, the John S. Phipps estate built in 1906, is open to visitors. The private homes that grew up on those subdivided estates often sit on bigger lots, behind gates and long drives, with pool houses, cottages and detached garages that are well beyond the reach of a 60-foot cable. On those properties the first ANNKE question is not which camera, but how the signal crosses the lawn.

Then there is the reason people call at all. Long Island news in the last year has carried a steady run of stories where home cameras did the work. NBC New York reported in November 2025 on a crew of four charged with burglaries in Manhasset and Jericho, including a home where two girls were alone, and noted that security cameras showed how quickly the suspects reached the second floor. Daily Voice reported in May 2026 that a Roslyn Heights homeowner spotted would-be burglars on his own system and called police, leading to arrests. In January 2024, ABC7 reported on a multi-agency burglary task force and quoted police as believing the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways. In November 2025, CBS New York reported that the Suffolk District Attorney had charged 14 men in a porch-theft ring that hit 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, taking more than 200 phones and tablets. News 12 has run porch-theft clips from Walters Avenue in Wantagh and Atlantic Avenue in Massapequa Park, and CBS reported a thief who waved at the camera on Rider Avenue in Patchogue. Long Island Life & Politics reported that Nassau burglaries rose 5 percent, from 234 to 245, in January through May of 2026 compared with the year before, even while overall property crime fell.

Those stories have a common thread that matters for an ANNKE system: the footage that helped was footage someone could find quickly and hand over. A driveway camera that records in 4K is no use if it is pointed at the sky after a nor'easter, if the recorder's hard drive filled up months ago, or if nobody in the house knows how to pull a clip off it. The weather here makes that worse. ABC7 reported that during Isaias in August 2020, almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million Long Island customers lost power at the peak. In August 2024, the state Department of Financial Services described the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County, the failure of the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam, and flood damage to more than 2,000 residents and business owners. Cameras on Long Island are mounted on the side of the house that takes the wind, and wired to recorders that sit in garages and basements that can flood. We plan for both.

Finally, there is the brand itself. ANNKE is a popular choice for Long Island homeowners who want a wired, recorder-based system without a monthly plan, and it is also a brand with a complicated pedigree. IPVM, the security-industry research publication, lists ANNKE in its directory of brands it says sell Hikvision-made hardware. In June 2026 the FCC voted to expand its restrictions on Hikvision and related equipment, and a public notice effective July 16, 2026 now limits the import and marketing of certain previously authorized equipment for public-safety, government and national-security purposes, while saying that continued use of lawfully purchased equipment is not affected. Not every source agrees about ANNKE: one NDAA database describes ANNKE as China-based and not on the FCC Covered List, with some shared lineage with Hikvision-style hardware in older models. We explain what that means for a homeowner, and for a business that takes federal money, in the questions further down. The short version is that a homeowner who already owns an ANNKE kit can keep using it, and we will install it properly.

We want to be clear about what our research did not find. We searched for Reddit threads, Yelp complaints and local news specifically about ANNKE installations on Long Island and found none. We found no county-wide rule from Nassau or Suffolk on exterior cameras, and no village rule we could cite on camera placement in a local historic district. Where this page describes what we find in houses of a certain age, it is drawing on the public record about when and how those houses were built and on our own field practice, not on a survey of Long Island homes.

Nassau: dense, older, and short on basements

Nassau was the first wave of suburbia, and it shows in the houses. With a median build year of 1955, the county is full of the postwar types the Levitts and their imitators built by the thousand: small ranches and Capes on slabs, later split-levels that add a half-basement under part of the house, and colonials from the 1960s. On a tight Nassau lot the cameras are rarely far from the recorder in a straight line; the difficulty is the path. With no basement under a slab ranch, the cable has to go up, not down. That means the attic, and in a house that has been remodeled two or three times, the attic may have been cut up by dormers, knee walls and new insulation. We look into the attic before we quote whenever the photos do not show it, and we plan the camera positions around where a cable can actually come down through a soffit, rather than putting a camera where it looks good and discovering later that there is no way to wire it.

Nassau is also a patchwork of villages. Two cities, three towns and 64 incorporated villages means that the rules on contractors, building permits and anything mounted on the outside of a house can vary from one side of a street to the other. One Long Island installer's FAQ says that in most cases no permit is required for a standard residential or commercial camera installation on Long Island, and that matches what we see for low-voltage cameras on a private house, but it is an installer's statement, not an official ruling. We check the village or town for your address before the visit, and where a homeowners' association or a co-op board has rules about exterior equipment, we ask you to get them to us in advance.

Suffolk: newer houses, longer distances

Suffolk is younger and much bigger. A median build year of 1970 means more houses with full basements, more attached and detached garages, and wiring and insulation that are easier to work around than in a 1950 Cape. The challenge in Suffolk is space. On a two-acre lot in the middle of the county or a farm property on the North Fork, the camera that matters most may be on a barn or a detached garage well over a hundred feet from the house, and a gate camera at the end of a long drive can be further still. An ANNKE kit's included cables are not built for that, and a single copper Ethernet run has a distance limit that a large property can exceed. We measure, and where a run is too long we plan a weatherproof switch at the far building or a different path, rather than stretching a cable past what it can carry.

Suffolk's licensing also works differently. The Suffolk County Code, Chapter 563, Article XI, makes it unlawful to carry on a regulated trade for hire in the county without a license from the Office of Consumer Affairs, and it allows a restricted license for a limited area within a trade. The same article says it does not apply in a town or village that has passed its own licensing law, or in a town of the second class that opts out. One trade source also notes that some East End towns, Southampton among them, use their own inspectors. So "is this installer licensed for Suffolk?" can depend on which town you live in. We hold New York State license #12000287431 for low-voltage security work and we tell you, before the visit, what applies at your address.

The East End and the seasonal houses

Past Riverhead the island splits into the North Fork and the South Fork, and the jobs change again. Many homes there sit empty for part of the year, and the East Hampton Star has reported police releasing a still from an indoor camera in one of two burglarized houses on Sammy's Beach Road. For an owner who is not there, the ANNKE system has to do three things a year-round family takes for granted: keep recording through a winter of storms with nobody to reset it, warn the owner when something changes, and keep the recorder somewhere a thief cannot simply carry it out. We plan East End jobs around fewer, longer visits and a recorder that is hidden, powered through a battery backup, and set to alert the owner if the cameras go offline.

From the Kit Box to the Farthest Corner: What to Check Before an ANNKE Install

Most of the time lost on an ANNKE job is lost before the first camera goes up: a recorder that will not accept the cameras it came with, a cable that turns out to be twenty feet short of the garage, a router in an upstairs bedroom on the far side of the house, or an attic that turns out to have no way in. Ten minutes with the kit box, a tape measure and a phone camera prevents nearly all of it. Please do not mount cameras or drill holes in the siding to test positions; just look, measure and send us what you find.

Decide what each camera must prove

The best way to plan an ANNKE system is to stop thinking in cameras and start thinking in questions. Who came up the driveway at three in the morning? Which car was it? Who took the package from the step? Who opened the side gate? Each question needs a camera at a particular height, distance and angle, and a camera that answers one well often answers another badly. A wide panoramic camera over the garage is excellent for seeing everything in the driveway, and poor for reading a plate at the curb. A turret aimed down at the front step will show a face, and nothing of the street. Write down the three or four things you most want your footage to show if something happens, and we will tell you which cameras, in which places, will show them.

Pick the recorder's home with care

The recorder is the part of an ANNKE system a burglar would most like to take, and the part a flood or a power cut hurts most. A shelf next to the router in an open hallway is convenient for cabling and easy for a thief. A garage floor is easy to reach and the first place water goes, which the August 2024 Suffolk floods made plain. The best spot is usually a closet or a high shelf inside the house, near the router or with a cable to it, on a battery backup, out of sight from the windows. If that spot is far from the cameras, the cables get longer, and we would rather run longer cables than leave the recorder where it can be carried off in thirty seconds.

ANNKE Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Long Island's houses look alike from the street and turn out very different from the attic. Each type below changes three things about an ANNKE job: how the cables reach the recorder, what the cameras are fixed to, and what the weather will do to them. These are the types the public record and our own Long Island pages describe, and how we handle each.

Levitt-Era Ranches on Slabs

The 1949 to 1951 Levittown ranches, built on concrete slabs with an expandable attic and hot-water radiant heat. No basement, so every cable goes up into the attic and comes down through a soffit or an exterior wall.

Remodeled Capes

The Cape Cods of Levittown, Westbury and Roslyn, almost all of them reworked since. Dormers, raised roofs and finished attics change where cable can travel; we look before we promise a camera position.

Split-Levels and 1960s Colonials

The houses of the 1950s and 1960s sales Newsday counted: partial basements, attached garages, more wall depth. Often the simplest Long Island ANNKE job, with a basement to run along.

Suffolk Houses from 1970 Onward

Newer homes on bigger lots, with full basements, detached garages and long drives. The house is easy to wire; the distance to the outbuildings is the work.

North Shore Estates and Subdivided Estates

Homes on the old Gold Coast land between Great Neck and Huntington, with gates, cottages and pool houses. Long runs, more cameras, and a recorder that must stay hidden.

South Shore and Waterfront Homes

Wood-frame and cedar-shingle houses near the bays and the ocean, where salt air, wind-driven rain and storm surge decide which housings, bases and seals we use.

On slab houses. A Levitt ranch is a good house for cameras once you accept that everything goes overhead. Keri Murray Architecture's history notes the radiant heating in those homes, and we treat the slab as a floor we do not drill. The attic over a ranch is usually low at the eaves, sometimes packed with blown-in insulation, and always hot in July, so we plan the work early in the day in summer and keep the runs along the joists where we can reach them. The recorder in a slab house often ends up in a hall closet or a bedroom closet with a cable up through the ceiling, which is a good hiding place as long as the router can be reached. If the router is at the other end of the house, we run a second cable to it rather than relying on Wi-Fi between the recorder and the router, because the recorder's remote viewing is only as reliable as that link.

On salt air and storms. One of our own Long Island field notes is that waterfront jobs need weather-rated housings for salt air, and that detached garages need their own cable runs. Near the South Shore bays, the North Shore harbors and the East End, we use exterior junction boxes behind each camera so the cable connector sits in a sealed box instead of hanging in the wind, leave drip loops so water runs off the cable before it reaches the wall, and seal every hole in the siding. The Sandy record in Wikipedia's account of the storm describes Nassau's voluntary evacuations of the surge area south of Sunrise Highway. In homes in that zone, the recorder goes on a high shelf inside the house, not on the garage floor.

What a business ANNKE system has to do on Long Island

A homeowner looks at footage when something has happened. A business has to hand it over, often to someone else, often in a hurry, and sometimes with the law watching how long it took. In May 2026, News 12 Long Island reported on a proposed Suffolk County law that would require any business where a felony occurs to preserve all footage and turn it over to police within one day if asked, with fines for failing to do so. The bill followed the killing of a CVS employee in Lindenhurst during a botched robbery, where Suffolk police accused the business of delaying evidence, which the company denied. Long Island Life & Politics said the bill aimed to prevent cases like police waiting nearly 18 hours for surveillance video from a murder scene. Citizen Portal later reported that the sponsor agreed to pursue amendments after civil-liberties objections and the item was recessed for further negotiation. We could not confirm whether it has passed since. Either way, the lesson for a business ANNKE system is the same: the recorder must keep enough days of footage, the clock must be right, and someone on staff must know how to export a clip without calling anyone.

That shapes how we set up a commercial ANNKE recorder. We size the hard drives for the number of days the owner wants to keep, set the time and date to sync automatically so timestamps hold up, label every camera by what it sees rather than by channel number, and show at least two people how to find an incident and copy it to a USB stick or send it from the app. In a flex building in Hauppauge or a store on Jericho Turnpike, we also look at where the recorder sits: behind the counter is the first place a robber looks, and a back office with a locked door is better.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • A New York State license is required. The NYS Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under General Business Law Article 6-D, and legal guidance on that law says the license applies to CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.
  • Suffolk adds county licensing in many towns. Suffolk County Code Chapter 563, Article XI requires a license from the Office of Consumer Affairs for regulated trades for hire, except in towns and villages with their own licensing laws and second-class towns that opt out.
  • Federal money changes the brand question. The restrictions on Hikvision-related equipment mostly bite on federal projects and agencies, and IPVM lists ANNKE among brands selling Hikvision-made hardware. If your business, nonprofit or school is paying with federal or grant money, tell us, and we will check the specific models against current rules before anything is ordered.
  • No cameras where people expect privacy. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and Penal Law 250.45 makes covert recording in places like restrooms and changing rooms a class E felony.
  • Audio is a separate decision. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, according to the Digital Media Law Project; a camera recording conversations nobody present has agreed to is the problem. We leave audio off on business cameras unless the owner has a clear reason and has taken advice.
  • Police registries are voluntary. SCPD's camera registry lets businesses register where their cameras are, free, and the department says it does not get access to the cameras. Garden City PD runs a similar private camera registration. We can help you fill either in.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Why the ANNKE Kit Gets Unboxed on the Kitchen Table First

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one Long Island address. It is the routine our technicians follow on any ANNKE job where the customer bought the kit before calling us, which on Long Island is most of them.

Before a ladder comes off the van, the kit comes out of the box. We set the recorder on a table near an outlet, plug in a monitor, and connect every camera with a short patch cable. We read the recorder's firmware version off the screen and compare each camera's resolution with the recorder's, because ANNKE's own support pages say a camera above the recorder's resolution will not work, and its support told one buyer that an older recorder firmware would not take the FCD600 at all. We watch each camera come up, check the picture, and note anything that does not appear. Only when every camera shows on the screen do we unplug them and walk the property. It takes a few minutes. It is far quicker than finding out from the top of a ladder, with a camera already screwed to the soffit and a cable already pulled through the attic, that the camera and the recorder were never going to talk to each other.

Your Questions Answered

ANNKE Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

The brand and the federal rules

Is ANNKE just Hikvision with a different name on the box?

Not exactly, and the honest answer is that sources disagree. IPVM, the security-industry research publication, lists ANNKE in its Hikvision OEM directory of brands it says sell Hikvision-made hardware, verified through shipping records, documentation or testing. Tec-Tel's compliance list says some ANNKE models are OEM from Hikvision. The cctvinfo NDAA database describes ANNKE as China-based, not on the FCC Covered List, with some shared lineage with Hikvision-style hardware in older models. On the IPCamTalk forum, experienced users describe ANNKE versions as generally stripped-down versions of Hikvision, different in hardware and firmware. For a Long Island homeowner, the practical point is that an ANNKE camera is its own product with its own firmware and its own app, ANNKE Vision, and we set it up and support it as ANNKE, not as a Hikvision.

After the FCC's 2026 action, can I keep using the ANNKE cameras already on my house in Massapequa?

Yes. The FCC's June 2026 public notice, effective July 16, 2026, restricts continued importation and marketing of certain previously authorized covered equipment, and it states that continued use of lawfully purchased equipment is not affected. Sources summarizing the order describe the restricted uses as public safety, government facilities and other national-security purposes, and say the ban mainly applies to federal projects and agencies rather than personal or private use. Whether a particular ANNKE model is even covered is disputed, as the answer above explains. If you own an ANNKE kit today, we will install it, wire it properly and secure it with strong passwords. What may change over time is which new ANNKE models are on sale, so if you are buying, ask us before you order.

Our nonprofit in Hempstead is buying cameras with a federal grant. Is ANNKE a safe choice?

Probably not, and we would check before ordering anything. The restrictions on Hikvision-related equipment bite hardest on federal projects and on spending tied to federal money, and IPVM lists ANNKE among Hikvision OEM brands, even if another database disagrees. A grant-funded buyer does not want to discover after installation that the equipment fails the grant's compliance terms. If your money comes from a federal or federally passed-through grant, tell us at the start; we will read the grant's equipment terms with you, check the specific models against current FCC and NDAA lists, and suggest an alternative where ANNKE is a risk.

Cables, distance and the kit

My ANNKE kit came with 60-foot cables. Will they reach the detached garage at the back of my Commack lot?

Often they will not, and it is better to find that out on paper. ANNKE kits include 60-foot PoE cables, and installation guides note that bigger lots and detached garages often need longer runs. Sixty feet disappears fast once a cable climbs from the recorder into the attic, runs along the house, drops down the outside and crosses the yard. We measure the actual path, not the straight line, and use bulk outdoor-rated cable cut to length. If the garage is far enough that one copper run is a stretch, we put a small weatherproof switch in the garage fed by one cable from the house, which also lets you add a second camera out there later without another trench or another trip through the attic.

I bought one extra ANNKE camera online and my recorder will not show it. Why?

Usually because the camera and the recorder do not match. ANNKE's help center states the rule plainly: the camera's resolution must be equal to or lower than the recorder's, so an 8MP camera works with an 8MP recorder but will not work with a 5MP one. Firmware matters too; ANNKE support told one buyer that a recorder running an earlier firmware, V4.30.010 Build 210511, was not compatible with the FCD600 camera. We read both model numbers and the firmware version, and if a firmware update on the recorder solves it, we do the update carefully, after recording settings are noted, because an interrupted update can leave a recorder unusable. If the recorder simply cannot take that camera, we tell you which ANNKE camera it will take before you buy another.

Can ANNKE cameras record onto a Lorex or Uniview recorder I already have?

That exact question comes up in ANNKE buyer threads on Slickdeals, and the honest answer is that it depends on the specific camera and recorder, and that cross-brand pairings often lose features even when they show a picture. Motion detection rules, smart alerts and two-way audio tend to be the first things that do not carry across brands. We do not promise a mixed setup works until we have connected the actual camera to the actual recorder on the bench. If it works, we set it up and tell you which features you have and which you do not. If it does not, the choice is between matching cameras to the recorder you have, or moving everything onto one ANNKE recorder.

My Wi-Fi ANNKE camera on the back deck keeps dropping off. Is it the camera?

Often it is the connection, not the camera. A third-party ANNKE setup guide notes that ANNKE Wi-Fi cameras support only the 2.4 GHz band, not 5 GHz, and many newer routers combine both bands under one network name, which can confuse a camera during setup. Distance and walls matter too; a back deck on the far side of a brick chimney or an aluminum-sided wall from the router may get a weak signal. One Long Island installer recommends wired over wireless cameras but notes that wiring is much harder once walls are closed up, and we agree with both halves. Where a wired run is practical, we would replace a problem Wi-Fi camera with a PoE one; where it is not, we look at the router's settings and placement first.

The house and the weather

My Levittown ranch has no basement. How do the camera cables reach the recorder?

Through the attic. Levitt ranches were built on concrete slabs, so there is no basement or crawlspace to run along, and we do not drill into the slab. Instead each camera's cable goes from the recorder's closet up through the ceiling, along the attic joists, and down to the camera through the soffit or an exterior wall at the eaves. Where the attic has been finished or blocked by a dormer, we look for chases, closets that stack, or a neat exterior run along the trim painted to match. We look into the attic before we quote whenever we can, because the camera positions on a slab house depend on where a cable can actually come down.

We are a few blocks from the bay in Freeport. Will the cameras survive the salt air and the storms?

They will last much longer if they are installed for it. Our own Long Island field experience is that waterfront jobs need weather-rated housings, and the place salt air does its damage first is usually the cable connector, not the camera body. We put each camera on a sealed junction box so its connector sits inside instead of in the open, leave a drip loop on every cable, seal every hole in the siding, and choose stainless or coated fixings where the salt is worst. The recorder goes inside the house, off the floor, because a garage near the bay is a poor place for it in a surge. We also suggest checking the camera mounts after a big wind event, which takes a few minutes from the ground with binoculars.

When the power went out during the last storm, the whole system went dark. Can it keep recording?

For a while, yes, if it is set up for it. An ANNKE PoE system takes all its power from the recorder, so a battery backup on the recorder keeps the cameras running too, for as long as the battery lasts. During Isaias, almost half of PSEG's Long Island customers lost power at the peak, and the hours after a storm are exactly when people want to see their property. We size a battery backup for the recorder and its cameras, plug the router into it as well so remote viewing survives while the cable or fiber line is up, and set the recorder to restart and resume recording by itself when the power returns.

Neighbors, police and privacy

My neighbor in Garden City says my new driveway camera looks into her yard. What are the rules?

New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance in a place where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and that is the line we install to. A front driveway or a street that anyone can see from the sidewalk is generally different from a neighbor's back patio or the inside of her windows. One installer's guidance is to use digital privacy masking on exterior cameras so they do not capture a neighbor's backyard, and ANNKE recorders let us black out parts of a camera's view so they are never recorded. We set those masks at installation, show the neighbor the result if you want, and keep audio off unless you choose otherwise, since recording a conversation needs at least one participant's consent in New York.

Should I register my ANNKE cameras with the police, and does that let them watch my feed?

Registering is voluntary, and in the programs we found it does not give police your feed. The Suffolk County Police Department's camera registry lets residents and businesses register where their cameras are, it is free, and SCPD says the department does not have access to the cameras; police may ask you to check your video for a date and time, at your convenience. SCPD SHIELD also runs a separate Emergency Remote Camera Access program that partners can opt into or out of at any time. In Nassau, Garden City PD runs a private camera registration that, the department says, does not give it access to privately owned systems. We found no county-wide Nassau County police registry. Whether to register is your call; we can show you how.

"ANNKE is banned, so the cameras have to come down."

This is the claim we hear most since the summer of 2026, and it is wrong for a homeowner. "ANNKE has no connection to Hikvision."

The opposite claim is also too simple. IPVM lists ANNKE in its directory of brands it says sell Hikvision-made hardware, and Tec-Tel notes that some ANNKE models are OEM from Hikvision. Our Installation Process

Every Long Island ANNKE job follows the same eight steps, whether it is a four-camera kit on a Wantagh Cape or a twenty-camera system across a Melville office. The order matters most on the island's houses, where a camera mounted before the cable route is settled is the usual cause of an extra hole in the siding.

1. Send the address and the kit details

The town or hamlet, the house or building type, and the ANNKE camera and recorder model numbers, or a note that you want us to supply the system. We confirm whether the Nassau or Suffolk price list applies and book the visit, usually within 2 hours of your request.

2. Check the kit before it goes on the wall

Every camera connected to the recorder on a table, the recorder's firmware read, and each camera's resolution confirmed as equal to or lower than the recorder's.

3. Walk the property with you

Doors, driveway, side yards, garage and outbuildings walked together, what each camera must show agreed, and neighbors' windows and yards noted for privacy masks.

4. Plan the cable routes

Through the attic of a slab house, along a basement ceiling, across to a garage or along a commercial ceiling pathway, with every run measured rather than trusted to the kit's cables.

Cameras at more than 2 meters, as ANNKE recommends, on bases and junction boxes sized for the siding, brick or block, with every hole sealed and a drip loop on every cable.

6. Set up recording, passwords and masks

A strong password you set and keep, recording schedules and motion zones, privacy masks on neighbors' property, and audio left off unless you choose otherwise.

7. Set up remote and local viewing

The ANNKE Vision app on the phones you choose, alerts for the zones that matter, and a monitor on the recorder so you can watch and play back even when the internet is down.

8. Test, export and hand over

Every camera checked by day and after dark, a clip exported in front of you, and a written note of model numbers, firmware, cable routes and where the recorder and backup sit.

Long Island ANNKE Camera Installation FAQ

How is an ANNKE camera installation priced on Long Island?

From the price list for your county. Do you put in ANNKE systems for Long Island businesses as well as homes?
+

Yes. We install ANNKE systems in stores, offices, flex buildings and warehouses across Nassau and Suffolk, scheduled around opening hours, with recording set up so staff can find and export a clip quickly. If the business is spending federal or grant money, tell us first so we can check the models against current rules.

Is ANNKE a solid brand for a wired PoE camera system?
+

For a home or small business that wants local recording on a recorder it owns, ANNKE is a reasonable choice, and buyers ask the same question on deal forums. Its weak points are the ones this page covers: kit cables that are too short for many lots, strict resolution and firmware matching between cameras and recorders, and an ongoing debate about its Hikvision lineage. Installed carefully, it does the job well.

Besides ANNKE, which camera brands do you repair on Long Island?
+

Our Long Island repair work covers ANNKE 4K kit reconfiguration, ANNKE Vision app pairing and ANNKE-to-Hikvision firmware compatibility, and we also work on Lorex, Dahua, Hikvision, Uniview and other recorder-based systems. If you are not sure what brand you have, a photo of the recorder's label is enough for us to tell you.

How long does a typical ANNKE install take at a Long Island house?
+

It depends far more on the house than on the kit. A four-camera system on a split-level with an open basement is a much shorter day than eight cameras on a slab ranch with a finished attic and a detached garage. Once we have seen photos of the house and know the model numbers, we tell you how long to set aside before we book.

Do I need special tools to put in a wired ANNKE camera myself?
+

Mounting one camera near the recorder needs little more than a drill, but a whole Long Island house usually needs long flexible bits for attic and soffit routes, masonry bits for brick or block, a cable tester, sealed exterior boxes and bulk cable cut to length. If you are weighing doing it yourself against hiring us, the deciding questions are whether your house has a basement and how far the farthest camera is.

The last owner of our house left ANNKE cameras bound to their account. Can you release them?
+

Usually, yes. A camera or recorder tied to someone else's app account has to be released from that account before it can be added to yours. If the previous owner can be reached, that is the quickest route; if not, we reset the devices and set them up fresh under your account, with new passwords, after checking whether there is any footage you want to keep first.

Can we watch our ANNKE cameras on a desktop computer, not just a phone?
+

Yes. Besides the ANNKE Vision app on phones, we can set up viewing on a computer in the house or the office, and we connect a monitor directly to the recorder, which ANNKE notes lets you view the stream without the internet. Many Long Island businesses keep a screen in the back office for exactly this.

Is there a no-drill option if I rent my Long Island house?
+

There are peel-and-stick and battery doorbell cameras on the market, and an ANNKE recorder can sit on a shelf with indoor cameras on furniture. A wired outdoor ANNKE system does mean holes in the siding and the attic, so a renter needs the owner's written agreement first. We are happy to talk the owner through what the holes will be and where.

How high do the cameras go on a one-story Long Island ranch?
+

ANNKE recommends mounting cameras more than 2 meters up, and on a ranch the soffit at the eaves usually gives that height while keeping the camera within reach of a ladder for cleaning. Too high and you see the tops of heads instead of faces; we set the angle at each door so the footage shows the face of someone standing on the step.

Do you travel out to the North Fork and the Hamptons for ANNKE jobs?
+

Yes, East End properties are covered under the Suffolk price list. Because the drive is longer, we plan East End jobs as full-day visits and try to do everything in one trip, so we ask for model numbers, photos and measurements in advance. For seasonal homes we also set up offline alerts so the owner knows if the cameras stop.

How many ANNKE cameras does a typical Nassau or Suffolk home need?
+

There is no single number. Count the doors people use, the driveway, any side gate and any detached garage, then add a camera wherever you would want to know who was there. Many houses end up between a four-camera and an eight-camera kit, but a panoramic camera over the garage can sometimes do the work of two. We plan it with you on the walk-through.

If I add an alarm to my ANNKE cameras, does it need a permit in Nassau or Suffolk?
+

Cameras on their own are not alarms, but if you add an alarm system, Nassau County Police require the residents they serve to register it through their permit program, and Suffolk Police run an alarm management program with its own registration, currently handled by mail. The Nassau Sheriff's Office also runs a separate voluntary registration. We tell you which applies at your address.

Should our ANNKE cameras be indoor or outdoor models?
+

Anything outside, including under a porch roof or inside an unheated garage, should be an outdoor-rated model, because Long Island winters, humidity and salt air will shorten an indoor camera's life quickly. Indoor models suit hallways, stairs and the room with the recorder. We check the rating on every camera before it is mounted.
