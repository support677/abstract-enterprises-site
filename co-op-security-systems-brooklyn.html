The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Co-op Buildings Need Co-op Security Systems Built for How They Are Run

A Brooklyn co-op is a building where the people who live behind the front door also own the front door. That one fact shapes every security decision. When the lobby intercom stops calling apartments, nobody files a complaint against a distant landlord; a board member gets the texts, a treasurer has to find the money, and the managing agent, if the building has one, has to find a contractor who will actually show up. Co-op building security in Brooklyn is therefore less about buying a gadget and more about running a small capital project with volunteers, a budget and shareholders who want their deliveries back.

The borough's cooperative housing is not one type of building, and that matters more here than anywhere else in the city. A real estate closing practice that works these buildings every week describes Brooklyn co-ops as two populations that share almost nothing: small brownstone and limestone corporations in Park Slope, Clinton Hill and Prospect Heights, "frequently four to twelve units and run by the shareholders themselves," and very large postwar complexes near Coney Island and Sheepshead Bay, several of which began as limited-equity or Mitchell-Lama developments. Between those two poles sit the prewar elevator buildings along Eastern Parkway, Ocean Avenue and Shore Road that converted to cooperative ownership in the 1980s, and newer small conversions in Bushwick and Crown Heights where the corporation is only a few years old.

Add the borough's HDFC co-ops, which the same firm places in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Crown Heights, Clinton Hill and Bushwick and describes as small, self-run corporations, many "of under a dozen units, where every shareholder is effectively on the board." The same guide notes that small Brooklyn HDFCs "frequently defer roof, boiler and facade work because the shareholder base cannot fund it." Entry systems get deferred too. In those buildings the intercom is often the oldest working machine in the house, and when it finally fails, there is no maintenance department to call.

So a co-op security system in Brooklyn has to fit the building's governance as much as its walls. A twelve-unit brownstone corporation needs a contractor who will explain options in plain words to five volunteer directors and give them one written number to vote on. A 300-unit prewar co-op on a parkway needs scope language a managing agent can drop into an RFP, phasing that keeps the building open, and a crew that can work around a doorman shift and a freight elevator schedule. A postwar complex with several buildings needs one access control database that every lobby shares. We work in all three, and this page is written for the people who have to make those decisions.

Self-managed and managed co-ops call for different help

Who picks up the phone to call a security contractor tells us a lot about how the job should run. In a managed Brooklyn co-op, the managing agent sends the request, collects the quotes, puts them in front of the board and handles the certificate of insurance and the shareholder notice. The agent wants clean paperwork: a scope that matches the RFP, a quote addressed to the corporation, and a single point of contact for scheduling. In a self-managed co-op, the caller is a shareholder who happens to be the board president or the person who lives on the parlor floor, and who will also be the one letting us into the cellar on a Saturday morning.

Both are normal, and we work with both every week. For self-managed buildings we keep the explanation plainer and the options fewer, because five volunteer directors reading a quote at a kitchen table need to understand it without a consultant. For managed buildings we match the agent's format and timeline. In both cases the corporation, not an individual shareholder, is the client, and the quote says so.

How often Brooklyn entry systems break

New York City publishes every Housing Maintenance Code violation that HPD issues. We pulled the violations whose description mentions an intercom and that were issued in calendar year 2025 from the HPD Housing Maintenance Code Violations dataset on NYC Open Data. Brooklyn buildings received 1,157 of them. By HPD class, 196 were class A, 956 were class B and 5 were class C. Across the city the 2025 totals were 1,637 in the Bronx, 1,157 in Brooklyn, 1,020 in Manhattan, 342 in Queens and 24 on Staten Island.

The difference is that in a cooperative building the shareholders are both the people inconvenienced and the people who pay.

What shareholders complain about

The complaints we hear from Brooklyn co-op boards are remarkably consistent, and they line up with what New Yorkers post publicly. One resident on r/AskNYC described a building that "upgraded" to a buzzer system that rings residents' phones as a call and then failed repeatedly, in a thread titled "Intercom breaks once a month – 311?". Another, on a fifth-floor walk-up, wrote that a dead intercom meant they could not let in "guests, packages, food delivery" without walking down (r/AskNYC). In a co-op those posts become agenda items. The usual list is: the lobby panel calls some apartments but not others; the door release works from the third floor but not the fifth; the phone-dial entry stopped after the building changed its phone service; the side door is propped open because only one person has the key; packages vanish from the vestibule; and nobody can find the footage from last Tuesday.

Each of those has a different fix, and most of them are cheaper than a full replacement. That is the first thing we tell a board: diagnose before you budget. The second is that the intercom is almost always the place to start, because it is the system every shareholder touches every day, and it is the one New York law speaks to directly.

Brooklyn co-op facts that shape the work

  • 1,157 HPD violations mentioning an intercom were issued to Brooklyn buildings in 2025, 956 of them class B (NYC Open Data, HPD Housing Maintenance Code Violations).
  • Four to twelve units: the typical size of Brooklyn's self-managed brownstone and limestone co-op corporations in Park Slope, Clinton Hill and Prospect Heights (Nacmias Law Firm).
  • 1980s conversions: prewar elevator co-ops along Eastern Parkway, Ocean Avenue and Shore Road converted to cooperative ownership in that decade, so many still run on entry wiring older than the conversion (Nacmias Law Firm).
  • Eight or more apartments: the threshold in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a for the lobby intercom requirement in class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 (NY Senate, MDL §50-a).
  • One intercom panel per door opening: the maximum LPC staff can approve on a landmarked building, installed within or next to the door opening (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11).
  • 1965: the year Brooklyn Heights became New York City's first historic district, which is why so many Brooklyn co-op entrances fall under landmarks rules (DNAinfo, Nov. 23, 2015).

Most Brooklyn co-op boards that start with the intercom end up pairing it with key fob access on the side and cellar doors within a year or two, because once the front door is fixed, the propped-open back door becomes the weak point everyone notices.

Co-op intercom systems: repair, upgrade or replace

The intercom is where most Brooklyn co-op security projects begin, and it is where a board's money goes furthest. We repair existing audio and video intercoms, replace lobby panels and apartment stations, and carry out co-op intercom upgrades from audio to video, from analog wiring to IP, and from apartment handsets to smartphone answering. For a board, the decision is rarely technical. It is a co-op board intercom upgrade question: fix the system we have for a known cost, or replace it as a capital improvement and stop paying for repairs. We give boards both numbers when both are honest options, and we write the replacement scope so it can be compared line by line against other bids.

Every co-op intercom replacement we quote names the number of entrances, the panel type at each door, what happens at each apartment (new station, reused station, or app only), whether the existing riser wiring is reused, the door release hardware, and what the board must provide: access to units, power at the panel location, and a building contact. That structure is what lets a treasurer put three bids side by side for an RFP without guessing which one left out the apartment stations. For the installation side in depth, see our page on intercom installation in Brooklyn; when the existing system is the problem, our Brooklyn intercom repair page covers the faults we find most often.

Door buzzers and door releases

In smaller Brooklyn co-ops the "intercom" is often a buzzer system: a button panel at the stoop, a talk-and-listen station in each apartment and a door release on the frame. When a shareholder presses the release and nothing happens, the fault can be the release itself, the transformer in the cellar, a broken wire in the riser or the station in that one apartment. We trace it, fix the failed part and test the door from every floor before we leave. Our door buzzer repair in Brooklyn page lists the part-by-part repair ranges.

Key fobs and access control

Co-op access control in Brooklyn usually means replacing metal keys on the front, side, cellar, laundry, bike room and roof doors with fobs, cards or phone credentials that the board can turn off the day a shareholder sells or a contractor finishes. We install readers, electric strikes and magnetic locks, door contacts and request-to-exit devices, and the controller and software that keep the entry log. A board can see who used the cellar door at 2 a.m. and can stop a lost fob in a minute instead of re-keying the building. The full service is on our page for access control installation in Brooklyn.

We set retention so the board's footage policy can actually be followed, and we give specific board members their own logins. Elevator access control

Larger Brooklyn co-ops with doormen, garages or amenity floors sometimes want the elevator to serve only the floors a fob is allowed to reach. Package rooms and mailroom access

A package room with a fob reader and a camera solves most of what boards describe as package theft, because it moves deliveries out of the open vestibule and leaves a record of who opened the door. We install the reader, the lock hardware and the camera; the board decides who gets access and whether couriers get a time-limited code.

Cabling and network for the systems we install

Modern intercoms, IP cameras and fob readers all ride on low-voltage cable and network gear: Cat6 runs, PoE switches, a riser path from cellar to roof and a small rack in the super's office or the cellar. We run and label that cabling as part of the job. For larger cabling scopes, our page on structured cabling installation in Brooklyn explains how we test and document it. We don't do electrical work: if a panel location needs a new high-voltage circuit, the building's electrician provides it.

A security needs assessment for Brooklyn co-op boards

Before the board votes on any single system, it helps to walk the building with a short list. These are the questions we work through on a free on-site assessment, and a board can use them on its own first:

  • Every way in: front door, vestibule door, service or side door, cellar door, yard gate, roof door, garage. Which of them lock by themselves, and which are propped open during the day?
  • Who has keys: how many copies exist for each door, who holds them (shareholders, the super, the porter, the managing agent, contractors, the storefront tenant), and when the locks were last changed.
  • How visitors and deliveries get in: does every apartment's intercom call and release the door, where do couriers leave packages, and what happens when nobody answers?
  • What is recorded: which entrances and common areas have cameras, how many days of footage the recorder keeps, and who can actually retrieve a clip.
  • Where the equipment lives: power supplies, transformers, controllers and recorders, and whether that room stays dry, cool and locked.
  • Who supports it: the installer's name, the admin logins, the manuals, and whether anyone has been able to reach the original contractor recently.

A board that can answer those six questions knows where its real weak points are, and usually finds that the fix is narrower than it feared: one door, one panel, one missing camera, one recorder that has been full for a year.

Winter and the end of the year

Late autumn is when Brooklyn co-op boards call us most, for two reasons. Holiday deliveries pile up in vestibules, and cold weather exposes weak door hardware. An intercom maker's own guide to cold-weather problems notes that "metal parts contract in the cold", that moisture can get into exterior panels and lock hardware, and advises boards to "review strike alignment and look for doors that need extra pressure to latch or release." A front door that needs a shove in January is a strike that needs adjusting, not a door that needs replacing. Boards that want the system checked before winter or before the annual meeting can book a maintenance visit; we don't offer seasonal discounts, but an early-season booking gets the earliest dates.

Building Specialists

Co-op Building Security for Brooklyn's Cooperative Architecture

The same lobby intercom costs a different amount, takes a different number of days and needs different paperwork depending on what kind of Brooklyn cooperative it goes into. These are the building types we plan around, and what each one changes.

A

Brownstone and limestone corporations

Four to twelve units, stoop entrance, often self-managed. One door panel, a handful of apartment stations, short risers, and a board that may also be the people holding the ladder. Landmarks rules apply on many blocks.

B

Prewar elevator co-ops

The six- to fifteen-story buildings along the parkways and avenues that converted in the 1980s. Long risers, plaster walls, a lobby with two sets of doors, a service entrance on the side street and a managing agent who runs the bid.

C

Postwar towers and complexes

Large southern-shore developments with several buildings, many entrances, garages and a staffed desk. One access control database for every door matters more here than any single panel.

D

HDFC co-ops

Small walk-ups and brownstones, many formed from City-owned buildings, run by shareholders with tight budgets. Repair-first plans and phased upgrades fit best.

E

Mixed-use co-ops

Apartments over storefronts on commercial avenues. The residential entrance, the store and the cellar each need their own access rules and often separate camera views.

F

New small conversions

Recently converted buildings in Bushwick and Crown Heights where the corporation inherited whatever the developer installed, sometimes with no manuals and no admin passwords.

Brownstone and limestone co-ops: small panels, short runs, landmarks

A brownstone co-op is the easiest building in Brooklyn to wire and one of the hardest to get approved, because so many of them sit in historic districts. The physical job is small: a panel at the top of the stoop or on the gate, four to twelve apartment stations or app accounts, a door release on the outer or inner door, and a short riser up the stair hall. Where the original buzzer wiring is intact, a two-wire video intercom can often run on it, which spares the plaster and keeps the job to a day or two.

The paperwork side is where these buildings differ. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook (chapter 11) says staff can approve intercom panels if they are "installed within or adjacent to a door opening," that an intercom "can also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway," that "a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening," and that the design, size and finish must be "neutral and discreet." For a brownstone co-op board, that rules out the oversized stainless panel bolted to the brownstone next to the parlor-floor door, and it favors a compact flush or surface unit set into the door surround or the areaway gate post.

The same chapter limits security cameras on small residential buildings to "no more than two security cameras … for every 25 feet of street frontage," well spaced to avoid "a cluttered, haphazard appearance." A typical 20- or 25-foot brownstone front therefore gets two cameras at most on the facade, which is why we usually put the stoop camera inside the vestibule looking out, and the second one under the stoop or at the areaway.

Prewar elevator co-ops on the parkways

The prewar co-ops along Eastern Parkway, Ocean Avenue and Shore Road are the Brooklyn buildings where intercom projects get most complicated. They have long, tired risers that run through occupied apartments, lobbies with an outer vestibule door and an inner lobby door, a separate service entrance, and sometimes a staffed desk. Many still use apartment handsets installed decades ago. When some apartments can't be reached from the lobby, the fault is often a broken conductor somewhere in a riser that passes through a dozen kitchens.

For these buildings the board's real choice is usually between three paths: repair the existing system floor by floor, replace the lobby panel and keep the apartment wiring with a compatible system, or move to an IP or app-based intercom that needs only network at the lobby and no new apartment wiring at all. The third path avoids entering most apartments for the install, but it changes how older shareholders answer the door, so we always recommend that the board keep an in-apartment option for anyone who wants one.

Postwar complexes along the southern shore

The big postwar co-ops near Coney Island, Brighton Beach and Sheepshead Bay are a different scale of project: several buildings, a dozen or more entrances, garages, laundry rooms and a security desk. Here the building-wide design matters more than any single device. One access control platform should run every door, so a fob issued at the management office works at the right doors in every building and stops working everywhere at once. Cameras should land on one recorder system the desk can watch. And the intercom, if it is being replaced, should be phased building by building so no entrance is ever without a working panel. These complexes also sit in flood zones; the closing guide we cite above flags flood coverage for exactly these buildings, so we keep controllers, power supplies and recorders off cellar floors wherever the board gives us a dry room.

HDFC co-ops in Bed-Stuy, Crown Heights, Clinton Hill and Bushwick

An HDFC board usually has the least money and the most patience of any client we work with. A repair-first plan fits these buildings: fix what can be fixed for a known cost, then replace the oldest component when the budget allows. A small HDFC walk-up can often go from a dead buzzer system to a working video intercom with app answering for less than a larger building spends on one panel, because there are only a few apartments, a short stair hall and one door. We price the repair and the upgrade side by side so the board can vote on one without being sold the other.

What changes with materials and methods

  • Brownstone and brick facades: we fasten into mortar joints, not carved stone, and route cable inside the vestibule or door frame rather than across the face of the building.
  • Plaster risers: we fish new cable through existing chases and pipe sleeves where possible; where the old riser is sound we reuse it.
  • Prewar wood-and-glass doors: we choose door release hardware that fits the stile; an electric strike usually suits a heavy wood door with a good frame, while a mag lock needs a clear header and a code-compliant release.
  • Cellars: power supplies, controllers and recorders go on the wall or a shelf, never the floor, and we label every cable so the next contractor can follow it.
  • Occupied buildings: we work floor by floor with notice, and we need access to every apartment whose station is part of the repair.

Landmarked co-op entrances beyond Brooklyn Heights

Brooklyn Heights was designated New York City's first historic district in 1965 (DNAinfo), but landmarks rules now reach well past the Promenade. In November 2020 the Commission designated the East 25th Street Historic District, which its newsletter called "the first in the neighborhood of East Flatbush, Brooklyn", a short walk from our Troy Avenue office. Before a co-op in any historic district orders a new entry panel or facade camera, we check the address against the LPC map and size the equipment to the staff-level criteria so the permit is routine rather than a hearing.

Commercial Co-op Security

Commercial and Mixed-Use Co-op Security in Brooklyn

Commercial co-op security in Brooklyn mostly means mixed-use buildings: a cooperative that owns the whole structure and leases ground-floor stores, a doctor's office or a garage. The co-op is the landlord of those spaces, and its security systems have to keep the commercial tenants and the shareholders out of each other's doors without making either one's day harder.

Where the mixed-use co-ops are

Mixed-use cooperatives are a feature of Brooklyn's commercial avenues, where four- and five-story buildings with stores below were common long before conversion. On those blocks the residential entrance usually sits beside a storefront, the cellar is shared, and the store's delivery path may run through a side door the building also uses for garbage. We see the same layout repeat on the borough's shopping streets, and the security plan is always the same in principle: three access zones (residential, commercial, shared service), each with its own credentials and its own camera views.

Separating the store from the building

The commercial tenant usually gets its own cameras on its own recorder, paid for and managed by the tenant, with the co-op's system covering only the residential entrance, the hallways and the shared cellar. Where the board prefers one system, user permissions on the recorder let the store manager see only the store's cameras. For doors, the shared service entrance gets a reader that accepts both the store staff's credentials and the super's, while the residential lobby accepts only shareholders and building staff. The co-op keeps the master list.

Commercial tenants and the co-op's own rules

When a commercial tenant wants its own cameras or an access reader on a shared door, the co-op's lease and alteration rules decide what the tenant may install and who pays. We work for whichever party hires us, but on a shared door we ask that the board or agent confirm the work in writing first, so the building's master credential list stays in the building's hands. A tenant's reader on a shared service door should be enrolled in the co-op's system, not run as a separate island that nobody on the board can see.

The same applies to doctors' offices, daycare programs and other professional tenants in Brooklyn co-op ground floors, which often have their own visitor flow. A separate entrance with its own intercom panel calling the office, and no path into the residential lobby, keeps the two worlds apart without anyone holding a key they shouldn't.

Garages, parking gates and loading areas

Larger co-ops with garages need gate and garage-door control tied to the same credentials as the building. We install readers, keypads and vehicle-gate interfaces on the low-voltage side and coordinate with the gate company for the operator itself. A camera that reads the gate approach and one that covers the inside of the garage door answer most of the incidents boards ask us about.

The rules that apply

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system at the self-locking lobby door that allows "voice communication between the occupant of each apartment and a person outside" and lets the occupant "release the locking mechanism of said door from the apartment." Buildings converted before 1968 come under a consent-based provision in the same section. The law also makes it a misdemeanor to "wilfully destroy, damage, or jam" that equipment. A Brooklyn co-op board should ask its counsel how the section applies to its own building; our part is to keep the system that satisfies it working.

Tenant Data Privacy Law. Local Law 63 of 2021 added a Tenant Data Privacy chapter to the NYC Administrative Code. It defines a "smart access system" as any system that uses "electronic or computerized technology, a radio frequency identification card, a mobile phone application, biometric identifier information, or any other digital technology" to grant entry to a class A multiple dwelling, its common areas or an apartment. HPD's guidance says owners of buildings using such systems must give residents a data retention and privacy policy and may collect only the minimum data needed. Whether and how that applies to a particular co-op is a question for the board's attorney; what we do is configure fob and app systems to collect only what the board's policy allows and show the board where that data lives.

Landmarks. Entry panels and facade cameras on landmarked buildings follow the LPC criteria described above. Electrical work. We are a low-voltage contractor; new line-voltage circuits are the building electrician's work. Insurance. We carry general liability insurance and issue a certificate of insurance naming the co-op and the managing agent when the job is awarded.

Intercom decisions for Brooklyn boards

Should a Brooklyn co-op board repair a decades-old audio intercom or replace it?

Repair it if the riser wiring is sound and parts are still available for the lobby panel and stations; replace it when the wiring itself has failed in several places or the manufacturer no longer makes the parts. The $250 service call tells the board which situation it is in. We test the panel, the power supply, the release and a sample of apartments, and report what failed and what is worn. If the system is fixable, the board gets a repair number. If it is near the end, the board gets a replacement quote with the same building measurements, so the two can be compared honestly.

Can a Park Slope or Clinton Hill brownstone co-op get a video intercom without opening walls?

Usually, yes. Many brownstone buildings still have the original two- or four-conductor buzzer wiring in the stair hall, and two-wire video intercoms are designed to run over a pair like that. Where the old wire is broken or too thin, an app-based system answers on shareholders' phones and needs no apartment wiring at all, only network at the door. We confirm which is possible during the walk-through by testing the existing conductors.

How should a Brooklyn managing agent write an intercom RFP so the bids line up?

Specify the building, not the brand. List each entrance and what it needs (panel type, release hardware, camera at the panel or not), the number of apartments and what each gets (indoor station, app, or both), whether existing apartment wiring may be reused, the hours work may happen, how apartment access will be arranged, the warranty term the board wants, and that bidders must state what the building provides. Ask every bidder to price apartment stations as a separate line, because that is where bids most often differ. We answer RFPs in that format; our guide for boards on co-op intercom upgrades covers it in more depth.

Who has to be home when an intercom is repaired in a Brooklyn co-op?

Anyone whose apartment station is part of the problem. Intercom faults in older buildings are linked: a short in one apartment can kill the line for the floors above it, so a repair that can't reach every affected unit often can't be finished. We ask the board or agent to send a notice with the visit date and arrival window and to arrange keys or a building escort for units where nobody will be home.

Does a landmarked Brooklyn co-op need Landmarks approval for a new entry panel?

Yes, new exterior work on a landmarked building needs an LPC permit, but an entry panel that meets the staff-level criteria in chapter 11 of the Permit Guidebook (inside or next to the door opening, one panel per opening, neutral and discreet) can be approved by staff without a public hearing. We size and place the panel to fit those criteria and provide the product specifications the application needs.

Access, fobs and doors

Can a Brooklyn co-op switch the side and cellar doors to fobs and keep keys at the front?

Yes, and many boards start that way. The doors that cause trouble are usually the secondary ones, propped open for deliveries, contractors and laundry. Putting fob readers on those first stops the key copies and gives the board an entry log, while the front door keeps its intercom and key. When the front door is later added, it joins the same system and the same fob list.

What happens to fob access when a Brooklyn co-op apartment is sold?

The board or managing agent deactivates the seller's credentials on the closing date and issues new ones to the buyer after the board approves the sale. With a fob system that takes a minute in the software. With keys, the only equivalent is re-keying every door the seller could open, which is why so many boards make the switch after a sale goes badly.

Mag lock or electric strike on a prewar Brooklyn co-op front door?

On most heavy prewar wood-and-glass doors, an electric strike in the frame is the better fit: it keeps the existing lockset, the door still opens mechanically from inside, and it doesn't hang hardware across the top of a decorative opening. A magnetic lock suits metal doors and frames where a strike can't be fitted, and it needs the release devices the code requires so people can always get out. We look at the door and the frame before recommending either one.

Upgrades, phasing and occupied buildings

Can a Brooklyn co-op replace its intercom in phases over two budget years?

Yes, and in larger buildings it is often the sensible way. A common split is to replace the lobby panel and door release in the first phase, keeping the existing apartment wiring and stations working, and then add video stations or app answering apartment by apartment in the second. In multi-building complexes the phases follow the buildings. We price each phase separately and design the first one so the second doesn't undo it.

What do shareholders notice on the day a Brooklyn co-op lobby panel is changed?

Very little, if it is planned well. The old panel stays connected until the new one is mounted and tested. Then the directory is loaded, each apartment is called from the new panel, and the release is tested from each floor. Shareholders who switch to app answering get an invitation to set up their account. The board should tell residents the date in advance and post a short note in the lobby with a contact for anyone whose station doesn't ring.

How should a Brooklyn co-op board compare three intercom bids that look nothing alike?

Put them on one sheet with the same rows: entrances covered, panel at each entrance, what each apartment gets, whether existing wiring is reused, door hardware, programming and resident setup, warranty term, ongoing software fees, and what the building must provide. A low bid that leaves out apartment stations or resident setup is not a low bid. If a line is missing from one proposal, ask that bidder to price it before comparing totals.

Can the super manage the fob list in a Brooklyn co-op, or only the managing agent?

Either, depending on how the board sets it up. Access control software allows several administrator accounts with different rights, so a board can let the super issue and deactivate fobs while only the agent and one officer can change door schedules or pull entry reports. We create the accounts the board approves and record who holds which one.

What happens to the old intercom wiring after a Brooklyn co-op upgrades?

If the new system reuses the old riser, the wiring stays in service. If it doesn't, the old cable is usually left in place inside the walls, disconnected and labeled, because pulling it out of occupied apartments would mean opening plaster for no benefit. Old lobby panels and apartment handsets are removed and the openings covered or reused for the new equipment.

Cameras, packages and footage

Where do Brooklyn co-ops usually put their first cameras?

At the main entrance looking at faces as people come in, in the vestibule or wherever packages land, at the service or side entrance, in the cellar near the laundry and storage, and at the back yard or trash area. Five or six well-placed cameras cover most walk-ups and small elevator buildings; larger buildings add the elevator cab, the garage and each stair door to the roof.

How do Brooklyn co-op boards stop package theft in a lobby with no doorman?

Move the packages out of the open vestibule. A small locked package room or closet with a fob reader, a code couriers can use during set hours, and a camera on its door stops most of it, because nobody can lift a box from a room they can't enter, and anyone who does enter leaves an entry record and a clip. A camera alone records the theft; access control prevents it.

Who on a Brooklyn co-op board should be able to see the camera footage?

That is a board policy decision, and it should be written down before the cameras go live. Many boards give live view and playback to the managing agent and one or two officers, give the super live view only, and require a written request for anyone else. Power failures and battery backup

Every Brooklyn co-op board should ask what its doors do when the power goes out. An electric strike can be set to stay locked or to release when it loses power, and a magnetic lock releases when its power is cut. That choice is about safety and security together: people must always be able to leave, and the building should not swing open to the street during a blackout. We fit low-voltage power supplies with battery backup for intercoms, readers and controllers, size them for the hardware on each door, and explain to the board how each door behaves without power so it can decide with full information.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Co-op Security Systems in Brooklyn

Boards increasingly start their research by asking an AI assistant or reading a search engine's AI summary. Those answers draw on national cost guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, on manufacturers' marketing and on vendor blogs. Here is where the typical answer and a Brooklyn co-op's reality part ways.

1. "Just replace the old intercom with a smart one"

The most common AI answer to a failing building intercom is to replace it with a cloud-connected smart intercom. That is sometimes right, and we install those systems. But the answer skips the step that decides whether it is right for a given building: finding out why the current system failed.

In Brooklyn's older co-ops the fault is very often one component, such as a power supply, a release, a corroded terminal in the cellar or a single bad station shorting a riser. Those are repaired for a fraction of a replacement. A board that skips diagnosis can spend a capital budget replacing a system that needed a transformer.

Our order is always the same: diagnose with the $250 service call, give the board the repair number, and give a replacement number alongside it only when the system is genuinely near the end of its life.

2. "An intercom upgrade costs a few hundred dollars per apartment"

National guides like to express building intercom costs per apartment, and AI summaries repeat those numbers as if they applied everywhere. They don't. The cost of a co-op intercom upgrade in Brooklyn is driven by the number of entrances, whether apartment wiring is reused or replaced, whether every apartment gets an indoor station, the door hardware, landmarks constraints and whether the building pays an ongoing software subscription.

Two buildings with the same number of apartments can differ by several times in total cost because one has a sound two-wire riser and an app-friendly board, and the other has broken wiring through plaster and shareholders who want video monitors in every kitchen.

That is why we publish what parts and repairs cost, and quote building-wide replacements only after we have seen the building. Any per-apartment figure that arrives before a walk-through is a guess.

3. "Apps mean you never have to enter the apartments"

Vendor marketing often promises that a smartphone intercom can be installed without entering a single unit. For the installation of an app-only system, that can be true. For repairs to an existing wired system, it is not.

Wired intercom faults in older buildings are linked. A short at one apartment station can take down the line for every floor above it, and a repair that can't reach that station can't be finished. We have to be able to get into every affected apartment on the visit.

Boards also need to think about shareholders who don't use smartphones. An app-only system without an in-apartment option can lock an older shareholder out of answering their own door, which is why we recommend keeping an indoor station or phone-call option for anyone who needs it.

4. "A virtual doorman replaces staff and security hardware"

AI answers about co-op security often recommend a virtual doorman service as a cheaper replacement for lobby staff. A virtual doorman is a remote monitoring and answering service, and it still depends on the intercom, cameras and door hardware in the building.

We don't run a virtual doorman or a managed security service. We install and maintain the equipment such a provider uses, and we can integrate with a provider the building already has. A board comparing a virtual doorman with a doorman should get the hardware priced separately from the monthly service, so it knows what it owns.

If the board decides against a monitored service, the same hardware still works: shareholders answer on their stations or phones, and the board's cameras record in the building.

5. "Cloud video storage is the modern standard"

Many AI answers present cloud recording as the default for any modern camera system. For a co-op, cloud recording means a monthly fee per camera, footage stored by a third party and a building connection that has to carry every camera's upload.

Most Brooklyn co-ops we work with prefer local recording on a recorder in the building, with remote viewing for the people the board authorizes. The corporation owns the footage, pays no per-camera subscription, and can set retention to whatever its policy says.

Cloud makes sense in specific cases, such as a building with no secure room for a recorder or a complex where several buildings need one shared archive. We explain the trade-off rather than defaulting to either one.

6. "Any licensed electrician can install building security"

AI answers often treat security installation as an electrician's job. Intercoms, access control and cameras are low-voltage systems, and the skills that matter are tracing riser wiring, programming panels and controllers, and setting up networks and recorders.

We are a New York State licensed low-voltage contractor, license #12000287431, and we work alongside the building's electrician when a panel or controller location needs a new high-voltage circuit. We don't do that electrical work ourselves.

For a board, the practical point is to ask any bidder who programs and supports the system after it is installed. A wiring contractor who leaves without setting up the directory, the fob list and the shareholders' apps leaves the board with hardware nobody can use.

7. "Security upgrades lower your building insurance"

AI summaries sometimes state that cameras and access control will reduce a building's insurance premiums. Some insurers do consider security measures, but there is no rule that guarantees a discount, and any change depends on the co-op's carrier and policy.

We don't promise insurance savings. If the board wants to know, its insurance broker is the right person to ask, and we provide the system description the broker may need.

What we can say from experience is that cameras and entry logs help boards answer the questions insurers and police ask after an incident: who came in, when, and through which door.

Field notes from our Brooklyn crew

The first thing we ask a Brooklyn co-op board is not "what system do you want" but "who can let us into the apartments." On an older wired intercom, the fault is rarely at the lobby panel. It is usually a station in one apartment, a splice in the riser behind a kitchen wall or a corroded terminal block in the cellar, and finding it means testing floor by floor. A visit where half the units are locked is a visit that ends with a partial answer.

The second thing we ask is where the equipment lives. In a lot of Brooklyn buildings the intercom power supply, the door release transformer and the recorder share a damp corner of the cellar with the boiler. Moving them onto a dry wall with a battery backup and labels fixes problems that look like electronics failures.

The third is who holds the passwords. Boards that inherited a system from a sponsor or a departed contractor often have no admin login for the panel or the recorder. We document every credential we set and hand the list to the board, not to one volunteer.
