The Neighborhood

Why City Line Buildings Need Fire Alarms Planned for Converted Spaces and Crowded Hours

City Line needs carefully planned commercial fire alarm work because so many of its buildings now do a job they were never built for. The neighborhood began as farmland and truck gardens at the old edge of the City of Brooklyn, filled in with wooden cottages and then brick row buildings, and took its name from the boundary with Queens County that existed before the 1898 consolidation. Its streets were laid out for one- and two-family homes and corner stores. Today many of those buildings are prayer halls, after-school programs, social service offices and groceries with families living upstairs. A converted building can carry far more people at certain hours than it ever did as a house, and the fire alarm question follows the people, not the facade.

The congregations are the clearest example. When City Limits profiled the neighborhood in August 2013, it counted one main mosque on Forbell Street, built in adjacent houses that Bangladeshi families had bought, and six smaller mosques drawing new immigrants, seven in all. Forbell Street above Liberty Avenue was co-named Mizanur Rahman Way. The same report described about 3,000 men praying together in a parking lot on Eid, and quoted resident Burhan Uddin on how the gathering felt: "Like my country. Really, like my motherland." For fire alarm design, that is the important detail: worship in City Line is a crowded, scheduled, recurring event, often in buildings with narrow stairs and residential framing. Whether a given hall needs a building fire alarm depends on its legal use and occupant load, and that is decided by a design professional reading the NYC Building Code and Fire Code against the actual floor plan.

The commercial streets add a second layer. Liberty Avenue is the neighborhood's main shopping spine, with South Asian, Caribbean and Latino businesses side by side, and Pitkin Avenue, Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue carry more stores, offices and auto-related uses. Brooklyn Community Board 5's own list of community organizations places the Bangladeshi American Community Development and Youth Services office at 1101-B Liberty Avenue, MUNA Social Services at 464 Crescent Street, NAHAR Relief at 2668 Pitkin Avenue, Eihab Human Services at 3021 Atlantic Avenue and Catholic Charities at 3060 Fulton Street. Offices like these are business occupancies, often on a ground floor under apartments, and they host classes, clinics and food distributions that bring in more people than a typical store.

So what is a commercial fire alarm system in a City Line building? It is a listed fire alarm control panel; the smoke detectors, heat detectors and manual pull stations that start an alarm; the horns, strobes and speakers that warn everyone inside; the relays and modules that shut down fans, release held-open doors and recall elevators; and the communicator that sends signals to an FDNY-approved central station, all designed for that one building and approved by the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work when there is a real fire? A detector or pull station signals the panel over a supervised circuit, the panel decides whether it is an alarm, a supervisory condition or a fault, and then it sounds the building, runs its programmed outputs and reports the event. When does a building need a fire alarm system? The NYC codes answer by occupancy group, occupant load, height and fire area, which is why a two-story house used for daily prayers, a grocery with apartments above and a new six-story building with stores can land on very different sides of the line.

Need Fire Alarm Repair in City Line? Read the Panel Before Anyone Presses Reset

When a fire alarm panel in a City Line prayer hall, grocery or office building starts a trouble tone, photograph the display, note the time, and call (347) 934-8335; we book the visit within 2 hours. The panel's message and its history log are the best evidence a fire alarm service technician has, and a reset clears them. In New York City, fire alarm repair, fire alarm system repair and all commercial fire alarm repair or commercial fire alarm service is performed by an FDNY-certified fire alarm company working with a licensed electrician, so the next question is whose service account the building is on. In converted buildings that answer is often lost when ownership or the board changes.

Owners regularly ask why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble if there is no smoke anywhere. Trouble is the panel reporting a problem with itself: an open or shorted circuit, a ground, a device that has gone missing, a weak battery, lost AC power, or a dropped connection to the central station. A fire alarm panel trouble light means a fire alarm system trouble condition is active, and proper fire alarm trouble signal repair fixes the cause rather than silencing the buzzer. A fire alarm supervisory trouble is a separate category: something the panel watches, such as a sprinkler control valve or a duct detector, is not in its normal position, and the building may be less protected than it looks.

City Line's buildings produce their own patterns. In prayer halls created by joining two houses, devices are sometimes left in the old party wall or above a dropped ceiling that was added later, and the panel shows a missing address or a fire alarm intermittent trouble whenever the heating cycles. In cellars on the low blocks south of Liberty Avenue, dampness is the first thing we check when owners ask what causes a fire alarm ground fault; fire alarm ground fault repair and fire alarm ground fault troubleshooting mean breaking the circuit into sections and measuring each one until the leak to ground shows itself. In halls that are packed on Fridays and quiet the rest of the week, the beeping that starts on a Tuesday is usually a fire alarm battery trouble repair waiting to happen, and a fire alarm panel beeping repair often ends with new batteries and a proper load test.

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for City Line's Building Types

Commercial fire alarm systems in City Line have to fit buildings that were mostly built as homes and shops between the 1900s and 1930s, plus a newer generation of elevator buildings. Each group below brings its own design questions, and each is answered on the sealed drawings before any device goes up.

Masjids, churches and prayer halls

A prayer hall in City Line is usually a place of assembly in the eyes of the code. The NYC Department of Buildings' code notes explain that an indoor space where 75 or more people gather needs a Place of Assembly Certificate of Operation, and that a fire alarm system with emergency power is required where the occupant load reaches 300 or more. A hall made by joining two houses on a side street can cross the first line easily and approach the second on a holiday. The design professional calculates the occupant load from the floor area and layout, not from an ordinary weekday, so the commercial fire alarm system design starts with how the space is used at its fullest: the main prayer floor, a women's section on another level, a basement used for overflow or meals, and any classroom where children study. Manual fire alarm pull station installation goes at the exits, smoke detection in the rooms where the design calls for it, and notification appliances are laid out so that a hall full of people, sometimes with a microphone and speakers running, hears and sees the alarm. Where a public address system is used, a relay from the panel can mute it so the alarm signal is clear.

Church buildings in City Line have their own history. St. Sylvester's Roman Catholic Church at 416 Grant Avenue fills the north side of McKinley Avenue between Eldert Lane and Grant Avenue, and its former parish school at 396 Grant Avenue was merged into Salve Regina Catholic Academy. Since 2013 the parish has been administered by the pastor of St. Fortunata. Older parish buildings like these tend to carry decades-old panels and wiring in plaster, and when a former school wing or basement hall is put to a new use, fire alarm system modification or a full fire alarm system renovation is often the result.

Storefront buildings with apartments above

Mixed-use row buildings are the backbone of Liberty Avenue and Pitkin Avenue. The apartments above are covered by HPD's smoke and carbon monoxide alarm rules for each dwelling unit, which are not the same thing as a building fire alarm system. Whether the building or the store needs a system, and what kind, is a design professional's decision based on the occupancy, the number of stories, the fire area and the work being filed. Where one is required, a mixed use building fire alarm installation has to handle a store open late, a cellar used for storage, and a residential stair the store's customers never see. Multifamily building fire alarm installation and apartment building fire alarm installation in larger buildings follow the NYC rules for residential occupancies, where the code allows some systems to sound only at the panel and in specific locations, so the design professional's reading of that section matters as much as any device choice.

Restaurants, sweet shops and groceries

Restaurant fire alarm installation in City Line usually means a small kitchen, sometimes with a hood and suppression system, inside a building with homes above. Heat detectors near cooking equipment, smoke detectors in the dining or sales area, fire alarm kitchen hood suppression monitoring where a hood system is installed, and CO detection where gas-fired ovens, fryers or water heaters exist in a fire-alarm-protected building are the usual elements. Sweet shops and bakeries that run fryers and boilers before dawn are a good example: their detector placement has to match the real heat and steam in the room, or the building will hear false alarms every morning. Retail store fire alarm installation in a grocery adds stock rooms and walk-in coolers, where device placement has to account for shelving and cold air.

Community offices, clinics and youth programs

Office building fire alarm installation in City Line is rarely a tower; it is a ground-floor or second-floor office in a row building that runs English classes, benefit enrollment, tutoring or a food pantry. Medical office fire alarm installation follows the same pattern when a clinic or doctor's office takes a storefront. These spaces are often busiest when they hold events, and the fire alarm design has to reflect the room's real use, its exit doors and any kitchen. School fire alarm installation applies where a charter school, a religious school or an after-school program occupies part of a building.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Two Houses, One Hall, and a Stair Nobody Counted

This is a typical scenario, not a specific job. A mosque committee in City Line calls because their congregation has outgrown its space. Years ago they bought two attached houses, opened the party wall on the ground floor and the cellar, and built one long prayer hall with a smaller room upstairs for women and a classroom in the back. Now they want to finish the cellar as an overflow hall for Fridays and Eid, and a member who works in construction has suggested "putting in some smoke detectors" before Ramadan.

On the walk-through we count the floor area of each level, look at how many people actually stand shoulder to shoulder at the busiest prayer, and follow the routes out: the front door, a side passage, and an interior stair from the cellar that comes up into one of the old house halls. We find battery smoke alarms on two levels, an old heat detector in the cellar wired to nothing, and a sprinkler line in only one of the two cellars. Nobody can remember whether the building ever had a fire alarm filing.

The honest answer is not a box of detectors. Turning residential floors into an assembly space is a change of use, and with the cellar counted the hall may need a Place of Assembly certificate and could approach the occupant load where a fire alarm system becomes mandatory. That is the engineer's or architect's call, so the first step is getting a design professional to measure and calculate before the cellar work starts. Meanwhile the committee should collect what it has: the deed, any old filings, the sprinkler history and the dates the cellar was changed. If a system is required, the drawings will set pull stations at the exits, detection where it belongs, horns and strobes that reach every level including the women's room, and a link to a central station, and the work will be filed with the FDNY before installation begins.

What usually surprises a committee is that the schedule is set by the approval, not the installation. The FDNY's own guide puts plan submission to approval at about 20 to 24 weeks. A congregation that starts the design in autumn has a realistic chance of an approved system before the next Ramadan; one that starts a few weeks before does not. We say that on the first visit, because it is better to change the plan than to open a hall the code does not yet allow.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in City Line: Questions Answered

These are the questions City Line owners, congregations, merchants and program directors actually bring to us, grouped the way the conversations usually go. Each answer is short and specific; the FAQ further down covers different ground.

Cost and paperwork

Our masjid committee wants a figure for the board meeting before we hire an engineer. What can you tell us?

We can tell you what the figure will be made of, but not the figure itself, because nobody can price a fire alarm honestly before the building is designed. The total will include the design professional's fee for sealed drawings, the FDNY filing, equipment, installation labor, programming and the acceptance test, and in a converted house the design alone can change the scope a great deal. For the board, ask us for a survey: you get a written description of the building's situation and the steps ahead, and the written proposal follows once the drawings exist.

We bought a building on Pitkin Avenue that may have had a fire alarm filing years ago. How do we find out what was approved?

Start with the paperwork the seller left: a Letter of Approval from the FDNY, approved drawings, inspection reports and a central station contract. If none exist, the design professional can research records with the city, and the survey will show what equipment is physically there. Until you know what was approved, treat any existing panel as unverified, because modifying an unapproved system only adds to the problem.

Is there a city fee we should set aside before our City Line hall's fire alarm is filed?

Government fees are separate from the contractor's price and are set by the city, so the design professional lists the ones that apply to your filing. One example with a published figure is the FDNY's $105 fee for an FA-27 submission when a building answers a Notice of Defect. Ask the engineer or architect for the filing fees at the same time you ask for the design proposal.

Quality and trust

A relative offered to install our hall's fire alarm cheaply. What should we check before saying yes?

Ask for two things in writing: the FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate the company works under, and the name of the licensed electrician who will be on the filing. Then ask who the design professional is and when the FDNY plan exam will be filed. If any of those answers is vague, the price does not matter, because an installation that cannot pass the FDNY acceptance test has to be done again.

How do we judge whether a fire alarm proposal for our Liberty Avenue building is complete?

A complete proposal names the certified company and electrician, refers to the sealed drawings by date, separates design coordination, filing support, installation, programming and testing, and says who arranges central station monitoring and who attends the FDNY test. A proposal that lists only devices and labor is a parts list, not a fire alarm job.

Who can do the work

Can the electrician who rewired our grocery also connect the building's fire alarm?

Only if the work is done under an FDNY-certified fire alarm company and filed where required. In New York City the electrical license and the fire alarm company certification are separate requirements, and a principal of the certified company must hold the FDNY S-97 Certificate of Fitness. A good electrician can be part of the job, but the fire alarm work has to sit under the certificate and the approved drawings.

Our nonprofit leases a floor on Crescent Street. Are we allowed to hire our own company for fire alarm changes in our space?

Usually the landlord controls that, because the building's fire alarm and its FDNY approval belong to the owner. Your lease and the landlord's service company decide who works on the system. Raise it before you change walls or rooms, so the modification is planned with the owner instead of discovered at the next inspection.

Technical

Incense and the heating in our prayer hall seem to trip the smoke detectors. What is the right fix?

The fix is a design review, not a removal. Depending on what the engineer finds, a detector may be relocated away from incense burners and heating discharge, replaced with a type better suited to the room, or the room's use may be adjusted. Months ahead. With design time plus the FDNY's estimate of about 20 to 24 weeks from plan submission to approval, a new system for a holiday opening should be started the previous autumn or earlier.
