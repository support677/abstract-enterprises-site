The Neighborhood

Why New Lots Properties Need Security Camera Repair

New Lots is the southern, older part of East New York, the core of the old Town of New Lots that split from Flatbush in 1852 and became Brooklyn's 26th Ward in 1886. Today it is a neighborhood of brick two-families and attached houses over cellars, three- and four-family houses, walk-ups, public housing such as Boulevard Houses, a handful of new buildings along Livonia Avenue, and storefront corridors on New Lots, Livonia, Sutter and Van Sinderen Avenues, with the 3 line running overhead on Livonia to its last stop at New Lots Avenue. That is all the history this page needs. What matters for a camera repair is who owns the cameras and how they are looked after, and in New Lots the answer is almost always: a small owner, alone or with one helper, who bought a system once and has not had a reason to open its menu since.

The city's own numbers show it. In its Avenue NYC district study of the New Lots, Livonia, Sutter and Van Sinderen corridors, carried out with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation between July 2024 and June 2025, the Department of Small Business Services counted 367 storefronts. The merchants it surveyed had a median of two full-time employees, 72 percent rented their space and 77 percent had no website. Salons and barbershops were the largest group, at 48, followed by 41 delis, bodegas and smoke shops, 33 houses of worship and nonprofits, 22 medical offices, 14 auto businesses, 10 laundromats, 7 check cashers and 6 phone stores. When the study asked what should change on the corridors, safety came first for 66 percent of merchants and 67 percent of shoppers. These businesses have cameras, and rarely anyone whose job is to notice when one stops recording.

The second reason repairs pile up is the way the corridors work. The same study found storefront gates "closed at odd hours", graffiti on gates, illegal dumping, and an elevated line over Livonia Avenue that keeps the street in shade and makes it feel less safe at night. It also found that 21 percent of the 367 storefronts were empty, against a citywide average of 12.1 percent, and on Van Sinderen Avenue the figure was 81 percent. A camera on a block like that watches a gate that is down while the owner is out, a side wall where people dump bags at night and an empty storefront next door. When it fails, nobody notices, because it was the only thing looking.

The third reason is how the homes are built and shared. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council's 2023 study of Community District 5, which takes in East New York and New Lots, counted about 16,000 one- to three-family properties, nearly 9,000 of them two-family homes with space below grade. Of the homes it assessed, 69 percent had cellars and 31 percent had a door leading straight out of the basement or cellar. In those houses the recorder almost always ends up in the cellar, beside the boiler and the internet modem, often on a shelf an installer picked years ago, and the cameras often draw power from whichever apartment's outlet was nearest. The cellar is also where water arrives: a USGS study in 1976 recorded the water table under this part of Brooklyn rising from about 12 feet below sea level in 1936 to 8 to 10 feet above it after the wells stopped pumping, with basement flooding reported from 1975 on. A recorder on a damp cellar shelf, powered from a tenant's circuit, is one of the most common New Lots repair calls we get.

Public housing adds its own layer. In 2014 the city began a $500,000, City Council-funded camera installation at Boulevard Houses, and the development's current PACT partner lists "increased security cameras throughout the campus" among its work. Those cameras belong to the landlord, and residents describe how slowly fixes can come: "A lot of the kids come in, and the door is not fixed," Boulevard Houses resident Latoya Druck told BK Reader in January 2025 about a broken entrance lock. The private houses and stores across the street from those campuses want their own systems working because they cannot count on anyone else's.

So a camera repair in New Lots means getting the system recording again, and making it report on itself so that the next failure is a text message on a Tuesday afternoon rather than a black screen on the morning a detective asks for video. Many owners pair the repair with a check of the door buzzer or video intercom at the same entrance, because in these buildings the same front door carries both.

Two apartments stacked over a cellar, one often owner-occupied, with the recorder on a cellar shelf and cameras at the front door, the side path, the yard and the cellar door.

Three- and four-family houses

Larger frame and brick houses with several households, shared halls, more than one front door, and cameras added over the years by owners and tenants alike.

Walk-ups and older apartment buildings

Four- and five-story buildings with a vestibule, a staircase, a cellar and a super, where the camera system and the intercom share the front door and often the same neglect.

New buildings on Livonia Avenue

Store with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on New Lots, Livonia and Sutter Avenues with a shop at street level and apartments upstairs, where the store's cameras and the building's cameras share a cellar and sometimes a recorder.

The brick two-family: one recorder in the cellar, two households upstairs

The commonest New Lots house we work in is a two-story brick two-family with a cellar below, a stoop or a few steps at the front, a side path to the yard and often a separate cellar door. The CHPC study counted nearly 9,000 two-family homes with below-grade space in Community District 5 alone, and in our experience the recorder in most of them sits on a cellar shelf near the boiler, the water meter and the internet modem, because that is where the installer found an outlet and the cables from the front and back could meet. Cellars here are damp, the recorder breathes that air through its fan all day, and a low shelf near the washing machine catches every leak.

The bigger surprise for owners is power. In many two-families the cameras or the recorder were plugged into the nearest outlet, and the nearest outlet belonged to the downstairs apartment's circuit. When that tenant moves out and the electric account is closed, or a breaker on that panel trips, the cameras die with it, and the owner upstairs has no idea why. On a repair we trace which panel feeds the recorder, the switch or the power box and every camera, and where it makes sense we move the system onto the owner's own circuit. We do not do electrical work ourselves, so if a new outlet is needed on the owner's side we tell you exactly what and where, and an electrician adds it before we move the equipment. Then the recorder goes up off the floor, onto a shelf or a wall bracket above the damp, with a battery backup beside it.

Attached row houses: one installer, one mistake, a whole block

Rows of attached houses tend to have been wired the same way, often by the same small installer working door to door. That means the same faults show up all along the row: the same cheap power adapters failing in the same winter, the same connector type taking in water at the same spot under the cornice or the porch roof, and the same cable route through the same corner of the cellar ceiling. When one house on a row calls us, we can often tell from the first camera what the next one will look like.

In the newer owner-occupied rows, cables were often laid before the basement ceiling went up, so we find a break by measurement from both ends before opening anything, and price any access panel first.

Three- and four-family houses: cameras from everybody

The larger houses carry more households and more history. In a three- or four-family the cameras are often a mix: a wired system the owner put in, a Wi-Fi camera a tenant stuck in a window, a video doorbell on one of the two front doors, and a door station or buzzer panel at the other. None of them know about each other, and the first hour of a repair is often spent working out which cameras exist, where each records and which belong to a tenant.

The repair starts with a drawing of the house as it is now: each camera, the recorder it feeds, the outlet that powers it and the person who can see it. Then we fix what failed on the owner's system, move the owner's cameras onto the owner's power and internet where that is practical, and set up separate viewing for each household where the owner wants that. A tenant's own camera is left alone without their permission.

Walk-ups and older apartment buildings: the front door gets the attention, the cellar gets the recorder

The four- and five-story buildings along and between the avenues follow a pattern: an outer door and a vestibule, a staircase, a cellar with the boiler and the meters, and a super who may or may not live on site. HPD's open data shows how much attention the front doors of these buildings get: 489 intercom violations at 227 buildings in East New York–New Lots, including violations written in 2024 at 316 New Lots Avenue for a broken intercom in the foyer. The cameras at the same doors are not covered by any such rule, so they tend to fail without anybody being ordered to fix them.

The faults follow from that. Drives are full and set to record over themselves in a few days, so by the time a tenant asks about a stolen package the clip is gone. The vestibule camera hangs low enough to be turned or covered. And the recorder login belongs to a former super or the company that put it in. A repair here means fixing the cameras, setting the drive to hold enough days for the building to answer a request, putting the login in the owner's or managing agent's name, and, where the owner wants it, checking the intercom at the same door on the same trip.

New buildings on Livonia Avenue: big systems, short memories

The newest buildings in New Lots are along the Livonia Avenue corridor. The New York Housing Conference describes Livonia Commons as four buildings with 278 affordable apartments and 51 supportive units and about 28,000 square feet of stores and community space. Buildings like these come with large camera systems, a telecom room full of switches, and a management office that inherited the passwords from the builder's contractor.

The faults we see in new buildings are rarely the hardware: a switch that lost power when a breaker was reset, cameras that stopped recording after a firmware update, a drive set to keep too few days, and stair cameras dark until motion lights come on. We map the telecom room and settings before any parts. Where the building's own contractor still holds a service agreement, we check that before we work, so the owner is not paying twice or voiding something they rely on.

The store with apartments above: two owners, one cellar

Along New Lots, Livonia and Sutter Avenues many buildings are two or three stories, with a store at street level and apartments above. The store's cameras watch the counter, the door, the stockroom and the gate; the building's cameras, if there are any, watch the residents' door beside the shop and the stairs. Both systems usually end up in the same cellar, and in some buildings on the same recorder, because whoever put the second system in found the first one already there.

The repair starts by sorting out ownership. Which cameras are the store's, which are the building's, who pays for each fix, and who should be able to see what. Then we make the two systems independent where they need to be, so the store can change its recorder or its internet without blacking out the residents' door, and a landlord's repair upstairs does not cut power to the store's cameras.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Bringing a New Lots Store System Back From a Silent Failure

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical New Lots deli, laundromat or small shop whose owner has just found out the recorder has not saved anything useful for a while. It is our working order, not a story about one address.

Save what is there. Before anything is restarted, the technician exports whatever the recorder still holds for the dates the owner cares about, and notes the recorder's clock against the real time.

Read the recorder's own record. Most recorders keep a log of disk errors, video loss, power loss and logins. That log usually says when the system actually stopped working, which is often weeks before anyone noticed, and whether it was the drive, the power or a single camera that went first.

Check the shelf before the ceiling. The technician looks at where the recorder lives: the heat around it, the dust or lint on its vents, the outlet and power strip it shares with a cooler, a dryer or a compressor, and whether it is on a battery backup at all.

Walk to every camera that matters. At each dark or weak camera, power and signal are tested at the camera, and the connector, bracket and cable entry are checked for water, paint or damage.

Price before parts, then make it report on itself. The owner hears the cause and gets a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, every camera is checked by day and under the real night lighting of the block, and the recorder is set to send the owner's phone an alert for video loss, disk errors and a full or missing drive. The owner leaves with the logins and a list of what is connected where.

Fault by Fault

Worn Drives, Full Disks, the Wrong Meter, Changing Light and Spray Paint: Why New Lots Camera Systems Fail Quietly

Six causes account for most of the camera failures we find on New Lots houses, buildings and storefronts. What they share is that none of them announces itself. Knowing which one you have is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Worn drives: the recorder that never gets a day off

A recorder in a deli, a bodega or a laundromat writes video every minute of every day, and on many systems it is set to record continuously rather than only on motion, because the owner wants the whole day. The hard drive inside does nothing but write, and it is the part that wears out. Installers on CCTVForum describe DVRs commonly lasting five to seven years, and point to the drive as the usual first failure; a failing drive can leave a recorder stuck on its start-up screen, freeze it, or simply stop it saving while the live picture carries on. The repair is a drive rated for round-the-clock recording, sized to the days you need, with the old one's footage copied off first if anything on it matters.

Full disks: the system that is recording over itself

"Running out of storage is the most common reason" security cameras stop recording, one New York service company writes, and it matches what we find. A drive that fills up does one of two things, depending on a single setting: it records over the oldest video, so you only ever have a few days, or it stops recording altogether and waits for someone to notice. When a theft is reported a week later, a recorder that keeps four days is no better than a broken one. On a repair we check which mode the recorder is in, work out how many days the drive actually holds at the cameras' current settings, and change the settings, the drive or both until the system keeps as much as the owner needs to answer a request.

The wrong meter: cameras powered by somebody else's apartment

In New Lots two-families and three-families, and in stores with apartments above, cameras are often powered from the nearest outlet rather than the owner's. That works until the apartment's account is closed, a breaker trips in a panel the owner cannot reach, or someone unplugs the power strip. It looks like a dead system, and owners sometimes replace a recorder that was never broken; the real repair is moving the system onto the owner's own circuit, as described above.

Changing light: the picture that went wrong without the camera changing

A camera is aimed and set once, under the light of that day. Then an awning goes up, a neighbor adds a bright sign, stair lights go on motion sensors, an iron security door is fitted, or the afternoon sun swings onto a glass door. On Livonia Avenue the elevated line keeps much of the street in shade, which the city's district study noted makes the corridor feel less safe at night; in shade like that, cameras spend more of the day in their low-light settings than an installer may have expected. The fix is usually exposure, backlight and day-night settings, a small change of angle, or a light added on purpose, and only sometimes a different camera.

Spray paint and reach: the camera someone dealt with first

The same district study lists graffiti on store gates among the corridor's problems, and cameras above those gates get the same treatment. A dome sprayed black at two in the morning shows nothing from that moment on, and unless someone checks, it stays that way for weeks. Photographs in the NYCHA Federal Monitor's 2023 safety report show what happens to cameras across the city's public housing when nobody follows up: spray-painted, taped over, missing. On a private building the fix is a cleaned or new cover, a mount out of reach, and an alert for a covered lens.

Cables across lots, yards and cellars: the run that takes the abuse

Auto shops near Van Sinderen Avenue, churches with a parking area, and houses with a long side path and a garage at the back all have cameras at the end of a long run. Those runs get driven over, dug into, chewed in the cellar and soaked where they pass through the brick. A camera that drops out after rain, or that went dark the week a fence or a driveway was redone, almost always has a damaged cable or a wet connector somewhere along the way. We find the bad section and replace it on a protected route rather than the one that failed.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in New Lots: Questions Answered

These are situations New Lots store owners, homeowners, landlords and building managers describe when they call. Each answer is written for the houses, buildings and storefronts here.

Shops on New Lots, Livonia, Sutter and Van Sinderen

Our laundromat's recorder sits on a shelf a few feet from the dryers, and twice this year it has switched itself off in the middle of the afternoon. Could lint be doing that?

Very likely. A recorder cools itself with a small fan that pulls air through the case, and next to the dryers that air carries lint, which builds up on the fan and the vents until the drive and the board overheat and the recorder shuts down to protect itself. We open and clean it, replace the fan if it is worn, read the drive's health, and move the recorder to the coolest, cleanest spot the store has, ideally a closed cabinet or back room with its own air, on a battery backup.

The camera over our deli's front door faces the street, and every afternoon the sun through the glass turns everyone walking in into a black shape. Is the camera bad?

No, it is being asked to see a dark doorway against a bright street, and it sets itself for the bright part. Most cameras have backlight or wide dynamic range settings that bring up the faces, and we turn those on and test them in the afternoon light. If the camera cannot handle it, we move it to face the door from the counter side.

Twice this year someone sprayed black paint over the camera above our gate on Livonia Avenue. Can it be repaired so the next can of paint does not blind it for weeks?

Yes, in three steps. We clean or replace the dome cover, which is often a small part rather than a whole camera; we move the camera higher or under a hood so nobody can reach it from the gate box; and we turn on the recorder's alert for a blocked or covered lens so you hear about it the same night.

Our check-cashing window has a pass-through tray under thick glass. Should the camera there watch the customer's face or the tray?

Both, and that usually takes two cameras. One camera on the customer side of the glass, or angled so it does not see the store lights reflected in it, shows faces; a second, closer camera on your side shows hands, cash and documents in the tray. One camera set wide to catch both tends to show neither clearly. We price a second camera only if the first cannot be re-aimed to do the job.

The cable to the camera at the back fence of our auto shop's lot runs under the gravel where the tow trucks drive, and the camera has been dead since the winter. How is that usually fixed?

The cable has almost certainly been crushed or cut, and it will happen again if the new one takes the same path. We test the run to find the damaged stretch, then replace it in a buried conduit deep enough for the trucks, along the fence line in a protected channel, or, where neither is practical, give that one camera its own wireless link back to the building.

Houses and two-families

The camera beside our front door looks out through the bars of our iron security door, and at night the picture is a white glare. Is the camera failing?

Probably not. At night the camera turns on its own infrared light, and the bars a few inches in front of it reflect that light straight back into the lens, which washes out everything behind them. The fix is to move the camera so the bars are not in front of it, for example above the door frame looking down, or to turn off its built-in infrared and light the stoop another way. We test the new position after dark before we leave.

The cameras on our two-family went dark the same week our downstairs tenant moved out and closed the electric account. Why would the tenant's account affect our cameras?

Because the cameras, the recorder or the power box were almost certainly plugged into an outlet on the downstairs apartment's circuit, and when that account was closed, or the breaker turned off, they lost power. We trace every part of the system to its panel, and where your own circuit can reach, we move the system onto it. If a new outlet is needed on your side, an electrician adds it first, since we do not do electrical work, and then we move the equipment and add a battery backup.

Walk-ups, apartment buildings and new buildings

In our building the stairwell lights only switch on when someone walks in, and the stair cameras show black for the first few seconds of every clip. Can that be fixed?

Yes. The camera is waiting for light, and by the time the lights come on the person is halfway up the stairs. The usual repair is to set those cameras to use their own infrared in the dark so they see the person before the lights respond, and to set the recorder to keep a few seconds from before motion begins. Where the owner prefers, an electrician can set the stair lights to stay at a low level instead of going fully dark, and the cameras then work in both.

Our building's front door has a video door station with its own camera, but it only shows a picture when someone presses a button. Is that camera being recorded?

Usually not. Many door stations show video only during a call and keep no recording unless they are connected to a recorder or a cloud service. We check yours: if its camera can send a stream, we add it to the building's recorder so the entrance is saved like any other camera; if it cannot, we make sure a recorded camera covers the same doorway.

The recorder is in our building's cellar, and only the super has the key. When a tenant asks about something that happened, we can wait days to see the video. Can the owner reach the footage without the cellar?

Yes. Most recorders can be set up so the owner or managing agent plays back and exports clips from a phone or a computer, with a login that cannot change settings or delete video. We set that up, test an export from your phone, and leave the recorder itself locked in the cellar where it belongs.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we find on New Lots houses, walk-ups, stores and lots, and the faults each one tends to develop here.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common on two-families and stores wired ten or more years ago. Typical faults: wet connectors where the cable enters the brick, failed plug-in adapters, and infrared that has dimmed with age.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected through one data cable, standard in newer buildings and in many stores. Typical faults: dead switch ports after an outage, address conflicts after a router change, and lost passwords.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the footage, on a cellar shelf, under a counter or in a back room. Typical faults: worn drives from round-the-clock recording, full disks, lint- or dust-clogged fans, and settings lost after a power cut.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with an adjustable zoom, common at counters, doors and lots. Typical faults: a lens set too wide to recognize anyone, focus that drifted after a knock, and covers that are scratched, sprayed or clouded.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving cameras over lots and store floors, and fisheye cameras on shop ceilings. Typical faults: lost presets and sticking motors.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Plug-in and battery cameras in windows, on shelves and in back yards. Typical faults: weak signal through brick, router changes, cold-weather battery drain, and accounts tied to a tenant or relative who has left.

Video door stations

Door stations with their own camera at the residents' door or the gate. Typical faults: video that reaches one phone but not another, failed call buttons, and pictures that are never recorded at all.

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, often on a cellar shelf or in a telecom room without backup. Typical faults: dead ports after an outage and overloaded power budgets.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, adapters on the wrong apartment's outlet, and backups whose batteries quietly died years ago.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in New Lots

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere, and each sends New Lots owners toward the wrong repair, a lost clip or a bill they did not need.

"Your camera system will warn you when something breaks"

Quick answers often describe modern recorders as watching over themselves, sending alerts the moment a camera drops or a drive fails.

Many can, but out of the box most are not set to. Alerts for video loss, disk errors and a full drive are usually switched off, or switched on but sent to an email address nobody reads, or to the phone of whoever set the system up years ago.

Assume nothing is warning you until you have seen a test alert arrive on your own phone. On every repair we switch on the alerts that matter, send a test to the owner, and show how to check playback in under a minute.

"The hard drive will last as long as the recorder"

Buying guides tend to treat the recorder as a single appliance with a single lifespan.

The drive is the part that moves and the part that wears, and in a store that records around the clock it does nothing else. Installers on CCTVForum put many DVRs at five to seven years, and name the drive as the most common point of failure. A recorder with a tired drive can keep showing live video while it quietly fails to save.

Treat the drive as a part that gets replaced, and check its health on a schedule. We read it on every visit, and when we replace it we fit a drive rated for continuous recording, sized for the days you need.

"If the wiring is a problem, switch to wireless cameras"

Answers about failed cables often suggest replacing wired cameras with Wi-Fi models and skipping the wire altogether.

A Wi-Fi camera still needs power, so a cable or an outlet still has to reach it, or it runs on a battery that needs charging and drains faster in the cold. And Wi-Fi has to get through brick. In a New Lots two-family with the router upstairs and the camera on the back wall, the signal often is not there.

Wireless is a good answer for the one camera a cable cannot reach, such as the far fence of a lot. For the rest, repairing the existing cable is usually cheaper and far more reliable.

"Your landlord has to keep the building's cameras working, so call 311"

Some answers lump cameras in with intercoms, heat and locks as things a tenant can get the city to order fixed.

HPD does write violations for broken intercoms, and its data shows 489 of them in East New York–New Lots. Cameras are different. Brick Underground has reported, citing a New York real estate attorney, that city buildings are not required to have functioning cameras, and that landlords generally do not have to show tenants footage outside a legal process. A dark camera in a walk-up is usually a decision for the owner, not an order from the city.

For tenants that means asking the owner or managing agent in writing. For owners it means the camera system is yours to keep working, and a dark one helps nobody when a tenant, an insurer or a detective asks for video.

"Unplug the recorder and plug it back in"

Restarting is the first step in almost every quick troubleshooting answer.

A restart can clear a frozen screen, but it can also make things worse. One installer guide on recovering systems after an outage warns that "Repeated power cycling can worsen storage and power faults." A recorder that keeps restarting after a New Lots outage is often telling you about a failing drive or a weak power supply, and every unplug risks the footage on it.

If you need footage from the past few days, do not restart anything. Call first, and we save the video before we touch the power.

"Any electrician can fix a camera system"

Because cameras use power, some answers send people to an electrician for every camera fault.

Camera systems run on low-voltage cable, network switches, recorders and software, and New York requires a Department of State license for companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for other people. An electrician is the right person for a new outlet or a tripped breaker, and in New Lots two-families an electrician is often exactly who is needed to put the recorder on the owner's own circuit. But recovering a recorder password or reading a drive's health is camera work.

The two trades work together. We do not do electrical work, so when a camera repair needs a new outlet, we tell you exactly what and where for your electrician, and we handle everything on the camera side.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

Cost answers often quote a low national range taken from single consumer cameras.

HomeAdvisor's own Brooklyn page shows how wide the real spread is: an average of $1,416 for surveillance camera installation or repair, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and jobs from $160 to $3,500. That page blends new systems with small fixes, which shows why one number tells you nothing.

On our Brooklyn list most single repairs land between $150 and $700, and you get a written price for your fault after the diagnosis, before any part is replaced.
