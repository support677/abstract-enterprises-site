The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Built Around Empty Hours

In Westchester the question is rarely "how do we get in?" It is "who gets in while we're gone?" A typical house in Scarsdale, Larchmont, Chappaqua, Irvington or Pelham sits locked, or is supposed to, from the time the household leaves for the train, the office or school until the evening. In those hours the doors are opened by a surprising number of people who are not family: the cleaner on Tuesdays, the dog walker at noon, the nanny collecting a child at three, the landscaper who needs the garage, the pool company in summer, a plumber or painter during a renovation. Each of them has, in the old way of doing things, a key, a hidden spare or the garage keypad code. A smart lock project here is mostly about replacing that loose web of keys and shared codes with individual credentials you can see, schedule and cancel.

The county's housing stock explains why. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts table for Westchester counts roughly 399,600 housing units and about 1.02 million residents, and puts the owner-occupied rate for 2020–2024 at 61.8 percent, with a median owner-occupied home value of $663,200. These are, in the main, houses their owners intend to stay in, often for decades, and they were built over a century: Tudors and colonials in the old railroad suburbs, post-war capes and split-levels further out, and newer houses on larger lots in the north of the county. Each generation brought its own doors — heavy oak front doors with separate handlesets, French doors to a terrace, aluminium sliders onto a deck, and second-floor doors onto balconies and porch roofs.

Those back and upper doors are where local police keep pointing. When Scarsdale police reported a run of burglaries in November 2015, they described one group climbing to and forcing a second-floor window and another breaking a rear window or sliding glass door; the same report noted that police were looking into everyone who knew the code to a garage keypad, because a ladder kept behind that keypad had been used in an attempt on a house. In April 2023, Patch reported that Greenwich police had obtained arrest warrants for burglary crews working Fairfield and Westchester County towns, and that those crews struck high-end homes in the evening while residents were out, entering through a second-floor window or door and fleeing when an alarm sounded. In January 2025 the State Police alerted residents of Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties to a series of targeted burglaries and asked them to lock every door and window, "including second-floor entry points." And in September 2024 News 12 Westchester showed security video of two men forcing a back sliding door with a crowbar before dawn in Pelham Manor.

None of that is a reason to think a smart lock is a force field. It is a reason to fit the right lock on the right door, to make sure the doors nobody thinks about — the slider, the balcony door, the door from the garage — are actually locked when the house is empty, and to know about it at once when they are not. That is the job a connected lock does well, and it is the part of the work we spend the most time on in Westchester.

The other half of the county's story is age. QuickFacts puts the share of Westchester residents aged 65 and over at 19.7 percent. Many of them live in the same house they raised a family in, now alone or as a couple, with adult children in Brooklyn, Connecticut or further away. For them the smart lock question turns around: not keeping people out, but making sure the right people can get in — a neighbour, an aide, a son who drives up on Sundays, and above all a paramedic if the person inside has fallen and can't get to the door. We plan that access deliberately, with the local fire department's options in mind, rather than leaving it to whoever happens to have a spare key.

Finally, Westchester is not one housing market. Cooperator News, citing county planning figures, counts close to 42,000 co-ops and 25,000 condominiums in the county, spread with its rental housing across six cities, 14 towns and 22 villages. Downtown New Rochelle alone has added more than 4,500 new apartments since its 2015 rezoning, Governing reported in 2025. Yonkers, Mount Vernon and New Rochelle have blocks of pre-war elevator co-ops and two- and three-family houses, and they sit just over the city line — which means New York City's apartment-lock rules do not apply there, while state rules do.

Smart Lock Installation for Westchester County's Architecture

Westchester was built along its railroad lines, then filled in by car, and every era left a different set of doors behind. These are the property types we work on most across the county, and what each one asks of a smart lock.

Railroad-Suburb Tudors and Colonials

Bronxville, Scarsdale, Larchmont, Pelham and Rye: 1910s–1930s houses with heavy front doors, a handleset with a thumb-latch and a separate deadbolt, side lights of leaded or plain glass, and a back door to a terrace. The front door usually needs care; the back doors usually need attention.

Post-War Capes, Ranches and Split-Levels

Yorktown, Cortlandt, Greenburgh, Eastchester and much of the mid-county: standard bored doors that take a smart deadbolt easily, plus a garage entry and a slider onto the deck. The challenge is usually signal to the far doors, not the doors themselves.

Larger Houses on Bigger Lots

Armonk, Bedford, Pound Ridge, North Salem and parts of Chappaqua: several entrances, French doors across the back, second-floor balconies, a detached garage or pool house, and long distances from the router. A whole-property plan beats door-by-door buying.

River-Town Victorians

Irvington, Dobbs Ferry, Hastings-on-Hudson, Tarrytown and Ossining: older frame houses with porches, tall narrow doors and frames that have moved with a century of humid summers. Strikes are realigned and reinforced on nearly every job.

Pre-War Elevator Co-ops

Yonkers, Bronxville, New Rochelle, Mount Vernon and White Plains: brick buildings with steel or wood unit doors on interior corridors, managed by boards and agents who set the rules for what goes on a door. Retrofit units and finish-matched hardware are common requests.

Two- and Three-Family Houses

Yonkers, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle and Port Chester: owner downstairs, tenant upstairs, often a shared front hall. Separate credentials for each household, and lock changes that respect the tenant's rights under state law.

Downtown Rental and Condo Towers

New Rochelle and White Plains: new buildings near the Metro-North stations with building-run entry systems in the lobby. Work inside the unit is usually a retrofit and a connectivity plan rather than new hardware in the door.

Condominium and 55-Plus Communities

Heritage Hills in Somers, which describes itself as 2,600 condos in 30 condo groups on more than 1,100 acres, and smaller communities across the county: uniform exteriors, association rules about what can change, and owners who want simple, phone-free entry.

Door and hardware details we check on Westchester houses

Handlesets with a separate deadbolt. Many older Westchester front doors have two pieces of hardware: a decorative handleset with a thumb-latch on the outside and a lever or knob inside, and a deadbolt a few inches above it. A smart deadbolt replaces only the upper lock, so the handleset can stay — and should, if it matches the house. We check that the two finishes will sit happily together and that the handleset latch actually holds the door closed, because a smart deadbolt can only throw into a frame the door is resting against.

Sliders and French doors. Aluminium and vinyl sliding doors have a narrow stile and a hook latch rather than a bored deadbolt. Some can take a keyless mortise or surface lock designed for sliders; many are better served by a quality secondary lock or a bar in the track, with a door contact that reports when the door opens. French doors to a terrace may have a multipoint lock that throws hooks or bolts top and bottom when the handle is lifted; those need a lock made for that gearbox, or a keypad unit that leaves the multipoint mechanism in charge.

Balcony and porch-roof doors upstairs. Second-floor doors are often lightweight, half-glazed and fitted with the cheapest lock in the house, because nobody imagines anyone coming in that way. Police in Westchester and Greenwich have been describing exactly that for years. We look at these doors from inside and out, and at what a person could stand on to reach them.

Frames that move with the seasons. Wood doors in older houses swell in July and shrink in January. A motorised bolt is less tolerant of a tight strike than a hand-turned one: if the bolt drags, the motor strains, the batteries drain and the app reports a jam. We test each door shut by hand and by its own weight, move or enlarge the strike where needed, and come back to a door that we know will change with the season.

One turn to get out. Every lock we fit on a residential door opens from inside with a thumb-turn or lever — no key, no code, no phone. The state's model residential code text calls for egress doors to be readily openable from inside without a key or special knowledge, and that is the standard we hold to on every house door, whatever else the lock does.

A driveway gate or a shared two-family entrance is often the next thing on the list once the house doors are done. For those, see our intercom installation in Westchester County, and for small apartment buildings whose buzzer has given up, the AESS door buzzer installers in Westchester.

Compliance callouts for Westchester business doors

  • The 2025 state code. The 2025 Uniform Code took effect statewide on December 31, 2025; the Town of Harrison's building department, for one, posted the change in advance. The 2025 Building Code of New York State is built on the 2024 International Building Code, and electrified locks on egress doors fall under its means-of-egress rules.
  • Power off, door free. Under the model code's door-hardware-release rules, operating the inside hardware must directly cut power to the lock and unlock the door at once, and losing power must unlock it. Sensor-release doors add a push-to-exit button 40 to 48 inches up and within five feet of the door.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure, door by door. Whether a strike releases or holds when power drops depends on whether the door is part of the way out. We specify it per door with the building's code official in mind, never by default.
  • County licensing. Westchester's Department of Consumer Protection says county law requires home improvement contractors to hold a county licence, and runs an online licence search; plumbers and electricians are licensed separately, and Yonkers issues its own home improvement licence. Check whoever you hire.
  • Not legal advice. Where we mention a code or a law, we name it so your architect, code official or lawyer can confirm how it applies to your building.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Westchester County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Westchester homeowners, landlords, practice managers and congregations ask before a smart lock goes on a door, grouped by situation: houses that sit empty all day, the back and upper doors burglars favour, owners who live alone, co-ops and two-families on the city line, pools, and business doors. Price and scheduling questions are in the FAQ further down. Nothing here is legal advice; where a law, a code or a police report is mentioned we name it so you can read it yourself.

Houses that are empty all day

We're on Metro-North from early morning until after dark — what should the locks be doing while the house sits empty?

Three things, without anyone touching a phone. First, locking themselves: every smart deadbolt should be set to auto-lock a minute or two after its door closes, so the door the last person forgot is locked anyway. Second, reporting: the app should send an alert if any smart-locked door is unlocked, or is opened, during hours when nobody is expected, and a daily "all locked" check is easy to set. Third, letting in only who is scheduled: the cleaner's code works on Tuesday between nine and one, the dog walker's at midday on weekdays, and nobody else's at all.

The police advice that accompanies most Westchester burglary reports adds one more job: make the house look occupied. Scarsdale police asked residents to put lights on timers and keep all exterior doors locked with deadbolts; the State Police alert of January 2025 suggested timed interior lights when away. Many lock apps and hubs can tie a "house empty" routine to lights as well as locks, so the same tap that confirms the doors are locked switches the evening lamps on at dusk.

What the locks can't do is cover the doors that don't have them. If the slider at the back and the door from the garage are on a key, the app knows nothing about them. That's why we walk every door first and decide, with you, which ones need a smart lock, which need a contact, and which just need a proper deadbolt that somebody remembers to throw.

Police warnings keep mentioning second-floor doors — should the balcony door upstairs get a smart lock too?

It should get attention, and sometimes a smart lock is the right answer. The pattern described by Greenwich police in 2023, and reported by Patch for Westchester and Fairfield towns, was crews entering high-end homes in the evening through a second-floor window or door while the family was out. The State Police alert in January 2025 asked residents of Westchester, Dutchess and Putnam to lock "second-floor entry points." Upstairs doors are often the weakest in the house: a light half-glazed door onto a balcony or porch roof, a cheap knob lock, and nobody in the habit of checking it.

If the upstairs door is used regularly — a bedroom balcony that people step out onto in summer — a smart deadbolt makes sense, because it can lock itself and tell you if it hasn't. If it is opened twice a year, a solid keyed deadbolt plus an alarm contact is usually better value; the contact reports the door opening whether or not anyone locked it, and the Patch report noted that the crews tended to flee when an alarm sounded. Either way we check the door itself: a deadbolt only holds as well as the frame and strike behind it.

Look up as well as at the door. A porch roof, a trellis, a stack of patio furniture or a ladder left in the yard is how people reach those doors. Scarsdale police described a burglar piling up lawn furniture to reach a second-storey window in 2015.

The landscaper, the cleaner and two contractors all know our garage keypad code — does that undercut the smart locks on the house?

Yes, if the garage leads into the house or holds what someone would need to get in. In the Scarsdale police report of November 2015, a ladder kept in a garage that could only be reached with the door keypad code was found next to a house's damaged second-floor windows, and police said they were looking into the people who had access to that code. A garage keypad code that has been the same for years, shared with every crew who ever worked on the property, is effectively a key that you can't take back.

The fix has two halves. Change the garage keypad code now, and from then on give it only to people who need the garage, changing it whenever a crew finishes. Then make the door from the garage into the house a real lock: a smart deadbolt there gives each person their own code and a record of when it was used, so the landscaper can get to the mower without being able to walk into the kitchen.

Where the garage opener itself is app-connected, check what it can and can't talk to before planning around it. Chamberlain, for instance, moved in 2023 to block unauthorised third-party apps from its myQ system, which ended some home-automation links owners had relied on. We plan the house door so that it doesn't depend on the garage system at all.

Our kids get off the school bus mid-afternoon while we're at work — can the lock tell us each one is home?

Yes, and it's one of the most popular reasons Westchester parents give us for going keyless. Give each child their own code or fingerprint, and the app can send a notification naming who unlocked the door and when. Most apps let you choose which users trigger an alert, so you hear about the children and not about yourself coming home.

Make sure the door they use is the one with the lock. Children tend to come in through the garage or a side door rather than the formal front door, so that's often where the keypad belongs. A keypad with physical buttons is easier for small fingers and gloved winter hands than a touchscreen, and a fingerprint can't be left at a friend's house. Auto-lock behind them matters as much as the notification: a child coming in with a backpack, a scooter and a friend will not always turn round to lock up.

Two cautions. Don't use a child's code to unlock for someone else — give the sitter or a neighbour their own. And keep a physical key somewhere a child can reach in an emergency, such as with a trusted neighbour, because a lock with flat batteries or a door that has swollen shut is rare but possible.

Back doors, sliders and the way burglars come in

The door we all use is a sliding patio door onto the deck — can a slider get a smart lock?

Sometimes, but with caution. A sliding door has a narrow frame and a hook latch that grips the jamb, not the bored hole a standard smart deadbolt needs. Keyless locks made specifically for sliders exist, as do keypad units designed to mount on a patio door, and on some doors they work well. On others the frame is too thin, the door rattles in its track, or the existing latch is too worn to hold anything.

Sliders are also a known way in. Scarsdale police described burglars entering through a rear window or sliding glass door in their 2015 report, and in September 2024 News 12 Westchester showed video of two men prying open a back sliding door with a crowbar in Pelham Manor before dawn, then leaving with a handbag, car keys and the family's car. A smart lock on a weak slider does not change that. So we look first at the basics: an auxiliary lock that pins the moving panel, a bar or block in the track, and an alarm contact that reports the door opening.

If the family really does use the slider as the main door, there are two routes. Fit a slider-specific keyless lock where the frame can carry one, or move the daily keyless entry to an adjacent hinged door — a mudroom or garage door — and keep the slider locked and alarmed. The second route is usually the more secure.

Owners who live alone

My father lives on his own in Elmsford — if he falls, how would paramedics get through a smart-locked door without breaking it?

Plan it before anyone needs it. There are three common routes, and the best arrangements use two. The first is a residential key box: the Elmsford Fire Department, for one, describes a Residential Knox-Box that lets first responders "let themselves in" with a high-security master key held by the department, aimed at seniors living at home who may not be able to reach the door. You buy the box through the fire department's programme; we can mount it and make sure the key inside works the door it is meant for. Ask your own department whether it takes part.

The second route is a person nearby with their own credential: a neighbour or relative who has a code, kept for emergencies and deleted if they move. The third is remote unlocking by a family member, which only works if someone answers the call, is near their phone and knows who is at the door — a doorbell camera makes that last part much safer. What doesn't work is relying on the lock's app alone, held on a phone that belongs to the person who has fallen.

Make sure the lock itself suits him too. A keypad with large physical buttons, a fingerprint enrolled on two fingers, or a fob on his keyring all spare him a phone. And keep the inside dead simple — one turn to open, nothing to remember — because the most likely person to need that door in a hurry is him.

Co-ops, two-families and the city line

Does New York City's apartment-lock law reach our Yonkers or New Rochelle apartment?

Not directly. The city's rules on extra locks and duplicate keys come largely from the state Multiple Dwelling Law and the city's own Housing Maintenance Code. The New York Attorney General's office explains that the Multiple Dwelling Law applies to cities with a population of 325,000 or more, while the Multiple Residence Law applies to cities with fewer than 325,000 people and to all towns and villages. By that population test, every city in Westchester, Yonkers included, falls on the Multiple Residence Law side of the line, and the city's Housing Maintenance Code doesn't apply here at all.

Some state rules do follow you over the line. The Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law, section 768, makes it unlawful for anyone to evict an occupant by, among other things, changing the lock on the entrance door without giving the occupant a key, and the Attorney General's guidance says this is a class A misdemeanour statewide. Beyond that, what you may put on a co-op or rental door in Westchester is decided mostly by your lease or proprietary lease, the house rules, and your city's or village's code.

So the practical route is the same in Yonkers as anywhere: read the lease and house rules, ask the managing agent in writing, and choose hardware that can be removed cleanly if the board or landlord objects. If you need a firm answer for your building, that's a question for a tenant advocate or a lawyer who practises in Westchester, not for a lock installer.

Pools and outdoor gates

Can the gate in our pool fence take a keypad lock without breaking the state's pool-barrier rules?

Usually, if the keypad is added to a gate that already does what the code requires rather than replacing any part of it. The New York Department of State's technical bulletin summarising residential pool rules, written for the 2020 Uniform Code, says all gates must be self-closing, pedestrian gates must open outward away from the pool, and gates must be self-latching with the latch handle on the pool side at least 40 inches above grade — with extra conditions if it is lower than 54 inches. It also says gates must be securely locked "with a key, combination, or another child-proof lock" when the pool isn't in use or supervised.

A keypad or combination lock can be that lock. What it must not do is defeat the self-closing hinge or the self-latching latch: a keypad that holds the gate open, a latch that only catches when someone pushes it, or a lock mounted where a child can reach the release from outside all undo the barrier. We fit gate-rated, weather-resistant keypad locks that work alongside the existing hinges and latch, and we test the gate swinging shut from fully open.

The 2025 Uniform Code is now in force, and your town's building department inspects pool barriers, so confirm current requirements there before changing a gate. And give the pool gate its own code, separate from the house: the pool service needs the gate, not the kitchen.

Practices, offices and congregations

Our practice on the Platinum Mile keeps patient records on site — what does HIPAA expect of our door locks?

HIPAA's Security Rule doesn't name a lock, but it does ask for physical safeguards you can show. Under 45 CFR 164.310, a covered entity must have policies and procedures that limit physical access to its electronic information systems and the facility they're housed in, while letting properly authorised people in. The rule's addressable specifications include controlling and validating each person's access based on their role, including visitors, and documenting repairs and changes to the physical security of the facility — "for example, hardware, walls, doors, and locks."

A keypad or reader with individual credentials fits that well. Front-desk staff can open the reception door; only clinical and IT staff can open the server closet or records room; the cleaning company's code works after hours and nowhere near the medication store. The lock's log then becomes part of your evidence that access is controlled by role. HHS's own guidance on physical safeguards mentions door locks that rely on a card or badge reader as one common control.

We hand over what your compliance file needs from us: a list of each door, the hardware fitted, who holds which credential, and the date of the work. Your privacy officer decides the policy; we make the doors able to enforce it.

Our congregation was awarded a state security grant — can it cover keyless doors, and what paperwork will the grant office want from you?

Possibly, but the grant's own documents decide. New York's Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes programme, run by the Division of Criminal Justice Services, funds safety and security projects at nonprofits at risk of hate crimes because of their ideology, beliefs or mission. The Governor's office describes it as covering interior or exterior security improvements such as alarms, panic buttons, fences, shatter-resistant glass and public address systems, "among other items," plus training and cybersecurity. Whether access control or keyless locks fall within your award depends on the request for proposals you applied under and the budget DCJS approved.

Before we start, check that the doors and hardware in our quote match the line items in your approved budget; changing scope after an award usually needs the funder's agreement. We provide an itemised quote naming each door, the hardware and the labour, an invoice that matches it, and photographs of the finished doors if your grant administrator asks.

For the doors themselves, the usual plan is one or two controlled entrances with readers or keypads, push-to-exit hardware inside that never needs a credential to leave, and the other doors kept locked from outside with panic hardware for exit. Schedules let the office door open during weekday hours and stay locked during services when ushers manage the main entrance.

Our office landlord says the 2025 state code now applies — does that change what can go on our suite door?

It may change the details, and it confirms the principle. The 2025 Uniform Code took effect statewide on December 31, 2025, and the 2025 Building Code of New York State is based on the 2024 International Building Code. For electrified locks on egress doors, the model code's door-hardware-release rules require that operating the door's inside hardware directly cuts power to the lock and unlocks it immediately, and that losing power unlocks the door. Sensor-release doors need a sensor on the inside that detects someone approaching, plus a "PUSH TO EXIT" button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that keeps the lock released for at least 30 seconds, and they unlock when the fire alarm activates.

In practice that means a keypad or reader outside, an electric strike or a lock built into the lever, and an inside release that needs no knowledge or credential. A battery-powered keypad deadbolt on a suite door is a different kind of device; whether it is acceptable on your particular door depends on your occupancy, the number of people the room serves, and the code official's view. That's a question for the building department, through your landlord.

Bring us the landlord's tenant-work rules and any letter from the building department, and we will quote hardware and wiring that fits both. If a permit or filing is needed, your landlord or architect usually holds that process; we supply the product data sheets and wiring details they ask for.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Westchester County

Be clear about where these come from: Google's AI Overview was out of reach while this page was researched, so none of the seven lines below is quoted from it. Each is a claim we keep meeting in cost guides, lock advertising and how-to pieces — the material answer engines summarise — followed by what Westchester doors actually show us.

“Smart lock installation costs about $300”

National cost guides publish a tidy figure. Fixr's guide, updated in January 2025, puts a smart lock installed at $250 to $400 nationally, averaging $300; Homewyse's January 2026 estimate works out at roughly $95 to $227 per lock before contractor markup. Those numbers assume one ordinary door, already bored, with a strong Wi-Fi signal.

Westchester jobs rarely look like that. A resident on the r/Westchester forum, describing household costs, put a single deadbolt-and-camera lock at "$400ish" for the hardware alone. Then come a Tudor's two-piece front hardware, a slider that needs a different solution, a garage entry at the far end of the house, and six people to enrol.

We publish the Westchester figures we can price in advance — strikes, keypads with strikes, setup visits — and quote house doors flat after seeing them. Use the national figure as a floor for one easy door, not as a budget for a house.

“Installing a smart lock is a 20-minute DIY job”

On a modern, pre-bored door in a square frame, a replacement smart deadbolt really is a screwdriver job. Plenty of Westchester owners do exactly that and it works.

The trouble is the doors that aren't like that: a 1920s front door whose frame has moved, a French door with a multipoint gearbox, a slider, an upstairs door no one has opened since the house was painted, or an office door where an electric strike, a power supply and an exit device all have to work together under the state code.

If you do hire someone for the harder doors, check them. Westchester's Department of Consumer Protection says county law requires home improvement contractors to be licensed and runs an online licence search, and Yonkers issues its own home improvement licence.

“A stronger lock is what stops burglars”

A good deadbolt that is actually thrown is excellent protection for the door it's on. But the Westchester reports we've read describe burglars avoiding the front door altogether: a second-floor window reached from stacked lawn furniture and a rear sliding door in Scarsdale in 2015, second-floor windows and doors in the 2023 crews Patch reported on, a crowbar on a back slider in Pelham Manor in 2024.

None of those is solved by a better front-door lock. What helps is every door actually being locked when the house is empty, upstairs doors that are as solid as the downstairs ones, and an alarm that sounds — Patch reported the 2023 crews fled when one did.

So our door plans in Westchester start at the back and the top of the house, not the front, and smart locks go where they make "locked" the default rather than where they look best.

“With remote unlock, you can let the paramedics in from anywhere”

Remote unlock is useful when a person with the app is awake, has signal, sees the call and can confirm who is at the door. In a medical emergency at the house of someone who lives alone, often none of that is true.

Fire departments have long had another tool for this. The Elmsford Fire Department, for example, describes residential key boxes that let first responders let themselves in with a master key the department controls, aimed at seniors who may not be able to reach the door.

A sound plan uses the key box or a nearby neighbour's code as the main route, with remote unlock as a backup. The lock should also open from inside with one plain turn, so the person can let help in if they can reach the door.

“New York apartment-lock rules are the same everywhere in the state”

Articles about tenants' rights to add a lock usually describe New York City, where the Multiple Dwelling Law and the city's Housing Maintenance Code set the rules. The Attorney General's office explains that the Multiple Dwelling Law covers cities of 325,000 people or more; smaller cities, towns and villages fall under the Multiple Residence Law.

That puts Yonkers, New Rochelle, Mount Vernon, White Plains, Rye and Peekskill outside the city framework. One state rule that does apply everywhere is RPAPL 768: changing an occupant's entrance lock without giving them a key is an unlawful eviction.

For a Westchester co-op or rental, read the lease and house rules and ask in writing before changing hardware.

“Any electric lock is fine on a business door”

Lock listings rarely mention egress. On a business door they matter more than any feature. The 2025 Building Code of New York State, effective December 31, 2025, is based on the 2024 International Building Code, whose rules for electrically locked egress doors require that inside hardware cuts lock power directly and that a power failure unlocks the door.

Magnetic locks and some strikes are fail-safe by design; some battery locks and fail-secure strikes are not, and whether they're acceptable depends on the door's role in the exit route and the occupancy.

We specify every commercial door with that in mind, put a push-to-exit or request-to-exit device inside where the code calls for it, and leave the decisions about specific doors to the building department.

“A keypad lock on the pool gate does the same job as the latch”

It doesn't. The Department of State's summary of residential pool rules separates the two: gates must be self-closing and self-latching, with the latch on the pool side at a set height, and separately must be locked with a key, combination or other child-proof lock when the pool isn't in use or supervised.

A keypad that replaces the latch, or that holds the gate open after use, can leave a gate unlatched that looks locked. That is the opposite of what a pool barrier is for.

We add keypads alongside the latch and hinges, test that the gate swings shut and latches from fully open, and recommend confirming current rules with your town's building department under the 2025 code.

Landlords

Smart Locks on Rented Doors in Yonkers, Mount Vernon and New Rochelle

Many smart lock calls from the southern cities come from owners who live in one half of a two- or three-family house and rent the rest. A keyless street door is a genuine convenience for everyone in that arrangement, but it changes how lock changes and tenant access work, so it's worth setting up carefully.

Keep the tenant's access independent of your account. A tenant whose only way in is a code that lives in your app is one lapsed subscription or dead battery away from being locked out. Never use the lock to resolve a dispute. When a tenancy ends properly, remove the departing tenant's credentials and issue new ones to the next tenant on the day the keys change hands, and re-key or replace any mechanical cylinder they held. A lock that needs a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi network will struggle at a mudroom door two rooms away from a router that only reaches it faintly, and a Thread or Z-Wave lock needs the matching hub or border router in the house. Look, too, at what the lock does with codes: some hold a few dozen users, others well over a hundred, and only some allow schedules by day and hour, which matters a great deal in a house with a weekly cleaner, a sitter and a seasonal pool service.

Finally, think about the finish. Westchester front doors often carry aged brass or oil-rubbed bronze hardware that has been on the house for decades. Most smart lock lines come in a handful of finishes, and the inside housing is larger than an ordinary thumb-turn, so hold the box up to the door before you open it.

Field Notes

Field Notes From the Back of Westchester Houses

How our installers walk a Westchester house — the routine we follow on every visit, not the story of one job

We don't start at the front door. We ask which door the family used this morning and which the cleaner, the walker and the children use, and we go there first. Often it's the garage entry or a mudroom door rather than the front, and whichever it is will be opened far more than the rest.

Then we go round the back. We slide the patio door, lift its latch, and see how much the panel moves in the track. We open the French doors and look at what holds the passive leaf. Then we go upstairs and open every door onto a balcony or porch roof, check its lock, and look down at what is underneath it.

Then the garage keypad. We ask how long the code has been the same and who knows it. If the answer is "years" and "everyone," a new code goes on the list before we leave.

Last, the people. We sit down with whoever will use each door, including anyone who lives alone or will be managing a parent's house from a distance, and agree a method and a schedule for each of them. Only then do we talk about which lock.

FAQ

Westchester County Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Is the price you quote at the walkthrough the price on the invoice?
+

Yes. House doors are quoted flat, in writing, after the free walkthrough, and that figure is what you pay for the work described. If you add a door or a gate on the day, we price the addition before doing it. Business-door work follows our published Westchester rates, such as $1,875 to $3,125 for a single-door keypad or reader with an electric strike.

Should we turn on the auto-unlock feature that opens the front door as we pull into the driveway?
+

We usually advise against it on the front door of a house that sits empty all day. Auto-unlock relies on your phone's location and Bluetooth, and location is not precise, so the door can unlock while you are still parked in the drive, unloading the car or walking the dog nearby. Most households are better served by auto-lock, an alert for any door left unlocked, and a one-touch or keypad unlock at the door itself. If you do want auto-unlock, we set it on one door only and pair it with a short auto-lock timer.

Our back door has a multipoint lock that throws hooks top and bottom when the handle is lifted — can it go keyless?
+

Often, but not with an ordinary smart deadbolt. Multipoint doors need either a keyless unit made to work with that brand's gearbox or a keypad and motor kit designed for multipoint doors, and some models have no compatible option. We identify the gearbox at the walkthrough and tell you which route, if any, suits that door before you buy anything.

Can the lock's entry history be saved for a police report or an insurance claim after a break-in?
+

Usually. Most connected locks keep an activity log in the app showing which code, fob or phone opened the door and when, and many apps let you export it or take screenshots. How long the log is kept depends on the brand and plan, so after any incident save it straight away. A log will not show a forced slider or window, which is why we pair locks with alarm contacts and cameras.

Can the front door be left unlocked on purpose for a party or a graduation open house?
+

Yes. Most smart locks have a mode that keeps auto-lock off, or a passage setting that holds the bolt back, until you turn it off again. The risk is forgetting to switch it back, so we show you where the setting is and set an alert or a scheduled re-lock for the end of the evening. Leave the upstairs and back doors locked while guests use the front.

What stops someone who gets into our email from opening the front door?
+

The lock account's own security. Use a unique password for the lock app, turn on two-factor sign-in where the app offers it, and keep administrator access to two trusted people. Review the user list now and then for anyone you don't recognise. The lock's physical key and keypad keep working independently of the account, so you are never locked out of your own house by an account problem.

Can a realtor's showings run on temporary codes instead of a lockbox on the front door?
+

They can, if your agent agrees. A code that works only during a showing window, or one per agent with an end date, gives you a record of each visit and nothing to steal from a box on the railing. Some brokerages have their own lockbox rules, so check with your agent first, and delete the showing codes on the day the house goes under contract.

Can one app run the locks at our house and at my mother's house in the next town?
+

Usually, if both houses use the same brand. Most lock apps let one account manage several homes, each with its own lock list, users and alerts, so your mother's neighbour can have a code for her door without appearing on yours. Make sure someone at or near her house is also an administrator, and keep her emergency-access plan separate from your own household's codes.

Will the inside of the lock look bulky on our older door?
+

Some will. Smart deadbolts carry their batteries and motor in the inside housing, which is larger than a plain thumb-turn, and retrofit units are larger still. On a Tudor or Victorian door with narrow stiles or detailed trim we measure the space, show you the housing size of the models that fit, and point out slimmer options where they exist.
