Property map. Curb, run, house, entries — the horizontal diagram drawn on every call out here, and the reason faults get found instead of dug for.

Long run. The private wiring from gate or curb box to the house — buried or fence-line — and the middle link in every driveway-entry chain.

Yard span. One section of a buried or aerial run. Faults get bracketed to the span, spliced at the point — feet of repair, not yards of trench.

Curb box. The call point at the street or gate — outdoor hardware with outdoor failure modes, serviced and specified for the weather it faces.

Multi-entry. Front, side and garage on one household system — each entry with its own branch, each branch failing alone.

Entry spur. One door’s slice of a multi-entry system: its button, strike and short run. Dead spur, healthy house — repair at the spur.

In-law line. The second household’s circuit on a two-family — kept honestly separate so each floor rings exactly who it should.

Release path. Button to strike, end to end, gate or door. Heard but never admitted means the fault lives here and nowhere else.

Bay-rated. Outdoor hardware chosen for the water-side blocks — enclosures and terminations that treat weather as a design input, not a surprise.

Kitchen-table quote. The homeowner version of the written finding: plain language, photographs, both paths priced — decided where families decide things.

Station. The in-house wall unit, any generation. Fails alone, replaced alone — the rest of the property never notices.

Platform vs. part. The whole repair-or-replace question in four words: parts fail cheap and often, platforms rare and dear. Diagnosis keeps them separate — gate box or garage door.
