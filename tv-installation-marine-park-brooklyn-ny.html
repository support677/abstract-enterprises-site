The Neighborhood

Why Marine Park Properties Need TV Installation

Marine Park sits in southeast Brooklyn, bounded for the most part by Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway, with the 530-acre park of the same name at its southern end. It is one of the few parts of the borough where almost every TV we hang goes into a house. Brick Underground's 2022 guide to the neighborhood described most of the housing as one- and two-family detached and semi-detached homes built between the 1910s and the 1940s, with co-ops on the southern and western borders, a few small low-rise condos in the middle and no rental apartment buildings to speak of. People who rent here usually rent a floor of a two-family or an apartment above a shop.

That one fact changes how a TV job goes. In an apartment tower the question is usually "concrete or drywall?" In a Marine Park house the TV wall could be plaster on wood lath, plaster laid straight onto brick, solid brick on a party wall shared with the house next door, a stone accent around a fireplace that was taken out decades ago, or fresh drywall from a basement finished last winter. Brick Underground put the frame houses and duplexes at around 1,100 square feet and the brick houses at 1,200 to 2,200 square feet. Within one block you can meet three different walls, and within one house you can meet four.

The houses also carry the marks of the people who bought them. The same guide described Marine Park as a place where generations of city employees, police officers, firefighters and teachers, have settled, and where families stay. That shows up on the wall. We see living rooms that have been redone two or three times, with a 1990s paneled wall over 1930s plaster, a fireplace closed up in one decade and the opening patched in the next, and a rec room in the basement built by a relative with a weekend and a box of drywall screws. None of it is a problem if it is found before the bracket goes up. All of it is a problem if it is found after.

Getting here shapes the work too. Marine Park has no subway station; Wikipedia lists the nearest as Kings Highway on the Brighton line in Midwood, and the neighborhood runs on buses and cars. For a TV job that is good news. The streets are lined with driveways, so the technician can usually park close and carry a 65-inch box a few steps instead of half a block. It also means that a new TV often arrives by car or by delivery to the driveway, and the technician's first job is getting the box from the hallway into the right room without scraping a doorframe.

There is also the question of what a TV on display invites. In March 2026 News 12 Brooklyn reported that on February 23 at 4:30 in the afternoon, four people entered a home on East 34th Street and Avenue N and removed two smart TVs before getting away; no one was hurt, and the report did not say how they got in. A bracket is not a lock, and we will not pretend one is. What placement can do is keep a big screen out of the line of sight from the sidewalk and the front window, and that is one of the things we talk through in the front rooms of Marine Park houses, where the living room window often faces the street a few feet from the stoop.

Finally, there are children and grandchildren. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's February 2022 report counted 581 tip-over deaths involving furniture, TVs or appliances since 2000, and found that 71% of the children's deaths involved a television. In houses where grandkids visit on weekends and the bedroom TV still sits on top of a dresser, getting it onto the wall, or at least strapped, is the most practical safety work we do.

Six Marine Park facts that shape a TV job before we bring a drill

  • Houses, not towers. Brick Underground describes most homes as one- and two-family detached and semi-detached houses from the 1910s to the 1940s, so the TV wall is a house wall, and the house's age tells us what is likely behind the paint.
  • Tudor details sit where the TV wants to go. Brownstoner's February 2022 report on 3615 Avenue T, a 1931 brick Tudor Revival, described a sunken living room with a "beamed and stone-accented ceiling" and "glass-fronted built-ins," with stone accents carried onto the dining room walls that it said "may make hanging art harder." A TV is a large, heavy piece of art.
  • Fireplaces come and go. The 1931 house on Avenue T appears to have lost its fireplace; a 1920s house at 3622 Avenue R was listed by Brownstoner in 2020 with a working wood-burning fireplace. Either way, the wall above or where a fireplace was is often the wall the family wants the TV on.
  • The basement opens onto the yard. The same reports describe lower levels with their own entrance from the rear yard, second kitchens and rec rooms. Basement TVs are common here, and they bring block walls, paneling and damp into the job.
  • Garages become rooms. Brick Underground noted a Quentin Road listing whose detached garage had a bathroom and could be turned into a guest room or office. A TV in a building at the back of the lot needs a power and network plan, not just a bracket.
  • The neighborhood runs on cars and buses. With no subway station in Marine Park, technicians come by van and can usually park close to the house, which makes carrying a large screen far easier than on a crowded apartment block.

Same-Day Repair in Marine Park

Need Repair in Marine Park? A TV That Sags, a Mount That Rattles, a Screen Stuck Too High

The urgent calls we get from Marine Park houses are rarely about a brand-new TV. They are about a screen someone hung years ago, often on plastic plugs in plaster, that has started to lean forward at the top, or a full-motion arm that drops a little every time someone pulls it out. Sometimes it is a TV that was put up over a mantel so high that nobody in the family can watch it without tipping their head back, and the household has finally decided to fix it.

If a TV is pulling away from the wall, do not push it back or try to tighten it from the front. Unplug it if you can reach the plug safely, keep children and pets out of the area, and put a cushion or a folded blanket on the floor under it. Then send a photo to (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, and if it is going back up on a proper fixing, the rehang is priced from the same list as a new mount. We book quickly, usually within two hours of your message, and we will tell you on the phone whether the fixing can be saved or whether the bracket has to move to solid material.

The most common cause we find is simple: the bracket was screwed into the surface of the wall and nothing behind it. Old plaster is hard on the outside and crumbly underneath, and a short plug grips the crumbly part. Over months, the weight of the screen and the pull of a tilting or swinging arm work it loose. The fix is to find what is really behind the plaster, whether that is a wood stud, a run of lath or solid brick, and fix into that.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Marine Park's Architecture

Six kinds of home cover nearly every TV job we do here. Each one has its own walls, its own wiring paths and its own surprises. Before a visit we try to work out which of these you live in, because it tells us what to pack.

The brick Tudor

The 1920s and 1930s English-style houses that period ads sold as "brick period" homes, often with a sunken living room, beams, stone trim and built-in cabinets. Beautiful rooms, crowded walls.

The semi-detached brick pair

Two houses sharing one solid party wall, each with a side driveway and a garage behind. The shared wall is usually the strongest wall in the house for a TV.

The frame house and frame two-family

Wood-framed houses with siding outside and plaster or later drywall inside, in the size range Brick Underground put at about 1,100 square feet. Studs are easier to find; space is tighter.

The two-family with a tenant floor

An owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sharing a stair and sometimes a basement where the cable and internet come in. The wiring plan has to respect both households.

The finished basement

Rec rooms, second kitchens and gyms with their own door to the yard, built over decades on block foundations, sometimes with paneling over masonry and drop ceilings over the pipes.

The edge co-op and the flat above a store

The co-op buildings on the southern and western borders and the apartments over the shops on the avenues, where building rules, elevators or stairs and shared walls come into play.

The brick Tudor: beams, stone and built-ins around the only good wall

The brick Tudors are the houses people picture when they think of Marine Park. Brownstoner's 1931 example at 3615 Avenue T had a sunken living room with a beamed, stone-accented ceiling and glass-fronted built-ins, and it is typical of what we meet: a handsome room where most of the wall space is already spoken for. The beams can sit lower than the top of a big screen would reach. Stone trim can run along the edge of the wall the family wants. Built-in cabinets can flank the one stretch of clear plaster, and their glass doors do not like vibration from a hammer drill a foot away.

The answer is careful layout, not force. We measure the clear area between the trim and the beams, pick a screen size and a bracket that fit inside it with a margin, and plan the drilling so the hammer drill stays away from the cabinet glass. Where the wall behind the plaster is brick, which is common on the outside walls of these houses, the fixing goes into the brick; where it is a framed interior partition, it goes into the studs. And because a sunken living room is watched from two levels, from the sofa below and from the step or the hallway above, we check the height from both before the bracket goes up.

The semi-detached pair: one party wall, two families

Semi-detached houses share one wall with the house next door, and in a brick pair that wall is usually solid masonry from the basement to the roof. For a TV, that is often the best wall in the house: no studs to hunt for, no hollow spots, and brick that will hold a sleeve anchor for decades if it is drilled and set properly. The Brownstoner forum thread on mounting a TV over a fireplace put it plainly, in the words of one Brooklyn homeowner: "the fireplaces are on the party walls with no studs, just plaster over brick."

Two cautions come with it. First, the brick behind the plaster has to be deep enough for the anchor; we drill a test hole and check before we commit. Second, a party wall carries sound. A soundbar mounted tight to the shared wall can be heard in the neighbor's living room, and drilling is heard there too. We drill during the day, keep the drilling short, and where the household is worried about sound we suggest a soundbar on a console or a bracket that holds it a little off the wall rather than bolted to it.

The frame house: studs you can find, rooms that are tight

Frame houses are wood-framed from top to bottom, so a good scanner finds the studs, and on a framed wall the in-wall power and cable kit can usually go in cleanly. The challenge is space. At around 1,100 square feet, a frame house's living room may be the passage between the front door and the kitchen, with windows on one side and a radiator on another. We look for the wall where the screen will not catch glare from the front windows, will not sit over a radiator, and will not force everyone to sit with their back to the door.

Some frame houses have had their original plaster covered with drywall during a renovation, and some have had the plaster removed and replaced. The scanner and a pilot hole tell us which. Drywall over plaster is thick and needs longer fasteners to reach the studs; we size them on site rather than trusting what came in the bracket's bag.

The two-family with a tenant floor: shared stairs, shared wiring

In a two-family, the internet and cable usually come into the basement, and both floors' lines start there. If the owner wants a wired network line to the living room TV and the tenant upstairs wants the same, the cables share a path, and the owner and the tenant share an interest in it being tidy and labeled. We label every line we run at both ends, keep each household's cables separate, and never borrow a pair from someone else's line to save time.

If you are the tenant, the question is permission. Ask the owner before you book, tell them which wall and what size, and send us their answer. Patching and painting the holes when you move out is not something we do, so it is worth agreeing in advance who handles that.

The finished basement: block, paneling and a door to the yard

Marine Park basements are often real rooms. Brownstoner described the lower level of the Avenue T Tudor as having a second kitchen, a rec room, a laundry area, a full bath and its own entrance from the rear yard. The walls in these rooms vary: block foundation walls, paneling nailed to furring strips over block, drywall on wood or metal studs, and sometimes a mix within one room. A big TV on paneling alone is a bad idea; the fixing has to go through the paneling into the block or the framing behind it.

Basements also bring water into the conversation. A Reddit post about the neighborhood called it one of Brooklyn's biggest floodplains and warned people off basement apartments, and while that is one resident's comment, we ask every basement customer where water has reached before, if anywhere. The TV, the cable box and the power strip go well above that line, and a wired network line coming from the utility area is routed along the ceiling, not across the floor.

The edge co-op and the apartment over a shop

Brick Underground placed Marine Park's co-op buildings on its southern and western borders, and many of the neighborhood's renters live over the stores on the avenues. In a co-op, the board may want a certificate of insurance before a contractor works in an apartment, and some buildings limit drilling to weekday hours. Send us the building's rules and the exact name the certificate has to show, and we arrange it before the visit.

Over a store, the walls are often masonry on the street side and framed partitions inside, and the stair to the apartment can be narrow. We ask about the stair before we bring an 85-inch box, and we bring a second person when the screen's size calls for one.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Hanging a TV Between the Beams and the Built-Ins

These notes describe how our technicians handle the kind of living room Brownstoner described on Avenue T, a sunken Tudor room with a beamed ceiling, stone trim and glass-fronted cabinets. They are the steps we follow in rooms like that, not a story about one particular house.

Measure the window, not the wall. The usable space is the rectangle between the beam, the trim and the cabinets, minus a margin so the screen does not look jammed in. We measure it before talking about screen size, because a 75-inch TV that does not fit the clear space is worse than a 65-inch that does.

Protect the glass. Before any drilling near glass-fronted built-ins, the doors are opened or taped, and anything on the shelves that could rattle is moved. The hammer drill is used only for the masonry holes, and only for as long as it takes.

Look from both levels. In a sunken room the TV is seen from below and from above. We tape the screen's outline on the wall, then check it from the sofa, from the top step and from the hallway, and adjust until it works for the seat that matters most.

Find the brick, then trust the brick. On an outside wall of a brick Tudor, the plaster usually sits on masonry. A test hole tells us how deep the plaster is and whether the brick behind it is solid. Then the anchors go in deep, into the brick, and the plaster is only the finish they pass through.

Leave the room as it was. A dust-shroud vacuum catches most of the red brick dust at the drill, the drop cloths catch the rest, and the cabinet doors are closed and wiped before we leave.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick, Stone or Paneling: What Holds a TV in a Marine Park House

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, assumes drywall over wood studs, and that is the easiest wall to work on. Marine Park houses mostly predate drywall, so the wall charge on our list is part of almost every quote here. Here is how each wall behaves and what it adds.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The usual interior wall in a house from the 1920s and 1930s. The plaster is hard on the surface and weaker underneath, and the wood lath behind it is thin. The bracket has to be fixed into the studs behind the lath, so we find them with a scanner and confirm with a pilot hole hidden behind where the bracket will sit. The extra charge covers the slower, careful drilling that keeps the plaster from cracking.

Plaster laid onto brick (+$65). On outside walls and party walls of brick houses, the plaster often goes straight onto the masonry with no studs at all. The fixing goes through the plaster into the brick, with anchors long enough to reach well past the plaster. We check the depth first; the homeowner in the Brownstoner forum thread had a contractor who was not sure the brick behind his plaster was deep enough, and that is exactly the question a test hole answers.

Solid brick or stone (+$65). Exposed brick walls, stone fireplace surrounds and stone trim. Brick holds very well when the anchors are set into the brick itself rather than the mortar joints, and when they are the right length. Stone varies: some is solid, some is a thin veneer over another wall, and we drill a test hole to know which.

Paneling over block or framing. Common in basement rec rooms. The paneling itself is not structural. The fixing goes through it into the block or the furring behind, and the wall is priced by what is found behind the paneling.

Drywall over plaster. Found in houses that were renovated without stripping the old walls. It is thick and needs longer fasteners to reach the studs, and it can hide old openings, so the scanner work takes a little longer.

Hiding the wires without breaking the rules

The most common request after "put it on the wall" is "make the wires disappear." On a framed wall, the right answer is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list: a recessed power inlet behind the TV, connected inside the wall to a second plate lower down that plugs into a normal outlet, with a pass-through for the HDMI and network cables beside it. It is made to go inside a wall. An ordinary extension cord or the TV's own power cord pushed down through two holes is not, and it is one of the shortcuts we refuse.

Where the job needs more than that, the rules are clear. The NYC Department of Buildings says no installation or modification to any electrical system can be done without a permit, and that the work is done by a NYC Licensed Master Electrician. If a new circuit or a new outlet wired into the house is the right answer, that is electrician's work and we say so; we do not do electrical work. On brick, stone and plaster-on-brick walls, nothing can be fished inside the wall, so the choice is a $59 paintable cord cover from the TV down to the outlet, a console below the screen that hides the cables, or an outlet added by an electrician behind the screen.

Fireplaces

Over the Mantel in Marine Park: Working Fireplaces, Gas Inserts and Openings That Were Closed Up

Fireplaces are where a lot of Marine Park living rooms point, and where a lot of families want the TV. Some still burn wood, like the 1920s house at 3622 Avenue R that Brownstoner featured in 2020. Some have gas inserts. Some were taken out, like the one that appears to be missing from the 1931 Tudor on Avenue T, and the opening was bricked or plastered over. Each case is different, and above-fireplace mounting adds $130 on our list because of the extra checks and the masonry work it involves.

Working wood-burning fireplaces. Heat rises, and a TV directly over a working fire takes it. HomeAdvisor's guidance is to test the temperature at the mounting spot and avoid putting a TV where it goes above 100°F. We ask how often the fireplace is used and whether the mantel is deep enough to deflect heat, and we tell you honestly when the answer is a different wall. Height is the other problem: the Brownstoner forum poster's neighbor warned that mounting over a mantel "is awfully high and invites a sore neck." Taskrabbit's guide puts a comfortable center of screen around 42 inches off the floor for seated viewing, and a mantel puts the bottom of the screen well above that. A tilting bracket set as low as safely possible helps.

Gas inserts. These can run hot at the face, and the insert's manual often gives clearances for anything above it. We read the manual with you before we agree to a position.

Closed-up openings. A fireplace that was bricked in and plastered over leaves a wall that looks flat but is not uniform. The patch may be newer brick, block, or a frame of wood with drywall or plaster on it, and the old chimney breast may be solid masonry around it. We find the edges of the patch with the scanner and a test hole and fix the bracket into the old masonry around it, not into the patch.

Cables over a mantel. A chimney breast is masonry, so cables cannot be hidden inside it. The usual answers are a paintable cord cover running down the side of the breast to the outlet, or a cable box and soundbar on a console beside the fireplace with only the HDMI and power going up to the screen. We will show you both on the wall with tape before you choose.

Basements, Garages and Back Rooms

TVs Below Grade and Out Back: Rec Rooms, Garage Gyms and Backyard Offices

A lot of Marine Park TV work happens away from the living room. The houses here have usable basements, garages at the end of driveways and, increasingly, garages and back rooms turned into offices, gyms and places for teenagers to watch a game without taking over the main floor. These rooms need a little more planning than a living room, because power, signal and moisture all behave differently there.

Power. Basement and garage outlets are often few and far between, and sometimes there is only one, on the wrong wall. An in-wall kit can move power up behind a screen on a framed wall. On block, a cord cover runs down to the outlet. If the room really needs a new outlet, that is a job for a licensed electrician with a permit, and we will plan the TV around where the electrician puts it.

Signal. Wi-Fi from a router on the main floor often does not reach a basement well, and a detached garage at the back of a lot may be beyond its reach entirely. A wired network line from where the internet comes in is the most reliable answer for a streaming TV. For a detached garage, the line has to leave the house and enter the garage, and the route has to be planned around the yard, the fence and the driveway. Some households choose a wireless access point in the garage instead. We talk through both.

Moisture and temperature. A finished basement that has taken water before, or a garage that is not heated, is not a friendly place for electronics on the floor. We put the TV high enough on the wall that a wet floor never reaches it, keep the cable box and power strip on a shelf or the wall rather than the floor, and tell you when a room's conditions are outside what the TV's maker allows.

Walls. Garages and basements bring the full range: bare block, poured concrete, paneling over block, drywall on wood or steel studs. The fixing goes into whatever is solid behind the surface, and we price it from the wall line that matches what we find.

Safety and Security

Kids, Grandkids and Front Windows: Where a TV Goes in a Marine Park House

Two kinds of safety come up in almost every Marine Park living room, and neither is about how the screen looks.

Tip-overs. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's February 2022 report counted 581 tip-over deaths involving furniture, TVs or appliances since 2000. Of those, 472 were children, and 71% of the children's deaths involved a television. The same report estimated that 22,500 people a year went to an emergency room after a tip-over in 2018 to 2020. Many Marine Park households have grandchildren over on weekends, and many still have an older TV on a dresser in a bedroom. Mounting it on the wall takes it off the furniture entirely. If a TV stays on a stand, it should be strapped to the wall or the furniture behind it, and the furniture itself should be anchored. Tudor living rooms, beams and stone

Our living room is sunken two steps below the front hall. Where should the screen sit so it works both from the sofa and from the top of the steps?

The stone trim in our 1930s front room runs right along the edge of the wall we want. Can a bracket go next to it without cracking the stone?

Yes, if the bracket is fixed into the wall beside the stone and not into the stone itself, and if the hammer drill is kept a sensible distance from the trim. We measure the clear area first, choose a screen and bracket that fit inside it with a margin, and drill the masonry holes with the trim protected. If the only way to fit the screen is to drill into the stone, we test it first, because some stone trim is solid and some is a thin facing over brick.

A previous owner removed our fireplace and plastered over the opening. Is the patched part of the wall safe for a TV?

Usually the patch itself is the weakest part of the wall, so we avoid fixing into it. We find its edges with a scanner and a test hole, then fix the bracket into the original masonry of the chimney breast around it. If the patch is large and the screen has to sit over it, a bracket with a wide wall plate lets the fixings reach the solid brick on either side.

Our living room has glass-fronted built-in cabinets on both sides of the only clear wall. Will drilling between them shake the glass?

It can if nobody plans for it. We empty the shelves nearest the drilling or ask you to, open or tape the cabinet doors, and keep the hammer drill on only for the seconds each masonry hole takes. "Just find a stud"

Generic guides start with a stud finder. On drywall over wood studs that works. On the outside wall of a brick Tudor, there may be no studs at all, just plaster laid onto brick.

It is not the answer for a sunken Tudor living room watched from two levels, for a screen over a mantel, or for a basement rec room where people watch from a sectional set back against the far wall. Even the same guide says that above a fireplace the TV should go "as low as safely possible above the mantel."

We mark the screen with painter's tape and check it from the seats that matter in your room before anything is drilled. Height is set for the people who watch, not for a number.

"You can mount over any fireplace"

Angi notes that there is no license for TV mounting itself, and app marketplaces list thousands of people willing to do it. On a new drywall wall, a careful handyman can do a good job.
