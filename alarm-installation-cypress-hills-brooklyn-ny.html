The Neighborhood

Why Cypress Hills Properties Need Alarm Installation

Cypress Hills is the northeastern corner of Brooklyn, and the edges explain a lot about the houses inside them. Wikipedia’s article on East New York, which is where its Cypress Hills entry leads, puts the Cemetery Belt and the Queens line along the north and east, with Highland Park, Ridgewood and Glendale to the north and Woodhaven across the line to the east. Cypress Hills Cemetery alone covers 225 acres at 833 Jamaica Avenue and straddles the borough line, with one main gate on Jamaica Avenue in Brooklyn and the other on Cooper Avenue in Glendale, Queens. Highland Park, 141 acres laid out from 1901 around the old Ridgewood Reservoir, sits on the same border. Local writers do not all draw the lines the same way: a 2024 BK Reader profile describes the neighborhood as running from the Jackie Robinson Parkway down to Atlantic Avenue and from Highland Park across to Queens, while Wikipedia folds it into the wider East New York district. Either way, it is a neighborhood of parks and burial grounds on one side and a busy shopping street on the other.

The streets are older than the city they belong to. Wikipedia notes that Fulton Street, Jamaica Avenue and Broadway were named for the old roads to Brooklyn, Jamaica and Williamsburg, and Rob Stephenson’s 2023 neighborhood essay on Substack describes the Jamaica Plank Road, an early nineteenth-century wooden toll road, running through the middle of the area. Elevated lines followed: Wikipedia dates the Jamaica Avenue line to 1885 and the Fulton Street line to 1889, and the Cypress Hills station at Jamaica Avenue and Hemlock Street opened in 1893. The area was annexed to the City of Brooklyn as its 26th Ward in 1886, and Stephenson quotes the Brooklyn Eagle of 1894 observing that “the fields of corn and cabbage have been superseded by cottages.”

Those cottages, and what replaced them, are the reason a Cypress Hills alarm is planned differently from one in a block of identical walk-ups. Writing about a Queen Anne house at 218 Arlington Avenue in 2014, Brownstoner’s Suzanne Spellen described Cypress Hills as a late nineteenth and early twentieth century suburban neighborhood, with row houses mixed among detached and semi-detached homes, and noted that a stretch of Arlington Avenue was known as Doctor’s Row. Two years earlier she had written about a run of brick row houses on Sunnyside Avenue built between 1900 and 1920 by a developer called Richards Real Homes, with deep porches and tile roofs, ninety on that one street alone. She also pointed out the hills themselves: glacial ridges that left steep streets and houses built into the slope, with the houses on one side of Sunnyside Avenue raised above the sidewalk on long flights of stairs.

For an installer, that history becomes a list of particular openings. A wood-frame house usually has a porch whose roof sits just below the second-floor windows, a bay window with three separate sashes, a side driveway and sometimes a separate garage at the back. A brick row house has a deep porch that hides the front door from the street, a cellar door under the stoop or at the rear, and a back yard shared visually with a dozen other yards. A house built into a hill may have a basement that is below ground at the front but opens straight onto the yard at the back. A two-family has two households and often a shared front vestibule. And a storefront on Fulton Street has a rolling gate, a glass front behind it, a back door onto a yard or alley, and the elevated train overhead.

The local record touches several of those points. In February 2021, CBS New York reported that a 78-year-old woman came home near Grant Avenue and O’Brien Place to find a burglar already inside; he left with cash, a laptop, an e-reader and jewelry. Later that year the same station reported that a 68-year-old man on Crescent Street, between Atlantic Avenue and Fulton Street, was slashed across the head after going outside to confront two people taking a package from in front of his home. In June 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported a burglar who took a safe said to hold $100,000 from a business near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street at about 2:15 a.m., and four nights later forced his way into a second business on Conduit Boulevard and emptied a register. Wikipedia’s summary of the 75th Precinct’s 2023 figures, which cover East New York as well as Cypress Hills, lists 354 burglaries and 1,073 grand larcenies.

Read together, those reports describe problems an alarm can actually address: knowing whether a house is safe before you walk in, never having to step outside to deal with someone at the door, and getting a call within moments when a business is entered in the middle of the night. They do not mean the neighborhood is a fortress-or-nothing place. Residents posting on Reddit describe Cypress Hills in very different terms, from a sleepy residential area to somewhere they would rather not walk late at night, and both views can be true on different blocks at different hours. An alarm should be planned around the specific house and street, not around a reputation. Most owners who ask about an alarm also ask about cameras; those are covered on our Cypress Hills security camera installation page, and both can be installed in one visit.

Need Alarm Repair in Cypress Hills? Dead Batteries, Porch Zones That Read Open, Panels That Stopped Reporting

A good share of the calls from Cypress Hills are about an alarm that already exists. A family buys a frame house on one of the side streets and finds a keypad by the door that nobody has the code for. A two-family owner’s system starts beeping at three in the morning. A Fulton Street shop discovers its panel has not reported to the monitoring station since the phone line was cancelled. These faults come from a short list, and most are fixed in one visit of an hour or two.

A slow, regular chirp nearly always means a weak battery, either in one wireless sensor or in the panel’s own standby battery, which in older systems may be well past its life. A zone that reads open when the door is shut usually points to a contact that has shifted on a porch door that swells in summer, or a wire nicked where it crossed a window frame during painting. A panel that says it cannot communicate generally lost a landline or an old 3G radio and needs a cellular communicator. A siren that sounds again a few minutes after being silenced suggests a zone that keeps re-triggering, and in New York City an audible building alarm has to be able to shut itself off within fifteen minutes, under Administrative Code § 24-238, so a siren that keeps going is a repair, not a nuisance to live with.

Our technicians carry common batteries, contacts, keypads and communicators on the truck. When the old panel is worth keeping, we reset it, delete the previous owner’s codes and bring it back onto your phone. When it is not, the replacement line on the Brooklyn price list applies. Call (347) 934-8335; same-day arrival windows are offered whenever the schedule allows, and in most cases the faulty part is replaced within an hour or two of the technician walking in.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Cypress Hills

Every job starts from the same question: which openings can someone reach without a ladder, and which can someone reach unseen? The services below are how those answers turn into equipment.

Whole-house alarms for frame houses

Contacts on every porch, side and rear door, on bay-window sashes and on upstairs windows that a porch roof brings within reach; motion detectors on the stair landing and the main hall; glass-break detectors where old single panes face the yard. Wire is run through the cellar ceiling and hollow frame walls wherever it saves time; wireless covers the rest.

Row-house systems for brick blocks

A layout built around the deep front porch, the rear yard door, the cellar door and any attic dormer. Because every house in a developer-built row tends to share one floor plan, we place keypads and motion detectors where a visitor to the house next door would not expect them.

Two-household partitions

One panel divided into separate areas for the owner and the tenant, each with its own codes and app logins, plus a shared area for the street door, the vestibule and the cellar, armed automatically when both households are out.

Garages, driveways and yards

Wireless contacts and motion detectors in a detached rear garage that report to the house panel by radio, a driveway or side-gate contact, and a porch or yard floodlight camera that switches on when the alarm trips.

Shop and restaurant alarms

Commercial panels for storefronts on Fulton Street, Jamaica Avenue and Atlantic Avenue: contacts on the rolling gate and on the door behind it, glass-break across the window, back-door and roof-hatch coverage, a hold-up button, opening and closing reports, and video verification where it is wanted.

Takeovers and panel replacements

Reviving an inherited system: testing every wired zone, replacing the dead communicator, clearing a previous owner’s codes and putting the keypad onto your phone. When the panel itself is beyond saving, it is replaced on the existing wiring.

Smoke, heat and carbon monoxide

Listening modules and monitored smoke, heat and CO detectors tied into the same panel, which matters most in a two-family where the owner rents out a floor and the rental unit has fuel-burning equipment.

Doorbells, keypads and fobs at the top of the steps

Video doorbells and keypads placed so they work for a front door that sits a full flight above the sidewalk, and key fobs with enough range to arm the system from the bottom of the stairs or the driveway.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Cypress Hills’ Architecture

Four kinds of building make up most of our Cypress Hills work. The descriptions are drawn from Brownstoner’s architecture and listings coverage of particular addresses and from Wikipedia’s summary of the area’s housing; what follows each description is how we approach that type, not a claim about any specific house.

Detached and semi-detached wood-frame houses

Brownstoner’s 2018 listing roundup featured an Italianate wood-frame house at 339 Jamaica Avenue with a front porch, a bay window, a driveway and a garage at the rear; its 2014 profile of 218 Arlington Avenue showed a Queen Anne house of about 1900 whose fish-scale shingles had since gone under vinyl siding. Frame walls are hollow, which makes wiring easier than in brick, and porches, bays and garages multiply the openings.

Brick row houses with porches

The Sunnyside Avenue rows Brownstoner described in 2012 are red or buff brick with tile roofs, deep porches and bays, built between 1900 and 1920. A 2026 listing on Grant Avenue was a single-family brick row house with the kitchen on the parlor level. Party walls are solid masonry, so wire runs through the cellar and up a closet or chase, and wireless sensors fill in where plaster should not be opened.

Two- to four-family houses

Wikipedia counts two- to four-family houses among the area’s main building types, and listings on streets such as Nichols Avenue describe solid two-family homes. These are partitioned systems: one panel, separate areas and codes per household, and a shared area for the common door and cellar.

Apartment buildings and NYCHA developments

Apartment buildings range from small walk-ups to the seven-story buildings of Cypress Hills Houses, which Wikipedia places between Sutter Avenue and Linden Boulevard and Fountain and Euclid Avenues, completed in May 1955. Inside an apartment the work is wireless and removable, with nothing fixed to the building’s common areas unless management agrees.

Materials and methods, type by type

  • Frame walls with siding: wire is fished from the cellar up through the stud bays and drilled through the top of a window frame from inside, so nothing is cut into the siding. Where two layers of siding or old sheathing block the bay, we switch that opening to wireless rather than open the outside wall.
  • Brick fronts and party walls: masonry is not drilled for cable runs between floors. Wired zones stay on the cellar level and the ground floor; upper floors use wireless contacts whose signal we test from every room to the panel before anything is fixed in place.
  • Porches and bays: each sash of a bay is its own opening, and the porch door is often a second front door with its own lock. Both get contacts, and a porch roof that reaches the upstairs windows brings those windows onto the list.
  • Old wood sash and storm windows: contacts go on the inner sash, the one that actually keeps someone out, with a magnet sized for the gap; aluminum storm windows on the outside are left alone unless a customer specifically wants them monitored.
  • Cellars and garages: a cellar door under the stoop or at the back gets a heavy-duty contact; a detached garage gets wireless devices that reach the house panel, with signal strength measured before the visit ends.
The Lay of the Land

Stairs, Slopes and Porches: How the Hills Decide Which Openings Matter

Most alarm advice assumes the front door is at street level and the ground floor is the floor you walk into. In parts of Cypress Hills neither is true. Brownstoner’s Sunnyside Avenue profile describes the area’s topography as the work of glaciers, with steep streets and houses on the north side of that street raised above the sidewalk on long stairs and graded lawns, while the houses opposite sit level. A house on a slope can have three different relationships with the street at once: a front door a full story above the pavement, a side that steps down along the hill, and a back that opens at yard level onto a floor that is underground at the front.

That changes what a burglar sees and reaches. A raised front makes the entry door and the front windows harder to reach from the sidewalk, but it also hides anyone standing at them. Nobody walking past at street level can see a person crouched on a high porch working at a lock, and the porch railing and the steps themselves screen the view further. A front door at the top of a flight of stairs is therefore not automatically safer than one at grade; it trades reachability for privacy. On the other side of the house, a cellar that opens directly onto the yard is really a ground floor with its own door and windows, often with less light, older hardware and no one sleeping nearby.

So on a sloped lot the technician walks the house twice. The first pass is from outside: up the steps to the front door, around the side to see where the grade falls away, and down to the yard to see which level opens onto it. The second pass is inside, floor by floor, marking every door and window that the outside walk showed can be reached by someone standing on the ground, on a porch, on a porch roof or on a garage roof. That list, not the floor numbers, becomes the zone plan.

Porches deserve their own note. In the frame houses and the brick rows alike, porches were built deep, and their roofs often sit only a few feet below the second-floor windows. A porch roof is a platform. Anyone who can climb a railing or a column can stand on it, out of sight of the street behind the roof edge, and reach the upstairs sash. We treat any window that opens onto a porch roof, a bay roof or a low garage roof as a ground-floor window, with a contact on the sash and, where the room has more than one such window, a glass-break detector.

Front steps raise one more practical issue: how the household arms and disarms. A keypad inside the door works only after you have climbed the stairs and opened it, and the entry delay has to allow for that. For people who come up the steps with groceries, a stroller or a walker, a key fob that disarms from the bottom of the stairs, or a phone app that does it as they turn onto the block, saves a rushed climb against the countdown. A video doorbell at the top of the stairs also lets someone inside see who is on the porch without coming to the window, which on a raised porch is the only clear view there is.

Yards and Edges

Back Yards, Driveways and Detached Garages Between Jamaica Avenue and the Parkway

The houses closest to the cemeteries and to Highland Park sit at the end of the built-up city. Depending on the block, what lies behind a back fence may be another row of yards, a cemetery wall, parkland or a slope. We have not found any count of how many Cypress Hills houses back onto open ground, and this page makes none; but where a house does, the rear of the property is watched by nobody. A back door or cellar door that cannot be seen from any neighbor’s window needs to be on the alarm whether or not it is ever used.

Driveways and garages are the other recurring feature. The Jamaica Avenue frame house in Brownstoner’s 2018 roundup had a driveway beside the house and a garage at the rear, and the Bulwer Place house near Highland Park in its 2023 roundup also came with a garage. A side driveway is a private path from the street to the back of the house, screened by the house itself on one side and often by a fence on the other. A detached garage at the end of it holds cars, tools, bicycles and the ladder that reaches the upstairs windows.

We cover these with three layers. First, the doors: the side door into the house from the driveway, the back door, the cellar door and the garage’s people door and overhead door each get a contact, the garage devices reporting to the house panel by radio so no cable has to cross the yard. Second, the path: a motion-activated floodlight camera at the back corner of the house, aimed down the driveway or across the yard, records whoever comes through and can be set to switch on when the alarm trips. Third, the ladder: if a ladder lives in the garage or yard, it is chained or stored inside, because no alarm design makes a second-floor window as safe as it is when there is nothing to climb.

Outdoor motion sensing needs restraint here. A yard that backs onto parkland or a cemetery sees cats, raccoons, birds and windblown branches all night. We do not connect outdoor motion detectors to the siren on these properties. Outdoor devices record and light up; the siren answers only to doors, windows and interior detectors, which almost never false-alarm on wildlife.

Two Households, One Building

Owners, Tenants and Relatives Under One Roof: Partitioned Alarms for Cypress Hills Two-Families

A large share of Cypress Hills houses hold more than one household. Wikipedia lists two- to four-family houses among the area’s main building types, and many owners live on one floor and rent another, or share the house with grown children or parents. A single alarm with a single code does not fit that arrangement. Either the tenant knows the owner’s code, or the system is armed only when everyone happens to be out at once, which in practice means it is rarely armed at all.

A partitioned panel solves it. The house is divided into areas: one for each household’s own rooms, and a shared area for the street door, the front vestibule or hallway, the stairs and the cellar. Each household has its own codes and app logins and arms its own area whenever it leaves. The shared area arms itself automatically once every household is away, so the front door and cellar are never left unprotected just because the upstairs tenant went out after the owners did.

The cellar is usually the contested space. It holds the boiler, the meters and often a laundry both households use, and in many houses it has its own door to the yard. We put the cellar door on the shared area, add a motion detector only if nobody uses the cellar at night, and give the cellar door a distinctive chime so that either household hears it open. Where the boiler or water heater burns gas, a monitored carbon monoxide detector on the same panel reports to both households. New York City’s housing agency, HPD, requires carbon monoxide detectors in two-family houses that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances, so in a rental the detectors are not optional in any case.

Notifications follow the same split. An event in the owner’s area goes to the owners; an event in the tenant’s area goes to the tenant; an event in the shared area, or any fire or carbon monoxide alarm, goes to everyone. When a tenant moves out, their codes and app access are deleted in a few minutes from the owner’s phone, and the new tenant gets their own on moving in. Nobody ever needs to learn anyone else’s code.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Cypress Hills

Cypress Hills does most of its business on a few long streets. BK Reader’s 2024 profile names Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue as the main commercial corridors, and the city’s Neighborhood 360° study of East New York and Cypress Hills, carried out in 2015 and 2016, counted the storefronts on each. Each corridor has its own building types, hours and risks, and the alarm follows them.

Fulton Street under the J and Z

The city study counted 346 storefronts on Fulton Street with 11% vacant, and listed clothing, shoe and jewelry stores, laundromats, supermarkets and grocers, fast food, barbershops and salons as typical. It recorded that the street runs beneath the elevated J/Z line, that lighting under the tracks is poor, and that the stretch between Eldert Lane and Chestnut Street was seen as unsafe. Shops here need gate, glass and back-door coverage, and a siren and strobe that can be seen and heard under train noise.

Atlantic Avenue

The same study counted 229 storefronts on Atlantic Avenue with 10% vacant, weighted toward tax and legal offices, religious organizations, appliance, hardware and furniture stores, wholesale and construction businesses and auto shops. Wider lots, side yards and overhead doors mean contacts rated for roll-up doors and more attention to the rear. The 2026 safe theft reported by News 12 was at a business near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street.

Jamaica Avenue along the cemetery side

Jamaica Avenue carries the J on its eastern stretch and runs past the main gate of Cypress Hills Cemetery at number 833. The commercial study did not survey it, so we make no claim about its shops; from our own work, mixed-use buildings with a ground-floor business and apartments above are the usual pattern, and the alarm has to keep the shop and the homes upstairs separate.

Crescent Street, Conduit Boulevard and the side streets

Corner stores, auto businesses and small offices sit on the cross streets between the main corridors. News 12’s 2026 report placed one break-in on Conduit Boulevard, where the burglar forced his way into the building. Free-standing commercial buildings here often have more exterior walls and doors than a storefront in a row.

Merchants themselves put security first. When the Neighborhood 360° surveyors asked what change would bring in more customers, 25% of merchants named security, more than any other answer, ahead of cleanliness at 18%. The city has since organized the Cypress Hills Fulton Business Improvement District, announced in November 2024, and the Department of Small Business Services reported $100,000 in fiscal year 2024 for new commercial lighting on Fulton Street. Better street lighting helps cameras and makes the block feel watched, but it does not tell anyone that a back door has just been forced at two in the morning. That is the alarm’s job.

A typical storefront plan on these streets looks like this: a contact on the rolling gate, so the system knows the moment it is lifted; a contact on the door behind it; an acoustic glass-break detector covering the front window, tested with a train passing overhead; a heavy-duty contact on the back door and any roof hatch; a motion detector in the stockroom or office; a hold-up button out of sight behind the counter; and opening-and-closing reports, so the owner’s phone shows when the shop was armed each night and by whom. A camera on the register or the back door lets the monitoring station see what tripped the alarm before calling the police.

Rules every Cypress Hills business alarm has to respect

  • Siren cutoff: under New York City Administrative Code § 24-238, an audible alarm on a building must be able to stop by itself within fifteen minutes of starting. Every siren we install is programmed that way, which matters on Fulton Street, where most stores have apartments overhead.
  • Licensed installers: New York State requires anyone installing or servicing security alarms for others to hold a license under General Business Law Article 6-D, issued by the Department of State. AESS works under license #12000287431, and our technicians carry identification.
  • Exits stay usable: no contact, lock or gate added for the alarm may stop anyone leaving in an emergency. Rear doors that are exits keep their panic hardware; the alarm watches them rather than blocking them.
  • Fire alarms are separate work: a burglar alarm with smoke detectors is not a building fire alarm system. Businesses that need one are referred to our fire alarm team.

This summary is general information about rules that commonly apply to alarm work in New York City, not legal advice for a particular building.

Hillsides, steps and porch roofs

Our house sits up a long flight of steps above the sidewalk, so nobody on the street can see the front windows. Does being higher than the street make those windows safer or riskier?

Both, in different ways. Height makes the front windows harder to reach from the sidewalk, so a raised house rarely needs glass-break coverage on its front parlor windows the way a street-level house does. But the same height hides anyone who is already on the porch. A person at the front door of a raised house is screened by the railing and the steps and is invisible to anyone walking past below.

So we spend the protection on the door rather than the windows: a contact on the front door and on any porch door, a short entry delay sized to the climb, and a video doorbell at the top of the steps so you can see the porch from inside. Front windows that can only be reached from the porch get contacts; those that cannot be reached at all are covered by the interior motion detector.

Our house is built into the slope, so the basement is underground at the front but opens at ground level in the back. Which floor counts as the ground floor for the alarm?

For alarm purposes, every floor that has a door or window someone can reach from the ground is a ground floor. In a house like yours that means two: the floor you enter at the front, and the basement level that opens onto the yard. The back of the basement often has the oldest door in the house, the least light and nobody sleeping nearby, which makes it the more attractive of the two.

We give the yard-level door a heavy-duty contact and the basement windows contacts or a glass-break detector, put the basement on its own zone so the keypad says exactly which door opened, and place a motion detector at the foot of the basement stairs so anyone coming up into the house is detected even if they got through a window.

The porch roof on our frame house reaches almost to the second-floor bedroom windows. Do those upstairs windows belong on the alarm?

Yes. A porch roof is a platform out of sight of the street, and anyone who can climb a railing or a column can stand on it and reach the sash. We treat every window that opens onto a porch roof, a bay roof or a low garage roof as if it were on the ground floor: a contact on the sash, and a glass-break detector in rooms with more than one such window.

Because these are bedroom windows, they are usually armed in the household’s night setting, when the downstairs motion detectors are off. That way the windows above the porch are protected while you sleep, and you can still walk to the kitchen without setting anything off.

Our back fence faces open ground with no neighbors behind us. Should that side of the house be treated differently?

Yes. On a typical block, the rear of a house is overlooked by a dozen windows in the houses behind it; when the yard backs onto parkland, a cemetery wall or an empty lot, nobody overlooks it at all. Every rear door, cellar door and reachable rear window should be on the alarm, and a floodlight camera at the back corner of the house becomes more useful than it would be mid-block.

What we avoid is connecting outdoor motion detectors to the siren on such a lot, because animals and blowing branches would trigger it night after night. Outdoor devices light up and record; the siren responds only to doors, windows and indoor detectors.

Old frame and brick houses

Can alarm wire be fished through the walls of a frame house that was re-sided over the original clapboard?

Usually, yes, because the work is done from inside. Siding added over clapboard thickens the outside of the wall but leaves the stud bays between the plaster and the sheathing open. We drill up from the cellar into a bay, fish the wire to the window and come through the top of the frame from inside the room, so the siding is never cut.

Where a bay is blocked by fire stops, old knob-and-tube wiring or blown-in insulation, that one opening switches to a wireless contact rather than an opened wall. Most frame houses end up as a mix of wired zones on the lower floors and wireless ones upstairs.

The stair landing in our early-1900s house has a fixed stained-glass window that has never opened. What protects a window that cannot take a contact?

A contact only reports a window moving, so a sealed window needs a different kind of device. The simplest is a motion detector on the landing itself, aimed across the stairs so that anyone who comes through the window, or anyone climbing the stairs from below, crosses its field. Because it watches the space rather than the glass, it does not matter how the old panes break or whether the leading holds them in place.

Where the household wants the landing covered at night while people are sleeping upstairs, the detector goes in the night setting only if nobody uses the stairs after bedtime; otherwise an acoustic glass-break detector, placed close to the window and tested on site with a simulator, takes over that job. Brownstoner’s 2023 listing of a Bulwer Place house near Highland Park mentioned original stained glass, and windows like it are common enough in houses of that age that we plan for them on every walk-through.

Our row was built by one developer and every house has the same floor plan. Does a burglar who has been inside one know his way around all of them?

To some extent, yes. Brownstoner’s account of the Sunnyside Avenue rows describes one developer building ninety houses on that street to variations of one design. Anyone who has been inside one of them, as a guest, a worker or a burglar, knows where the stairs, the back door and the cellar are in the others. They also know where a keypad would normally go.

We use that. The keypad goes somewhere a visitor to the house next door would not expect, a motion detector covers the route from the back door to the stairs rather than the obvious front room, and the panel itself is hidden away from the keypad, so smashing the keypad does nothing to stop the alarm reporting.

Our rowhouse has a tile roof with dormer windows in the attic. Can anyone reach a dormer, and should it be wired?

An attic dormer on a three-story row is rarely reachable from the ground, but it may be reachable from the roofs next door, since attached houses often share a continuous roof line. If the neighbor’s roof is flat or gently pitched and connects to yours, the dormer is a way in; if a steep tile roof drops away on both sides, it is not.

We look at the roof line from the street and, where possible, from the attic. Reachable dormers get contacts; unreachable ones are covered by a motion detector at the top of the attic stairs, which is cheaper and covers every attic window at once.

Our driveway runs past the side of the house to a separate garage in the back. Can the garage be on the alarm without trenching a cable across the yard?

Yes. Wireless contacts on the garage’s people door and overhead door, and a motion detector inside, report to the house panel by radio. Range depends on distance and on what is in the way, so the technician measures signal strength from the garage to the panel before fixing anything. When the signal is weak, a wireless repeater inside the house, on the wall nearest the garage, closes the gap.

The garage usually goes on its own zone so you can leave it armed while the house is disarmed, or the other way round. If the garage holds a ladder, store it inside the garage: it is the one tool that turns a second-floor window into a ground-floor one.

Coming home and answering the door

How do I know, before I unlock the front door, that nobody got in while I was out?

Check the app before you climb the steps. A monitored or self-monitored system shows whether it is still armed, whether any zone has opened since you left and whether the siren has sounded. If something opened while you were away, the phone will already have alerted you; if the system is still armed with no events, nobody has come through a protected door or window.

For older relatives who do not use an app, a keypad with a status light visible through the front-door glass, or an exterior status indicator by the door, gives the same answer. If anything has tripped, the rule is simple: do not go in. Step back to the street, call 911 and wait.

Can we sound the siren or speak to someone at the door from inside, without opening it?

Yes, and in our view that is the right way to deal with anyone taking a package or loitering on the porch. A video doorbell with two-way audio lets you speak through it from your phone, from inside the house or from work. Most alarm apps also have a panic function that sounds the siren on demand, and a panic button can sit by the door or the bed.

The point is to never step outside to confront anyone. Packages can be replaced. Record the clip, sound the siren if you want them gone, and report it.

Fulton Street, Jamaica Avenue and Atlantic Avenue businesses

The el trains run a few feet from our second-floor storeroom windows over Fulton Street. Can a window at track level be a way in?

We treat it as one. A window that sits beside the elevated structure can, in principle, be reached by anyone on the structure, and in practice the noise of the trains covers the sound of a window being forced. We have no record of a break-in made that way in Cypress Hills, and do not claim one, but a contact on each such window costs little.

The bigger issue with track-level windows is false alarms. Passing trains shake the glass, so we avoid shock sensors there, use contacts on the sash and, if the room needs glass-break coverage, test the detector with several trains passing before we leave it armed.

The storefront next to ours on Fulton Street has been empty for months. Does a vacant unit beside us change what our alarm needs?

It can. An empty store is an unwatched space on the other side of your wall, and anyone who gets into it can work on the shared wall or ceiling without being seen from the street. The 2016 city study found 11% of Fulton Street storefronts vacant, so many shops have an empty neighbor at some point.

Contacts on doors will not notice someone coming through a wall. Motion detectors inside your stockroom and back office will, as soon as anyone enters the room, wherever they came from. For a shop with valuable stock against the shared wall, a vibration sensor on that wall gives earlier warning.

Fulton Street now has a business improvement district. Does the BID do anything that affects our own alarm?

Not directly. The city’s 2024 announcement describes the Cypress Hills Fulton BID as bringing street cleaning, beautification, wayfinding and marketing, and the Department of Small Business Services separately funded new commercial lighting on Fulton Street. Those are improvements to the street, not to any one shop’s security, and the BID does not monitor alarms or respond to them.

What better lighting does do is make your cameras’ night footage more useful and give a monitoring operator a clearer picture when an alarm trips. It is worth reviewing exterior camera angles once new lights go in, since glare can blind a camera aimed into them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Cypress Hills

A note on method first: Google’s AI-generated summary for these searches was not reachable from the tools we used to research this page, so we do not pretend to quote it. The seven claims below are paraphrases of advice that circulates widely in quick-answer boxes, chatbot replies and national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. After each claim comes what porches, slopes, two-families and corridor shops in this neighborhood actually require.

“Protect the ground floor first; upper floors are rarely entered.”

This is sound advice for a house on a flat lot with nothing to climb. It is the most common starting point in home-security guides, and on many Brooklyn blocks it is right.

In Cypress Hills, the definition of a ground floor shifts. A porch roof puts second-floor windows within reach; a house built into a slope has a basement that opens onto the yard; a raised front puts the parlor windows out of reach entirely.

Instead of counting floors, list every opening a person can reach standing on the ground, a porch, a porch roof or a garage roof. Those are the ground-floor openings, whatever floor they are on.

“A typical home needs one door sensor per entry door and a motion detector.”

Starter kits sold online are built around this idea, and cost sites often price an average home on that basis. For a small apartment with one door, it is close to true.

A Cypress Hills frame house can easily have a front door, a porch door, a side door off the driveway, a back door, a cellar door and a garage, each a separate entrance with its own lock. A kit with two or three door sensors leaves half of them uncovered.

Count the doors before buying anything. In houses with porches and garages, the sensor count is set by the number of entrances, not by the square footage.

“Outdoor motion sensors give the earliest warning, so put them around the yard.”

Earlier warning is real: an outdoor detector can notice someone before they touch the house. Some national brands market outdoor detection heavily for that reason.

On a yard that backs onto parkland, a cemetery wall or overgrown ground, outdoor motion sensors also notice raccoons, cats, birds and moving branches, all night. Tied to a siren, they produce false alarms until the household stops arming the system.

Use outdoor devices to record and light up, not to sound the siren. Let doors, windows and indoor detectors decide when the siren goes off.

“Wireless alarms work anywhere in the house.”

Modern wireless sensors do reach a long way, and in a frame house they usually work everywhere. That is why many sellers present wireless as the universal answer.

Solid brick party walls, a panel in the cellar, a detached garage across the yard and an attic three floors up are the conditions that weaken a wireless signal, and a Cypress Hills row house can have all four. A sensor that reaches the panel on a dry day can drop out in heavy rain or when a neighbor’s router changes channel.

Measure signal strength from every sensor location before fixing anything, and plan repeaters or wired zones where the reading is marginal. We do this on every install.

“Every alarm should call the police directly.”

Many instant answers describe monitored alarms as if a trip automatically brings a patrol car. Monitoring is valuable, and it is what many insurers ask about.

In practice, a central station first calls the people on your list and verifies, then contacts the police. In a busy precinct such as the 75th, which covers East New York and Cypress Hills, an alarm confirmed by video or by a second zone tripping generally gives the operator more to report than a single unexplained trip.

Give the station a call list of people who actually answer, and add a camera or a second detector on the most important entrance so an alarm can be confirmed quickly.

“Professional installation costs $1,000 or more before equipment.”

National averages are pulled upward by long monitoring contracts, financed equipment and large suburban houses, and some cost sites mix all three into one headline figure.

Our basic residential system starts at $600 installed, on the same Brooklyn list that applies across the borough, with monitoring optional and without a long-term contract. A complete house with glass-break detectors, life-safety devices and sirens starts at $2,500.

Ask any company for the installed price, the monthly fee and the contract length as three separate numbers, in writing.

“Alarm stickers and a sign are enough of a deterrent.”

Signs do discourage some opportunistic burglars, and alarm companies supply them for that reason. A visible keypad or siren box adds to the effect.

On a raised porch, a sign at the top of the steps is not visible from the street, and a burglar who has watched a house long enough to know it is empty during the day is not discouraged by a sticker.

Put the sign where people approach the house, and make sure the system behind it is armed whenever the house is empty. Before anyone opens your walls, ask for a New York State alarm license number; ours is 12000287431.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk the Slope Before You Walk the Rooms

This describes how we survey a house on a hill, not a story about a particular address. The technician does not go straight inside. He stands on the sidewalk and counts the steps up to the porch, then climbs them and looks back down: from the front door, what can someone on the street actually see? He checks how high the porch roof sits under the upstairs windows and whether a railing or column would make a ladder unnecessary. Then he walks down the side of the house, following the grade, to find where the ground meets the basement and which door or window opens there.

Only then does he go in, and he goes in by the back if he can, because that is the route that matters. Inside, he marks every door and window the outside walk showed can be reached from the ground, a porch, a porch roof or a garage roof, and he finds that the front half of the house often needs little while the back and the basement need most of the sensors. The written price follows that order. On a flat lot the walk takes five minutes; on a slope it takes fifteen, and it is the most useful quarter-hour of the visit.

Do sensors work on aluminum storm windows fitted over old wood sash?
+

They can, but we normally put the contact on the inner wood sash, because that is the window that actually keeps someone out. The storm window can be left unmonitored, or given its own contact if you want to know when it is opened, using a surface contact rated for metal frames.

Can wireless contacts be painted to match our window trim?
+

We do not paint the devices themselves, because paint can seal the battery cover and interfere with the case. Most contacts come in white and brown, and recessed contacts disappear into the frame entirely. Where color matters, such as on dark original trim, we choose the recessed type or place a slim surface contact on the hinge side out of view.

Can our porch floodlight camera switch on automatically when the alarm trips?
+

Yes, if the camera and the panel work together. An Alarm.com floodlight camera on an Alarm.com-connected panel can be given rules that record a clip when the alarm goes off, and on models that allow it, switch the light on as well. With a different camera brand, it usually means using the camera’s own app alongside the alarm rather than one automatic rule.

Is there a way to test the alarm without the monitoring station sending police?
+

Yes. Call the station or use the app to put the account in test mode for a set time, then open doors and walk past detectors; the station logs the signals without dispatching. When the test ends, confirm with the station that it received them, which tells you the communicator is working as well as the sensors.

Does the alarm need its own outlet, or can it share a circuit with the porch light?
+

The panel runs from a plug-in transformer on an ordinary outlet, ideally one that is not controlled by a wall switch and is not on a circuit that trips often. It can share a circuit with lighting, but not a switched outlet, because someone will eventually turn it off. The panel’s battery carries it through an outage either way.
