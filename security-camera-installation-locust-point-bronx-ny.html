The Neighborhood

Why Locust Point Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Locust Point is a small peninsula, and almost everything about camera work here follows from that. Wikipedia bounds it with Harding Avenue on the north, the Throgs Neck Bridge on the east and Eastchester Bay, and places it in Bronx Community District 10; CityNeighborhoods carries the western edge to Hammond Creek, and some people still call it Pirate Cove. City-Data measures it at 0.416 square miles with 1,409 residents, the ZIP code is 10465, and the 45th Precinct patrols it. It was once an island. Wikimapia's history says it was first Wrights Island, after its owner, Captain J.T. Wright, then Locust Island, until the channel that separated it from the mainland was filled and built over, part of that fill carrying the bridge's northern ramp and toll plaza. CityNeighborhoods describes Locust Point Drive and Pennyfield Avenue winding through a compact grid whose streets end in cul-de-sacs at seawalls or small inlets, and the Locust Point Civic Association has said more than once that the point has only one access road. Put those together and you have a place where almost nobody drives down a given street unless they live there, are visiting someone, or are looking for something. That works in a camera's favor: a stranger on a dead-end block in the small hours stands out on a recording in a way he never would on a through street. It is also why each camera has to be pointed at exactly the right spot, because on a quiet block there may be no other witness at all.

The houses are the second thing that shapes the job. In the city's PLUTO tax-lot records, the blocks that hold Locust Point Drive, Tierney Place, Glennon Place, Hatting Place, Giegerich Place and the east end of Chaffee Avenue come to 265 lots and 319 homes: 167 one-family lots, 68 two-family lots holding 136 homes, five three-family lots holding 15 homes, and not one building with four or more homes. Nothing on those blocks rises above three floors, and recorded build years run from 1899 to 2013. City-Data's count by decade has 90 homes from 1939 or earlier, 41 from the 1940s, 100 from the 1950s and smaller numbers after that, and StreetEasy lists a one-story house on Tierney Place built in 1915 and a three-story house on the same street built in 1925. CityNeighborhoods says the land was subdivided in the 1920s and 1930s, when small bungalows replaced older farms, and other local histories describe the point as a summer getaway and a bungalow colony before it became a year-round neighborhood. The lots are narrow: a Compass listing on Locust Point Drive gives one as 25 feet by 100. So a camera job here is a house job, not a lobby job. It means a front door a few steps from the street, a side passage between close-set houses, a back door or deck, a yard that may run to the water, often a lower-level entrance, and, in the two- and three-family houses, a second household with its own door and its own idea of what should be on camera.

Water is the third. Of those 265 lots, 231 carry the flag for the 2015 preliminary flood maps, and StreetEasy lists Tierney Place houses in hurricane evacuation Zone 1. In Sandy, the Bronx Times reported, Eastchester Bay came over the seawall behind the houses on the south side of Tierney Place and flooded the streets, basements and ground floors there; one downstairs rental took three feet of water and had to be rebuilt from scratch. The National Weather Service's coastal flood records list inundation on Tierney Place and at the entrance to SUNY Maritime, and a foot of water on Chaffee Avenue along Hammond Creek on January 23, 2016. The civic association's president told the Bronx Times her mortgage company had moved her house from a Zone B to a Zone A flood zone, and in October 2017 City Planning brought its flood resilience zoning update to the association with the point's old bungalow homes in view. For a camera system that comes down to two plain rules. The recorder, the network switch, the battery backup and the internet equipment they depend on belong somewhere water has never been. And any camera that has to live low, at a lower-level door or under a deck, is treated as a part that may one day need replacing, so it is chosen and wired with that in mind.

The fourth is the local record, which is short and specific. The Bronx Times reported that grand larceny auto in the 45th Precinct rose 85.3 percent, from 197 incidents to 365 over the same period a year apart, in a precinct that takes in 10.6 miles of waterfront. In 2026 a 45th Precinct officer posted on Nextdoor about complaints of burglary, moped theft and basic package theft, and about a suspect caught in the act of taking more packages from a nearby building. ABC7 reported in August 2026 that shootings in the precinct were well down on the year before. On the point itself, the Bronx Times reported in 2015 that 19 boats laid up for the winter at the Locust Point Yacht Club on Longstreet Avenue were broken into over a single weekend, with police reviewing the club's surveillance cameras afterward, and in 2008 that teenagers kept breaking into an empty house at 3177 Glennon Place while a neighbor's calls to 311 produced a fine, a minor repair and then another break-in. On East Tremont Avenue, between Bruckner Boulevard and Harding Avenue, NYPD Argus cameras watch the shopping street and are monitored in real time by the precinct and police headquarters, but those cameras stop at the avenue. None of this says Locust Point is unsafe, and we are not ranking any block. What it shows is where a camera plan here earns its keep: cars at the curb, mopeds and boxes left in yards and on porches, empty or rarely used houses, boats out of season, and the dead end of the street.

Three sets of rules decide where the cameras go. The first is licensing. New York's Department of State licenses businesses that install and service security systems, and a course provider for that license says it covers cameras as well as alarms; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431. A homeowner putting cameras on his own house is not in the business of installing them and needs no license. The second is the neighbors. On lots this narrow, the next house's windows are close, and an analysis on recordinglaw.com warns that deliberately aiming a camera at a neighbor's windows or a privacy-fenced yard could support harassment, nuisance or privacy claims, while New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a, known as the backyard camera law, lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera. So every view is framed on your own door, walk, drive, yard and curb, and we check each one from the screen before we leave. The third is the shared and public ground: the marina and parts of the waterfront are city property, the civic clubhouse is a city-owned building, and nothing private goes on a lamppost, a utility pole or a seawall that belongs to someone else. No historic district turned up for Locust Point in our research, so Landmarks review is not normally part of a house job here, though an owner should still confirm the address on the city's map.

What Locust Point owners often add next. A house that starts with cameras at the curb, the front door and the yard often comes back asking for a door or window alarm that can be checked against the camera view before anyone calls the police, or for a smart lock on the lower-level door so a relative can be let in without a spare key under a mat. Camera Dark After a Blow Off the Bay, or a Car Hit Overnight? Fast Help on the Point

Two kinds of urgent call come up on a peninsula like this. In the first, a camera has gone dark, most often the one on the water side or at a lower-level door, after a storm, a power cut, a provider swapping the modem, or wind and spray knocking a loose mount off its aim, and nobody knows how long it has been out. In the second, something has happened at the curb or in the yard overnight, a car window smashed, a moped gone, a box taken from the stoop, and the recorder will start writing over that night as soon as its drive fills. For a dark camera, note which one it is and when it last showed a picture, check whether the recorder and the battery backup still show lights, and do not reset anything to factory settings, because that can wipe the passwords and the remote link together. If the recorder sits anywhere that took water, do not touch it or anything plugged in near it while the floor is wet; leave it off at the breaker and tell us. For an incident, leave the recorder running, write down the time you need, and if you know how, export that stretch to a USB stick or your phone before you do anything else. Then call (646) 490-0629. We book every request within 2 hours, walk you through what can be checked over the phone, and send a technician when the fix needs hands on the equipment.

From the Curb to the Seawall: Reading a Locust Point Lot Before Choosing a Camera

Most people calling about cameras for a house on the point start with the doorbell. The doorbell matters, but on a narrow lot with a dead-end street in front and water behind, it covers only one of the places where trouble actually happens. We break every lot into the same set of spaces, from the curb at the front to the seawall or the back fence at the rear, and ask the same two questions about each one: who should be there, and who would notice if the wrong person were. The answers change from block to block, a house facing the turnaround at the end of Tierney Place sees different traffic from one halfway along Glennon Place, but the list stays the same, and the table shows how it works.

The dead end is the most useful view on the block

On a through street, a car pulling up and sitting for twenty minutes means nothing. On a Locust Point street that ends at the seawall, it can mean a visitor watching the water, a couple of neighbors talking, or someone waiting for a house to go dark, and a camera that shows the turnaround lets you tell which. The view does not need to be wide. A varifocal camera, mounted high on the house that faces the end of the street, zoomed so the turnaround fills most of the picture, records when a car comes in, how long it stays, which way it faces and whether anyone gets out. On a recorder that can sort people from vehicles, the alert can be set to fire only when a vehicle stops in that zone after midnight, so you are not woken by every neighbor turning around. If your house is halfway along the block rather than at the end, the same idea applies to your own stretch of curb: the street is quiet enough that every arrival is worth having on the recording, so the camera's job is to show each arrival clearly, not to try to see the whole street at once.

Narrow lots: framing a side passage without the neighbor's kitchen window

On a 25-foot lot, the passage between your house and the next can be little more than a walkway, and the neighbor's windows face straight into it. That is exactly where a person goes when they want to reach a yard without being seen from the street, so it needs a camera, but it needs one aimed with care. We mount it on your own wall at the back end of the passage, looking toward the street along your side of the walkway, so whoever comes through is walking toward the lens and the neighbor's windows sit at the edge of the frame or outside it. Where a window still shows, the camera's privacy masking blacks that part of the picture out before anything is recorded. A camera aimed this way also tends to see a short slice of the sidewalk at the front, which tells you whether the person came from the street or from the yard. Night light is the other half: a passage between two houses is usually the darkest strip on the lot, and a small motion light on your own wall improves the picture far more than any camera setting.

The water side after dark

A camera's infrared night vision works by lighting up whatever is in front of it and recording the light that bounces back. Open water sends almost none back, so a camera aimed out over Eastchester Bay at night shows a black field, sometimes broken by reflections from boats, the bridge or houses across the water, and the movement of those reflections can trigger motion alerts all night. So on the water side we frame the land: the yard, the deck steps, the gate or stairs at the seawall, the bulkhead cap if it is yours, and anything stored outside. The water can sit at the top of the picture for context in daylight, but the alert zones are drawn only on the ground. Where the yard is dark, a low motion light aimed at the ground does more than any camera upgrade, and it gives a color picture instead of a grainy black-and-white one. In daylight, morning or late-afternoon sun off the water can wash a picture out, so cameras on that side are angled slightly down toward the yard and shaded by an eave where possible.

Where the equipment lives on a lot that floods

On a lot where water has come over the seawall, the camera system has a top half and a bottom half. The top half, the recorder, the network switch, the battery backup and, ideally, the internet modem, goes on the highest practical floor, in a closet or cabinet that locks, with enough air around it to stay cool. The bottom half, the cameras on a lower-level door or under a deck, is wired so that if one is ruined by water, it can be unplugged and replaced without touching anything else. Outdoor cable joins sit in sealed boxes above the ground, every cable entering the house dips down into a drip loop before it climbs in, and every hole is sealed afterward. We ask each owner how high water has reached on that lot, from the marks or from memory, and plan around that number rather than around a map. If the provider's modem lives in a flood-prone basement, we say so in the plan, because a recorder that keeps recording without internet is still useful, but alerts and remote viewing stop until the connection comes back.

Pre-1940 bungalows: low roofs, light frames and a lot of history

The oldest houses on the point were built for summers and later made into year-round homes, so what we find inside the walls varies from one to the next. Some have a usable attic and some have only a few inches between ceiling and roof. Many have been re-sided, raised, extended at the back or rebuilt on the lower floor after Sandy. Before planning a cable path, we look in the attic or crawl space, check the siding to see what sits under it, and trace any old wiring that looks like it once served a camera or an alarm. On a light frame, cameras go on solid framing rather than on thin trim, with mounting blocks where the surface is uneven, and every hole is sealed so wind-driven rain off the bay cannot follow the cable in. Where there is no attic route, a short run under the eave in a slim painted cover is often neater and kinder to the house than opening ceilings. The recorder usually goes in a main-floor closet, the highest dry spot a one-story house has, with the battery backup beside it.

The 1940s and 1950s houses: more room inside, the same narrow lot outside

The houses from the decades after the war tend to have a full attic and a more regular frame, which makes interior cable runs simpler: down from the attic inside a closet wall, out through the eave to each exterior camera, and to a recorder upstairs or on the main floor. Outside, the challenge is the same narrow lot as the older houses. The front camera has to cover the stoop and the curb without taking in the windows of the house across a narrow street, so it is angled down and to one side rather than straight out. Side cameras live under the eave looking along your own wall. If the house has a porch or a stoop roof, that roof is often the best spot for the door camera: sheltered, high enough to be out of reach, and close enough to show a face. Where a lower level has been finished, its door gets its own camera, wired as the replaceable bottom half of the system.

Two-family houses: one system, two households

PLUTO shows 68 two-family lots on the Locust Point blocks, and on many of them the second household lives on the lower level with a door of its own. That door needs a camera as much as the front does, but whose system it sits on and who can see it should be decided before anything goes up. Our usual arrangement is one recorder controlled by the owner, in a locked spot on the owner's floor, with the tenant given an account that shows their own door and nothing else, and the owner's views of the yard and drive kept separate. Cameras never look into either apartment, and the tenant is told in writing which outdoor spaces are recorded. If the tenant would rather have their own small indoor camera inside their apartment, that stays theirs and stays off the owner's system. Where the two households share the yard, the yard camera is framed on the ground and the gate, and both households know it is there.

Three-family houses: the shared stair and the shared door

The five three-family houses PLUTO shows on the point are the closest thing Locust Point has to an apartment building, and they bring the questions that come with one. A shared front door, a shared stair and three households mean the owner decides the plan, the tenants are told in writing what is recorded, and no camera ever looks into an apartment when its door opens. A camera inside the entrance door, aimed so people walk toward it, records who comes in; a dome at a stair landing, framed so it stops short of the apartment doors, records who goes up. The recorder goes in a locked cabinet in the owner's space or a common closet that only the owner opens. In a house this size the cable usually climbs the stair hall inside a closet or in a slim painted raceway, with every floor opening sealed.

Newer and rebuilt houses: check what the walls already hold

Houses built or rebuilt in later decades, including those rebuilt after Sandy, sometimes already have low-voltage cable or empty conduit pulled to the eaves or the deck, left by a builder who expected cameras or by a previous owner who never finished the job. Before quoting, we test whatever is there; a cable that tests clean from the eave to a closet can save a day of work. Field Notes: How We Walk a House on the Point, From the Turnaround to the Tide Line

This is how a Locust Point walk-through goes, written as our method, not as a story about any one job. We park where we can and start at the street, not at the door. From the curb we look both ways: how far the house is from the end of the block, whether it faces the turnaround, where cars park in front and how close the house across the street sits. Then we stand at the front steps and look back, because the doorbell's view is the view from there. Next is the side passage, walked from front to back, noting the neighbor's windows, the darkest stretch and where a light could go on our side. At the back we ask where water has come up to in past storms, look for the marks, and decide what has to live above that height. The yard is walked to the seawall or fence, with a note of anything stored outside, a kayak, a shed, a moped, a boat, and of which way the light falls in the evening. If there is a lower-level door, we stand at it and look up, because that is where a face will be. Inside, we open the attic or crawl space, find the modem, and pick a locked closet for the recorder as high in the house as practical. If the visit is late in the day, we look again once it is dark; if not, we ask for a phone photo of the yard after sunset. Only then do we count cameras, and the count comes out of the walk rather than from a package list.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Locust Point: Questions Answered

The street, the neighbors and the empty house

Cars sometimes drive down to where our street ends at the seawall and sit there with their lights off late at night. Can our camera tell us when that happens?

Yes, if the camera is aimed at the turnaround and the recorder is told what to look for. We use a varifocal camera mounted high on the side of your house that faces the street end, zoomed so the turnaround fills most of the picture rather than appearing as a speck at the edge of a wide shot. On the recorder we draw a zone over the turnaround and set an alert for a vehicle that stops inside it and stays longer than a few minutes, only between the hours you choose, such as midnight to 5 a.m. Many current recorders can tell a vehicle from a person or a passing gull, which keeps the alerts to the events you care about. A plate on a parked car may or may not be readable, depending on which way it faces and how much light falls on it, so we do not promise plates; what you will reliably get is the time it arrived, how long it stayed and whether anyone got out. If what you see worries you, call 911 rather than going out to the car.

The house next to ours sits empty, and kids keep getting into it the way they did at Glennon Place years ago. Can our camera record that without filming inside their house?

It can record the approach without looking into the building. The Bronx Times story from 2008 about 3177 Glennon Place described a cycle of break-ins, 311 calls, a fine, a minor repair and more break-ins, and footage is what breaks that cycle, because it shows when people go in and how. We aim your camera along your own side passage or yard toward the street or the back of the lots, so anyone heading for the empty house through the gap or across the back is on the recording, and we mask out the empty house's windows so nothing inside it is recorded. The front of a house is visible from the street anyway, so a curb camera that shows people walking up to the neighbor's front door from the sidewalk is fine; the line we keep is windows and private yards. Save clips with the time stamp intact and pass them to the 45th Precinct and, through 311, to the city when you report an open building. Tell the house's owner too, if you can reach them.

Our front door faces the neighbor's front door across a very narrow street. Is it a problem that our doorbell camera sees their door?

On its own, usually not. A front door seen from across a public street is something anyone walking by can see, and a doorbell that happens to show it is different from a camera deliberately aimed into someone's windows or a fenced yard, which is what the privacy warnings and New York's backyard camera law are about. Still, on Locust Point's narrow streets a wide-angle doorbell can take in a lot of the house opposite, and neighbors notice. We usually tilt the doorbell slightly down and toward your own steps, or fit a small angled wedge, so it shows your visitor's face and your stoop rather than the whole house across the street, and we use the doorbell's privacy zones to black out any window that remains in view. We set it to record video only. If a neighbor raises it, showing them the actual picture on your phone usually settles things. This is our practical view from installation, not legal advice.

We keep a moped chained up in the side yard, and the precinct has had complaints about moped thefts. Where does a camera have to be to catch someone taking it?

Where the thief has to walk toward it with the moped. A 45th Precinct officer's 2026 post on Nextdoor mentioned moped theft alongside burglary and package theft, and a moped in a side yard can only leave one way: out through the passage to the street. So the camera goes at the back end of the side passage, under the eave on your own wall, looking toward the street, so the person walks toward it on the way in and pushes the moped toward it on the way out. A second view from the front of the house, looking along the passage opening, catches the moment they reach the sidewalk and which way they go. Light matters more than resolution here: a motion light on your wall gives a color picture of clothing and face. An alert for motion in the passage after midnight sends a notice to your phone. A heavy chain to something fixed, out of view of the street, still does more to keep the moped than any camera does.

Houses in the floodplain

Our house is one story with no real attic, and the basement has taken water before. Where can the recorder go?

On the main floor, in a closet or cabinet that locks, as high as the space allows. In a one-story bungalow the main floor is the highest dry place in the house, so that is where the recorder, the network switch and the battery backup live together, on a shelf rather than the floor, with enough air around them that the recorder doesn't run hot. A bedroom or hall closet is common; a kitchen cabinet over the counter works in some houses. Cable from the outdoor cameras reaches it through the walls or in a short covered run under the eave, depending on what the house allows. The internet modem matters too: if the provider installed it in the basement, the cameras will keep recording during a flood but alerts and remote viewing stop when the modem goes under, so we suggest asking the provider to move it up when that is possible. Nothing about the system needs to be in the basement except, perhaps, a camera on a lower-level door, which is wired as a replaceable part.

Our rebuilt lower level has flood vents in the foundation wall. Can the camera cable just go out through one of those?

No, and we would not do it. Those openings are there so floodwater can pass in and out of the lower level instead of pushing against the walls, and anything threaded through a vent, fixed across it or sealed into it works against that and may not satisfy whoever inspected the work. Cable for yard or lower-door cameras gets its own small opening, drilled through the wall or the rim of the floor frame above the height water has reached on your lot, with a drip loop outside and a seal on both faces of the wall. If the only practical route is lower, we bring the cable down the outside of the house in a covered run from above and keep the indoor equipment high. It takes a few more minutes than borrowing a vent, but it keeps the vents doing their job and keeps the camera cable out of the water's path.

We put in a backup generator after the last big storm. Can the cameras run off it when the power goes out?

Yes, with a battery backup between them. Most standby generators take a little time to start and switch over, and a portable one waits until someone starts it, and in that gap a recorder without a battery shuts down, takes time to restart, and can lose a stretch of recording at the moment the power failed. So the recorder, the network switch that powers the cameras and the modem plug into a battery backup unit, and that unit is plugged into a circuit the generator feeds. The battery carries the system through the switchover, and the generator keeps it running after that. Cameras on wired runs draw their power from the switch, so they come along automatically. We do not wire generators or transfer switches; that work belongs to a licensed electrician. Tell us which circuits your generator feeds, and we will put the camera equipment on one of them.

The lower-level apartment in our two-family has its own electric meter. Whose power should the camera system use?

The owner's. The recorder, the switch and the battery backup should be plugged into a circuit on the owner's meter, in a space the owner controls, for two reasons: the tenant should not be paying to run the owner's security system, and nobody in the other apartment should be able to switch it off by flipping a breaker on their own panel. On a wired system the cameras, including the one at the tenant's own door, draw their power through the cable from the owner's switch, so no camera needs to plug into an outlet in the tenant's apartment. If an outlet is missing where the equipment has to go, a licensed electrician adds one on the owner's side before we install. If the tenant wants their own indoor camera inside the apartment, that one is theirs, on their power and their own account.

Clubs and docks
