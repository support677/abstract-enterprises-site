The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need Reolink Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley was settled as a scatter of small communities, and its properties are still laid out that way. The Historical Marker Database records the Dutch setting up a trading post near the future site of Albany in 1614, permanent settlement at Fort Orange in 1624 and Esopus, now Kingston, in 1653. Huge land grants followed: the Van Rensselaer patroonship and the Livingston, Philipse and Van Cortlandt manors. The Hudson River Valley Greenway notes that by 1750 most of the region had been settled as a network of small local communities that still survives. What that means for a security camera is distance. Instead of a lobby and a hallway, a typical valley job has a house, a detached garage, a barn or a cottage, a long driveway and sometimes a gate at the road, each with its own reason to be watched and its own problem with power and signal.

That is exactly the kind of property Reolink is built to cover, and also exactly where a box of Reolink cameras most often disappoints. Reolink sells three quite different families of camera: wired PoE models that take power and data down one network cable to a recorder, Wi-Fi and battery models that need no cable but depend on a good wireless signal and regular charging, and cellular models such as the Go PT Ultra, which, as one Slickdeals listing puts it, runs over 3G and 4G LTE for places without Wi-Fi or power. On a quarter-acre lot in Scarsdale any of the three can work. On a Dutchess farm, where the USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counts 620 farms averaging 161 acres, the wrong choice shows up as a camera that drops its connection every evening or a battery that is flat by Thanksgiving. One TractorByNet forum member summed up a common experience: their Reolink cameras work most of the time but do lose connection sometimes. Most of those dropouts come down to choosing Wi-Fi where a cable belonged.

Weather is the second reason. The valley loses power more, and for longer, than people moving up from the city expect. On July 4, 2026, storms left Central Hudson reporting 669 active outages affecting 41,323 customers as of noon the following Sunday, according to the Mid-Hudson Times, and Central Hudson later said about 17,200 customers were still out, most heavily in Dutchess and Ulster Counties. Less than three months later, Mid-Hudson News reported Central Hudson restoring power to more than 24,000 customers after a nor'easter brought strong winds and heavy rain. A Reolink recorder with no battery backup simply stops when the power does, and Wi-Fi cameras that lose their network can drain their own batteries searching for it, as one forum user described with another brand. A camera system for a valley home has to be planned around the outage, not just the burglar.

Lightning is the third. Earth Networks' 2020 lightning report for New York ranked Orange County first in the state for lightning pulses, with 275,059 that year over 41 thunder days, followed by Ulster with 182,760 and Westchester with 168,634. Dutchess logged 166,641, Putnam 75,093 and Rockland 64,575. Every camera cable that runs outside to a barn, a pole or a gatepost is a path for a surge back into the recorder and the PoE switch. That does not mean cable is a bad idea; it is still the most reliable way to power and connect a Reolink camera. It means the cable has to be grounded and protected properly where it enters the building, and the recorder should sit behind a battery backup that also smooths out the spikes.

Then there is crime, which in the valley looks different from the city. In January 2025, Hudson Valley Post reported New York State Police investigating residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam Counties, with troopers advising residents to install or activate security systems and cameras. WRRV reported police in the Lower Hudson Valley confirming that burglars had placed camouflaged cameras in bushes outside homes in Scarsdale to watch them. News 12 has covered car break-ins around the region, including Port Chester neighbors sharing camera footage of incidents around 4 a.m. near Tower Hill Drive, Rye police reporting 99 vehicle thefts since 2020 that all involved unlocked cars with the key fob inside, and the Ulster County Sheriff's Office warning of burglaries and car break-ins on and around Union Center Road in Esopus. The Times Union has reported Dutchess County agencies linking vehicle break-ins to the county's closeness to Connecticut. A Reolink system that covers the driveway, the cars and the doors, and records locally so the footage is there the next morning, answers most of what those reports describe.

Privacy is the fourth, and it matters more in small valley towns than many installers admit. Hudson Valley One reported in May 2023 that the Town of Ulster dropped a proposed outdoor lighting law that had grown out of a neighbor dispute, including a motion-activated camera fixed on a backyard; residents cited state Civil Rights Law section 52-a, and the town attorney was unsure cameras could be regulated at all. Posts on r/hudsonvalley have shown residents uneasy about licence-plate cameras in Pine Plains and Wappingers. We take that seriously: we aim cameras at your own ground, use the privacy masks Reolink's software offers where a view crosses a neighbor's yard, and switch off audio unless the law allows it to stay on.

We also want to be honest about what our research could not find. We searched for Reddit threads, local news and community posts about Reolink installations specifically in the six counties and found none. We could not view Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches, AnswerThePublic or Bing and DuckDuckGo results from our build environment, so the questions on this page come from real questions published on Justia, Yelp, manufacturer and installer FAQ pages, and forums. Where this page talks about typical building types, it describes the region as county records, historic-district listings and agricultural census figures show it, not a survey of any particular home.

  • Long outages are normal: 41,323 Central Hudson customers affected after the July 4, 2026 storms, and more than 24,000 restored after the September 2026 nor'easter.
  • Lightning ranks high: Orange County led New York in lightning pulses in 2020, and Ulster had the most thunder days of the six counties, at 49.
  • Farms and acreage: 620 farms in Dutchess and 486 in Ulster in the 2022 agricultural census, averaging 161 and 150 acres.
  • Historic review is common: the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, designated in 1990, is the largest on the contiguous mainland, and Kingston has five historic districts whose visible exterior work goes to its Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Bears are expanding: the DEC's black bear report says populations are increasing, especially in southern New York, and Teatown counted Westchester sightings rising from 2 in 2019 to 16 in 2021.

What owners often add once the cameras are up. A Hudson Valley property that has cameras covering the house and the barn usually asks next about the network that carries them: a better Wi-Fi signal in the outbuilding, or a dedicated cable so the barn camera stops leaning on the house Wi-Fi. If you already know you want that, say so when booking, so the recorder, the switch and the cable are planned together rather than added on a second trip.

Drive north from the Westchester shore to the Ulster hills and the Reolink job changes on the way. Lots grow, cable runs lengthen, the share of buildings without good Wi-Fi rises, and the questions about power and licensing shift from one county to the next. Westchester: tight lots, neighbors close, cable usually wins

Most of southern and central Westchester is the easiest Reolink territory in the valley, and that is why we steer it toward cable. A house in White Plains, Yonkers or Port Chester typically has the router somewhere central, a driveway along one side and neighbors within a few feet. Wired PoE cameras record continuously, never need charging and do not compete with the household Wi-Fi for bandwidth. The challenge here is aim. On a narrow lot, a camera on the driveway side can look straight into the neighbor's kitchen window. We set the view, then draw privacy masks over anything that is not yours, and walk the result with you in the app before we leave. Northern Westchester is different: the NYSEG towns of Bedford, Lewisboro, North Salem, Pound Ridge and Somers have larger, wooded lots where a garage or a gate can sit beyond cable reach, which brings in the same battery-and-solar decisions as the counties further north.

Rockland: suburban houses and busy commercial strips

Rockland splits into suburban neighborhoods, village downtowns and a few heavy commercial corridors, Route 59, Route 304 and Route 202 among them, with the Palisades Center at West Nyack. For houses, the plan looks like Westchester: PoE cameras to a recorder in a dry closet or basement, a Reolink doorbell at the front door, and a camera on the driveway. Rockland is also bear country on its southern edge; the DEC's report says the southern black bear range extends to southern Orange and Rockland Counties at the New Jersey border, and that bird feeders are the most common attractant. A camera covering the trash and the back deck will record bears long before it records a burglar, so we set person detection for alerts and leave animal clips to be found on playback rather than waking you at 3 a.m.

Orange: lightning, distance and big buildings

Orange County combines the longest cable runs with the most lightning. A horse farm in Warwick or a property in Montgomery may have a barn several hundred feet from the house, and Earth Networks counted Orange first in the state for lightning pulses in 2020. Runs that leave the house get surge protection where they enter, and if the barn is beyond the 100-metre limit we plan a separate PoE switch in the barn fed by a protected link rather than stretching a cable past what it can carry. Orange also has the region's largest new commercial buildings, including a 1,015,740-square-foot Amazon distribution centre in the Town of Montgomery reported by the Hudson Gateway Association of Realtors, where camera work is planned with the building's own security team.

Putnam: lake houses and owners who are not there

Many Putnam calls come from people who own a second home or a lake house and want to see it from the city. That changes the priorities. The system has to come back by itself after an outage, because nobody is there to reset it; in August 2020, during Tropical Storm Isaias, the Daily Voice reported 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers out at one point. So we put the recorder, PoE switch and router on battery backup, set the cameras to reconnect automatically, and test recovery by switching the house off at the main breaker. Putnam's own code also matters: Chapter 145 licenses low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians, including video surveillance cabling, which we cover when we quote.

Dutchess: estates, farms and historic landscapes

Dutchess holds much of the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, the Rhinebeck Village Historic District and estates such as Wilderstein, Montgomery Place and the Mills Mansion at Staatsburgh, alongside 620 working farms. A Reolink job on a historic property is mostly about visual impact: small, dark or painted cameras placed under eaves, conduit kept out of sight, and no new poles where an existing building can carry the camera. On farms, the main house gets PoE, and the far fields and outbuildings get LTE or solar models chosen after we check the sun in winter, not in September.

Ulster: stone walls, weak internet and seasonal cabins

Ulster brings the thickest walls and the weakest internet. Kingston's Stockade District, listed on the National Register in 1975, keeps Colonial Dutch stone houses on all four corners of Crown and John Streets, and getting a cable through a stone wall is a slow drill job. In the mountain towns, the internet itself can be the weak link; Ulster County announced a ConnectALL broadband award in September 2025 aimed at nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities. Where the internet is poor, a recorder on site still records everything, and an LTE camera or cellular backup can make sure at least the alerts get out.

Driveway, Barn and Router: What to Check on a Hudson Valley Property Before We Arrive

The time lost on a valley camera job is usually lost to distance: a barn that turns out to be further from the house than anyone thought, a router hidden in a corner of the basement, or a gate with no power that nobody mentioned until the van was unloaded. Ten minutes with a phone camera and a tape measure, or just pacing the distance, saves most of it. Please do not drill holes or open electrical panels; just look, measure and send us what you find.

Walk the route a visitor would take

The most useful thing you can do before we arrive is walk your property as a stranger would. Start at the road, come up the driveway, go to the front door, then around to the back door, the garage, the barn and anywhere a delivery is left. Note where the path narrows, where a car would stop, and where it is darkest at night. Those spots are where the cameras earn their keep. On a large lot, two well-placed cameras covering the driveway entrance and the parking area often tell you more than six cameras spread evenly around the house, because everyone who arrives by car passes through the same few metres of gravel.

Think about where the recorder will live

A Reolink recorder is a small box with a hard drive inside, and it wants a dry, ventilated, indoor spot near the router, ideally where it is not the first thing a burglar sees. In valley houses that is often a basement utility area, a hall closet or a home office. It should not sit in an unheated barn or a damp garage, and it should not be the only copy of anything important if the house is empty for weeks. Tell us where you would like it, and whether there is an outlet there; if a new outlet is needed, it is electrical work for a licensed electrician, and our quote will say so.

Reolink Camera Installation for the Hudson Valley's Architecture

The six counties hold almost every kind of building the state has: Colonial stone houses, river-city row houses, Victorian village homes, post-war suburbs, farms and barns, lake cottages and modern warehouses. Each one changes three things about a Reolink job: what the camera mounts on, how the cable gets inside, and who has to agree before we drill. These are the types the historic records, agricultural census and county sources describe, and how we handle each.

Colonial Stone Houses

Kingston's Stockade and older houses across Ulster and Dutchess. Walls of fieldstone or cut stone, often well over a foot thick. Cameras go under eaves or on wood trim, and cable enters through one carefully drilled, sealed hole.

River-City Row Houses

Newburgh's East End, which the city calls the second-largest designated historic site in the state, with Italianate row houses. Shared walls, narrow rear yards and alleys, and fronts that face historic review.

Estates in the Landmark District

Riverfront estates such as Wilderstein, Teviot and Rokeby along the Sixteen Mile District, with carriage houses and long drives. Visual impact matters as much as coverage.

Suburban Houses

Colonials, ranches, split-levels and capes in Westchester and Rockland. Wood frame, siding, attics and basements that make cable runs straightforward.

Farms and Barns

Dutchess, Ulster and Orange farmhouses with barns, sheds and paddocks. Long distances, dust, animals and lightning; the job is planned building by building.

Lake Houses and Cabins

Putnam and Ulster second homes, often empty for weeks. Remote viewing, outage recovery and battery models for buildings without year-round power.

On stone and block walls. The first question in an old stone house is not where the camera goes but where the cable comes in. A thick fieldstone wall can hide voids, rubble fill and old mortar that crumbles under a drill. We prefer to bring cable in at a wood sill, a window frame or a basement wall of newer block where one exists, and we drill stone only with a masonry bit at slow speed, testing as we go. Every penetration is sealed against water and insects, because a hole in a stone wall that lets in rain will do more damage than any burglar. The camera itself goes on wood wherever we can, which is easier to patch later and keeps anchors out of the original stone.

On barns and outbuildings. Barns are hard on electronics. Dust gets into everything, animals chew, and a metal roof can block Wi-Fi almost completely. We mount barn cameras under eaves or inside the gable where they stay dry, choose models rated for outdoor use, and run cable in conduit wherever animals or equipment could reach it. If the barn has its own electric service, we pay attention to how the camera cable connects the two buildings, because separately powered buildings can sit at different ground potentials, and a cable between them carries that difference along with the video. Where the distance or the risk is too high, the barn gets its own small switch and a protected link, or an LTE camera that needs no link at all.

What a business camera has to do differently

A home camera mostly watches for something unusual. A business camera has to show ordinary things clearly: the face at the register, the hand on the cash drawer, the van at the loading door, the car that clipped another in the lot. That means cameras placed for identification rather than just coverage, set at the right height and angle for faces, with lighting checked at closing time, when most incidents happen. It also means a recorder sized to keep enough days of footage for the owner to find something after a weekend, and an export process that the manager can follow without calling us. For a Poughkeepsie shop or a Route 59 store, we start with a walk-through of how customers and staff move, mark the places where a clear face matters, and plan the system from there.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Licensed installer. The MBFAA licensing course page notes that installing security systems, which include cameras and CCTV, in New York without a state licence can bring heavy fines. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Private spaces stay private. New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes it illegal to record people in a bedroom, bathroom, changing room or fitting room without consent. Staff restrooms and changing areas never get cameras.
  • Audio usually stays off. As a Justia answer put it, outdoor and garage cameras should have audio disabled unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation. We set business Reolink cameras to video only unless you have confirmed audio is lawful for that space.
  • County electrical rules. Westchester requires a county trade licence for electricians, Rockland's Chapter 250 covers electrical contractors working on circuits of 50 volts or more, Orange licenses electricians under its own local laws, and Ulster's Chapter 185 includes signalling and data transmission. A new outlet for a recorder or camera is a licensed electrician's job; the quote says who does it.
  • Putnam low-voltage licence. Putnam's Chapter 145 licenses low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians, including video surveillance cabling, working from an outlet installed by a licensed electrician.
  • Historic fronts. In districts such as Kingston's, visible exterior work goes to the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission before a building permit; we plan cameras on the less visible sides first.

Your Questions Answered

Reolink Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Choosing the right Reolink for each building

Our barn is a long walk from the house. Will a Reolink Wi-Fi camera reach it, or does it need a cable?

It depends on the distance, what stands between the two buildings and what the barn is made of. A metal roof or siding can block Wi-Fi almost completely, trees in full leaf weaken it further, and a Wi-Fi camera on a weak signal records but drops its connection when you most want to watch. A cable is more dependable as long as the run stays under 100 metres, about 328 feet, from the switch. Beyond that, we put a small PoE switch in the barn and feed it with a protected link, or use a cellular camera if the barn has no power. Pace the distance and send us a photo of the barn wall facing the house, and we can usually tell you which way it will go.

Will a solar-charged Reolink keep running on a shaded, north-facing lot through a valley winter?

Sometimes, but not on promise alone. A solar panel that looks fine in September can sit in shadow all December, when the sun is low and a ridge, a pine stand or the house itself blocks it for most of the day. We check each spot for winter sun before we suggest solar, looking south at the horizon and at evergreens rather than at bare branches. If the panel can see open sky for a good part of a winter day, a solar Reolink can work well on a shed or a gate. If it cannot, we would rather put the panel on a nearby pole or roof edge that does get sun, run a cable, or choose a different camera, than install something that dies every January.

The gate at the bottom of our road has no power and no Wi-Fi. What Reolink can watch it?

A cellular model. Reolink's Go range, including the Go PT Ultra described in a Slickdeals listing as operating over 3G and 4G LTE, is built for exactly this: a battery camera with a solar panel that sends clips over a mobile data plan instead of your home network. It needs decent mobile coverage at the gate, which we check with a phone before ordering, and a data plan from a carrier. It records on motion rather than continuously, so we aim it at the approach to the gate and set the motion zone tight around the road, which keeps the data use and the battery drain down.

A battery Reolink only records when something moves. When does that become a real problem?

When the thing you need to see happens slowly, at a distance or just outside the motion zone. A Slickdeals commenter put the trade-off plainly: battery cameras record motion events, while PoE cameras can record around the clock. On a driveway, a motion clip usually catches the car. In a barn, where you might want to check later whether a door was left open at some point overnight, continuous recording is far more useful. Our rule of thumb is simple: where the cable can reach, we wire it; battery and solar models go where cable is unreasonable, and we tell you what they will and will not record.

Storms, outages and lightning

After the July storms our cameras came back on, but there were hours missing from the recording. What should have been set up differently?

Two things: battery backup and recovery settings. When the power goes, a Reolink recorder and its PoE switch stop instantly, along with every wired camera, and the router goes with them. A battery backup on the recorder, switch and router keeps the system recording through shorter outages and lets it shut down cleanly in longer ones. Central Hudson's figures for July 2026, with tens of thousands of customers out, show why that matters here. We also check that the recorder restarts and resumes recording on its own when power returns, and we test it by switching the house off at the main breaker before we leave. If you have a generator, we confirm the network equipment is on a circuit it actually covers.

Can a summer thunderstorm reach the Reolink recorder through the camera cables, and how is that guarded against?

It can. Any cable that runs outside, along a fence, up a pole or across to a barn, can carry a surge from a nearby strike back to whatever it is plugged into, and in a PoE system that is the switch and the recorder. Orange, Ulster, Westchester and Dutchess all logged well over 160,000 lightning pulses in Earth Networks' 2020 report. We protect outside runs where they enter the building with surge protection grounded properly, keep cables off fences and metal where we can, and put the recorder behind a battery backup that also filters the supply. None of that makes a system lightning-proof, but it greatly improves the odds that one camera fails rather than the whole system.

Rules, licences and neighbors

Our property is in Putnam. Does the county's licensing law cover the person who runs our camera cable?

Yes, as Putnam's code is written. Chapter 145 sets up licensing for low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians, and the work it describes includes cabling for video surveillance, running at up to 90 volts from an outlet installed by a licensed electrician. Our camera work is done under NYS license #12000287431, and on a Putnam job we confirm the county's current requirements when we quote. If the job also needs a new outlet, that part is for a licensed electrician, and the written quote names who does what so there is no question afterward.

Our house is in one of Kingston's historic districts. Does a camera on the front need the commission's approval?

It might, and it is better to ask first. The City of Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work visible from the public right of way in its historic districts before a building permit is issued. Whether a small camera triggers that review is a question for the commission, not for us to guess. What we can do is plan the system so the visible part is as small as possible: cameras on the rear and sides, dark or painted housings under eaves, cable inside the walls, and nothing on the front unless there is no other way to see the door. We will give you a sketch and product photos to take to the commission if you need them.

Our driveway camera also takes in part of the neighbor's yard. How do you set it so it does not start a dispute?

We aim first and mask second. The camera goes where it sees your driveway, door or yard with as little of the neighbor's property in frame as the angle allows. Then we use the privacy mask feature in Reolink's software to black out anything that is not yours, so it is never recorded. Hudson Valley One's 2023 report on the Town of Ulster's lighting proposal, which grew partly out of a dispute over a motion-activated camera fixed on a backyard, shows how quickly these things escalate in small towns. We switch audio off unless it is lawful to keep, and we show you the masked view in the app before we leave, so you can show it to the neighbor if they ask.

Existing systems and businesses

We put in our own Reolink kit a few years ago and the cables are a mess. Can you take it over rather than start again?

Usually, yes. Most self-installed Reolink systems have sound cameras and a sound recorder; the problems are in the cabling and the settings. We trace every cable with a tone generator, label each run at both ends to match its channel on the recorder, test the runs for faults, replace connectors that were crimped poorly, and move any camera whose view has drifted or whose mount has loosened. We update the recorder and cameras, set up motion zones and person detection properly, check the drive is recording, and give you a written map of the system. If a camera or the recorder has actually failed, we tell you before replacing anything.

Cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers with national figures; they are useful for a rough idea, but they are not a quote for a farm in Dutchess.

"Reolink cameras are DIY, so you never need an installer."

It is true that a homeowner can put up their own Reolink cameras without a licence; Low Voltage Nation notes that New York requires no state licence for homeowner self-installation, and one review describes Reolink's battery and Wi-Fi cameras as DIY-friendly.

The gap is between installing a camera and installing a system that still works after a winter. On a valley property, the hard parts are the distances, the cable entries through stone and block, surge protection on outside runs and battery backup for outages.

Plenty of owners do the first half themselves and call us for the rest, or for a take-over. Anyone paid to do the work, though, needs a state licence, which is why ours, NYS license #12000287431, is on this page.

"Add a solar panel and the camera charges itself forever."

Solar panels charge batteries when they see the sun. In a valley December, a panel facing a ridge, a pine stand or the north side of a house may see very little of it for weeks at a time.

Self-published figures from Reolink and Eufy users put battery cameras at three to five months per charge without help, and cold weather generally shortens battery life. A small panel in shade can fall behind the camera's use, especially on a busy driveway that triggers it often.

We check winter sun at the spot before suggesting solar, and where it is poor we move the panel, run a cable or pick a different camera rather than promise something the hills will not deliver.

"Wi-Fi cameras work anywhere on the property."

Wi-Fi weakens with distance, walls, metal and leaves. A Reolink camera on a barn a few hundred feet away, behind trees in full leaf, will often record well but drop its connection in the evening, which is the experience the TractorByNet user described.

Reolink's own installation tips add a less obvious rule: keep cameras at least a metre from Wi-Fi routers and other wireless devices. Too close is as bad as too far.

For anything beyond easy Wi-Fi range, cable is more dependable as long as each run stays under the 100-metre Ethernet limit. Beyond that we add a switch or choose a cellular camera.

"A battery camera records everything that happens."

Most battery and solar cameras record motion events, not continuous video, to save battery. A Slickdeals commenter made the comparison directly: battery cameras record motion clips, while PoE cameras can record without stopping.

That is fine for catching a car on a driveway, but it misses slow events, things outside the motion zone and moments before the trigger. For a barn door or an equipment shed, continuous recording is often what you actually want.

We set battery cameras with tight motion zones and person detection, and we tell you plainly which cameras will record continuously and which will only record when something moves.

"There are no licensing rules for camera installers in New York outside the city."

State licensing applies across New York, not just in the five boroughs. The MBFAA course page says security systems, including cameras and CCTV, fall under it, with heavy fines for unlicensed installers.

The counties add their own layer. Westchester requires a trade licence for electricians, Rockland's Chapter 250 covers electrical contractors on circuits of 50 volts or more, Orange licenses electricians under its own local laws, Putnam's Chapter 145 licenses low-voltage technicians including video surveillance cabling, and Ulster's Chapter 185 counts data transmission as electrical work.

Dutchess is the one county where we found no county contractor licence, though towns such as Fishkill require third-party electrical inspection. We check the rules for your county and town at the quote stage.

"Professional installation costs the same everywhere."

"Once it is installed, a camera system just keeps working through storms."

A Reolink recorder and its PoE cameras stop the moment the power does. Wi-Fi cameras keep their batteries but can lose the network when the router goes down, and a forum user describing another brand noted that cameras searching for lost Wi-Fi drain their batteries fast.

Hudson Valley Reolink Camera Installation FAQ

Is it legal to have audio recording switched on with my Reolink cameras in New York?
+

Usually it is safer to switch it off. New York's eavesdropping rules allow recording a conversation only with the consent of at least one participant, so a camera that picks up strangers talking in your driveway or garage when you are not there can create a problem. A Justia answer to a homeowner with outdoor and garage cameras advised disabling audio unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation. We set cameras to video only unless you have confirmed audio is lawful for that spot.

Do I need a licensed company, or can I put my own Reolink cameras up?
+

You can install cameras on your own home yourself; New York does not require a state licence for homeowner self-installation. Anyone you pay to install them does need a state licence, and some counties, Putnam among them, add their own low-voltage licensing. Many owners mount a battery camera themselves and call us for the wired cameras, the outbuildings, the recorder and the backup power. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.

Will a Reolink system leave me paying a monthly fee?
+

Not for recording. A Reolink recorder stores every camera's footage on its own hard drive in your house, so you do not need a subscription to keep or play back video. Reolink offers optional cloud services, and LTE cameras need a mobile data plan from a carrier, but a wired system with a recorder runs without any monthly charge from us or from Reolink.

How long does a Reolink battery camera last between charges here?
+

Owners commonly report a few months per charge on a quiet spot, and less on a busy driveway that triggers the camera often. Cold weather also shortens battery life, so a camera that lasts well in summer may need charging more often in a valley January. A solar panel with real winter sun can keep it topped up; we check the sun at the spot before recommending one.

How close to the Wi-Fi router should a Reolink camera or recorder sit?
+

Reolink's installation tips say to keep cameras at least a metre away from any wireless device, including Wi-Fi routers, because interference can disrupt the signal. The recorder, by contrast, should be connected to the router by a short cable, so they usually sit in the same closet or room with a little space between the recorder and any wireless gear.

Are there rooms where a Reolink camera may not be placed under New York law?
+

Yes. New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes it illegal to record people in a bedroom, bathroom, changing room or fitting room without consent, where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. That applies to homes, rentals, guest cottages and businesses. We do not install cameras in those spaces, and we aim indoor cameras so they do not see into them through an open door.

If the internet at the house goes down, does the Reolink system stop recording?
+

A wired system with a recorder keeps recording, because the cameras and the recorder talk over your own cables, not the internet. What you lose is remote viewing and phone alerts until the internet comes back. That is one of the main reasons we recommend a recorder rather than cloud-only cameras on rural properties where the internet is slow or unreliable.
