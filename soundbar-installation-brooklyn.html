The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Homes Need Soundbar Installation

In Brooklyn the hard part of a soundbar job is almost never the soundbar. It is the wall the bar has to hang on and the floor the subwoofer has to sit on. A borough of roughly 2.65 million people and more than 1.14 million housing units, according to the Census Bureau's 2025 estimates, has built its homes in waves: brick and brownstone rowhouses through the late nineteenth century, wood-frame houses in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Bushwick and Flatbush, pre-war elevator buildings along the parkways, post-war towers on the edges of the borough, and, since the rezonings of the 2000s, a new skyline of concrete residential towers. Each wave left a different wall behind the TV.

Walk into a parlor floor in Fort Greene or Park Slope and the obvious TV wall is often the party wall, a masonry wall shared with the house next door, or the chimney breast that juts out between the front and back parlors. Neither has studs. Both take masonry anchors well, but both have a catch: the party wall is thinner than most people assume, and a chimney breast hides a flue. On Brownstoner's forum, one owner described the belief that rowhouse party walls are very thick as a myth after a neighbour's plumber broke straight through theirs; even an eight-inch wall, they wrote, is "frighteningly easy to break through." That is the reason we treat drill depth on a Brooklyn party wall as a measurement, not a guess.

Head a few blocks east into a Bed-Stuy or Bushwick frame house and the wall can be stranger still. A Brownstoner renovation diary from Bed-Stuy describes opening the walls of a wood-frame house and finding the party and exterior walls built with brick and mortar packed between wood timber posts, a method called brick nogging that the writer notes had fallen out of common use by 1882. A stud finder goes quiet over that kind of wall; a drill alternates between wood, soft old brick and voids. Mounting there is about reading the wall as you go, not trusting a single reading at the start.

Then there is the Brooklyn that did not exist twenty years ago. Since Downtown Brooklyn was rezoned in 2004, the neighbourhood has added close to 27,000 new housing units, Planetizen reported in August 2025, citing the New York Post. Across the borough on the East River, the 2005 Greenpoint–Williamsburg rezoning opened the waterfront to high-rise housing; Greenpointers reported in 2025 that 2,306 units were added in that community board in 2023 alone, the most of any Brooklyn board that year. In those buildings the TV wall is a steel-stud partition, a poured concrete column, or a floor-to-ceiling window, and the management office has an insurance and scheduling procedure of its own.

What ties all of this together is that almost nobody in Brooklyn lives alone in a building. The Census Bureau puts the borough's owner-occupied rate at 29.5 percent, so most households rent, and even the owners of a two- or three-family brownstone usually have a tenant on another floor. Noise is personal here. On Reddit's r/Brooklyn, a resident asking for help with a neighbour's loud music was told to submit 311 complaints after 10 PM and to consider a noise level meter, which is exactly the hour the city's Noise Code starts measuring sound inside a neighbour's home. A soundbar install that ignores where the bass goes is only half finished.

A Brooklyn soundbar usually goes into a multi-family building, onto masonry or a tower partition, for a renter or an owner-landlord who will hear about it if the bass carries. Getting it right means choosing the fixing for the wall, keeping drill depth under control on shared masonry, routing cable in a way the lease or the house permits, and tuning the low end for the people on the other side.

A soundbar booking in Brooklyn very often comes with a screen to hang above it, and doing both together means one set of holes planned as a pair. Masonry Mount on a Party Wall

The classic brownstone parlor job. Sleeve or screw anchors set in solid brick behind the plaster, holes kept to a measured depth so nothing reaches the house next door, and the bar levelled to the TV rather than to a ceiling that may not be level itself.

Chimney-Breast Installs

When the TV sits on the projecting breast between the parlors, we fix into the brick faces either side of the flue line and stay out of the flue itself. Cables usually come down a painted cord cover, because a working or capped chimney is no place for in-wall wiring.

Frame-House and Nogging Walls

Wood-frame rowhouses in Bed-Stuy, Bushwick and Flatbush can hide brick between the studs. We probe before committing, fix into timber where it lines up and into sound brick where it doesn't, and bridge irregular framing with a plywood backer when the wall calls for one.

Tower Partitions and Concrete Columns

Steel-stud and gypsum walls in Downtown Brooklyn, DUMBO and the Williamsburg waterfront take fasteners made for light-gauge steel; concrete columns and shear walls take a hammer drill and concrete anchors, with dust captured at the bit.

Renter-Friendly Setups

For leases that limit holes: console placement, a bracket that clips the bar to the existing TV mount, and cable dressed along the furniture. We tell you exactly what, if anything, will need filling when you move out.

Hidden and Dressed Wiring

In-wall concealment with a code-compliant power and cable kit where there is a cavity and the building allows it; paintable surface raceway on brick, block and concrete where there is no cavity to use.

eARC, CEC and Remote Setup

The bar on the TV's eARC or ARC socket, HDMI-CEC switched on so one remote handles power and volume, lip-sync checked, and firmware and the maker's app brought up to date on your Wi-Fi.

Subwoofer and Surround Pairing

Wireless subs and rear speakers paired, placed and levelled for a home with neighbours: off the bare boards, away from the party wall where the room allows, and set lower than the showroom default.

Bar Moves Between Brooklyn Apartments

Bracket and bar taken down at the old apartment and re-hung at the new one on anchors for whatever wall is waiting there — often brick on one end of the move and steel stud on the other.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Brooklyn's Architecture

Brooklyn's building types differ more from one another than the soundbars people buy. These are the homes we work in most across the borough, and what each one means for where the bar goes, what it hangs on and how its cables get from the TV to the outlet.

Brownstone and Brick Rowhouses

Brooklyn Heights, Park Slope, Fort Greene, Clinton Hill, Cobble Hill, Carroll Gardens, Prospect Heights and most of Bed-Stuy. Masonry party walls, plaster on the inside face, a chimney breast in each main room, timber floors and often a tenant on the garden or top floor. The TV wall is usually masonry, and the bass question is usually about the house next door as much as the floor below.

Wood-Frame Rowhouses

Older frame houses in Bed-Stuy, Bushwick and other nineteenth-century neighbourhoods, many re-clad over the years. Walls may be lath and plaster over timber studs, sometimes with brick nogging between them. Framing is irregular, plaster may be loose, and a plywood backer is sometimes the cleanest way to give a bracket something consistent to bite.

Victorian Detached Houses

Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park and the other Victorian Flatbush blocks: freestanding houses with lath-and-plaster walls, large rooms, bay windows and wide openings between rooms. No party wall to worry about, but long cable runs and plaster that needs gentle drilling.

Pre-War Elevator Apartments

The pre-war apartment buildings along Ocean Parkway, Eastern Parkway and through Flatbush, Kensington and Midwood. Plaster over block or brick, steam radiators on outside walls, a super and a managing agent, and neighbours on four sides.

Post-War Towers and Large Complexes

From Spring Creek Towers by Jamaica Bay to the post-war apartment towers of southern Brooklyn. Concrete floors, block or concrete walls, a management office with contractor rules, and lower ceilings. Very strong fixings once drilled, and no cavity for in-wall cable.

New Residential Towers

Downtown Brooklyn, DUMBO, Greenpoint and the Williamsburg waterfront. Steel-stud partitions with gypsum board, concrete columns and shear walls, floor-to-ceiling glass and building Wi-Fi in every unit. The building usually wants a certificate of insurance and a booked service-elevator slot before anyone drills.

How each Brooklyn wall is drilled, fixed and wired

Party walls behind plaster. We drill through the plaster with a sharp masonry bit and no hammer action until we reach brick, then switch to the method the brick needs. Depth stops are set on every bit so a hole for a sleeve anchor goes as deep as the anchor and no deeper. Anchors go into the body of a brick rather than the mortar joint, because lime mortar in an old Brooklyn wall can be soft enough to crumble under a fingernail. Soundbars are light, so two good fixings are usually plenty; what matters is that they are in sound masonry and not in a patched or hollow area.

Chimney breasts. The projecting breast in a brownstone parlor holds a flue, live or capped, and the brick around a flue can be a single course. We find the flue line first — it usually runs up the middle of the breast — and keep fixings to the solid brick either side. A Brownstoner building-code thread points out that it is illegal to pocket a floor joist into a masonry chimney breast partly because of that one-brick thickness, which is a good reminder of how little material can sit between a drill and a flue. Cable comes down the face of the breast in a raceway painted to match.

Nogging and irregular frame walls. A stud finder confused by brick and timber gives readings that mean nothing, so we drill a small pilot where the bracket will sit and read what comes out on the bit: wood shavings, red brick dust or nothing at all. Where the bracket can catch two timbers, it goes into timber. Where it lands on sound brick, masonry anchors. Where it lands on a void or loose plaster, we either move the bar a little along the wall or fit a plywood backer across the framing and mount to that.

Lath and plaster in Victorian houses and pre-war flats. Gentle drilling, toggles that spread the load behind the plaster when the framing doesn't line up, and a check for plaster that has let go of its lath — press lightly around the area and listen for a hollow, papery response. Loose plaster is fixed through to framing or bridged, never trusted on its own.

Concrete and block in towers and complexes. A hammer drill, concrete anchors or hollow-block anchors set in the solid web, and a vacuum held under the bit. Block walls in older complexes sometimes carry conduit or piping we cannot see, so we scan first and keep clear of the vertical line above outlets and switches.

Steel studs in new towers. Fasteners rated for light-gauge steel or toggles through the board, never wood screws, which strip in thin steel. Partitions between apartments in new buildings are often built as sound-rated assemblies, so we avoid cutting large openings in them and favour surface or local routing for cables rather than through-wall boxes on a demising wall.

While a brownstone or frame-house wall is open, the cheapest upgrade is a data cable behind the TV, sparing the bar and streamer from competing with a dozen neighbouring networks. That pull is handled by our structured cabling installation in Brooklyn, usually scoped on the same walkthrough.

Bars, restaurants, studios and co-working floors in converted industrial buildings and new waterfront blocks. Exposed brick, timber posts and concrete in the same room, screens over counters, and neighbours upstairs who expect the music to stop at the door.

Three commercial spines, each with its own business improvement district or merchants' group, lined with pubs, restaurants and shops that put the game on. One bar per screen, mounted clear of servers' heads, with a volume ceiling staff can find in a hurry.

Neighbourhood Strips Across the Borough

Salons, barbershops, gyms, laundromats and medical offices on Flatbush Avenue, Church Avenue, Kings Highway, Nostrand Avenue and every other Brooklyn shopping street. Screens run all day, so brackets need to be tamper-resistant and remotes need a home.

Lobbies, Lounges and Amenity Floors

Residents' lounges, gyms and screening rooms in the new rental towers. Management sets the hours, the audio has to stay inside the room, and cable has to be invisible because the room is on the leasing tour.

Compliance callouts for business soundbars in Brooklyn

  • Music limits under the city Noise Code. For businesses playing music, the Department of Environmental Protection's summary of the code sets a ceiling of 42 dB inside a nearby residence, a 7 dB margin over ambient on the street at 15 feet or more from 10 PM to 7 AM, and a separate bass limit of 6 dB(C) over ambient where the street is already above 62 dB(C). In a ground-floor space under apartments — most Brooklyn storefronts — we set bars and subs with the bass limit in mind first.
  • Storefronts in historic districts. The city's business portal says the owner of a building in a designated historic district needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before doing any work that needs a Department of Buildings permit, and changes to protected exterior features need LPC review. A soundbar inside the shop is neither; an outdoor speaker or cable run on the facade of a landmarked building is a question for the landlord and LPC before it is a question for us.
  • Walkways and the 4-inch rule. Anything fixed to a wall in a public route at a height from 27 to 80 inches may stick out no more than 4 inches under the 2010 ADA Standards, and most soundbars on brackets exceed that. In a clinic hallway or a shop aisle the bar therefore goes higher, into a recess, or off the route altogether.
  • What counts as electrical. HDMI, network and speaker wiring for a bar stays on the low-voltage side and has no DOB permit of its own. A new receptacle behind a display is a different trade — a licensed electrician's — and either way the landlord's rules for contractors govern the visit.
  • Landlord insurance requests. Commercial leases in Brooklyn nearly always require a contractor's COI listing the owner and agent; ours costs nothing and normally goes out the day you forward the request.
  • Cable rating. Anything run inside a wall or above a ceiling uses cable rated for that space rather than the patch lead from the box.

A shop or office adding soundbars is often due for new cabling, cameras or a door reader as well. Those jobs sit with our low-voltage installation team in Brooklyn, licensed for all of it.

Game-Day Screens

Soundbars for Brooklyn Bars, Restaurants and Storefronts

Most Brooklyn hospitality spaces sit on the ground floor of a mixed-use building, with apartments directly overhead. That single fact shapes every commercial soundbar we fit, from a pub on Third Avenue in Bay Ridge to a cafe on Bedford Avenue. The goal is speech and commentary that customers at the nearest tables can follow, without low end that reaches the bedrooms upstairs or the sidewalk at midnight.

One bar per screen, aimed at the seats

A long room with three or four screens usually works better with a modest soundbar under each one than with a single loud system. Each bar only has to reach the tables in front of it, so every screen can run at a lower level and the room stays conversational.

Subwoofers stay off or stay low

In a storefront under apartments, a separate sub is the component most likely to break the city's bass limit for commercial music. We generally leave it out, or set it well down and on an isolation platform away from the party wall and the structural columns. Bars with good built-in bass usually give enough weight for crowd noise and commentary.

Mounting heights that clear trays and heads

Screens over a bar back or a dining room are often high, and the soundbar goes with them, which usually means the $75 high-mount line. We keep the bar clear of shelving, coolers and server routes, and on exposed brick we fix into the face of sound bricks rather than mortar joints, with cable in a painted raceway or behind the bar back.

Controls that staff can use

The most common commercial complaint we hear is that nobody can find the right remote at 9 PM on a Saturday. We label remotes by screen, set each bar's maximum volume where the model allows it, and turn on HDMI-CEC so the TV remote can handle most of the work. For a venue with several screens, a programmed universal remote per screen is often worth the $99.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up when a Brooklyn soundbar job meets a Brooklyn building: what is behind the plaster, how far sound travels through a shared house, what a new tower allows, and what an old house's paperwork really requires. Booking, brands and moving questions are in the FAQ near the bottom.

Masonry, flues and old brick

Can the soundbar go on the chimney breast in a brownstone parlor, and does the old flue get in the way?

Yes, and the chimney breast is often the best-looking spot for a TV and bar in a parlor because it is centred on the room. The flue is the one thing to plan around. It usually runs up the middle of the breast, and the brick wrapping it can be a single course thick, so we locate the flue line before marking anything and put fixings into the solid brick on either side of it. A soundbar bracket rarely needs more than two fixings, and they can almost always sit outside the flue zone. If the fireplace below is still used, we also check how warm the breast gets at bar height, because electronics and rising heat do not mix well.

What the chimney breast will not take is in-wall wiring. Running cable down a flue or through the brick around it is not something we do, whether the fireplace works or has been capped. The finish there is a slim raceway down the face of the breast, painted to match, or a short run to the side and down the wall beside the breast where it meets the party wall.

How do you stop the drill from coming out the other side of a party wall into next door?

By deciding the depth before the drill starts. A soundbar anchor needs a hole a little longer than the anchor itself, usually a couple of inches into the brick. Every masonry bit we use on a party wall has a depth stop set to that length, so the hole ends where the anchor ends. We also drill without hammer action until we are through the plaster and can see what the brick is like, because hammering old soft brick can spall the far face even when the bit never gets there.

The other precaution is placement. Party walls are rarely uniform; some sections are two bricks thick, some have been patched, and some have old openings bricked in. A hole that suddenly breaks into a void or dust that changes colour tells us we are in a patch, and we move the fixing rather than push on. If the wall sounds or drills unusually thin at the planned height, the alternative is a TV-attached bracket that needs no new holes at all.

The wall in our Bed-Stuy frame house has brick between the studs — how does a bracket get a solid grip?

That brick-and-timber wall, known as nogging, is one of the more awkward surfaces in Brooklyn because no two fixings land on the same material. A stud finder reads it as noise. We drill a small pilot hole at each proposed fixing and look at what comes out: pale wood shavings mean timber, red dust means brick, and a sudden drop means a void or a mortar pocket. Fixings then go into timber with screws long enough to bite, or into the middle of a sound brick with a masonry anchor.

When the pilots tell us the wall is too irregular to trust in the place you want the bar, the clean answer is a plywood backer screwed across two or three timbers, with the soundbar bracket mounted to the plywood. It costs a line on the price list and gives the bar a flat, consistent base, which also helps on frame walls that bow or lean after a century of settling.

Can the new soundbar bracket reuse holes the last owner left in the brick?

Sometimes, but only after we look inside them. Old holes in brick are often oversized, filled with crumbled plastic plugs, or drilled into mortar joints rather than brick, and an anchor set into a widened hole will not hold the way the packaging promises. If an existing hole is clean, sits in solid brick and happens to match the bracket's spacing, we can clean it out and use a larger anchor in it. More often we put the new fixings a short distance away in fresh brick and leave the old holes for a mason or painter to fill. Either way, we tell you which holes we used before we leave.

Sound in a shared house

Our floor-through shares a party wall with the next house — does bass really travel sideways through brick?

It does, though usually less than it travels down through floors. Low bass passes into whatever the subwoofer is touching and whatever the room's air pressure pushes on, and in a rowhouse that includes a masonry wall shared with the neighbours and floor joists that run between the two party walls. A subwoofer pushed into the corner where the party wall meets the floor is in the worst possible place, because it excites the wall and the joists at once. Mid-range and dialogue from the bar itself are far less of a problem; brick is good at stopping them.

The practical answer is to keep the sub off the party wall and off the bare boards. We place it along the front or back wall of the room, a little out from any corner, on an isolation pad or a dense rug, and set its level against the bar so it fills in rather than leads. For many brownstone floor-throughs that alone turns a complaint into something the neighbours never notice.

What do night mode and dialogue enhancement actually change for late viewing?

Night mode, which makers also call dynamic range control or something similar, narrows the gap between the quietest and loudest sounds in the soundtrack. Explosions and music swells are pulled down and quiet speech is pushed up, so you can keep the overall volume low without missing lines. Dialogue enhancement is different: it lifts the frequencies where speech sits, and on bars with a centre channel it gives the centre more weight. Used together, they let you watch at a level that stays inside your own walls after 10 PM.

Neither switch turns off the subwoofer, and on some bars the sub level is a separate control. We show you where night mode, dialogue enhancement and sub level live on your remote or in the app, and if you watch late most nights we can set a lower sub level as the everyday default.

We own the brownstone and rent out the garden apartment — where should our living-room soundbar go?

On the floor above a tenant, the floor itself matters more than the wall. The soundbar can go wherever the TV looks best, because the bar's own sound is mostly mid-range and is stopped well by plaster and brick. The decision that protects your tenant is the subwoofer: which part of the room it sits in, what it stands on, and whether you need it at all. Rooms over a tenant's bedroom are worth mapping before you choose; a sub over their kitchen is far less intrusive than one over the bed.

If the parlor floor is the obvious TV room and the tenant's bedroom is directly below, a bar with good built-in bass and no separate sub can be the right purchase. We are happy to say so before you buy, and if you already own a sub, we set it up for that floor rather than for a showroom.

New towers in Downtown Brooklyn and on the waterfront

In a new Downtown Brooklyn rental, can you drill the concrete column or only the drywall?

Technically we can drill either, but in a rental tower the lease and the management office decide which, and many buildings treat structural concrete differently from partitions. Columns and shear walls in new towers can carry post-tensioning tendons or embedded conduit, and some management offices prohibit drilling into them outright. Partitions are steel stud with gypsum board and take toggles or steel-stud fasteners without trouble.

So before the visit we ask you to check the building's resident handbook or ask the management office, and we plan the mount for the wall they allow. Where only the partition is permitted and the TV is on the column, the answer is usually a TV-attached bracket for the bar or a console placement. We never drill a tower's structural concrete without the building's say-so.

The TV wall is the sound-rated partition between our apartment and the neighbour's — can a bracket go on it without spoiling the soundproofing?

Yes, if the fixings are small and nothing passes through the wall. Demising partitions in new Brooklyn towers are usually built as layered assemblies — steel studs, insulation and one or more layers of gypsum board, sometimes on resilient channel — designed to keep sound between units at a rated level. A couple of toggle or steel-stud fasteners for a light soundbar bracket barely affect that. What does affect it is a large hole: a recessed box cut into the board, a through-wall cable pass, or a box placed back-to-back with one on the neighbour's side.

So on a demising wall we keep to surface fixings, avoid recessed boxes, and bring cable down in a slim raceway or route it along the wall to the console instead of inside the partition. If you want in-wall wiring, we look for a non-demising wall to carry it, or ask the building which walls are rated before cutting anything. And because a bar on a shared wall sends some vibration into it, we set the subwoofer away from that wall and lower than the default.

Our router is in the garden apartment we rent out — how does the soundbar on our parlor floor get a reliable connection?

This comes up often in owner-occupied brownstones, where the internet service was installed on the lowest floor years ago and now serves a tenant. Sharing the tenant's network is rarely a good idea for either side, and Wi-Fi from two floors down through brick and plaster is often weak by the time it reaches the TV. The cleanest fix is your own internet service or your own router on your floors, placed close to the TV room.

Where the service has to stay downstairs, the options are a network cable run up through a closet or an existing chase to your floor, which our structured cabling team can do, or a separate access point on your level. Once your floor has its own connection, we wire the soundbar to it if the bar has an Ethernet port, or put it on the strongest band your new router offers, and pair the sub and rear speakers after the network is stable rather than before.

Landmark blocks and renovations

Does the Landmarks Preservation Commission have to approve a soundbar in a historic-district house?

No. LPC's jurisdiction is the protected exterior of the building and work that needs a Department of Buildings permit. The city's business portal explains that owners in a designated district need an LPC permit for work that needs a DOB permit, with a Certificate of No Effect issued for interior work that needs a DOB permit but leaves protected features alone. Mounting a soundbar and running its low-voltage cable inside the house needs no DOB permit, so it needs nothing from LPC either.

Where landmarks can come into it is anything on the outside: speakers on a front stoop, cable clipped up a facade, or a dish or antenna visible from the street. If a project grows in that direction, the owner checks with LPC before anything is fixed, and we keep everything we install inside the walls.

We are gutting the parlor floor — what should be roughed in for the soundbar before the walls close?

This is the best moment to get the soundbar right, because everything awkward about wiring a finished brownstone disappears when the wall is open. Decide where the TV and bar will go, then run an in-wall-rated HDMI cable and a network cable from that spot to wherever the cable box, console and router will live, with a recessed low-voltage box behind the TV and a matching one at the equipment end. Ask your electrician for an outlet behind the TV at the height the plan calls for, because adding one later is electrical work.

Add blocking between the framing at bar height if the wall is a stud partition, so the bracket has wood to bite later regardless of the bar's width. On a masonry party wall that is plastered or boarded during the renovation, mark the brick-face position of the TV and bar on the drawings before the finish goes on. We can come out during the rough-in, walk the plan with your contractor and run the low-voltage side ourselves.

The old walls bow and lean — how do you keep the bar looking straight under the TV?

A century-old Brooklyn wall is rarely flat or plumb, and floors and ceilings in old rowhouses can slope noticeably. If we levelled the bar perfectly to the horizon on a room where everything else tilts, it would look crooked to everyone who sits there. So we level the bar to the TV, check it against the mantel, window heads or ceiling line you see from the couch, and make a judgement with you before tightening.

Bowed plaster is a separate problem: a bracket pressed onto a hump rocks, and one bridging a hollow can pull the plaster in. On uneven sections we shim behind the bracket or use a small backer so the bar sits flat without stressing the plaster, and we keep the bar's gap to the TV even along its length.

Connections we set up on every Brooklyn job

  • HDMI eARC or ARC: the preferred link, carrying sound from the TV and its apps to the bar and letting the TV remote run volume and power through HDMI-CEC. eARC carries lossless formats; ARC still carries Dolby Atmos inside Dolby Digital Plus.
  • Optical: the fallback on older TVs and on bars like the Ray. It works well for stereo and 5.1, but volume control relies on the bar learning the TV remote's codes.
  • Ethernet: where the bar has a port and a cable can reach the router, a wired connection for streaming and app control, which matters in Wi-Fi-congested towers.
  • Power: the bar, any sub and any rear speakers each need a socket. We plan where every lead goes before mounting, because a beautifully hung bar with a cord across the floor isn't finished.
AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Brooklyn

Search for soundbar installation and an AI summary will give you a national price range, a list of steps and some reassuring generalities. Those answers are built from cost guides and product pages written for a house in the suburbs. Here is how seven common claims hold up in a Brooklyn rowhouse or tower.

Claim one: a soundbar install costs about the same everywhere

Cost-guide sites publish national averages, and AI summaries repeat them. HomeWyse's soundbar page put the estimated national average at $58.43 to $231 per soundbar in September 2026, and Thumbtack's TV-mounting cost page gives a national average range of $121 to $234. Those numbers are real, and for a bar on drywall over wood studs in a house with a driveway they are a fair guide.

They say nothing about the material that makes Brooklyn jobs take longer: masonry party walls, nogging, chimney breasts, concrete columns, and sometimes four flights of stairs with the gear. A national average blends all of that away, which is why one quote is right on the day and another grows once the drill hits brick.

Our answer is a published wall-by-wall list. A wall-mounted bar is $129 on drywall over wood studs, and the surcharges for metal studs, plaster, brick, concrete and tile are printed alongside it, so a brownstone party wall is priced before anyone arrives.

Claim two: the bracket in the box is all you need

Many bars ship with a wall bracket and a bag of screws and plugs, and AI answers often treat that as the end of the hardware question. The bracket itself is usually fine. The fixings in the bag are generally intended for a wood stud or solid backing, and Angi's TV mounting cost guide lists a separate soundbar bracket at $30 to $100 for cases where the supplied one won't do.

In Brooklyn the plastic plugs are the weak link. They spin in soft lime-mortar brick, crush old plaster and strip in steel studs. The hardware that holds is matched to the wall: sleeve anchors in brick, toggles behind plaster, steel-stud fasteners in towers, concrete anchors in block.

We use the supplied bracket whenever it is rated for the bar and suits the mount, and swap only the fixings. If the bar came without a bracket, the Sanus bracket on our list is the alternative.

Claim three: you need an eARC TV to get Dolby Atmos

This one appears constantly and is only half right. eARC is needed for lossless formats such as Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio. Ordinary ARC can still carry Dolby Atmos packaged inside Dolby Digital Plus, which is how most streaming services deliver it.

In practice that means a Brooklyn household with a perfectly good ARC television does not need a new TV to enjoy an Atmos bar from Netflix or a similar app. It does mean the bar has to be on the right HDMI socket — the one labelled ARC or eARC — with HDMI-CEC and the TV's audio output set correctly.

Getting those settings right is part of every install we do, and it is the most common fix on service visits after a TV software update quietly resets them.

Claim four: up-firing speakers work in any room

AI summaries often present Atmos height effects as automatic. Dolby's own setup guide says reflected height sound wants a flat ceiling between 7.5 and 14 feet. Up-firing drivers bounce sound off the ceiling, so the shape and height of the ceiling decide how convincing the effect is.

Brooklyn rooms test this both ways. Some brownstone parlors have tall ceilings with deep plaster cornices and medallions that scatter reflections; some tower apartments have low concrete ceilings with soffits and ductwork right above the bar's position.

We cannot change the ceiling, but we can measure it, place the bar so its up-firing drivers have a clear path, and run the bar's room calibration where it has one. We will also tell you honestly when a height effect is likely to be subtle in your room.

Claim five: a bigger subwoofer is always better

Product listings and review roundups reward bass, and AI answers echo them. In a detached house that is harmless. In a building where the floor joists sit in the same party walls as the neighbours', the subwoofer is the part of the system most likely to reach someone else's home.

Bass carries through structure in a way speech does not. That is why the city's Noise Code singles out bass with a separate C-weighted limit for commercial music, and why a Brooklyn resident on Reddit was advised to file 311 complaints after 10 PM when a neighbour's music would not stop.

The better question for a Brooklyn apartment is how well the sub is placed and set. A modest sub, isolated from the floor and set below the factory level, sounds fuller at the couch and quieter downstairs than a large one in a corner.

Claim six: permits only come into it for electrical work

Angi's guide, for example, says permits are required only for electrical work or structural changes. That is broadly right for the soundbar itself, which needs no Department of Buildings permit. It leaves out the paperwork Brooklyn buildings actually ask for.

Co-ops, condos and rental towers usually want a certificate of insurance and a scheduled time before any contractor visit, however small. Historic-district owners need LPC sign-off for exterior changes and for work that needs a DOB permit. Commercial landlords want their own names on the COI.

None of this is difficult, but it decides whether the installer gets past the lobby. We issue certificates at no charge and send them ahead, and we keep everything we install inside your walls.

Claim seven: any handyman can do it in half an hour

Gig platforms and AI answers alike describe soundbar mounting as a quick job, and on drywall it often is. A Brooklyn party wall with a depth limit, a nogging wall that needs reading hole by hole, or a tower partition beside a structural column is a different task, and the cost of getting it wrong is a hole in your neighbour's plaster or a bar on the floor.

The other half of the job is settings: the right HDMI socket, CEC, lip-sync, the sub's level, and Wi-Fi in a crowded building. Those are where most of the complaints we see on second-opinion visits begin.

So before booking anyone, ourselves included, ask for the price of each surprise: brick behind the plaster, nogging inside a frame wall, a concrete column where the drywall should be. An installer who can answer all three in writing has drilled Brooklyn walls before.

Floor by Floor

Where the Subwoofer Goes in a Brooklyn Rowhouse

A rowhouse is a stack of apartments sharing two long walls with the houses either side, so the right spot for a sub depends on which floor you live on. This is how we think about each level when a Brooklyn household asks where the low end should live. It is guidance, not a rule; your floor plan and your neighbours decide the final answer.

Garden floor

The garden or ground floor usually sits over a cellar rather than another home, so the floor below is rarely the worry. The concern is sideways and upwards: the party walls carry bass to the next house, and the parlor floor above is often a separate apartment. A sub here works best along the front or rear wall, away from both party walls, on a pad that keeps it from drumming the floor above the cellar. If the cellar ceiling is open joists, expect the bass to sound louder down there than you might guess.

Parlor floor

Usually the grandest room and the obvious place for the TV, and usually above someone else's bedroom or living room. On this floor we map what sits below before choosing the sub's position, keep it out of the corners where party wall meets floor, and favour a dense rug or isolation platform under it. If the parlor is open front to back, placing the sub near the opening between the parlors, rather than against either end wall, often gives even bass at a lower setting.

Upper floors

Bedrooms and smaller living rooms on the second and third floors have neighbours below and often a ceiling of lath and plaster that rings with bass more than you would expect. For many top-floor rentals a bar with no separate sub, or a sub run noticeably below the bar's default, is the considerate setup. If a sub is a must, it goes on the most solid part of the floor, close to an outside wall rather than mid-span between the party walls, where floors tend to flex more.

Frame houses and converted spaces

Wood-frame houses and older conversions can have springy floors and thin partitions between units. Here, isolation under the sub matters most, and it is worth listening from the apartment below or next door with the owner's permission before we finish. Converted industrial lofts in Williamsburg, Greenpoint and Gowanus behave differently again: heavy concrete or timber floors stop more bass, but open plans and hard surfaces make the room itself boomy, so the sub level usually comes down for the room's sake rather than the neighbours'.

What we leave you with

Whatever the floor, we finish by showing you the sub-level control, the night mode switch and the quickest way to turn the sub down without touching the bar's volume. A sub that can be dialled down in five seconds is one that stays installed rather than unplugged after the first knock on the door.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Brooklyn Party Walls and Frame Walls

How our crews handle Brooklyn rowhouse masonry and frame walls in general, rather than a write-up of a single visit

On a rowhouse, our installers start by working out which wall is shared. From the street, count the houses and note where the chimney stacks sit; inside, the party walls are the long side walls, usually without windows. Any fixing on a party wall gets a depth stop, and the first hole is drilled slowly with no hammer action so we can feel whether the brick is hard, soft or patched.

On a chimney breast, we locate the flue before marking the bracket. Tapping usually finds it, because the brick over a flue sounds slightly different from solid masonry, and the fireplace opening below shows where it rises. Fixings stay on solid brick either side.

On a frame house, we expect surprises. A small pilot at each fixing point tells us more than any scanner: wood, brick or air. When two pilots disagree, we either move the bar a little or put a plywood backer on and mount to that. And before the subwoofer goes anywhere, we look at the floor and ask who lives below or next door.

Brooklyn Soundbar Installation FAQ

Do you charge extra to come out to Coney Island, Canarsie or Bergen Beach?
+

No. The Brooklyn prices on this page are the same in every Brooklyn neighbourhood, from Greenpoint and Williamsburg in the north to Coney Island, Canarsie and Bergen Beach in the south. There is no separate travel or parking line for Brooklyn jobs; you pay only the install lines and any wall or walk-up add-on that applies.

We're moving between two Brooklyn apartments. Can you take the bar down at one and put it up at the other?
+

Yes. We take the bar and its bracket down at the old apartment, then re-hang them at the new one on fixings chosen for the new wall, which is often a different material. The take-down holes are left clean for your painter or super to fill. Book both addresses in one request so we can plan the two stops together.

What if my landlord says no holes after I've already booked a wall mount?
+

Tell us before the visit and we switch the job rather than cancel it. A console placement is $79 and a bracket that clips the bar to your existing TV mount needs no new holes. Changes made at least 24 hours ahead cost nothing; inside 24 hours the $150 return-trip fee can apply.

Can the soundbar share the TV's outlet, or does it need its own?
+

The bar needs its own socket, but that can be on the same outlet or surge protector as the TV. A two-gang outlet behind the TV, or a good surge strip, usually covers both. A separate subwoofer and any rear speakers each need a socket near where they sit. We don't add outlets; if there is no socket where one is needed, that is a job for an electrician.

Which soundbar brands do you install?
+

Any make you choose, including Sonos, Samsung, LG, Sony, Bose, Vizio, JBL, Yamaha, Denon and Polk. The method depends on the bar's weight, width and connections rather than the logo. Send us the model and we confirm the bracket and connection before the visit.

Do you need the power or water turned off while you work?
+

No. Soundbar installation is low-voltage work: we fix a bracket, run HDMI, network and speaker leads, and plug the equipment into existing outlets. We do not open electrical boxes or touch plumbing, so nothing in the apartment or the building needs to be shut off. We do scan the wall for pipes and wiring before drilling.

How quickly will someone get back to me after I send the form?
+

The same business day. A licensed installer reads every Brooklyn request and replies with a written price built from the lines on this page, or with a question if a photo of the wall would settle it. If you would rather talk now, call (347) 934-8335.

Will someone show me how to work the soundbar before you leave?
+

Yes. Before packing up we walk you through power, volume, input switching, night mode, dialogue enhancement and the subwoofer level on your remote and in the maker's app, and we check that the TV remote controls the bar. It takes a few minutes and saves most of the calls we would otherwise get the next evening.

Can you work from my architect's or designer's drawings for a renovation?
+

Yes. Send the drawings with the TV and bar positions marked and we will review the low-voltage side: box locations, cable paths, blocking and where the outlet should go for your electrician. We can then come out at rough-in, before the walls are closed, and again to hang the bar when the room is finished.
