The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Access Control

Boerum Hill is small and unusually uniform. Wikipedia’s summary of the neighbourhood, drawing on the usual definitions, puts its edges at Schermerhorn Street to the north, Fourth Avenue to the east, Smith or Court Street to the west and Warren or Wyckoff Street to the south, and describes most of its housing as three-storey rowhouses built between 1840 and 1870. The name itself is younger than the houses: it was coined in 1964 by Helen Buckler, who founded the Boerum Hill Association, for an area long called North Gowanus or simply part of South Brooklyn. The houses were often boarding houses in the early twentieth century, then family homes, and now very often an owner’s floors with one or two rental apartments. That history is written into the doors. Most have been fitted with four or five generations of locks, and most were never designed to be opened by anything but a key.

Much of the neighbourhood is protected. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Boerum Hill Historic District on November 20, 1973, with about 250 buildings, and on June 26, 2018 voted unanimously to designate an extension of about 288 more in three sections: Dean and Bergen Streets between Smith and Hoyt; Atlantic Avenue between Hoyt and Nevins together with Pacific Street between Hoyt and Bond; and Wyckoff and Bergen Streets between Bond and Nevins. The Commission’s materials describe Greek Revival and Italianate rowhouses from the late 1840s through the 1870s, a later row of Second Empire houses on Bergen Street and neo-Grec apartment buildings scattered through the blocks, along with historic storefronts on Atlantic Avenue. The State Street Houses — 23 rowhouses at 291–299 and 290–324 State Street, between Smith and Hoyt, built between 1847 and 1874 — were designated as landmarks on the same day in 1973 as the original district.

That matters for access control in a way it does not on a newer block. On a landmarked façade, the usual commercial approach — a metal reader box on the masonry, a raceway up the wall, a new hole through the hood — is exactly what the Commission’s guidance steers away from. Its permit guidebook lets staff approve intercoms and doorbells placed within or beside the door opening, attached to mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or ornament, with conduit hidden. A good Boerum Hill installation therefore looks like almost nothing from the sidewalk: a keypad where a key cylinder used to be, a reader set into the door or the inside of the vestibule, cable that arrives from the cellar. The hardware is ordinary. The discipline is in where it goes.

The second thing that shapes the work is who lives behind the doors. A house with an owner and two rental floors may be a multiple dwelling under city law, and that brings two sets of rules. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, governs electronic entry systems in Class A multiple dwellings: owners must provide a written data policy, collect only minimal data with express consent, destroy authentication data within 90 days and never sell it, and may not force a tenant to use the system to enter their own apartment. The Housing Maintenance Code separately requires each apartment door to keep a key-operated deadbolt with a thumb-turn inside. Our default design for a Boerum Hill house with tenants follows from both: electronic credentials on the shared street door, keys kept on apartment doors, and logs that delete themselves.

The third is the street. Brooklyn Paper reported in September 2024 that two businesses on one Boerum Hill block had their front glass doors smashed overnight, and in another report described a parcel thief on Bond Street who opened a front gate and ran down a stoop with about $3,500 in packages. In March 2026 the 84th Precinct, which covers Boerum Hill, warned businesses in its area about a rise in break-ins and offered free on-site security surveys. None of those cases is solved by a card reader alone. What a well-planned entry system does is put deliveries behind a locked door instead of on a stoop, record which staff member locked up a shop, and alert an owner when a back door is forced or left open — the gaps those stories describe.

Finally, the neighbourhood is changing at its edges. Brownstoner reported in August 2026 that the 1890s Romanesque Revival church at 360 Schermerhorn Street, at the corner of Third Avenue, had been sold for $28.25 million to a developer planning apartments, noting that it sits outside any city historic district. A block away, the new 15-storey detention facility on the block bounded by Atlantic Avenue, Smith Street, State Street and Boerum Place was close to topping out in April 2026, according to Field Condition’s construction update. Long construction projects bring scaffolding, crews and open doors to nearby blocks for years. Owners on those blocks ask us about self-relocking doors, held-open alerts and temporary contractor codes far more often than about biometric gadgets.

A street door that has swollen over a wet summer no longer closes far enough for the strike to catch, so the reader beeps, the strike buzzes and the door stays open. Building Specialists

Access Control for Boerum Hill’s Architecture

Boerum Hill has fewer building types than most Brooklyn neighbourhoods, but each one treats a door differently. These are the ones we plan for.

Greek Revival and Italianate rowhouses

The 1840s–1870s houses that make up most of the district. Heavy wood doors in deep frames, a vestibule behind the outer door, a stoop and an under-stoop entrance. Cable usually comes up from the cellar inside the frame.

Second Empire row on Bergen Street

The 1880s row the LPC singled out in the 2018 extension. Mansard roofs above, but at street level the same vestibule logic; finishes are chosen to sit quietly against the original woodwork.

Neo-Grec flats buildings

Small 1880s apartment buildings scattered through the extension, typically walk-ups with a shared street door, an old intercom riser and enough apartments to bring Multiple Dwelling Law rules into play.

Owner-plus-rental conversions

Rowhouses split into an owner’s triplex and a garden or top-floor rental. Two households, two entrances, two credential groups, and privacy-law settings for the tenants.

Atlantic Avenue mixed-use buildings

Two- to four-storey buildings with shops below and apartments above, many inside the 2018 extension between Hoyt and Nevins. The shop door and the residential door need separate zones.

Newer towers at the northern edge

Mid- and high-rise residential buildings along and near Schermerhorn Street toward Downtown Brooklyn, with lobbies, amenity rooms, garages and package rooms already on networked platforms.

Method on a mid-nineteenth-century rowhouse door

The doors on Greek Revival and Italianate houses are often thick, heavy and original or close to it, hung in deep moulded frames set into brick or brownstone. They carry the scars of every lock fitted since: the outline of a mortise lock, a filled rim-lock hole, a deadbolt from the 1970s with a second one above it. We remove what is no longer used, fill old bores with glued hardwood plugs cut flush, and then decide where the new lock sits by measuring the backset and the stile width rather than by reusing the nearest hole. A keypad deadbolt fits most of these doors directly. Where a reader and strike are wanted instead, the strike is cut into the frame on the latch side, and on a soft or patched frame we use a longer faceplate fixed with long screws into sound wood behind the jamb.

Inside, the vestibule is the most useful space in the house for access control. The outer door can carry the electronic credential while the inner door stays on a key, which means the house keeps two layers even if a code is shared. Power for a strike or reader comes from a small supply in the cellar directly below, and the cable climbs through the floor at the jamb. Nothing changes on the façade except the device on the door itself.

Method on neo-Grec flats and small walk-ups

The small apartment buildings of the 1880s have a different problem: the door works hard. A street door used by eight or ten households opens hundreds of times a week, so the closer, hinges and strike carry the job. Before a reader goes on, we check that the closer brings the door all the way shut against wind and that the strike is rated for the traffic. The existing intercom riser is usually reusable as a route, and the strike and power supply that release the door for visitors can be shared with a reader, so tenants use credentials and visitors still ring. Where the building has eight or more apartments, Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a expects the intercom and self-locking door to work, so we treat the intercom as part of the job rather than an extra.

On these buildings we generally avoid electromagnetic locks on the street door. The NYC Building Code allows them on residential egress doors only where the lock releases on loss of power, the exit hardware directly cuts power to the magnet and the units are listed to UL 294. A fail-secure electric strike keeps the building closed to the street during a blackout while still letting anyone inside turn the handle and leave, which is what most Boerum Hill owners and tenants actually want.

Method on owner-plus-rental conversions

This is the most common Boerum Hill job and the one with the most rules attached. We start by mapping who uses which door. The garden tenant usually has the under-stoop door; the owner and the upper tenant share the stoop door and the vestibule; the cellar door to the yard belongs to the owner. Each household gets its own credential group, and the system is set so that one tenant’s code never opens another household’s door. Apartment doors keep keyed deadbolts as the Housing Maintenance Code expects, and where an owner wants a keypad on their own door we fit the type with a key cylinder. Logs are set to delete on a short cycle, and the owner gets a one-page privacy summary to give tenants.

Method on mixed-use buildings and newer towers

On Atlantic Avenue, the shop and the apartments above usually share a façade and sometimes a hallway, but not a security policy. The shop’s rear or cellar door gets a reader with a door-position contact; the residential street door gets its own reader or keypad; and the two are separate zones on one controller so the landlord sees both. On the towers at the northern edge, the platform is usually chosen already, and our work is adding doors to it, replacing failed readers, and moving amenity, package and garage doors onto the same credential so residents carry one fob.

Owners who are rewiring a rowhouse for access control often also want the street and the stoop covered. Our Boerum Hill security camera installation page explains how cameras are placed under the same landmark rules.

Landmark District

Working Within the Boerum Hill Historic District

If your house is in the 1973 district or the 2018 extension, any change to the outside of the building falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That sounds daunting for a lock, but the Commission’s own permit guidebook for health and safety equipment is specific and practical. It says staff can issue permits for intercoms, doorbells, light fixtures and security cameras that meet published criteria, and that ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of existing equipment needs no permit at all. Where a question is close, the guidebook gives the Commission’s phone line and email for advice. We read the criteria the same way an expediter would and design so that the house fits them comfortably.

The criteria we design to are these. Intercom panels and doorbells go within or adjacent to the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance. There is a maximum of one intercom panel and two light fixtures per door opening, and design, size and finish must be compatible with the building. Equipment is attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Conduit is concealed behind the installation where possible, with a short visible run painted to match. Equipment should not go on a primary façade unless there is no other option, and should be as small as possible. Security cameras are limited to two per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings, finished to match.

A keypad or reader mounted on the door leaf itself, wired from inside, meets the spirit of all of that: it sits within the door opening, it is attached to wood, its cable is hidden, and it is about the size of the deadbolt it replaces. When a reader has to be on the masonry — for example on a building where the street door is glass — we put it beside the opening on plain stone, fix it into a mortar joint, and bring the cable through the joint from behind. We do not run raceway up a façade or drill decorative stone, and we do not touch cast iron. For each landmarked job we hand the owner a short description and marked-up photo of what is proposed, which is what the Commission or an expediter will ask for.

Two practical notes. First, the rules apply to the exterior; nothing in the guidebook stops you putting a controller, power supply or reader inside the vestibule. Second, the 2018 extension brought commercial buildings on Atlantic Avenue between Hoyt and Nevins into the district, including historic storefronts. Owners there, several of whom spoke against designation at the 2018 hearing according to 6sqft, now plan shop-door hardware within the same criteria as rowhouse owners. A reader set inside a storefront’s recessed entry, or on the shop door itself, is usually the cleanest answer.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on Atlantic Avenue and Smith Street

Boerum Hill’s commercial life runs along two streets and a few side blocks. Atlantic Avenue is the larger: the Atlantic Avenue Business Improvement District, approved by the City Planning Commission in 2011, runs from Fourth Avenue west to the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, one block north and south, and covers 738 tax lots and more than 400 businesses in Boerum Hill and Cobble Hill. Wikipedia’s neighbourhood summary calls Atlantic a design district; in practice it mixes furniture and design showrooms, restaurants, salons, offices and national chains in two- to four-storey buildings with apartments above. Smith Street, the other main commercial street, is best known as a restaurant row.

The access control needs on those two streets are different. A showroom or design studio on Atlantic Avenue typically has a glass front door used by customers during opening hours and a rear or cellar door used by staff and deliveries. The front is protected by a gate or glazing at night; the rear door is where we put a reader, a door-position contact and a strike, so the owner knows who opened it and gets an alert if it is propped. Restaurants on Smith Street have a heavier daily rhythm: early deliveries, late closes, kitchen staff who change often. Individual staff codes on the back door and the cellar hatch, removable the day someone leaves, replace the drawer of spare keys most kitchens accumulate.

Off the two main streets, the small offices, studios and practices on Hoyt, Bond and Nevins Streets and along Fourth Avenue usually need a single office door, perhaps two, with schedules: unlocked during reception hours, credential-only before and after. Our published Brooklyn figures for this kind of work are a keypad on a personnel door at $1,500 to $2,000 and card readers on several office or studio doors in a shared building at $4,000 to $12,000. Buildings with shops below and apartments above get two zones on one controller so the landlord sees both and neither group can open the other’s door.

Security on these blocks is a real concern rather than a sales line. Brooklyn Paper’s September 2024 report described two Boerum Hill businesses on the same block hit in one night through broken glass doors. In March 2026 the 84th Precinct warned shopkeepers in its area about a spike in break-ins, advising brighter lighting, visible cameras, stronger locks and alarms, and offering free security surveys. An entry system is one layer in that list. We are happy to plan it alongside the precinct’s advice rather than instead of it.

Compliance callouts for Boerum Hill doors

  • Electromagnetic locks (NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.9). Allowed on egress doors in R-2, B, M and several other occupancies only with door-mounted hardware that is obvious, one-handed and directly cuts power to the lock; loss of power must unlock the door; panic hardware where required must release it; units listed to UL 294.
  • Sensor-released doors (NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.8). Sensor on the egress side; loss of power unlocks; a “PUSH TO EXIT” manual device 40–48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door, holding the door unlocked for at least 30 seconds; unlock on fire alarm, sprinkler or fire detection activation where provided.
  • Entrance doors and intercoms (Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a). Self-closing, self-locking street doors on Class A multiple dwellings, and an intercom with door release for buildings of eight or more apartments, subject to the statute’s rules for older buildings.
  • Apartment doors (Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043). A key lock and at least one key for every unit; in Class A buildings a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt with key outside and thumb-turn inside, and a chain door guard.
  • Tenant data (Local Law 63 of 2021). Written privacy policy, minimal data, express consent, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, no sale of data, and no requirement to use the system to enter one’s own apartment.
  • Landmarked exteriors (LPC Permit Guidebook). Staff-level permits for small security equipment within or beside the door opening, attached to joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, with hidden conduit.

This is a plain-language summary to help plan a job, not legal advice. Where a rule turns on how your building is classified, check with the agency or your own counsel.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Boerum Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners, landlords, boards and shopkeepers in Boerum Hill bring to a walkthrough. They are about old doors, landmark rules, tenants and storefronts, because that is what entry systems here have to deal with.

Our house is inside the Boerum Hill Historic District. Can a keypad or card reader go on the original front door, or does Landmarks have to approve it first?

Both can happen, and they are not in conflict. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates changes to the outside of designated buildings, and its own permit guidebook treats small security equipment as something staff can approve when it meets set criteria, rather than a matter for a full public hearing. The criteria are practical. Intercom panels and doorbells go “within or adjacent to a door opening”, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance; one intercom panel and no more than two light fixtures per door opening; equipment attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and never through cast iron or a decorative surface; conduit concealed behind the device where possible, with only a short visible run painted to match. A reader or a keypad on the door itself is the least visible place for it, which is why we start there.

What we avoid on a Boerum Hill house is the shortcut that looks fine on a newer building: a surface-mounted metal box screwed to the brownstone beside the door, a plastic raceway running up the face of the stoop, or a reader bolted through the decorative hood. Those are the details that turn a staff-level permit into a problem. Where the lock electronics can live inside the door or on the interior face of the vestibule, nothing on the façade changes at all except a keyway that becomes a keypad of about the same footprint.

Practically, we photograph the entrance, mark where each component would sit and give you a one-page description you can file with the Commission or hand to an expediter. Ordinary maintenance and replacement of existing equipment does not need a permit under the same guidebook, so swapping a dead reader for a new one in the same place is usually the easier job. We are not the Commission and do not issue opinions on its behalf; if a design question is close, the guidebook gives the LPC’s phone line and email, and asking first costs nothing.

The stoop door and the garden-level door under it both open onto the street. Which one should get the reader in a two-entrance rowhouse?

Usually both get something, but not the same thing. Most Boerum Hill rowhouses were built between the 1840s and 1870s with a parlour-floor entrance up the stoop and a ground-floor entrance beneath it, behind the areaway gate. In a single-family house the stoop door is ceremonial and the under-stoop door is the one people actually use with groceries, strollers and bikes. In a house split into a garden rental and an owner’s triplex, the two doors serve different households, and each needs its own set of credentials.

Our default plan puts the more capable device on the door that carries the most traffic. Where the garden door is the everyday entrance, it gets the reader or keypad tied to logs and schedules, because that is where the dog walker, the cleaner and the deliveries arrive. The stoop door then gets either a matching keypad deadbolt with fewer users or stays keyed, depending on how often it is used.

The one arrangement we steer people away from is a single reader that releases both doors at once. It sounds tidy, but it means a tenant’s code opens the owner’s door, and a lost code opens two homes instead of one. Separate zones cost a little more and are worth it.

We rent out the top two floors of our State Street house. Does the city’s smart-access privacy law apply to a three-family brownstone?

Very possibly, and it is worth treating as if it does. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, sets rules for smart access systems in Class A multiple dwellings — key cards, phone apps, biometrics and similar electronic entry. A house with three or more apartments is generally a multiple dwelling under city and state housing law; whether yours is Class A depends on how it is classified and used, which your certificate of occupancy and your lawyer can confirm. We are not lawyers, and we do not decide that question for you.

What the law requires is not complicated to build around. Owners must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy; collect only a short list of data (such as name, unit number, preferred contact and lease information); get express consent in writing or through an app before collecting reference data; destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized; and never sell or lease the data. Owners may not require a tenant to use the smart access system to get into their own apartment, and there is a private right of action for unlawful sale of data. HPD publishes a plain-language summary.

So for a three-unit house we configure the system to keep less, not more: logs set to purge on a short cycle, no photos captured at the door unless you want a separate camera, tenant names entered as unit labels where that is enough, and a written one-page policy you can hand over with the lease. The street door gets the electronic credential; the apartment doors keep their keyed deadbolts.

Can a landlord on Pacific Street require tenants to use a phone app to get into their own apartments?

Not to get into the apartment itself. The Tenant Data Privacy Act says owners and their agents may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant’s dwelling unit. Separately, the city’s Housing Maintenance Code requires the owner to provide a key lock on each apartment entrance door and at least one key, and in a Class A multiple dwelling that door needs a heavy-duty latch set and a heavy-duty deadbolt that works with a key from outside and a thumb-turn from inside. A phone-only apartment door does not fit that picture.

The street door is different ground. Many small buildings in Boerum Hill already have a self-locking front door and a buzzer, and moving that door to fobs, codes or phone credentials is common and lawful when it is done with the privacy rules in mind. The practical rule we follow is simple: electronic credentials on shared doors, keyed deadbolts (with or without an added keypad) on apartment doors, and nothing that leaves a tenant unable to get home because a phone battery died.

If tenants want keypad convenience on their own door, the usual answer is a keypad deadbolt that still has a key cylinder, so the code is a convenience and the key remains the legal baseline.

Our shop on Atlantic Avenue had its glass door smashed overnight. What would an access system actually change the morning after?

Be clear-eyed about what it changes and what it does not. Brooklyn Paper reported in September 2024 that two businesses on one Boerum Hill block, Blank Street Coffee and Piccola Cucina Casa, were burgled after their front glass doors were broken, with $100 and $1,000 taken. A card reader would not have stopped a brick. Glass is glass. What access control does is answer the questions that follow: which staff member closed, at what time the door was last locked, whether the rear or cellar door was used, and whether anyone tried a credential afterwards.

For a storefront on Atlantic Avenue or Smith Street, the plan that makes a real difference is layered. The glass front is protected by the roll-down gate or a laminated security film, which is a glazing question rather than an access one. The back door to the yard or the hallway — often the door actually used by burglars on attached blocks — gets a reader and a door-position contact so it reports when it is propped. Staff codes are individual, so closing time is recorded by name, and a forced door or a door held open triggers an alert to the owner’s phone.

The NYPD’s 84th Precinct, which covers Boerum Hill, has publicly offered free on-site crime-prevention surveys to businesses; that is a good, independent first step before you spend money with us or anyone else.

Packages keep disappearing from behind our front gate on Bond Street. Would an entry system with a delivery code help?

It helps if the package ends up behind a locked door, not just behind a gate. Brooklyn Paper reported a Bond Street case in which a resident heard her front gate open and saw someone run down her stoop with a bag of parcels worth about $3,500, including a doorbell camera. A low areaway gate slows nobody. The fix is giving couriers somewhere locked to put things.

On a rowhouse, that usually means the vestibule between the outer and inner doors. We put a keypad or reader on the outer door, issue time-limited codes for regular carriers, or let you release the door from your phone after seeing who is there on a video intercom, and leave the inner door locked as before. The parcel sits between two doors instead of on the stoop. Each delivery code shows up in the log, so if something goes missing you know whether the door was opened and when.

Neighbours in nearby brownstone areas have argued the same point on Reddit — a Park Slope thread on packages in brownstones suggested a camera intercom so uniformed couriers can be let into the front entrance. That is the same idea, and on Bond Street it works best combined with a reader rather than a bare buzzer.

Should the magnetic lock on our walk-up’s vestibule door release when the power fails, and what does the city code expect?

On a door people leave through, yes: it has to. The New York City Building Code lets electromagnetic locks be used on egress doors in residential (R-2) and several other occupancies only under conditions, and one of those conditions is that loss of power to the electromagnetic lock automatically unlocks the door. The hardware on the door must have an obvious method of operation that works one-handed in any light and directly cuts power to the magnet. Where the release is a sensor rather than a handle, the code adds a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and the door must unlock on fire alarm or sprinkler activation where those systems exist. The locking units must be listed to UL 294.

That is why, on a walk-up’s street door, we generally prefer an electric strike or an electrified lockset to a mag lock. An electric strike can be fail-secure — the door stays latched from outside when the power goes, but anyone inside simply turns the handle and walks out, because the mechanical latch is never prevented from retracting from inside. A mag lock that releases on power failure leaves the building open to the street during a blackout, which is the opposite of what most owners want.

There are places where a mag lock is the right tool, such as a glass storefront door with no room for a strike. In those cases we wire it to the code’s requirements, label the exit device, and walk the owner through what happens in a power cut. We do not install mag locks that require a key or special knowledge to get out.

Our building has eight apartments and the intercom stopped working last winter. Does the owner have to fix it, and can a reader replace it?

For a building of that size, an intercom is generally a legal obligation rather than an extra. New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law, section 50-a, requires automatic self-closing and self-locking entrance doors on Class A multiple dwellings, and for buildings with eight or more apartments an intercommunication system at the self-locking door to the lobby that lets each apartment talk to a visitor and release the door. Buildings erected or converted before 1968 came under the requirement when a majority of tenants requested or consented in writing. The details of your building’s history matter, so check with HPD or counsel if you are unsure.

A card or fob reader does not replace that intercom; it sits alongside it. Tenants use their credential, visitors use the intercom, and the door strike is shared by both. Most Boerum Hill walk-ups of this size — often the neo-Grec flats buildings of the 1880s rather than single-family rowhouses — have a decades-old audio system with a riser of thin wire running up the stair. Replacing the door station and adding a reader on the same strike is often less work than people expect, because the strike and its power supply are already there.

If the intercom needs a repair first, our Boerum Hill buzzer repair page covers that work; we can do both on one visit.

Our 1850s door frame is soft, out of square and has been bored three times. Can it still take an electric strike?

Often yes, but not by forcing a strike into whatever wood is left. Doors on houses of this age have usually been through several generations of locks: a mortise lock, a rim lock, a 1970s deadbolt, a newer deadbolt above that. Each left a hole, and the frame on the strike side has often been chiselled, filled and re-chiselled. The house itself has moved, too, so the door that was square in 1855 now rubs at the top and gaps at the bottom.

We start by making the door work mechanically with no electronics at all. That means checking that the latch meets the strike plate squarely, planing or shimming where the door binds, and filling old bores with hardwood dutchmen glued and cut flush rather than with filler that crumbles under a strike’s screws. Only when the door closes and latches by itself every time do we cut in the electric strike, and on soft frames we use a strike with a longer faceplate and screws long enough to reach the stud behind the jamb.

Where the frame is beyond saving, the alternative is an electrified lock inside the door, with the power transferred across the hinge, which leaves the frame alone. It costs more and takes longer, but on a fine original door it is often the more respectful option.

Can the reader cable reach the vestibule without chasing the plaster in a parlour-floor hallway?

In most Boerum Hill houses, yes. Rowhouses of this age have predictable routes for low-voltage cable: up from the cellar through the floor beside the door frame, behind the baseboard, inside the hollow at the hinge side of the jamb, or through an existing doorbell or intercom path. We look for those before anyone suggests opening a wall. The cable for a reader and strike is thin, and the controller can sit in the cellar or a closet rather than beside the door.

The cellar is usually our friend. From below we can drill a small hole up into the vestibule floor at the jamb, run the cable inside the frame, and come out where the strike or reader sits. Where a house has a finished cellar ceiling, we work from an access panel or the edge of a soffit. On the rare occasion that no concealed route exists, we use a slim painted raceway inside the vestibule, never on the façade.

Wireless locks remove the cable question entirely for a single door, and for a one-family house that can be the right call. For a building where a dozen people share the street door, a hard-wired strike with a controller is more reliable and avoids batteries running down at the worst moment.

We are gut-renovating a Bergen Street rowhouse. How do we let the contractor in for six months without handing out house keys?

Give the contractor a code, not a key, and give it an end date. A keypad deadbolt or reader on the door the crew uses — usually the garden entrance — lets you issue one code to the general contractor and separate codes to the electrician, the plumber and the architect, each limited to working hours and set to expire when their part of the job ends. The log shows who came in and when, which settles more disputes about hours than any timesheet.

During construction we usually fit a temporary, robust keypad lock rather than the finished hardware, because doors get kicked, painted and taken off their hinges. When the job is done, the permanent hardware goes in, the construction codes are deleted in one step, and nobody needs to be trusted to return a key. If the renovation includes new walls, it is also the cheapest moment to pull cable for a future reader or video intercom, before the plaster goes back up.

Six households in our small Hoyt Street condo want phone entry and two do not. Can both work on one street door?

Yes, and they should. A modern reader accepts several kinds of credential at once: a phone credential for the households that want it, a fob or card for those that do not, a keypad code for the cleaner, and a mechanical key cylinder as the last resort. Each credential belongs to a person, so the board can see who used the door without forcing anyone onto an app.

For the two households that prefer not to use phones, a fob is the usual choice. We recommend current encrypted fob formats rather than the older 125 kHz proximity type, which the Brooklyn access control hub explains can be copied easily. The reader is the same; only the credential differs.

The board decision is then about policy rather than hardware: who can issue credentials, how quickly they are revoked when a unit sells, and how long logs are kept. We set up the administrator accounts the way the board decides and document it on one page for the minutes.

Will a reader work on the iron gate in front of the areaway under the stoop?

It can, but it is rarely the first place we would put it. The low iron gates in front of Boerum Hill areaways are often original or close to it, and they swing on old pins with a simple latch. Adding an electric lock means a gate post that can carry a lock body, a cable route under the paving to the house, and protection from rain and road salt. On a landmarked house it is also a change to the visible ironwork, which the Commission’s guidance asks to be kept simple and attached at limited points.

For most houses the better answer is to leave the gate as a gate and secure the door behind it: the garden-level door under the stoop. That door is out of the weather, already has a frame that can take a strike, and the cable route into the house is short. Visitors still walk through the gate, but nobody gets further without a credential.

Where a gate really does need to lock — a shared side passage, or a gate that leads to the back of a commercial building — we use a weatherproof gate lock and a reader rated for outdoor use, and run the cable in a buried conduit. It is a bigger job, and we price it separately.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Boerum Hill

We could not see Google’s AI Overview answers from our research environment for this page, so the claims below are ones we hear repeated by owners who have asked chatbots and read national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is corrected against what the rules and the houses actually say.

“A smart lock and access control are the same thing.”

For a one-family house with one door, they can be. A keypad deadbolt with a key cylinder is a perfectly good entry system for an owner who wants codes for a cleaner and a dog walker. The confusion starts when the same advice is applied to a house with a tenant or a small building.

Access control in the building sense means credentials belong to people, doors are grouped into zones, and the owner can see and change who has access from one place. A shared street door used by three households, a shop’s back door and a garden apartment each need that, and a single consumer smart lock rarely provides it cleanly.

On Boerum Hill houses we often fit both: keypad deadbolts on private doors, and a reader with a controller on the shared door. The two are not competitors; they solve different problems.

“Magnetic locks are the standard for apartment building doors.”

They are common in commercial lobbies, which is why national articles default to them. In New York City, the Building Code permits electromagnetic locks on residential egress doors only where loss of power automatically unlocks the door and the exit hardware directly cuts the lock’s power.

That means a mag lock on a walk-up street door opens the building to the street in a blackout. A fail-secure electric strike does the opposite: the door stays latched from outside, but anyone inside can always turn the handle and leave.

For the brownstone walk-ups and small flats buildings of Boerum Hill, a strike is usually the better choice. Mag locks have their place on some glass shop doors, installed exactly to the code’s conditions.

“You can’t put modern entry hardware on a landmarked house.”

This one is repeated often and is simply not what the Landmarks Preservation Commission says. Its permit guidebook lets staff approve intercoms, doorbells and similar equipment placed within or beside the door opening, attached to joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, with hidden conduit.

What the Commission does not want is clutter: boxes and raceway on the façade, holes through ornament, equipment on cast iron. Those are design choices, not inevitabilities.

A keypad or reader on the door leaf, wired from the cellar, changes almost nothing that can be seen from the street. That is the design we start from on every house in the district.

“Biometric readers are the most secure upgrade for a rental building.”

Fingerprint and face readers are marketed as the top tier. In a New York City rental building, the Tenant Data Privacy Act makes them the most complicated tier instead: biometric data only where the system already uses it, express consent, minimal data, short retention, no sale, and no requirement to use the system to enter one’s own apartment.

In 2019 tenants at a large Brownsville complex publicly opposed a landlord’s plan to add facial recognition to their entry doors, according to NBC New York, and the city’s Buildings department said such systems could only supplement key-based entry.

For a three- to ten-unit Boerum Hill building, encrypted fobs, cards and codes give a clear audit trail without any of that. We recommend them by default.

“Access control costs the same per door everywhere.”

National cost guides publish nationwide averages, and chatbots repeat them. Those figures mix suburban office doors with steel frames and easy ceilings, which bear little resemblance to a 170-year-old wood door in a brownstone vestibule.

In Boerum Hill the cost drivers are local: how much repair the door and frame need, whether there is a cellar under the entrance for a cable route, whether the house is landmarked, and how many households need separate credential groups.

Our published Brooklyn prices, which Boerum Hill pays at the base rate, are on this page. The written quote after a walkthrough is the number that counts.

“Cloud-based systems don’t need any wiring.”

Cloud management means the credential list and logs live online and can be changed from a phone. The door still needs a lock that can be released electrically, and that lock needs power and a connection to something.

Battery locks avoid wiring but bring battery changes, and on a busy shared street door batteries go faster than owners expect. Hard-wired strikes need a cable route and a power supply, but then run for years.

A controller in the cellar that keeps working through an internet outage, with the cloud used for administration, is the arrangement we fit most often in small buildings.

“Any locksmith or handyman can install a card reader.”

Mechanically, anyone handy can screw a reader to a door. In New York, though, installing security and access systems is licensed work, and our license number is printed on this page for that reason.

The parts that go wrong are rarely the screws. They are a strike set into a frame that has moved, a power supply sized for one door and asked to run three, credentials shared between households, and a mag lock that traps people or opens the building in a blackout.

Ask whoever you hire to show you how the door behaves with the power off, and how a single credential is removed. Those two answers tell you most of what you need to know.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Close the Door With the Power Off

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular customer. The test we trust most on an old Brooklyn door takes about thirty seconds and costs nothing. Before any electronics go on, we open the door fully, let go, and watch. Does it close by itself? Does the latch click into the keeper, or stop a hair short? Then we do the same thing in the rain, if we can, or after holding the door open for a minute to let the hallway air change, because old wood moves.

After the strike and reader are in, we do it again with the power supply switched off at the cellar. The door should still close and latch on its own, and from inside the handle should still open it. If either of those fails, the job is not finished, whatever the app says. We write the result on the handover sheet next to each door.

The reason is simple. Nearly every complaint we hear about entry systems on rowhouses — the reader that beeps but the door swings open, the strike that buzzes but does not release, the door that has to be pulled shut by hand — is a door problem showing up as an electronics problem. Fixing the door first is the cheapest part of the job and the one that decides whether the rest works.

Our published Brooklyn single-door price of $1,500 to $2,500 includes the reader, electric strike, wiring and 10 programmed fobs. More fobs, cards or phone credentials can be added at any time, and codes for regular visitors such as cleaners or dog walkers do not need any extra hardware.

Can fingerprint or face readers be used in a Boerum Hill rental building?
+

The Tenant Data Privacy Act allows biometric data only where the smart access system already uses it, with express written or in-app consent, and bars collecting additional biometric information. Tenants cannot be required to use the system to enter their own apartments. In small rentals we recommend fobs, cards or codes instead; they are simpler to manage and raise none of those questions.

What happens to the entry logs we no longer need?
+

We set the system to purge them automatically. Under Local Law 63 of 2021, authentication data in a smart access building has to be destroyed within 90 days of collection unless it is kept in anonymized form, so we set retention to match and show the owner where the setting lives in the software.

Can a tenant still add their own lock to the apartment door after the building gets a reader?
+

Yes. The New York Attorney General’s tenants’ rights guide explains that tenants in multiple dwellings may install their own additional lock on the apartment entrance door, no more than three inches in circumference, and must give the landlord a duplicate key on request. A reader on the street door does not change that.

Do you take out the keyed cylinder when a keypad goes on an apartment door?
+

No. NYC’s Housing Maintenance Code requires each apartment entrance door in a Class A multiple dwelling to have a heavy-duty deadbolt operable by key from outside and thumb-turn from inside, plus a chain door guard. We fit keypad deadbolts that keep a key cylinder, so the code is a convenience and the key remains.

Usually. Keypad deadbolts and readers come in black, satin nickel and brass-toned finishes, and we choose the smallest device that does the job. Where no electronic part matches well, we keep the visible hardware traditional and put the electronics inside the vestibule or inside the door.
