The County

Why a Rockland Condo Camera Plan Starts With the Loop, the River and the Resale Calendar

Rockland is compact. Wikipedia's county entry gives it 199.34 square miles and calls it the smallest county by area in New York outside the city, split into five towns (Clarkstown, Haverstraw, Orangetown, Ramapo and Stony Point) with eighteen villages inside them and the county seat at New City. A crew can cross it in under an hour, which matters less to a camera plan than what the condo stock looks like once you get there. And that stock is not one thing.

When we pull the active condo listings, the pattern is easy to read. Redfin showed 177 condos for sale in the county in late November 2026, spread across Pomona, Valley Cottage, Spring Valley, Nanuet, Suffern, New City, Tappan, West Nyack, Congers, Haverstraw, Piermont, Stony Point, Pearl River, Monsey and Bardonia. The listing notes keep repeating the same amenities: a community pool, a clubhouse, tennis courts, a gated entrance, garage parking, age-restricted living. Read as a camera designer would read them, those are the places where a community's shared life happens, and so they are where a board's money should go first.

Three shapes of community account for most of the condo security camera installation work we see in Rockland County. The most common is the townhouse loop: two-story attached homes along a private road that circles back on itself, with the pool, courts and a small clubhouse on an island of lawn in the middle. The second is the riverfront building in one of the Hudson villages, an elevator condo with a lobby, a garage and a view, often one of several buildings put up in phases. The third is the age-restricted community, which borrows the loop layout but adds a busier clubhouse and residents who expect to be consulted about every camera.

Turnover is the thread that runs through all three. OneKey MLS counted 543 Rockland condo sales in the twelve months through October 2026, a median twelve-month price of $475,000 and a median of 41 days on market, while condo inventory that October stood at 193 units, up 69.3 percent from 114 a year earlier. More listings mean more showings, more closings and more new owners asking how the cameras work. A camera system that depends on one volunteer remembering one password does not survive that pace, so we design the logins, the camera map and the footage policy to be handed over cleanly every time a unit changes hands. Rental buildings with a single landlord have different obligations and a different buyer, and they are covered on our page about apartment building security camera installation in Rockland County.

Community Types

Six Kinds of Rockland Condo Community and Where Each One Spends Its Camera Budget

No agency sorts condos this way; the six labels below are simply how our crews describe what they find on Rockland walk-throughs, and some communities fit two at once. The label matters because it predicts which part of the property will absorb most of the budget.

Townhouse loop with pool and courts

The classic Clarkstown and Ramapo layout: attached two-story homes with their own front doors, a private road that loops, guest parking pockets and an amenity island. Most of the spend lands at the loop entrance, the pool gate and the clubhouse door, plus whatever link carries those far cameras back to a recorder.

Phased riverfront elevator building

Nyack, Piermont and Haverstraw have condo buildings with lobbies, garages and river-facing amenities, often one of several buildings delivered over years. The plan concentrates on the lobby, the garage ramp, elevators and the parcel area, and it has to leave room for buildings that are not finished yet.

55-plus community

Stony Point, Pearl River and parts of Clarkstown have age-restricted condos with a clubhouse that functions as the community's living room. Coverage sits at doors, the lobby and the fitness floor rather than in the rooms where residents gather, and the board spends real time on the written policy.

Garden complex off a main road

Two- and three-story buildings from the 1970s and 1980s around open parking, many near Route 59 and the old village centers. The work goes into parking rows, building entries, laundry rooms and dumpster areas, and into replacing whatever the last board bought.

Gated community

A handful of Rockland developments, including listings in Nanuet, advertise a gated entrance. The gate needs a camera that sees plates and one that sees the whole approach, and those two jobs are kept on separate cameras.

Small village condo near a station or a main street

A dozen or two units in Suffern, Pearl River, Nanuet or Spring Valley, close to the Port Jervis or Pascack Valley line. Four to eight cameras usually cover the entry, the mail spot, the lot and the rear door, and the starting package is close to the whole job.

The same $1,099 four-camera starting system can be nearly the entire project for an eight-unit building in Pearl River and a first phase for a two-hundred-home loop in New City. What scales a Rockland budget is seldom the camera hardware. It is how many separate places the board wants recorded, how far apart those places are, and whether a link has to cross a lawn, a parking lot or a town road to reach the recorder.

The other early question is who will actually use the system. A riverfront building with a part-time superintendent needs alerts sent to someone off site. A townhouse HOA run by volunteers needs playback that a treasurer can work from a phone on a Sunday night. A 55-plus board wants to see exactly what each camera shows before anyone drills a hole. We build the plan around those people, not around a camera count.

Townhouse Loops

Covering a Townhouse Loop: One Way In, an Amenity Island, and Fifty Front Doors

A loop layout is generous to a camera designer, because traffic has fewer choices. If every car arrives through one or two openings off the town road, a well-placed pair of cameras at each opening records nearly everyone who enters the community. That is why we start a loop plan at the mouth of the road, not at the pool, even though the pool is usually what prompted the call.

The mouth of the loop

At the entrance we separate two jobs. One camera, mounted low and close to where cars slow for the turn, is aimed and zoomed to read plates and see a driver's side window. A second, wider camera mounted higher shows the approach, both lanes and the sidewalk, so the board can tell what happened even when the plate camera only captured a bumper. Where the entrance sign or a light pole sits on association land, it often makes a good mount; where it doesn't, a short pole goes on the plan with its own price.

The amenity island

Pool, courts and clubhouse usually share one patch of lawn in the middle of the loop, and that is convenient: one weatherproof cabinet at the clubhouse can serve all three. Cameras cover the pool gate, the deck in a wide view, the court gate and the clubhouse door. The courts themselves rarely need a dedicated camera unless a board is dealing with damage to nets or lights after hours.

Guest parking pockets

Loops tend to have small clusters of guest spaces tucked between buildings. These are where cars are rummaged and where a dent in a parked car turns into a dispute between neighbors. A camera on the end wall of the nearest building, looking along the row rather than across it, gives a usable view of anyone walking between the cars. We note on the plan whose wall it is and get the board's written direction before anything is mounted on an owner's end unit.

The backs of the buildings

Rear patios and decks belong to individual owners in many Rockland declarations, or at least are reserved for their use. We generally leave the backs of buildings alone unless there is a shared path, a wooded edge where people cut through, or a utility area. When a rear camera is warranted, its view is masked so that private patios and windows don't appear in the recording at all.

The front doors of individual homes are the last piece, and the most argued about. A loop with fifty front doors cannot put a camera on each one, and shouldn't try; that coverage belongs to owners who choose their own doorbell cameras under the board's rules. The association's cameras handle the shared spaces: the road, the island, the parking and the entrance. Setting that line in writing early keeps the plan affordable and keeps owners from feeling watched on their own steps.

River Villages

Riverfront Buildings in Nyack, Piermont and Haverstraw That Arrive One Phase at a Time

The river villages produce a different kind of condo job. Hudson Valley Magazine's 2019 profile of one Haverstraw waterfront development described a first phase of two buildings and sixteen units, with the final phases planned to bring the total to 40 units in five buildings, and amenities that included two swimming pools, a fitness center, an indoor basketball court and a café. We are not naming that property and we don't work from its plans here, but the shape is typical of riverfront condos in Rockland: buildings handed over in stages, amenities that open before the last building does, and a board that inherits whatever the developer installed.

Sizing the first recorder for the last building

When a community will grow, the recorder decision made in phase one shapes the next decade. A recorder with spare channels and drive bays, placed in a room that can reach the future buildings by fiber or conduit, lets phase two add cameras without a second system and a second app. We put the expected final camera count in the proposal next to today's count, so the board and the developer both see the headroom they are paying for.

The lobby, the garage and the river side

Inside, coverage follows people: the main entry and vestibule, the lobby, the parcel area, each elevator lobby, the garage entrance and ramp, garage stair doors and the trash room. Outside, the river side is usually a lawn, a terrace or a walkway, and owners on that side are protective of their view and their balconies. River-side cameras are aimed along the walkway and the building line, never up at the balconies, and the parts of the picture that would show a window are blacked out in the recorder settings.

When the developer still holds the keys

During the transition years, the sponsor may still control the board, still own unsold units and still employ the original contractors. That is a reasonable time to put cameras in, provided the association ends up owning the recorder, holding the administrator login and receiving the camera map. We address the proposal to the association, list the sponsor as a party where the board wants it, and hand every credential to the people the board names, not to the sales office.

The river also sets the location of the equipment. A garage level or a basement utility room is the obvious place for a recorder in a riverfront building and the worst place for it during a tidal flood. The weather and power section below covers how we pick the room. In short: as high as the cable paths allow, in a locked space, on a battery, and never on the floor.

Turnover

Fast Resales and Camera Access: Keeping Logins, Maps and Policy Intact as Owners Change

With hundreds of Rockland condo closings a year and inventory well above last year's level, a typical board sees units change hands every few weeks, and its own members change too. Each sale is a small security event. A seller who was treasurer may still have the camera app on a phone. A buyer may arrive asking what the cameras cover and who can see the footage. An agent may want to photograph the clubhouse for a listing and catch a camera in the shot. None of this is dramatic, but over a few years it is how associations lose control of systems they paid for.

Logins tied to roles, not to people

We set up the recorder with one administrator account owned by the association, recorded in the board's files, and separate named accounts for each person who needs access, each with only the permissions that role requires: view only for most directors, playback and export for one or two officers and the managing agent. When a director sells and leaves the board, removing one named account takes a minute and touches nobody else. Shared passwords written on a card in the clubhouse office are the thing we replace most often.

The camera map as a closing document

At handover the board receives a numbered camera map and a still image from each camera showing exactly what it records and what is masked out. That pair answers most buyer questions without anyone opening the recorder. Boards often attach the map and the footage policy to the documents a new owner receives, so a buyer knows before moving in that the pool gate is recorded, the private patios are not, and footage requests go through the managing agent.

What is never shared

Recorded video stays with the association. Prospective buyers, their agents and inspectors may be told where the cameras are and how long footage is kept; they do not get access to recordings, and they do not get logins. A showing is not a reason to give anyone a view of the live cameras, however harmless the request sounds.

There is a practical side benefit to all this. A community that can show a buyer a tidy camera map, a written policy and a recorder that is clearly maintained looks well run, and a well-run association is easier to sell into. We don't claim cameras raise prices, and we have no data that says so. We do see buyers' agents ask about security, and a clear answer reflects well on the board.

Between Buildings

Trenching, Wireless Bridges and Old Conduit: Connecting the Far Corners of a Rockland Site

The single most variable line in a Rockland condo camera proposal is the link between buildings. A camera on the clubhouse wall thirty feet from the recorder costs little beyond the hardware. The same camera at the pool gate, three hundred feet across a lawn and a parking lot, needs a way to get power and video back, and the cost of that path can exceed the cost of the camera several times over.

The distance limit that drives everything

Network cameras normally take power and send video over one copper network cable, and that cable is reliable up to about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from the switch. Past that, both the signal and the power fall off. On a loop community, the clubhouse, the entrance and the farthest guest lot can all sit beyond that limit from wherever the recorder lives, so the plan needs either an intermediate switch or a different kind of link.

Three ways across the property

Trenching is the most durable: conduit buried between buildings, carrying fiber or copper, protected from mowers, frost and animals. It is also the most disruptive, because lawn, curbing or pavement has to be cut and restored. A wireless point-to-point bridge, a pair of small radios aimed at each other from building to building, avoids digging entirely and works well where there is a clear line of sight and both ends have power. The third option is the one boards forget: conduit the developer already buried for future use. Many Rockland communities built since the 1990s have spare pipes between buildings or out to the entrance sign, and testing whether one is open can save a trench.

Before anyone digs

Any excavation in New York requires a call to the one-call center first. Under the state's Code Rule 753, as Dig Safely New York explains it, the notice "shall be served at least two but not more than ten working days" before digging, not counting the day of the call, so utilities can mark their lines. That marks gas, electric and phone. It does not mark private lines the association owns, such as irrigation, site lighting or the cable to the entrance sign, and those are found from the community's own drawings and the maintenance contractor's memory. Trenching is priced and scheduled only after both are done.

Power at the far end

A remote switch cabinet or a wireless radio needs electricity. If a light pole or the pool house already has a circuit, a licensed electrician can add a protected outlet there. We don't do electrical work. Rockland's county code treats wiring for light, heat or power as electrical contracting under a master electrician (Rockland County Code ch. 250), so that outlet is the association's electrician's job, and we coordinate the location and timing with them.

Every far link appears on its own line in our proposal, with its distance, its method and its price, so the board can see what the distant cameras really cost and decide whether to include them now or in a later phase. Trenches are restored to grass or patched pavement, and radios are mounted on building structure rather than gutters.

Floods & Outages

River Flooding, Summer Storms and Day-Long Outages on Rockland Condo Grounds

Rockland's weather problems are well documented, and both of them reach condo camera systems. The first is the river. The second is power.

Tidal flooding in the river villages

Rockland Daily reported in January 2024 that "the Village of Piermont was hit hard by tidal flooding from Friday night's storm," its worst since Hurricane Sandy. Writing after Sandy in 2012, the Rockland County Times said that "Rockland residents living near the Hudson River got the worst of Sandy." For a riverfront condo, those reports translate into a short list of rules: the recorder and the main switch go above any flood line the building has seen, not in the garage or the basement; outdoor connections are sealed and drip-looped; and garage cameras are mounted high on walls or ceilings, with their cable entering from above.

Outages that last more than a night

Inland communities face a different risk. Orange & Rockland Utilities said storms beginning July 1, 2026 affected more than 47,000 of its customers, and the Rockland County Times counted 6,466 Rockland customers still in the dark on the night of July 4, with one New City neighborhood without power for over 36 hours. No battery keeps a camera system running for a day and a half. What a properly sized battery backup does is ride out short interruptions without a reboot, then shut the recorder down cleanly in a long one so the drive and its footage survive, and restart everything on its own when power returns.

Generators, where they exist

Some clubhouses and a few riverfront buildings have standby generators. Putting the recorder and camera switches on a generator-backed circuit, with the battery bridging the seconds before the generator takes the load, is the best outage protection a condo camera system can have. Connecting equipment to a generator circuit is electrical work for the association's electrician; our part is to tell them exactly which equipment needs it and how much power it draws.

Surges on long runs

Copper cable running between buildings can pick up surges during summer storms and deliver them to the switch and the recorder. Every cable that leaves a building gets surge protection at both ends and a proper ground, and where two buildings are on separate electric services we lean toward fiber, which carries no current at all, for the link between them.

After any major storm we suggest the board or manager open the app the next morning and look at every camera tile. A dark tile after a storm is usually a tripped outlet, a radio that lost aim or a switch port that took a surge. Catching it in a day keeps the gap in the footage to a day.

Police Requests

Rockland Police Camera Registration and What It Means for a Condo Association

Rockland law enforcement has encouraged private camera owners to let police know where cameras are. News 12 reported on Rockland County Sheriff Lou Falco's Project Community Eyes, a database of private camera locations intended to show detectives "precisely where to knock after a crime or terrorist incident occurs," and on Clarkstown police asking residents and businesses to register their systems. As those reports describe it, registration is a list of locations; it does not give police a live feed.

For a condominium, registering is a decision for the board, because the cameras belong to the association. In practice it means providing the community's address and a general description of what the cameras cover, such as the entrance, the clubhouse, the pool and the parking areas, along with a contact who can check for footage when asked.

Registration doesn't change who releases video. The association still decides, under its written policy, and calls its counsel when a request is unusual. And registration only helps if the footage still exists when the detective calls. A recorder that keeps ten days of video is no use to an investigation that starts three weeks later, which is one reason we size storage to the policy rather than to the cheapest drive.

Signing up is the board's call, and the board does it directly; we never enroll a community ourselves and have no video-sharing agreement with any department. The camera list and coverage notes in the handover binder contain everything a registration form asks for.

Equipment

Condo Security Camera Systems Built for Rockland Loops and River Buildings

The parts list for a Rockland community is ordinary. What is specific is how the pieces are matched to a loop road, an amenity island or a riverfront garage.

Cameras

Turret cameras are our default under eaves and on clubhouse walls: they hold aim, shed rain and don't collect webs the way some domes do. Vandal-rated domes go where someone could reach them, such as garage ceilings and laundry rooms. Bullets with longer lenses watch the loop entrance and long parking rows. Dual-lens panoramic cameras cover a wide lawn or a pool deck from one corner, and full-color cameras with a soft white light help where the board wants color at night and the lighting is poor.

Recorder and storage

One network video recorder holds the community's footage in a locked room. We size its drives from the number of cameras, their resolution and the days of retention the board's policy calls for, then state that number of days in the proposal. For phased communities we choose a recorder with channels to spare for the buildings still coming.

Network and power

Cameras run on their own network, kept apart from the clubhouse Wi-Fi and the management office computers, and take power through the same cable from a PoE switch. Switches and the recorder sit on battery backup. Links between buildings use fiber, buried cable or point-to-point wireless, each chosen per link.

Detection that respects the woods

Much of Rockland backs onto woods, parkland or wetland, and deer, foxes and wind-blown branches would bury a board in alerts if detection weren't tuned. We draw detection zones around the places that matter, the pool gate after hours or the loop entrance at night, and set the cameras to classify people and vehicles so that animals and moving leaves are recorded but don't trigger messages.

Privacy & the Law

New York State Rules Behind a Rockland Condo Camera Plan

We install cameras; we are not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. A board with a legal question about its own situation should put it to the association's counsel. What we can do is point to the published state rules that shape how we install, and clear up one frequent confusion: New York City's local laws do not follow city commuters across the Tappan Zee, now the Mario M. Cuomo Bridge.

The board's authority

The state Condominium Act lists entrances and exits, recreational and community facilities, parking areas and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" among a condominium's common elements (Real Property Law §339-e), and it requires the bylaws to address the operation and maintenance of the property and the reserves kept for repairs and improvements (§339-v). The declaration and bylaws are therefore the first documents to read, because they say who controls the common areas where cameras go and how spending is approved. A Rockland HOA that is not a condominium works from its own declaration of covenants instead.

Video, not sound

Penal Law §250.05 makes eavesdropping a felony, and §250.00 defines it to include the "mechanical overhearing of a conversation" without the consent of at least one party. Plenty of cameras arrive with a microphone switched on. We turn audio off on every common-area camera and note that on the camera list the board receives.

Rooms that are off limits

Penal Law §250.45 names bedrooms, changing rooms, fitting rooms, restrooms, toilets, bathrooms, washrooms and showers among the places where recording a person without consent can be unlawful surveillance, and General Business Law §395-b separately bars hidden viewing devices in restrooms, showers and fitting rooms. Pool changing rooms, clubhouse restrooms and locker areas get no cameras. A camera may watch the door to those rooms from outside, never the inside.

Units, patios and balconies

A 2025 CooperatorNews legal column observed that the law gives the interior of a neighbor's unit "considerable privacy safeguards," and that a camera that "peers into a neighboring unit" can intrude on them. In a townhouse loop or a riverfront building, windows, balconies and private patios are almost always somewhere in a wide view. We aim to keep them out and use privacy masking in the recorder to black out whatever remains.

Owners' own cameras

Owners of attached homes often want a doorbell camera, and that is their own device, governed by the board's alteration rules. CooperatorNews pointed out that condominium documents usually stop owners from changing common elements unilaterally and that getting the board's consent first is the prudent route. A short house rule on where owner doorbells may go and which way they may face settles most disputes in advance. The association's recorder never ties in to those personal devices.

Who may install, and who may wire power

New York licenses security and fire alarm installers through the Department of State (General Business Law §69-m), and its rules at 19 NYCRR Part 195 count closed-circuit video used to detect or monitor intrusion as security work. Ours is license #12000287431. Line-voltage wiring is a separate trade under Rockland County Code ch. 250, which is why outlets and circuits for cameras are done by a licensed electrician hired by the association.

City rules that stay in the city

Footage Policy

A Written Footage Policy for a Rockland Condominium, Adopted Before the Cameras Go Live

Cameras rarely start arguments. Footage does. Who may watch it, who may copy it, how long it lasts and what happens when an owner asks for a clip of their neighbor are the questions that divide boards, and they are far easier to settle on paper before the first incident than in the middle of one.

What the policy covers

  • How long video is kept. FirstService Residential notes that condo footage is generally stored for 30 to 90 days, depending on building size. In communities where incidents are often noticed late, such as a dent found after a vacation, the longer end is worth the extra drive space.
  • Who may view. Typically the managing agent and one or two named officers; a superintendent may have live view of the lobby and garage only.
  • Who may export. One or two named people, so every copy of a clip can be traced to the person who made it.
  • Owner requests. An owner who wants to see a clip asks the managing agent in writing. FirstService Residential recommends that residents get footage only with the board's and counsel's approval, and that someone log every request and every person who opens the recordings.
  • Police, insurers and attorneys. One named person answers them, the relevant clip is exported the same day so the recorder cannot overwrite it, and anything beyond a routine police request goes to counsel first.
  • Moving cameras. Adding, moving or re-aiming a camera is a board decision, not something an owner arranges with the installer on site.
  • Resales. The camera map and the policy go into the information a new owner receives, and departing owners' app access ends at closing.

Telling owners and renters

Owners should get the camera map and the policy before installation starts. Where the declaration allows rentals, owners who rent their units pass the same information to their tenants, and the board's notice says so. Signs at the entrance and the pool tell visitors that the common areas are recorded.

Master associations and sub-associations

Some larger Rockland developments have a master association that owns the roads, the entrance and the clubhouse, and separate condominiums that own their buildings. Each body sets the policy for its own cameras. The simplest arrangement is one recorder per owner of the ground; the alternative is one shared recorder with each body's cameras visible only to that body's named users.

AI Overview Reality Check

What Generic Answers Miss About Condo Cameras in Rockland County

Ask a chatbot or skim a national cost guide (Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr all publish them) and you get answers built for one house on one lot, or for a Manhattan tower. Rockland condo communities are neither. These are the claims we correct most often when a board brings a printout to the walk-through.

"Figure a few hundred dollars per camera"

Per-camera averages quietly assume the camera is twenty feet from the recorder. On a Clarkstown loop, the camera that matters most, the one at the pool gate or the entrance sign, is often several hundred feet away, and getting video back from there through a trench, a radio pair or a powered cabinet is where the money goes. A camera's price tells a board very little about a far location's price.

So our proposals keep two lists: the cameras, starting from the $1,099 four-camera Rockland package, and the paths that connect them, each with a length and a method. When a competing bid is noticeably cheaper, look first for the missing path. It usually reappears later as a change order.

"Wireless cameras mean no wiring"

A Wi-Fi camera still needs a power source at the spot where it hangs, and its signal still has to find a router. Across a townhouse community the clubhouse router is rarely within reach of the guest lots, and summer foliage in Rockland's wooded settings weakens what little signal there is. Battery cameras trade that problem for another: they wake on motion, miss the seconds before, and need recharging.

Our wireless work uses purpose-built bridges, two radios aimed at each other between buildings and checked for signal margin before we leave. They carry the video of an ordinary wired camera on the far side, which keeps recording continuously to the association's recorder.

"More megapixels will identify anyone"

Megapixels are spread across the whole frame. Aim a 4K camera down a parking row and the person at the far end is still a handful of pixels tall. What identifies people is a camera placed where they come close and pause: the clubhouse door, the parcel shelf, the pool gate latch, the turn into the loop. We decide those spots first and fill the remaining views with wider, cheaper cameras that answer what happened rather than who it was.

"Put it all in the cloud and nothing is ever lost"

The July 2026 storms showed how that fails here: when a New City neighborhood lost power for over 36 hours, its internet went with it. A cloud-only camera records nothing while the connection is down, and twenty cameras uploading at once outstrip the upload speed of a typical clubhouse line anyway. Our house-brand systems store video on the association's own recorder, backed by battery, and remote viewing simply resumes when the internet returns. A board that wants a second copy of important clips can add one off site, and avoids paying monthly per camera for the privilege of storing everything there.

"Sign up with the police and they can watch your cameras"

Location programs such as the one News 12 described for the Rockland Sheriff record where cameras exist so detectives know whom to call. Nothing in that description involves a live connection, and the association keeps the decision about releasing footage. What decides whether a registration ever helps is storage: the recorder has to still hold the video on the day the detective phones.

"The entrance camera will keep outsiders off the property"

It will record them. Keeping them out is a job for a gate, signage and rules the board enforces. Trail walkers crossing the back lawn, commuters parking in guest spaces and drivers tailgating a resident through the loop entrance all appear on camera, and that record is what lets a board see a pattern and decide what to do about it.

"A handy owner could mount these over a weekend"

Hanging a camera is easy. Keeping a thirty-camera community recording through a Piermont flood or a two-day outage, with the right retention, the right permissions and no window in any view, is design work, and New York licenses the people who do it. Before signing with anyone, a board should see a state license number, a numbered site plan and a written promise that the administrator account goes to the association. The systems we are called in to rescue usually failed one of those three tests.

Questions We Hear

Questions Rockland Condo Boards, HOAs and Owners Ask Us

Our townhouse loop has only one road in and out. Can cameras at that entrance cover the whole community?

They can cover most of what boards care about, because every car has to pass them. A plate camera and a wide approach camera at the mouth of the loop record nearly every arrival and departure. What they can't show is what happened once a car or person was inside: who scraped a car in a guest pocket, who left the pool gate open. Most single-entrance loops start with the entrance pair plus the amenity island, then add a camera or two at the busiest parking pocket if incidents point there.

The developer is still building our last two buildings. Can conduit for cameras go in now, before the walls close?

Yes, and it is the cheapest time to do it. Empty conduit from each new building's riser or electrical room toward the recorder, plus pull boxes at the likely camera spots in the lobby and garage, costs little during construction and saves cutting finished walls later. We can mark the locations on the developer's drawings so the electrician or general contractor installs the pipe; we then pull cable and mount cameras when the building is turned over.

Our clubhouse has a standby generator. Will the cameras keep recording when it starts?

They will if the recorder, the camera switches and any wireless radios are on a circuit the generator feeds and a battery backup covers the few seconds before the generator takes over. Without that battery, the switch-over blinks everything off and the recorder reboots, losing a minute or two of video and sometimes the drive's health. Moving equipment onto a generator circuit is the association electrician's work; we supply the list of what needs to be on it and its power draw.

Some owners observe the Sabbath and object to lights that switch on when they walk past. Can the cameras work without motion-triggered lighting?

Yes. Many cameras come with a built-in light or siren tied to motion, and we can turn those features off entirely. The camera then relies on infrared night vision, which is invisible to people, or on existing steady lighting along the walkway. Where the board wants color at night, a fixed light that stays on from dusk to dawn, rather than one triggered by movement, serves the same purpose without reacting to anyone.

Icicles hang from our gutters all winter and block the eave cameras. Where should those cameras go instead?

Move them out from the drip line rather than tucking them tighter under the eave. A short wall arm that puts the lens a foot below the soffit and clear of the gutter edge, or a mount on a gable end without a gutter above, keeps ice and meltwater out of the picture. Where an ice-dam problem is chronic, that tells the board something about the roof; the camera fix is to avoid that stretch of eave.

Children wait for the school bus at our entrance on dark winter mornings. Can a camera cover the stop?

If the stop is on association property or visible from it, the entrance camera can include it, and a full-color camera with steady lighting gives a clearer early-morning picture than infrared alone. If the stop is on the town road outside the entrance, we aim to keep the view on the association's side and include only what is incidentally visible. A camera there records; it doesn't supervise, and the board's notice to parents should say so.

Our 1970s buildings have textured ceilings that may contain asbestos. Can cameras go in without disturbing them?

Usually, by planning around them. We mount on walls, beams or surfaces the board's records show are clear, run cable in surface raceway rather than drilling suspect ceilings, and stop if we find material we can't confirm. If the association has an asbestos survey, we work from it; if a ceiling must be penetrated and its content is unknown, testing by a licensed firm comes first. That keeps the camera project from turning into an abatement project.

Spider webs on our eave cameras set off motion alerts every summer night. What stops it?

A web strand a few inches from the lens catches the camera's own infrared light, glows white in the night picture and sways in the breeze, so the camera reads it as movement. Three things help: switching person and vehicle detection on so a moving strand isn't treated as an event, using the camera's external light or existing lighting instead of the built-in infrared where possible, and choosing turret housings, which give spiders less to anchor to than box-shaped domes. A seasonal wipe of the housing during routine maintenance takes care of the rest.

Our pool sits across a town road from the buildings. Can camera cable or a wireless link cross it?

Cable across or under a public road needs permission from whoever owns the road, which takes time and money most boards would rather avoid. A point-to-point wireless bridge from a building on one side to the pool house on the other usually solves it without touching the road, provided there is line of sight and power at both ends. Where the pool house has its own internet service, a second small recorder there, viewed in the same app, is another option.

Our buildings are on separate electric services. Is copper camera cable between them a problem?

It can be. Buildings fed from separate services can sit at slightly different ground levels, and copper data cable between them gives stray current and storm surges a path into the equipment at both ends. For links between separately powered buildings we prefer fiber, which carries light rather than electricity, or a wireless bridge. Where copper is used, both ends get surge protection and proper grounding.

We just repaved our parking lot and sidewalks. Can cable reach the far lot without cutting the new asphalt?

Often yes. The options are a wireless bridge, an existing conduit if one runs under the paving, routing along the edge of the lot in the lawn strip, or directional boring under the pavement, which avoids an open cut. Each has a different price, and the proposal lists the options side by side. Before the next paving or curb project, adding empty conduit under the new surface is worth asking the paving contractor about.

A seller mounted a doorbell camera on the common wall by their door. What happens to it when the unit sells?

That depends on the board's rules and on whatever approval the seller originally received. If the camera was approved as an owner alteration, the buyer may inherit it under the same conditions; if it wasn't, the board can ask the seller to remove it and patch the wall before closing. It is the owner's device, not the association's, so it isn't part of the association system and we don't connect it. Boards that want a uniform look often adopt a short rule on owner doorbell cameras for exactly this situation.

From the Field

Field Notes From Rockland Condo Camera Walk-Throughs

One pattern shows up in Rockland loops more than anywhere else we work: the developer's phase-one cameras. A handful of cameras went up around the first clubhouse or sales office, wired to a small recorder that nobody on the current board has ever logged in to. The later phases got nothing. The board thinks the community has a camera system; in practice it has four cameras watching a building that is no longer the center of anything. Recovering that recorder, and deciding whether it can grow or must be replaced, is usually the first hour of the visit.

The second pattern belongs to the river villages. After a flood or a long outage, a riverfront building's recorder, mounted in the garage or the basement because that was where the cable ended, has either drowned or died in a power spike when the electricity came back. Moving that equipment upstairs costs a cable pull and an afternoon; replacing it after the next storm costs the recorder and every clip on it.

Last comes the buried-cable mystery. Somewhere on the property a run to the pool house or the entrance sign went into the lawn bare, no pipe around it, and frost, aerators and burrowing animals have chewed through it since. The tile on the screen went black long ago and the board wrote the camera off. Nine times out of ten the camera still works and the wire is the casualty, so our crews put a tester on every old run before a proposal is written.

+

Some cameras can record to a card inside the camera, but for a condo community it is a weak choice: each card holds a few days, anyone who reaches the camera can take the card, and searching many cameras one at a time is slow. A central recorder in a locked room keeps all the footage in one place under the board's control.

We size the battery to ride through common short outages and then shut the recorder down cleanly in a long one, so the drive and its footage are protected.
