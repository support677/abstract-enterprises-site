Why Long Island Properties Need Tapo Camera Installation

This is our Long Island hub page, so it covers both Nassau and Suffolk, and it uses the Nassau Hub around the old Coliseum in Uniondale and East Garden City as its reference point. We chose that anchor because it shows, in a few square miles, nearly every kind of property a Tapo camera ends up on across the island. The Cradle of Aviation Museum's history of Mitchel Field records that the airfield took the Mitchel name in July 1918 and closed in 1961, when the property went to the County of Nassau. Since then the land has filled with the Coliseum at 1255 Hempstead Turnpike, Hofstra University, Nassau Community College, the Mitchel Athletic Complex and the Cradle of Aviation Museum itself, and Wikipedia describes the wider Hub as home to almost half of the county's economic activity. On one side of Hempstead Turnpike are office towers like RXR Plaza and the Omni; on the other, according to Homes.com, are streets of Cape Cods and minimal traditional houses, often with detached garages and private driveways. That mix, big commercial buildings beside modest postwar houses, is Long Island in miniature.

The houses explain most of what a Tapo installer actually does here. NeighborhoodScout reports that single-family detached homes make up 69.74 percent of Uniondale's housing and that 55.45 percent of it was built between the 1940s and the 1960s. A restoration contractor working in the area describes most local homes as Cape Cods and ranch houses from the early 1950s. Head east a few miles and the pattern repeats on a larger scale: Encyclopedia.com records that Levittown began in 1947 on potato fields and by 1951 held some 17,500 single-family homes, and Wikipedia's entry on Levitt & Sons describes them as Cape Cod and ranch-style houses on quarter-acre lots. The Census Bureau's 2023 survey puts Nassau's median home at 1955 and Suffolk's at 1970. None of those houses was planned around a wireless camera. The router lives wherever the cable company first dropped its line, often a basement or a back room, and the spots worth watching are the driveway, the side door, the back gate and the garage at the far end of the lot.

That geometry is why a Tapo box from the store so often disappoints on Long Island. TP-Link's own setup guidance says Tapo cameras join the home's 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi through the Tapo app, and 2.4 GHz travels farther than 5 GHz but still weakens with every wall, window and tree between the router and the camera. A camera on the front of a Cape Cod may sit twenty feet from the router through one interior wall and work perfectly. The same model on a detached garage sixty feet back, with the house's brick or concrete-block foundation and a hedge in the way, may connect during setup and then drop out at night when the neighborhood's Wi-Fi gets busy. A camera that drops out does not record to the cloud, does not send alerts and does not show live view. The honest fix is sometimes a better access point, sometimes a different Tapo model, and sometimes a PoE cable, and the only way to know which is to measure the signal where the camera will actually hang.

The second reason Long Island owners call is what they want the cameras to catch. Police and news reports from the past few years point in three directions. Cars: ABC7 New York reported in January 2024 that a multi-agency task force of Nassau and Suffolk police, the NYPD, State Police and the FBI had formed to fight a wave of burglaries and car thefts, and that police believed the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways. Packages: in November 2025 CBS New York reported that the Suffolk District Attorney had charged 14 men in a package-theft case that reached 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, and involved more than 200 phones and tablets; News12 has shown doorbell video of a porch thief in Massapequa Park; and Patch's Garden City crime log for the week ending September 15, 2026 includes packages taken from a front porch. Small businesses: News12 reported on September 21, 2026 that the owner of Morning Delights in Uniondale received a motion alert from his security camera, went to the restaurant, and an arrest followed. Each of those calls for a different camera in a different place, and a Tapo system only helps if the camera is where the event happens and online when it does.

We will say one thing plainly about that Uniondale story. We have no connection to Morning Delights and we do not know what camera it uses. We mention it because it shows how a cheap camera with a phone alert actually gets used: as an early warning. Our advice to every Long Island owner is the same, though. When an alert shows someone inside your home or business, call 911 and let the police go in; do not go and confront them yourself. A camera's job is to tell you something is wrong and to record a clear picture, and we set up alerts and motion zones with that in mind.

The third reason is weather and power. ABC7 reported that Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 left almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers in the dark at its peak. The New York State Department of Financial Services described the rain of August 18 and 19, 2024 as the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County, with the Mill Pond dam at Stony Brook failing and more than 2,000 residents and business owners reporting flood damage. When the power goes, a plug-in Tapo camera goes with it, and when the cable modem goes, Wi-Fi cameras lose their connection. TP-Link says that once a camera is set up, microSD card recording continues even if it loses its connection to the router, which is a real advantage of the brand, but only if a card is installed, formatted and set to record. A battery Tapo camera keeps running through an outage; a plug-in one does not. We plan each camera's power source around what you want it to see during exactly those nights.

Finally, Long Island has a lot of renters and a lot of rules that differ from the city's. NeighborhoodScout puts Uniondale at about 62 percent owners and 38 percent renters. Nassau is made up, according to ny.gov, of two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets; Suffolk has ten towns. New York City's housing agencies and building department do not reach here at all. What does apply is a mix of state licensing, county consumer-affairs licensing, town and village permit rules, HOA covenants, landlord leases and office-building house rules. Part of a Long Island Tapo job is working out which of those apply to your address before we drill.

We could not view Google's question boxes or AI summaries from our build environment. What owners often add once the cameras are up. After a Long Island family has a doorbell and a driveway camera working, the next question is usually the side door or the gate to the back yard, and for businesses it is who can open the back door after hours. A Tapo camera records who was there; it does not control the door. If you want both, mention it when you book so the cable and network are planned once, instead of a second visit through the same walls.

Tapo Camera Offline, Doorbell Dead or Something Just Happened? Fast Help on Long Island

A camera that was working last week and now shows "offline" in the Tapo app, a doorbell that stopped ringing after a router swap, or a break-in where you need the footage saved before it is overwritten, are the calls we move on first. If something has just happened, call the police before you call us. Then do not reset or unplug the camera that may hold the recording; a microSD card can be overwritten on a loop, so the sooner the clip is saved, the better. Many offline Tapo cameras on Long Island are not broken at all; the network they were set up on changed, and they need to be moved onto the new one.

Long Island's building history runs in waves: the airfields of the First World War, the potato fields that became Levittown after the Second, the postwar suburbs that set Nassau's median home in 1955, the later growth that set Suffolk's in 1970, and the office towers that rose at the Hub in the 1980s and 1990s. Each wave left a property type with its own walls, its own distances and its own spots where Wi-Fi gives out. The Levittown-style slab house

An architect's history of Levittown records that the first ranches were built on concrete slabs, came with an expandable attic, had no garage, and were heated by hot-water radiant pipes in the floor. That last detail matters more than people expect. Nobody should be drilling into a radiant-heated slab to route a camera cable, and we never do. On a slab house, a wired Tapo camera's cable goes up, not down: through the attic, out at the eave, and down to the camera position, or along the outside of the house in a neat, painted raceway. The garage, where there is one, was usually added later, sometimes as a separate structure at the back, and it is often the single hardest point for Wi-Fi to reach. Many of these houses have also been raised, widened or rebuilt so thoroughly that the original plan is hard to see. We look in the attic before we promise a route, because insulation, a finished second floor and later roof work change what is possible.

The 1950s Cape Cod or ranch with a basement

This is the house the Census median describes for Nassau and the one contractors describe for Uniondale: a Cape Cod or ranch from the early 1950s, a basement or partial basement, and a driveway leading to a garage that may be attached or set back. A restoration contractor in the area describes many such homes sitting on concrete-block foundations, with basements finished in the 1960s and 1970s. For a Tapo job, the basement is both the problem and the opportunity. The router is often down there, which buries the Wi-Fi signal below grade and behind block. But a basement also gives us a route: cable can run along the joists to the rim of the house and come out through the block or the sill to a camera on the outside wall. Where the walls are finished, we look for a utility room or an unfinished corner to work from rather than opening a finished ceiling.

The split-level and hi-ranch near Hofstra

Around Hofstra and the Coliseum, one local source describes the homes as mostly split-levels and hi-ranches from the 1950s and 1960s. Those are houses on several levels, with half-flights of stairs between them and the main living space raised over a lower level that is partly below ground. They are good houses for Tapo cameras in one way: the upper level sits higher than a Cape Cod's first floor, so a router placed upstairs can see more of the lot. They are awkward in another: getting a cable from the front of an upper level to the back of a lower one means crossing floors in a house where the floors do not line up. We plan cable routes level by level, and in these houses we often find that moving or adding one access point solves what three cables would otherwise have to.

Suffolk's later houses and bigger lots

Suffolk's median home is fifteen years younger than Nassau's, and the county is more than five times larger, so the lots tend to be bigger and the distances longer. A house on a larger property may have the router at one end, a pool or shed out back and a gate at the bottom of a long driveway. For Tapo cameras that usually means a mix: wired or strong Wi-Fi cameras near the house, and battery cameras with a solar panel for the far corners where no cable can reasonably reach. Battery cameras are not magic; they sleep until motion wakes them, they need light for the panel, and they are only as good as the Wi-Fi at the spot where they hang. We test the signal at the gate before recommending one there, because a battery camera that cannot connect is a camera that silently records nothing.

The Hub's office towers and suites

RXR Plaza, which a listing calls Long Island's largest office building, has two 15-story towers with raised access floors and backup generator support; the Omni, built in 1990, already offers its tenants round-the-clock secure key card access, according to its leasing page. A Tapo camera in a building like that is a tenant-level camera inside a suite: watching a reception desk, a stock room or a back corridor. The building's rules come first. Office landlords usually control what is fixed to their ceilings and walls, who may work in the building and when, and what joins their networks. TP-Link says Tapo cameras do not support WPA or WPA2-Enterprise networks, which is what many corporate networks use, so in an office suite we usually set up a separate network for the cameras with the tenant's IT provider rather than trying to put them on the office one.

Driveway to Back Fence: What to Check on a Long Island Property Before We Arrive

Most of the time lost on a Tapo job is lost in the first hour: a camera that will not pair because the router only offers WPA3, a doorbell with no transformer wire behind it, a garage with no outlet, or a TP-Link account nobody remembers the password to. Walk the property at night

The cameras will spend half their lives in the dark, and that is when they are most likely to matter. Walk the driveway, the side of the house and the back yard after sunset and look at what is lit and what is not. Note where the streetlight falls, where the porch light reaches, and where a motion light already switches on. Tapo cameras have night vision, and some models have a spotlight or color night mode, but every camera does better with some light, and none can see through the glare of a bulb pointed back at it. If there is a spot you want watched that is pitch black, tell us; it may change which model belongs there or where it should hang.

Look at what the camera will see beyond your lot

On most Long Island streets, a camera on the front of the house will see the sidewalk, the street and part of the house across the road. That is normal, but it is worth thinking about before we mount. A camera aimed down a driveway shared with a neighbor, or across a back fence into a neighbor's yard, may cause friction later. Tapo's app lets us draw privacy masks that black out part of the picture, and we would rather agree those with you on the day than have a neighbor ask about them later. If you know where you do not want the camera to look, that is as useful to us as where you do.

Find the TP-Link account details

Every Tapo camera is tied to a TP-Link ID through the Tapo app. If you already have some Tapo devices, smart plugs or cameras, they are on an account somewhere, and the new cameras should join the same one. Find the email address and make sure you can log in before the visit. If the cameras were set up by someone else, a previous owner, a family member or another installer, tell us, because a camera still bound to someone else's account has to be released or reset before it can be added to yours.

Condos, Townhouses and Rentals

Stores, Offices and Flex Space

What a store or office camera has to do

A camera at home is watched now and then by a family that knows the house. Recording is the other business question. Tapo cameras record to their own microSD cards, and TP-Link sells an optional cloud plan. Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • State licensing. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and the work on this page is done under it.
  • County licensing differs for commercial work. Nassau County's Office of Consumer Affairs says it is against the law to run a home improvement business in the county without a license, and its FAQ adds that anyone who works only on commercial buildings does not need that license. Homes and businesses can check a contractor through the Office of Consumer Affairs.
  • Town and village permits. A trade blog on Nassau cabling says New York's Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code has required permits for low-voltage electrical installations since 1984 and that most Nassau towns and villages enforce permit rules. We have not found an official list of which camera jobs need which permit, so we check with the municipality for your address when the scope warrants it.
  • Mains power is an electrician's job. TP-Link's floodlight camera instructions require an all-pole mains switch in the building's wiring. Where a business wants a floodlight camera or a new outlet high on a wall, that circuit work goes to a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Restrooms and changing areas are off limits. Under New York Penal Law section 250.45, recording inside a bathroom, changing room or similar private space without consent is a felony. We do not install cameras in those spaces and we check that no camera's field of view reaches into them.
  • Alarm permits only if you add an alarm. An alarm company's page says Nassau residents and businesses served by the Nassau County Police Department must register alarm systems. A Tapo camera on its own is not an alarm, but if your cameras are tied to an alarm system, that registration may apply.

Tapo Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Cost and planning

The cameras themselves were inexpensive. Why does installing Tapo cameras on a Long Island house cost more than the cameras did?

Because the camera is the smallest part of the job. The work is in finding a spot where each camera can see what matters, stays connected and can be powered, then fixing it securely to an old house, sealing the hole, setting up the network and the app, and testing it at night. On a Long Island lot, the spots people care about, the driveway, the garage, the back gate, are usually the farthest from the router, and that is where the time goes. Our Long Island price page lists every line so you can see where the money is going, and the quote names any access point, cable or electrician work before you agree to it.

How should a Long Island homeowner decide how many Tapo cameras to put up?

Start from the ways into your property, not from a number. On most Nassau and Suffolk houses that means the front door and porch, the driveway, any side or back door, and the garage. A doorbell covers the front step and packages; one camera on the corner of the house can usually cover the driveway and the side door together; and the back yard may need one more. For many Cape Cods and ranches that adds up to three or four cameras. If you have a long driveway, a detached garage or a gate at the bottom of a big Suffolk lot, add one for each. We would rather put up three cameras that stay online and see faces than eight that drop out.

Wi-Fi, range and the detached garage

Our router is in the basement of a 1950s Cape and the garage is at the back of the lot. Will a Tapo camera out there stay connected?

Possibly, but we would not promise it without measuring. A basement router sends its signal through the floor above, the foundation and the outer wall before it even leaves the house, and a detached garage adds distance and its own walls. Tapo cameras usually connect on 2.4 GHz, which reaches farther than 5 GHz but still fades quickly through block and brick. We test at the exact spot first. If the signal is weak, the fixes are usually moving the router upstairs or adding an access point on the garage side of the house, mounting the camera on the house looking at the garage, or running an outdoor-rated cable. We tell you which one we would choose and why.

Which Wi-Fi settings does a Tapo camera need, and will it join our newer mesh system?

TP-Link says most Tapo cameras use 2.4 GHz, with some models, including the Tapo C260, C460 and C840, also able to use 5 GHz. Its support pages say Tapo cameras do not work on WEP, portal-login, WPA or WPA2-Enterprise networks, or on networks set to WPA3 only. Many newer mesh systems combine both bands under one name and default to modern security, which usually works, but some ship set to WPA3 only, and some steer devices between bands in a way cameras dislike. We check the router's settings before setup, and if needed we switch it to a WPA2 and WPA3 mixed mode or create a separate 2.4 GHz network just for the cameras.

If a storm knocks out our internet, do the Tapo cameras keep recording?

The ones that still have power do, if they have a microSD card. TP-Link says that once a camera is set up, SD card recording continues even if it loses its connection to the router. You will not get alerts or live view on your phone until the internet comes back, but the footage is on the card. Power is the bigger issue: when Isaias left almost half of PSEG Long Island's customers in the dark in 2020, every plug-in camera on those houses went dark too. Battery Tapo cameras keep running, and plug-in cameras can be kept alive with a small battery backup on the router and their outlets. We set the cards up and tell you which cameras will survive an outage.

Power and wiring

Our ranch has almost no outdoor outlets. Which Tapo power option makes sense for it?

It depends on the spot. Tapo makes cameras that are wired only, wired with battery backup, and battery only, plus PoE models that take power and data over one network cable. For a front corner near an attic, a PoE or low-voltage wired camera fed from inside is usually the most dependable. For a back gate or fence with no power, a battery camera with a solar panel facing open sky is the practical choice. For a spot near an existing exterior outlet, a plug-in model works. What we avoid is running an indoor extension cord out of a window, or adding outdoor outlets ourselves; new outlets are an electrician's job. Most Long Island houses end up with a mix.

What cable does a wired Tapo PoE camera need outside, and does it have to connect to a recorder?

Tapo's own PoE installation guide says to use Cat5e or Cat6 cable, and that any run exposed to weather needs an outdoor-rated, UV-resistant jacket. Ordinary indoor cable on the outside of a Long Island house breaks down in sun and salt air within a few seasons. We use Cat6 rated for where it runs, seal every entry, and power the camera from a PoE switch or injector inside. Tapo's guide also takes up whether a PoE camera needs a recorder; in our practice a Tapo PoE camera records to its own microSD card where the model supports it, so a separate recorder is not needed for it to work, though some owners add one for longer storage.

Rules, licenses and privacy

Do we need a permit or a licensed installer for Tapo cameras in Nassau or Suffolk?

For most plug-in and battery cameras on a house, a permit is unlikely, but the rules depend on the town or village and on the scope, and we check when the job involves new wiring. Licensing is clearer. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a state license for businesses that install security systems, and AESS works under NYS license #12000287431. Nassau County also requires a home improvement license for contractors working on homes, and says it does not apply to purely commercial work; you can check any contractor with the county's Office of Consumer Affairs. Suffolk has its own county licensing for some trades, which some towns and villages replace with their own.

Can our Tapo doorbell record what people say on the driveway and the sidewalk?

Legally, it is more nuanced than most people think. The Digital Media Law Project describes New York's wiretapping law as one-party consent, meaning a recording is lawful if one person in the conversation agrees to it. A doorbell recording your own conversation with a delivery driver falls into that category. A camera quietly picking up neighbors talking to each other on the sidewalk, where you are not part of the conversation, is a different matter, and it is a reasonable source of friction between neighbors. Our default is to leave audio on for the doorbell and on cameras that face your own door, and to turn audio recording off on cameras that face the street, a shared driveway or a neighbor's yard.

Should we register our cameras with the police, and does that give them access to our video?

Registering is voluntary and does not hand anyone your feed. The Suffolk County Police Department's camera registry asks owners to list where their cameras are, so detectives can ask them to check footage for a date and time after a crime, with retrieval at the owner's convenience. Garden City's police department runs a similar private camera registration and states plainly that it does not give the department access to privately owned systems. We found no county-wide registry for Nassau County police. If you register, keep your microSD cards working and know how to export a clip; we show you how at handover.

TP-Link and security

We keep reading that TP-Link is being banned. Will our Tapo cameras stop working?

As of the latest sources we read, there is no federal ban on using TP-Link or Tapo products. IsMyRouterSafe reported on October 8, 2026 that no federal order bans TP-Link routers and that owners can keep using the ones they have, while a March 2026 FCC rule blocks approval of new foreign-made router models without a conditional approval, which TP-Link does not hold. On cameras, a U.S. senator has asked the Commerce Department to look at TP-Link's internet-connected cameras, and SmartHomePerfected reported that a targeted federal action was shelved in early 2026. That is a live situation, not a settled one. We set Tapo cameras up so that if anything changes, the microSD recording keeps working and the cameras sit on their own network.

How do you keep a Tapo camera from being a way into the rest of our home network?

By giving the cameras their own network. A common piece of advice, repeated in coverage of the TP-Link debate, is to put cameras, smart plugs, TVs and other connected devices on a separate guest network so they cannot reach your computers and phones. Most Long Island routers and mesh systems can do this. We set the cameras on that network, use a strong unique password for your TP-Link account, turn on two-step verification in the app, update the camera firmware, and remove any old shared users you do not recognize. None of that makes any camera perfectly safe, but it limits what a compromised device could reach.

What the cameras should catch

Thieves on Long Island have been targeting cars in driveways. Where should a Tapo camera go to actually help?

ABC7's 2024 report on the Nassau and Suffolk task force said police believed burglars were drawn by cars in driveways, so the driveway is the right place to start. One camera high on the corner of the house, angled down the length of the driveway, catches people walking up to the cars and to the side door. A second, lower camera near the garage or the back of the driveway catches faces at closer range. A wide camera mounted very high sees the whole scene but makes every face tiny, so we balance height against detail. We also aim cameras so passing headlights on the street do not trigger constant false alerts.

Our packages keep disappearing from the front step. Where does the doorbell need to sit to catch it?

Low enough to see the package and high enough to see the face. The package-theft case the Suffolk District Attorney announced in 2025 spanned 31 communities, and a doorbell camera's video is what News12 showed from a Massapequa Park porch. A doorbell mounted too high looks over the box; mounted too low it sees chests and hoods. Many Tapo doorbells have a tall field of view designed to see down to the step, and we test the view with a box on the step before tightening the mount. If the door is recessed or the porch has steps, a wedge or corner bracket can turn the camera toward the walkway.

Weather and the outside of the house

How do outdoor Tapo cameras hold up near the South Shore or the Sound?

They hold up if they are mounted and sealed with the weather in mind. Most outdoor Tapo cameras carry a weatherproof rating, but the bracket, the cable and the hole in the wall are where water gets in. Near the water, salt air also corrodes cheap screws and connectors. We use stainless or coated fixings, mount cameras under an eave or soffit where we can, give every cable a drip loop so water runs off before it reaches the hole, seal every penetration, and use outdoor-rated, UV-resistant cable for anything exposed. After a big storm, check that the cameras still point where they should; a strong gust can turn a loose mount.

Do you set up the Tapo app and accounts, or only mount the cameras?

Both, and we think the setup is half the job. A camera on the wall that is not in your account, has no card formatted, sends an alert every time a car passes and cannot be found by your spouse's phone is not a working camera. We add every camera to your own TP-Link account, not ours, set up the microSD cards, draw motion zones and privacy masks, name each camera so you know which is which, set alert schedules, share access with the family members or staff you choose, and walk you through playback and saving a clip. If you already mounted the cameras yourself and just need them set up properly, that is a job we take too.

What AI Gets Wrong About Tapo Camera Installation in Long Island

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Tapo cameras and about this part of New York, set against what the public record and our field practice say. "TP-Link and Tapo are banned in the United States."

This one spreads quickly because there has been real news. A U.S. senator has asked the Commerce Department to investigate TP-Link's internet-connected cameras, and in March 2026 the FCC adopted a rule blocking approval of new router models made abroad unless they hold a conditional approval.

But as IsMyRouterSafe summarized on October 8, 2026, no federal order bans TP-Link routers, and owners can keep using what they have. The sensible response is preparation rather than panic: microSD recording that works without the cloud, cameras on their own network, and updated firmware. That is how we set them up regardless.

"Tapo cameras work on any home Wi-Fi."

TP-Link's own support pages say otherwise. Most Tapo cameras connect on 2.4 GHz, with a few models such as the C260, C460 and C840 also supporting 5 GHz, and Tapo cameras do not support WEP, portal-login, WPA or WPA2-Enterprise networks, or networks set to WPA3 only.

On Long Island that matters in two places. Newer routers and mesh systems sometimes ship in WPA3-only mode, and office suites at places like the Hub often run enterprise networks that need a username as well as a password.

We check the router before setup. At home that usually means a mixed security mode or a separate 2.4 GHz network; in an office it means a network just for the cameras, set up with the tenant's IT provider.

"Wireless cameras mean no wiring at all."

Wireless describes the video signal, not the power. A plug-in Tapo camera still needs an outlet, a floodlight camera needs mains wiring, and a battery camera needs charging or a solar panel with enough sky.

Many of Long Island's postwar houses have few exterior outlets, and the spots that matter, the garage, the gate, the back fence, often have none. Those are also the spots farthest from the router, where Wi-Fi is weakest.

So a typical Long Island Tapo system mixes power sources: plug-in where there is an outlet, battery and solar at the edges, and PoE cable for the cameras that have to stay online. Pretending it can all be wire-free is how cameras end up dead in a drawer.

"No license or permit is ever needed for cameras on Long Island."

For a homeowner sticking a battery camera on their own porch, that is close to the truth. For a business that installs cameras for others, it is not. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a state license to install security systems.

Nassau County adds a home improvement license for contractors working on homes, while exempting purely commercial work. A trade blog on Nassau cabling says most towns and villages enforce permit rules for low-voltage work, though we found no official list of which camera jobs need one.

Long Island has dozens of villages and towns, each with its own building department. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and check the municipality for your address when a job involves new wiring.

"A floodlight camera is a simple DIY swap for your porch light."

Videos make it look like a ten-minute job. TP-Link's own floodlight instructions say the camera mounts on a junction box on a wall or under an eave, and that an all-pole mains switch must be part of the building's wiring.

That is line-voltage electrical work. On an older Long Island house, the box behind the porch light may be loose, undersized or wired in ways nobody has looked at since the 1950s, and a heavy floodlight on a weak box is a fall waiting to happen.

We plan the camera, its angle and its network; a licensed electrician handles the power. If you would rather avoid that step, a wired or battery camera beside the existing light often does the same job.

"One good camera on the house will cover the whole yard and the garage."

A wide-angle camera does see a lot, but it sees everything small. A camera on the back corner of a Cape Cod looking at a detached garage sixty feet away will show that someone was there; it will rarely show who.

Distance also hurts the connection. The farther a camera is pointed, the more people put it at the far edge of the house, which is usually the far edge of the Wi-Fi too.

We plan coverage by what each camera must identify: a face at the door, a person at the car, a vehicle in the driveway. On Long Island lots that usually means two closer cameras rather than one distant one.

"New York is a two-party consent state, so camera audio is illegal."

We have seen this repeated even on installer pages. The Digital Media Law Project describes New York's wiretapping law as one-party consent: a conversation can be recorded if one participant agrees.

That does not make every microphone a good idea. A camera picking up neighbors' conversations on the sidewalk, where you are not taking part, is a different situation, and New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes recording inside private spaces like bathrooms and bedrooms without consent a felony.

Our practice is simple: audio on for the doorbell and cameras facing your own entry, audio off for cameras facing the street, a shared driveway or a neighbor's yard, and no cameras in private rooms at all.

FAQ

Long Island Tapo Camera Installation FAQ

Can a SIM card go into a Tapo camera for a spot with no Wi-Fi, like a boat slip or a building site?
+

Most Tapo cameras are Wi-Fi or wired devices with no SIM slot, so a SIM card cannot be used in them; TP-Link addresses this in its own support FAQ. If a model does not list a cellular radio in its specifications, it cannot use a SIM. For a spot with no Wi-Fi, the realistic options are extending the network to it, running a cable, or choosing a camera designed for cellular use, and we will tell you which fits the place you have in mind.

Is it possible to run Tapo cameras without the Tapo app or a TP-Link account?
+

Setting up a Tapo camera goes through the Tapo app and a TP-Link account, which TP-Link covers in its support FAQ, so plan on having one. We create or use an account in your name, never ours, with a strong password and two-step verification, and we leave you the login details. Once a camera is set up, its microSD recording keeps working even if it loses its router connection.

Do Tapo cameras need a monthly subscription to record?
+

No, not for local recording. A Tapo camera with a microSD card records to the card without any subscription, and you can view and play back clips in the app. TP-Link also sells an optional cloud plan for off-site storage and extra features. For many Long Island homes the card is enough; for a business, we usually suggest cloud recording on the one or two cameras that matter most, in case a camera and its card are taken.

How long does a Tapo camera visit usually take at a Long Island house?
+

A doorbell and a couple of Wi-Fi cameras on a house with good signal is usually part of a day, including the Wi-Fi survey and the app setup. A whole-house system with an access point, a PoE cable through the attic or basement, or a camera on a detached garage takes longer. We give you the expected time when we quote, based on the photos and the number of cameras.

How often will a battery Tapo camera need recharging?
+

It depends on how often it wakes. A battery camera on a quiet back gate lasts far longer than one facing a busy sidewalk or a tree that moves in the wind, and cold weather and long recordings drain it faster. A solar panel aimed at open sky can keep many battery cameras topped up. We set motion zones and sensitivity to cut false wake-ups, and we tell you if a spot is so busy that a wired camera would be the better choice.

Do you travel to the North Fork and the South Fork for Tapo installs?
+

Yes, Suffolk addresses all the way out to the East End are covered and priced from the Suffolk County Tapo price list. For seasonal houses we often schedule the install while you are there to approve camera spots, and we set up the app so you can check on the house from wherever you spend the winter. Tell us the town when booking so we can plan the trip.

I rent a house in Nassau. Can I put up Tapo cameras without upsetting the landlord?
+

Usually, if you choose the right models and ask first. Indoor plug-in cameras need no holes at all, and battery doorbells and outdoor battery cameras can often be mounted with adhesive or a few small screws that are easy to fill when you leave. Anything involving cable through a wall, a new outlet or a floodlight needs the landlord's written agreement. Check your lease, and we can give you a short description of the work to send the landlord.

Can a Tapo floodlight camera replace the old light over our garage door?
+

Often, but the electrical part is for a licensed electrician. TP-Link's instructions call for mounting on a junction box and an all-pole mains switch in the building's wiring, and the box over an older garage door may not be suitable as it is. We plan the camera's angle and network and coordinate with the electrician, or suggest a wired or battery camera beside the existing light if you would rather skip the electrical work.

Why do Nassau and Suffolk have their own Tapo price pages?
+

Because our prices follow the area, and the two counties are priced as separate areas, each at its own rate above our city base. Suffolk alone covers more than five times Nassau's area, so travel is a bigger part of an East End visit. Your address decides which page applies, and the quote names the county so there is no confusion.

We bought a house with Tapo cameras still on the walls. Can you move them into our account?
+

Yes. A camera still tied to the previous owner's account has to be removed from that account or reset before it can join yours. If the sellers will remove them from their account, that is easiest; if not, we reset them on site. We then check each camera's condition, mounting and seals, format the cards, and add them to your account. Cameras that are failing or badly placed we will point out honestly.

Do you need our Wi-Fi password, and do you keep it?
+

We need the cameras' network password during setup, because each camera has to join a network to work. Where we set up a separate camera network, you can give us that password and keep your main one private. We do not record your passwords after the visit, and we suggest changing any password you shared once the job is done; the cameras will need to be reconnected only if you change the camera network's password.

Is Tapo good enough for a store, or should a business choose a recorder-based system?
+

For a small store, a single office suite or a front desk, well-placed Tapo cameras on their own network can do the job at a modest cost. For a larger site with many cameras, long retention needs, several managers or plate reading in a parking lot, a business-grade recorder system is usually the better fit. Tell us how many doors, how much floor space and what you need to prove after an incident, and we will give you an honest recommendation.

What do you leave behind so we know how the Tapo system was set up?
+

A written list of every camera: its name in the app, where it is mounted, how it is powered, which network it uses, and where any PoE cable runs and is labelled. We also note the TP-Link account email it is registered to. That list saves time the next time a router is changed, a camera goes offline, or you sell the house and need to hand the system over.
