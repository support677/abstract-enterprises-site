The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need CCTV Installation

Nassau looks like one continuous suburb from the Long Island Expressway, but on paper it is a patchwork. The state lists two cities (Glen Cove and Long Beach), three towns (Hempstead, North Hempstead and Oyster Bay), 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets inside the county line. A house in Levittown is in a hamlet governed by the Town of Hempstead and patrolled by the Nassau County Police Department. A house two miles away in Garden City is in an incorporated village with its own building department, its own code and its own police force. That split matters more to a camera owner than most people expect: it decides who reviews a pole or a fence you want to put a camera on, which detectives knock on your door after a break-in on the block, and how quickly the clip you export reaches the right desk.

The houses are the second thing that shapes a Nassau CCTV design. Census figures put 76.3% of the county's 477,860 housing units in single-family detached houses, and 47.5% of all units were built between 1940 and 1959, the years when potato fields in Levittown, Hicksville, East Meadow and Wantagh became subdivisions of capes and ranches. Add the hi-ranches and split-levels of the 1960s and you have the homes we wire most often. Their quirks are predictable once you have been in a few hundred of them: a cape whose upstairs was finished into bedrooms with a knee-wall crawlspace behind each one, a hi-ranch whose lower level is half below grade with the boiler and the electric panel, a split-level where the attic over the bedrooms is reachable but the attic over the living room is not. Each one gives us a different cable path to the soffit, and that path decides how clean the outside of the house looks when we leave.

The third thing is the driveway. Nassau houses sit close to the street with the car parked in plain view, and police have said as much about why thieves pick a block. When the Nassau County Police Department announced a multi-agency burglary and stolen-car task force in January 2024, ABC7 New York reported that police believe burglars are attracted by cars parked in driveways, and that the task force followed an attempted burglary at a Lake Success home. The good news is in the state numbers. New York's Division of Criminal Justice Services counts 551 motor vehicle thefts in Nassau County in 2024, down from 889 in 2023, and burglaries fell from 1,241 to 1,030 over the same year. Fewer cases means each one gets investigated harder, and the first thing an investigator asks a homeowner on a quiet street is whether a camera saw the car arrive.

Finally, Nassau's police have been building their own camera networks, and the village departments lean on private cameras more than people realize. Newsday reported in November 2024 that police departments across the county had installed nearly 70 license plate reading cameras in recent months, and that the Old Westbury department expected its plate readers to be complemented by 160 security cameras at golf clubs, schools and businesses that had agreed to share their video with police. A plate reader on a village corner tells detectives a car passed; your camera at the end of the driveway shows who got out of it. The two work best together, which is why we set up every Nassau system so the owner can find and export a time-stamped clip in a couple of minutes.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What the Four Tiles Told a South Hempstead Showroom

The job we point Nassau customers to is a window-and-door showroom with a working office in South Hempstead, a hamlet in the Town of Hempstead, which means Nassau County Police territory rather than a village department. The owners did not ask for a lot of cameras. They asked to be able to look up from his desk and answer four questions without getting up: who is walking toward the front door, who is pulling into the side lot, who is at the counter, and is anyone in the back room. Four cameras on a recorder, shown as four tiles on a wall monitor, answer exactly those four questions, and the way each tile looks on the screen is the real test of the installation.

The front camera covers the sidewalk and the door at the same time, so a customer is on video from the curb to the handle. Its tile shows the building number on the door, which is useful to an investigator and is the reason that tile stays out of the photos on this page. The side camera runs along the brick wall toward the lot; we used a wide lens there on purpose, accepting a little curve at the edge of the frame, because the goal was to see a car and a person anywhere in that lot rather than to read a plate at the far fence. The office camera watches the counter, the desks and the inside of the door, and the back-room camera watches the stock and the rear doorway.

Two details made the difference in that back room. The ceiling tiles were stained from an old leak, so we fixed the dome to the solid corner of the wall rather than to a tile that might be replaced or sag, and we kept the cable above the grid where it cannot be snagged by the trim stock leaning against the walls. And the monitor shows the date and time in the corner of every tile, which sounds trivial until you need to hand a clip to a detective; a recorder whose clock has drifted is the most common reason footage gets questioned. We set the clock to sync automatically before we left.

Nothing on that job was complicated, and that is why it is a good model for a small Nassau business. Most storefronts along Grand Avenue, Merrick Road or Hempstead Turnpike need the same four answers, plus maybe a fifth camera on the register and a sixth on the dumpster side. If your business looks like that, a 4-camera house-brand package or a small NVR system covers it, and the recorder in your back office keeps every tile on the screen whether the internet is up or not.

Building Specialists

CCTV Installation for Nassau County's Architecture

Nassau's housing was built in waves, and each wave left a different kind of house to wire. Before we quote, we ask what kind of house it is and when it was built, because a 1949 cape and a 1965 hi-ranch on the same street need completely different cable paths.

🏠

Levittown-Era Capes

The postwar capes of Levittown, Hicksville, Wantagh and Bethpage often have a finished upstairs with knee-wall crawlspaces at the eaves. Those crawlspaces are our route to the soffits on the long sides of the house. Dormers that were added later can block the path, so we check each side before we promise hidden wire.

🏚️

Ranches

One-story ranches in East Meadow, Plainview, Syosset and North Merrick usually have the simplest wiring on the Island: an open attic over the whole footprint. Cameras go under the eaves on junction boxes, the cable drops to a closet or the basement, and almost nothing shows outside.

🪜

Hi-Ranches

The raised ranch, called a hi-ranch here, puts the bedrooms upstairs over a half-buried lower level with the garage, the boiler and often a den. The upper attic reaches the high soffits; the garage and lower-level cameras come up from the utility room. The recorder usually lives on that lower level near the modem.

📐

Split-Levels

Split-levels in Seaford, Massapequa, Merrick and Plainview have two or three roof planes, and the attic over the living room is often a sealed void. We run the bedroom-side cameras from the reachable attic and route the others along the basement rim joist and up through the sill, so the front face stays clean.

🏛️

Tudors & Colonials

The prewar Tudors and center-hall colonials of Garden City, Rockville Centre, Malverne and Manhasset have plaster walls, stucco, half-timbering and slate or cedar roofs. We mount on trim boards and mortar joints rather than through decorative stucco, and fish cable through closets and chases instead of opening plaster.

🌳

North Shore Estates

Gold Coast properties in Old Westbury, Muttontown, Laurel Hollow and the Brookvilles have gates, long drives, pool houses and carriage houses. We put a small switch in each outbuilding, link it back with buried Cat6 or fiber, and keep each camera's run short enough to stay powered and reliable.

🌊

Raised South Shore Homes

After Sandy, many houses in Long Beach, Island Park, Oceanside, Baldwin Harbor, Freeport and Massapequa were lifted onto new foundations. The living space is a full story up, the area below is open or used for parking, and the recorder and switch belong upstairs, never at grade where water can reach them.

🏘️

Garden Apartments & Co-ops

Two- and three-story garden complexes from the 1940s through the 1960s spread entrances across a courtyard. Each building gets a small switch or recorder in a common closet, linked back to a main recorder, with cameras on doors, mail areas, laundry rooms and parking. Larger buildings are covered on our Nassau apartment building camera page.

Cable paths we use most on Nassau houses

On a house with any attic access, the cleanest path is attic to soffit: we drill down through the top plate or the soffit board, set a weatherproof junction box under the eave and mount the camera on the box, so the only thing visible outside is the camera itself. On a cape with a finished upstairs, the knee-wall crawlspaces do the same job for the long sides, and the gable ends are reached from the small attic above the ceiling. Where a section of roof has no reachable space above it, which is common over a 1970s family-room addition or a cathedral ceiling, we come from below instead, along the basement or crawlspace and up through the sill plate. Detached garages, common in the older parts of Valley Stream, Lynbrook, Malverne and Rockville Centre, get either a buried run in conduit or an aerial cable on a messenger, and we price each so you can choose.

Siding, shingles and stucco

Nassau houses wear just about every kind of skin: vinyl and aluminum siding on the postwar streets, cedar shingles on older South Shore and North Shore homes, brick veneer on the 1950s and 1960s ranches, and stucco on the Tudors. On siding we use a mounting block so the base sits flat and water runs around it. On shingles and brick we fix to solid backing and seal every penetration. On stucco we drill once, at a planned point, and seal it right away, because cracked stucco lets water into the wall behind it. If you are re-siding or re-roofing in the next year, tell us; it is cheaper to pre-wire and leave a labeled cable behind the new siding than to come back.

Where the recorder should live in a Nassau house

The recorder needs three things: a steady temperature, a network connection to the router, and a spot a burglar will not find in the first minute. In a cape, that is usually a bedroom closet upstairs or a basement shelf near the modem. In a hi-ranch, it is the lower-level utility room or a den closet. In a raised South Shore house, it is upstairs, above any water. We avoid unconditioned attics, where summer heat shortens hard-drive life, and detached garages, where a thief can carry the recorder off with the car. Wherever it goes, it sits on a small battery backup with the switch and the modem.

Who owns the cameras in a strip center

Most Nassau strip centers have two layers of CCTV, and the confusion between them causes most of the arguments after an incident. The landlord's system, if there is one, covers the parking field, the light poles and sometimes the service lane. Your system covers the inside of your store, your door and your stretch of the rear. The landlord's footage belongs to the landlord, retention is whatever they set, and you may not be able to get a copy quickly. That is why we recommend that each tenant covers its own rear door and its own slice of the parking field from its own building wall, even if the landlord has cameras too. Before we mount anything on the building, we check the lease or ask the property manager, because most commercial leases require approval for exterior attachments.

A typical Nassau business system

For a single store or office, the usual commercial CCTV installation is eight to sixteen cameras on an NVR with PoE, a battery backup for the recorder and switch, and a monitor where the owner or manager can see it. Restaurants add the kitchen back door and the grease-bin corner. Medical and dental offices add reception and the records room. Car dealerships and auto shops add the lot rows, the service bays and the key box. We set separate logins for owners, managers and staff, turn on motion alerts only for after-hours zones, and lock the recorder in a closet or cabinet. More on larger projects is on our commercial security camera installation page for Nassau County.

Compliance points before a business adds cameras

This is general information, not legal advice. New York Labor Law §203-c prohibits employers from video recording in restrooms, toilets, showers, locker rooms and changing rooms, and we will not install a camera in any of them. New York's eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.00 and §250.05) makes it a crime to record a conversation without the consent of at least one party, so we leave audio off on business cameras unless there is a clear reason and posted notice. Penal Law §250.45 covers recording people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Many Nassau villages have sign and exterior-fixture rules on their main streets, and commercial leases often require landlord approval for exterior mounts; we confirm both before drilling. A posted notice that video recording is in use is good practice for staff and customers.

Local Rules

Villages, Towns, Cities and Your Cameras

We have not come across a Nassau permit requirement for a camera mounted on an existing house or storefront, but the local layer still touches CCTV in a few predictable places. Because the county is split among so many governments, the first thing we do on a Nassau quote is identify which one your address belongs to. Your tax bill or the county's land records will tell you; so will the name of your police department. Here is what that answer changes. None of this is legal advice, and when a rule matters to your project, we tell you to confirm it with the village or town building department.

Village, town or hamlet

If you live in one of the 64 incorporated villages, such as Garden City, Rockville Centre, Freeport, Lynbrook, Mineola, Massapequa Park or Port Washington North, the village has its own building department and its own code. If you live in a hamlet, such as Levittown, East Meadow, Hicksville, Merrick, Elmont, Uniondale or South Hempstead, the town (Hempstead, North Hempstead or Oyster Bay) is your building authority. Glen Cove and Long Beach are cities with their own codes. For cameras mounted on an existing house, this rarely means a permit. It matters when the plan includes something new: a freestanding pole at the end of a drive, a camera post at a gate, a trench across a front lawn near a sidewalk, or exterior work on a building in a historic district, which some villages regulate.

Which police department answers

Most of the county is patrolled by the Nassau County Police Department through its precincts, but a number of villages and both cities run their own departments, among them Freeport, Garden City, Floral Park, Hempstead, Lynbrook, Malverne, Rockville Centre, Old Westbury, Kings Point, Lake Success and Sands Point, along with the Glen Cove and Long Beach police. After a break-in, the detective who asks for your footage comes from whichever department covers your address. Some village departments have built their own camera and plate-reader networks and invite residents and businesses to share video, as the Old Westbury department told Newsday. Sharing is voluntary. What we control is making sure your recorder can hand over a clean, time-stamped clip on a USB stick or by download in a couple of minutes.

The county alarm permit is not a camera permit

People often confuse CCTV with the burglar alarm permit. The Nassau County Police Department's Alarm Permit Section issues permits for alarm systems, with application and renewal fees listed at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business. That program is built around alarms that can bring a police response and the fines that follow false alarms. A camera system that records to a recorder in your closet is a different thing. If we add an intrusion alarm to your cameras, or your existing alarm company is involved, the alarm side follows the permit rules; ask us or the alarm company before the install.

Neighbors, trees and privacy

On small Nassau lots, a camera aimed at your own side yard can easily take in a neighbor's patio. Villages have noticed. In October 2017, ABC7 New York reported that the Village of Flower Hill was considering an ordinance after a resident complained that a neighbor had mounted two cameras high in a tree aimed toward her backyard; the proposed text aimed to “preclude the installation and use of an imaging device with no legitimate purpose other than viewing or recording another person in an area of that person’s property where the person would not otherwise be visible.” Whatever any village adopts, state law already makes it a crime to record people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Our practice is simple: aim at your own approaches, use the recorder's privacy masks to black out a neighbor's windows or yard when a view clips them, and never mount on a tree or a fence you do not own.

HOAs, co-ops and condos

Nassau has plenty of private communities with their own rules: gated developments on the North Shore, condominium townhouse complexes, and co-op garden apartments. Their governing documents can control exterior attachments and common areas regardless of what the village allows. For common-area systems, the board decides who can view and export footage, how long it is kept and where the recorder sits, and we set the system up to match that decision in writing.

Inherited Systems

When You Buy a Nassau House That Already Has Cameras

Nassau houses change hands constantly, and a lot of them come with a camera system the seller left on the wall. Sometimes it is a good system. Often it is a DVR in a basement closet with a password nobody knows, four analog cameras that went soft years ago, and an app that stopped connecting when the old internet account was closed. Before you rip it out or pay for a new one, it is worth an hour to find out what you actually inherited.

The cable is usually the valuable part. RG59 coax or Cat6 that already reaches the soffits of a cape or a hi-ranch saves the hardest labor on the job, and coax in good condition will carry modern HD-analog video up to 8MP 4K. We test each run, look at the connectors where they enter the house, and check whether any cable was stapled in a way that kinked it. Runs that test clean stay; runs that fail get replaced one at a time rather than all at once.

The recorder is usually the part to replace. A DVR from the early 2010s may not accept today's cameras, may not have had a firmware update in years, and almost always has the previous owner's admin account on it. Even when it still works, we recommend a factory reset at minimum so that you, and only you, hold the master password, and so the seller's phone no longer receives your alerts. If the old recorder cannot be reset, it gets replaced; a CCTV system you cannot fully control is a liability, not a feature.

The cameras are judged one by one. A camera aimed at the wrong thing is common, because the previous owner's car, fence or pool may be gone. We re-aim what is worth keeping, replace what is blurry or failing at night, and add a camera where the old layout missed the approach that matters to you, which on Nassau houses is usually the driveway mouth or the side gate. Done this way, an inherited system often turns into a good one for well under the cost of starting over, and we tell you which path costs less before any work begins.

Your Questions Answered

CCTV Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau homeowners, store owners and office managers ask us before booking, grouped by topic and answered the way we answer them on the phone.

Your address and the local rules

How do I know whether my address is in a village, a town or a hamlet, and why should a camera installer care?

Look at your property tax bill or the county's online land records: an incorporated village shows up as a separate village tax line, and a hamlet address is governed directly by the Town of Hempstead, North Hempstead or Oyster Bay. The name of your police department is a quick check too. We ask because it decides whose building department reviews anything freestanding, such as a camera pole at the end of a drive or a gate post, and which police department will be asking for your footage. For cameras mounted on the house itself, the answer rarely changes the job, but it is better to know before the first hole is drilled.

Which police department would ask for my footage after a break-in, and how do I hand it over?

Whichever department patrols your address: a Nassau County Police precinct in most hamlets and many villages, or the village or city department in places such as Freeport, Garden City, Rockville Centre, Lynbrook, Old Westbury, Glen Cove and Long Beach. The detective will want the original clip with the recorder's date and time stamp, not a phone video of your screen. Before we leave a job, we show you how to search by date and time, trim the clip to the event and export it to a USB stick or your phone, and we set the recorder's clock to sync automatically so the stamp is accurate.

Do I need the Nassau County alarm permit for a camera system?

The county police's permit program is for alarm systems, with fees listed at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, and it exists mainly because alarms can send police to your door. A CCTV system that only records to your own recorder is not an alarm. If your project includes a monitored intrusion alarm, or cameras that trigger your existing alarm company, the alarm side follows that program, and we will point it out when we quote. This is general information, not legal advice.

Nassau houses and how they are wired

On a corner lot, does each street side need its own camera?

Usually, yes. A Nassau corner house has two frontages, and the side street is often where people park, cut across the lawn or try the side door because it is out of view of the main road. One camera on each front corner, aimed along each frontage toward the corner, covers both sidewalks and both curbs; a third on the driveway side catches cars turning in. We keep the angles tight enough that the houses across both streets are not filling the frame, and mask out their windows in the recorder.

How do you cover the gate and the drive on a North Shore estate?

We treat the gate as the front door. One camera sits at the gate pillar or on a short post just inside it, at a height and angle where a car's front plate and the driver's window are both in frame as it stops. A second camera watches the gate from the house side to catch anyone on foot. The drive gets one or two overview cameras, and the house, garages and pool house are covered like any other home. Because the gate can be hundreds of feet from the house, we put a switch near it and link it back by buried Cat6 or fiber in conduit, priced after we confirm the layout.

Can the recorder go in the attic or the garage of my house?

We avoid both. A Nassau attic can pass well over 100 degrees in July, which shortens hard-drive life, and an unheated garage swings the other way in January. A detached garage also puts the recorder where a thief is already standing. A bedroom closet, a basement shelf near the modem, or the lower-level utility room of a hi-ranch is better. If the only spot near the modem is a hot one, we run one network cable to a cooler closet and put the recorder there.

We are re-roofing and re-siding next spring. Should the cameras wait?

Install the cameras now if you need them now, and tell us about the work. We can mount them on junction boxes that the roofer or siding crew can unscrew and set aside, and we label every cable end. Better still, if the siding is coming off anyway, we can come back during that window and leave cable behind the new siding for any future camera spots. What you want to avoid is a crew cutting cables they did not know were there; a labeled junction box at each camera solves that.

Should the pool and the pool gate have their own camera?

On most Nassau lots with a pool, yes. A camera on the back corner of the house that sees the pool gate, the deck and the fence line shows you when the gate is left open, who came over the fence, and what happened at a party you were not home for. We aim it so it covers your yard only, set a motion zone on the gate itself for alerts, and leave the rest of the yard recording without notifications so your phone does not buzz every time a leaf lands on the cover.

Stores, offices and day-to-day use

Who should own the parking-lot cameras in a Nassau strip center, the landlord or the tenant?

Usually both have a part. The landlord covers the parking field and the poles; each tenant covers its own interior, its own door and its own stretch of the rear lane. If you rely only on the landlord's cameras, you depend on the landlord's retention settings and the property manager's response time when you need a clip. We recommend at least one camera of your own on your rear door and one on the parking spaces in front of your store, mounted on your own wall with the landlord's written approval if the lease requires it.

Should a nail salon or a barbershop record the client chairs?

Most salon owners in Nassau record the front door, the reception desk and the register, and many add a wide view of the floor so disputes over a service or a payment can be checked. What stays off camera is any back room where clients change, the restroom and staff changing areas, and we leave audio off because recording conversations at the chair without consent runs into New York’s eavesdropping law. A posted notice at the door that video recording is in use is good practice. This is general information, not legal advice.

Can I get an alert only when a car pulls into my driveway, not for every car on the street?

Yes. On cameras with person and vehicle detection, we draw a line or a zone across the mouth of the driveway and set the alert to trigger only for vehicles that cross it. Cars passing on Merrick Road or a busy side street stay on the recording but never ping your phone. We test it before we leave by pulling a car in and out, and we adjust the zone if the headlights of passing traffic trip it at night.

Should one camera look down the block from a second-floor window or dormer?

It is tempting, because a dormer on a cape or the front bedroom of a colonial has the best view on the house. Behind glass, though, a camera’s infrared bounces off the window at night and the picture turns into a white glare, and screens and reflections soften the day image too. When a high view down the block is worth having, we mount the camera outside under the eave above that window instead, with the cable dropping straight into the attic, and turn its infrared off if a streetlight already lights the street.

What happens to my cameras when the internet company swaps my router?

The cameras keep recording, because they talk to the recorder, not to the internet. What usually breaks is remote viewing: a new Optimum or Verizon router can hand the recorder a new address or block the connection the app relies on. Recording on site is unaffected, and getting the app working again is normally a short fix on the recorder’s network settings. If you know a router swap or an upgrade is coming, tell us before the technician arrives and we will plan the recorder settings so it reconnects to the new equipment.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV Installation Across Nassau's 64 Villages, 3 Towns and 2 Cities

AI answers about CCTV are written for an imaginary average suburb. Nassau is a particular one: dozens of local governments, a housing stock built mostly in two postwar decades, a plate-reader network run by village police, and coastal storms that take the power out. Directory sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor add their own national averages on top. Here are seven claims we hear repeated and what our crews actually find on Nassau jobs.

“Nassau is one county, so the same rules apply everywhere”

It is a reasonable assumption if you have never looked at a Nassau tax bill. AI summaries tend to describe a county as if it had one building department and one police force.

The state counts 2 cities, 3 towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 hamlets in Nassau. A camera pole at the end of a drive in Garden City goes to the village; the same pole in Levittown goes to the Town of Hempstead. A burglary in Freeport is investigated by the Freeport police; one in Merrick by a county precinct.

For most camera installs on an existing house, none of that changes the work. It changes who you call about anything freestanding, and who calls you asking for footage, so we check it on every quote.

“Every suburban house can be wired the same way”

Generic guides describe one house: an open attic, a basement, vinyl siding. Nassau has that house, and it also has five others.

With almost half of the county's housing built between 1940 and 1959, we spend our days in capes with knee-wall crawlspaces, hi-ranches with half-buried lower levels, split-levels with sealed attic voids, Tudors with stucco and plaster, and South Shore houses lifted a full story after Sandy. Each one has a different path from the recorder to the soffit, and a different set of places where a cable simply cannot go without opening a wall.

That is why we ask what kind of house it is, and when it was built, before we promise hidden wire anywhere.

“A license plate camera reads every car that drives by”

The village plate readers in the news have made people expect any camera labeled LPR to read every plate. Dedicated plate-reading cameras do that well, but only because they are installed for it.

A plate is readable when the camera sees it nearly head-on, at a narrow choke point, with a short shutter speed and infrared that does not bloom off the reflective plate at night. A wide camera on the corner of a house, looking across a 50-foot front lawn at cars moving 30 miles an hour, will capture the car and its color but rarely the plate.

If plate capture matters, we put a narrow camera at the driveway or the lot entrance where cars slow down, and we tell you honestly what the overview cameras will and will not read.

“Cameras only help if you pay for professional monitoring”

Alarm companies sell monitoring, and AI answers often fold cameras into the same monthly plan. Monitoring is useful for an alarm, which needs someone to call the police at 3 a.m.

A CCTV system's main job is evidence: the clip that shows who came up the driveway and what they took. That footage sits on your own recorder with no monthly charge, and you can watch live or play it back from your phone. Alerts for people or vehicles in set zones reach you directly.

If you want both, an alarm can be monitored while the cameras record locally. You do not have to put the cameras on a subscription to get value from them.

“Wired cameras fail in snow and ice, so wireless is safer in winter”

This one gets the physics backward. A wired PoE camera is fed power continuously through its cable, and outdoor-rated models are built to keep running through freezing nights.

The cameras that struggle in a Nassau January are battery models: cold drains batteries faster, and a camera on the edge of the house Wi-Fi drops frames when wet snow sits on the siding between it and the router. Wired cameras do have one winter enemy, which is snow or ice sitting on the lens, and the fix is placement under an eave or a sunshield rather than a different kind of camera.

We mount outdoor cameras under the roof overhang wherever we can, and we seal every cable entry so meltwater cannot follow the wire into the wall.

“You need a permit before installing security cameras”

Searches for Nassau camera rules turn up the county police's alarm permit, and AI summaries sometimes stretch it to cover cameras.

The Alarm Permit Section's program covers alarm systems, with fees listed at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business. A camera system that records to your own recorder is not an alarm. Where local approval does come in is for something you build, such as a freestanding pole or a gate post, or for exterior attachments under a lease, an HOA or a co-op board.

We tell you which, if any, applies to your plan before we start, and we recommend confirming with your village or town building department when a structure is involved.

“Your property, your camera, so you can aim it anywhere”

On a one-acre lot that is close to true. On a typical Nassau lot, where the neighbor's patio is 15 feet from your side door, it is not.

New York already makes it a crime to record people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, and recording conversations without a party's consent is eavesdropping. Villages have also weighed their own rules: Flower Hill considered an ordinance in 2017 after a resident complained about cameras in a neighbor's tree aimed at her backyard.

We aim cameras at your own approaches, mask out neighbors' windows and yards in the recorder when a view clips them, and leave audio off. That keeps the footage useful and keeps you out of a dispute.

Often, yes. Many Nassau burglaries start at a garage door left open or a side door into the garage, and the door from the garage into the house is rarely watched. One camera inside the garage, aimed at the overhead door and the house door, shows whether the door was left up, who came in and what went out. We mount it high and away from the opener so it does not catch the headlights straight on.

What should I do when snow or ice covers a camera?
+

Most of the time, nothing: cameras mounted under an eave rarely get covered, and frost on the lens usually clears once the sun reaches it. If wet snow plasters a camera on an exposed corner, brush it off gently with a soft broom from the ground rather than climbing a ladder on ice. A camera that keeps getting covered is a placement problem, and we can move it under the overhang or add a sunshield.

Can I keep the cameras running while my house is listed for sale and buyers are touring it?
+

You can keep exterior cameras running, and many sellers do. Indoors, turn off audio recording during showings, because recording conversations you are not part of can violate New York's eavesdropping law, and tell your listing agent that video cameras are in use so buyers can be informed. When the house sells, we can reset the recorder for the new owner or remove the system, depending on what your contract says.

+

Yes. A camera on the front corner of the house or the garage can cover a curbside mailbox and the street in front of it. Because the mailbox sits near the sidewalk and the road, we frame the shot on the box and the car that stops beside it, and mask out the houses across the street. Mail theft is a federal matter, so a clear clip of the vehicle is what helps most.
