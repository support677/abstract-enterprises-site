The Neighborhood

Why Westchester County Properties Need Wireless Camera Installation

Westchester is two counties wearing one name. The Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey counts 1,006,447 people on 430.8 square miles of land, about 2,336 per square mile on average — but averages hide the shape. Half of the county’s housing units are in multi-unit buildings. Yonkers alone holds 211,062 people at roughly 11,700 per square mile, and Mount Vernon runs past 16,000. Then drive forty minutes north and the lots open up: horse farms, stone walls, long gravel drives, and houses set so far back from the road that the mailbox is out of sight. A wireless camera system that suits a Yonkers two-family is the wrong system for a Pound Ridge property, and the reverse is true too. The first thing we do on any Westchester call is work out which of the two counties you live in.

In the northern towns the problem is distance and ground. The Town of Bedford zones parts of the town at four acres per house; North Salem, Lewisboro and Pound Ridge share the same kind of large-lot, wooded landscape. On those properties the camera list is rarely just the house. It is the detached garage, the barn or stable, the pool house, the guest cottage, the generator shed and the driveway gate — buildings separated from the router by hundreds of feet of lawn, stone wall, mature trees and grade. Wi‑Fi from a living room will not get there. A dedicated wireless bridge, a mesh node with a clear path, or a cellular camera usually will, and choosing between them is most of the design work.

Hills make that choice harder than a tape measure suggests. In a presentation to the Bedford Town Board on May 7, 2025, the town’s wireless working group explained that “hilly terrain obstructs signals,” that a low ridge called Seminary Ridge shadows coverage, and that the top of Indian Hill blocks the signal entirely from the new Bedford Post cell site to Middle Patent Road, upper Long Ridge Road and parts of Route 172. If a carrier’s tower on a mast cannot see past a ridge, a camera bridge mounted on a garage eave will not either. That is why we walk every link on site, and why we confirm cellular signal at the exact spot a gate camera will hang before recommending an LTE unit.

Power is the other half. Most of Westchester is Con Edison territory, but NYSEG’s electric tariff lists Bedford, Lewisboro, North Salem, Pound Ridge, Somers and Yorktown in its service area, and NYSEG’s Brewster Division covers parts of Westchester, Putnam and Dutchess. Both utilities have had long nights here. When Tropical Storm Isaias hit in August 2020, the Public Service Commission recorded peak outages of about 290,000 Con Edison customers and 95,000 NYSEG customers, most of NYSEG’s in the Brewster Division; full restoration took until August 12. On July 4, 2026, Con Edison reported more than 20,000 customers still out after fast-moving storms with gusts up to 60 miles per hour, and said Westchester “experienced the greatest impact,” led by Greenburgh, Yonkers, Elmsford, Dobbs Ferry and Rye. A wireless camera system that goes dark with the house is not watching the house when it matters most.

The burglary picture adds urgency rather than drama. In January 2025 the New York State Police issued a public safety alert to residents of Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam about a series of targeted residential burglaries, and its first recommendation was to install or activate security systems and cameras. ABC7 New York reported that Pelham Manor police warned residents after several break-ins in which, according to the village’s police chief, the intruders went into homes to find car key fobs and drive away with the vehicle. And in June 2024 News 12 Westchester carried a New Jersey police warning that a burglar had used a Wi‑Fi jammer to knock out a homeowner’s cameras and phone; the police advice was to hardwire where you can rather than rely only on Wi‑Fi. We take that advice seriously, which is why the systems on this page record locally and use wired backbones wherever a cable can reasonably go.

Point-to-point bridges to outbuildings

A pair of directional radios, one on the house and one on the garage, barn or cottage, aimed at each other across the lawn. The far end gets its own small switch, so wired or wireless cameras there behave as if they were plugged in at the house.

Mesh coverage for pool houses and patios

Outdoor-rated mesh nodes placed in a chain with clear paths between them, for the terrace, pool deck, cabana and side yard where cameras and phones both need signal and a single bridge would leave gaps.

Gate and driveway cameras

Cameras at the road end of a long drive, powered from the gate operator, a pole circuit or solar, connected by bridge where the house can see the gate and by cellular where it cannot. Often paired with a second camera aimed down the drive toward the house.

LTE cellular cameras where Wi‑Fi ends

Cameras with their own data connection for gates, far paddocks and sheds out of sight of the house, chosen only after we test carrier signal at the mounting spot — Bedford’s own wireless report shows how a hill can cut service in one spot and not the next.

Solar-powered cameras and links

Panels sized for short December days and positioned clear of the tree canopy, feeding a battery that runs the camera or the remote end of a bridge, for places where trenching power across a lawn or a stone wall makes no sense.

Backup power for the whole chain

Battery backup on the modem, router, recorder, bridges and switches, sized for how long you want to see video during an outage, with automatic restart and generator transfer planned with your electrician.

Local recording without a monthly plan

A network video recorder or base station inside the house that keeps footage on its own drives, so a cloud subscription is optional rather than required and the video survives an internet outage.

Apartment and multi-family systems

Wireless cameras for parking pads, rear yards, side alleys and entrances of two- and three-family houses and small apartment buildings in Yonkers, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle and Port Chester, with separate access for owner and tenants.

Building Specialists

Wireless Camera Installation for Westchester County’s Architecture

Westchester housing spans more than a century and every price point, and each building type changes where a radio can sit, how power reaches it and how far a signal travels before a wall or a hill stops it.

Estate houses with outbuildings

Main house, detached garage with living space above, barn or stable, pool house and gate, spread across several acres in Bedford, Pound Ridge, North Salem, Lewisboro and Armonk. The design is a small campus network: a bridge or two from the house, switches at each building, and cameras hung where each building faces the drive.

Colonials, Tudors and center-halls on half-acre lots

The Scarsdale, Larchmont, Chappaqua and Rye house with a detached one- or two-car garage at the end of the drive and a deep back yard. Usually a single bridge or one strong mesh node reaches the garage; the work is in power and mounting without marking up the trim.

Two- and three-family houses

The frame and brick multi-families of Yonkers, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle and Port Chester, often with a rear parking pad, a shared side alley and separate entrances. Cameras cover the shared ground; access is split so tenants see their door and the owner sees everything.

Mid-rise apartment and mixed-use buildings

Brick and stone elevator buildings and walk-ups along the county’s main streets, with storefronts below and a parking court or lot behind. Wireless earns its place for the lot and the rear door where pulling cable across a courtyard would mean trenching.

Farms, stables and riding rings

Post-frame barns, run-in sheds and indoor rings in North Salem, Somers and Bedford, with dust, birds, animals and long distances. Cameras go high under eaves, cable is protected from chewing, and the link from the house is built to cross open pasture.

Materials and methods by building type

  • Bridge radios on houses: mounted on a gable end, chimney bracket or eave pole where they can see the far building over hedges and parked cars, never through a window, with a drip loop on every cable entering the house.
  • Bridge radios on outbuildings: the far radio on the side facing the house, as high as the roofline allows, feeding a weatherproof enclosure with a small PoE switch and surge protection for the cameras on that building.
  • Wood siding and trim: mounting blocks behind cameras and radios so fasteners land in framing, not just clapboard or cedar shingle, and holes sealed so water does not follow the screw.
  • Barns and run-in sheds: cable in conduit wherever horses, rodents or equipment can reach it, sealed connectors because condensation forms inside boxes on cold nights, and cameras set back from dust and spider webs near hay.
  • Poles at gates: a galvanized pole or a bracket on the gate pier with a lockable enclosure for the battery, LTE router or bridge radio, positioned clear of the snowplow’s path.

Trees deserve their own line. A sightline that is wide open in March can be gone by late May, when maples and oaks leaf out, and it can wobble on a windy day as branches move through it. We plan links on Westchester properties to work leaf-on, not just leaf-off, by raising radios above the canopy line, routing around a tree line through a relay point, or choosing a frequency and antenna that tolerate the path. Your Questions Answered

Wireless Camera Installation in Westchester County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Westchester owners, managers and caretakers ask once a wireless camera project is real. They are grouped by the problem behind them, from the far barn to the village building department, and each one is answered once.

Distance, Bridges and the Far Outbuildings

We have a garage, a barn and a guest cottage in different directions from the house. Does each building need its own pair of radios?

Not necessarily. Where the outbuildings sit within a reasonably narrow arc as seen from one spot on the house, a single wider-beam radio at the house can talk to small radios on each building at once — a point-to-multipoint layout. It means one radio and one mounting point on the main house instead of three, which also keeps the gable end or chimney from collecting hardware. Each outbuilding still gets its own small switch, so cameras out there are wired locally and only the link back to the house is wireless.

Where the buildings lie in opposite directions, or one of them is much farther away than the others, separate point-to-point pairs usually work better, because each link can be aimed and tuned for its own distance. Sometimes the answer is a chain: the house reaches the garage, and the garage, which can see the cottage, passes the signal on. We draw the options on your survey or an aerial view during the walk-through and price the layout with the fewest radios that still leave every link with spare capacity. This is general AESS design practice; the right layout always comes from the property itself.

The barn sits below the house, behind a stone wall and a row of old oaks. Can a wireless link reach it without a straight line of sight?

Sometimes, but not by brute force. Radio links need more than a thin line between the two antennas; they need a clear zone around that line, and a stone wall at the bottom of a slope plus a canopy of oaks can block most of it. Turning up the power rarely fixes that. What does work is changing the geometry. We raise one or both radios — a mast on the barn gable, a bracket on the house chimney — so the path clears the wall and the trees. If the ground still blocks it, we add a relay: a third radio on a building or pole that can see both the house and the barn, often a garage or shed partway between, so the signal bends around the obstacle in two straight hops.

The Town of Bedford’s own wireless report shows why we test rather than assume. It describes coverage from a single cell site that is solid in one spot and missing a few hundred yards away because a ridge or a sharp drop sits in between. The same thing happens on a smaller scale on a sloped Westchester lot. If neither height nor a relay will work, the honest answer may be a conduit and a fiber or Cat6 run, or a cellular camera at the barn, and we will say so before you buy radios.

Will a bridge radio on our roof interfere with the neighbors’ Wi‑Fi, or need some kind of license?

The outdoor bridge radios we install for homes run in unlicensed bands, the same general bands home Wi‑Fi uses, so no license is needed for a residential link. Because they are directional, most of their energy goes in a narrow beam toward the other radio rather than spreading across the street. On the dense lots of Yonkers, Mount Vernon or New Rochelle we still choose channels during setup that avoid the networks we can see from the mounting point, which keeps your link clean and avoids crowding anyone else’s.

On larger northern properties interference is rarely the issue; distance and terrain are. Either way, we aim the radios into your own property, not across a neighbor’s house, set transmit power no higher than the link needs, and record the channel and signal figures in your handover pack. If a neighbor ever raises a concern, those figures make it easy to show what the link is doing and to change channel if it helps. This is general AESS practice rather than a Westchester rule.

The Gate at the End of a Long Driveway

Our barn is across a town road from the house. Can the camera link go over the road instead of trenching under it?

Usually, yes, and it is one of the clearest cases for wireless. Trenching or boring under a road means a permit from whoever owns it; Mount Kisco’s building-permit instructions, for example, require a driveway, curb cut or street opening permit from the state, county or village depending on the road. A pair of bridge radios, one on the house and one on the barn, crosses the road through the air with no digging, no street opening and no lane closure.

What we check is height and clearance: the beam has to pass above trucks, utility lines and tree limbs along the road, so radios typically go at the roofline on both ends, or on a short mast if a building is low. We also make sure the radios aim at each other and not along the road into other properties. If the barn has no power to run the far radio and its cameras, that power comes from an existing circuit in the barn or from an electrician, priced separately.

Bedford has new cell sites planned near us. Should we wait for them before choosing a gate camera?

We would not wait, but we would plan for them. Bedford’s May 2025 report listed a Route 35 site in Katonah where construction was expected to start, an approved but unbuilt facility at the Bedford Hills water treatment plant with no construction date, and the Bedford Post site carrying Verizon with AT&T expected and T-Mobile anticipated later. It also stressed that the town cannot compel carriers to build, and that hills leave pocket gaps even near a tower.

So we design the gate camera to work today without a new tower — usually by bridge from the house, or with solar and local recording — and leave room for cellular later. If a new site goes live and your carrier’s signal at the gate improves, adding or switching to a cellular connection is a small change rather than a new system. Choosing a camera that only works on a carrier that has not arrived yet is the one decision we steer owners away from.

Storms, Salt Air and the Grid

A falling limb took down the wires to our garage in the last storm. How do you keep a tree from cutting the camera link?

Overhead cable strung between buildings is exactly what storms take out. Con Edison’s July 2026 storm release described heavy rain and gusts of up to 60 miles per hour pushing trees into overhead power lines, and private spans between a house and a garage face the same limbs. A wireless bridge removes that span: there is no cable in the air for a branch to tear down, only two radios fixed firmly to the buildings.

We also look up before we mount. Radios go where a dropped limb is unlikely to strike them, on solid structure rather than a gutter or a dead tree, and the cable from each radio runs down the wall in a protected path into the building. Where a cable between buildings is still the best answer, we run it underground in conduit rather than overhead. After a storm, a slightly knocked radio can usually be re-aimed in minutes, which is far quicker than replacing a torn cable.

Does it matter to the camera design whether our house is on Con Edison or NYSEG?

The cameras do not care, but your planning should. Con Edison serves most of the county; NYSEG’s tariff lists Bedford, Lewisboro, North Salem, Pound Ridge, Somers and Yorktown, and its Brewster Division covers parts of Westchester, Putnam and Dutchess. The northern towns it serves are also the most wooded, most spread out and most likely to have long private drives and overhead lines through trees, so when a storm comes through, restoration to a single long driveway can come after the larger circuits are fixed. Both utilities say the same in their restoration plans: critical facilities first, then the repairs that restore the largest numbers of customers, then smaller groups.

In practice, properties in the NYSEG towns, and estates at the end of long feeders anywhere in the county, are the ones where we most often recommend a generator connection or a larger battery for the camera network. Properties in denser Con Edison areas in the south of the county often need only a few hours of backup to ride through a typical outage. We ask which utility serves you and how long your last outage lasted, because those two answers say more about the backup you need than any spec sheet. As a rule of thumb from our own installs, a battery unit covering the modem, router, bridge radios, switch and recorder carries a typical home system for several hours; getting through a full day or more means larger batteries or putting the camera network on a generator circuit with a small battery to cover the seconds before it starts. Either way, we finish by pulling the plug and watching every device come back on its own.

We live on the water in Rye. Does salt air shorten the life of outdoor cameras and radios?

It can, and the damage usually starts at the small parts: screws, brackets, connector housings and the joints where cable enters a camera. On Sound Shore properties near Playland, the clubs and the marinas, we use stainless or coated mounting hardware, seal every outdoor connection, and mount cameras and radios under eaves or on the lee side of a building where spray and wind-driven rain hit less often.

We also choose housings with appropriate weather ratings and avoid putting exposed connections at the bottom of a drip path. A yearly look at the waterfront-facing cameras — cleaning salt film off lenses and checking seals — costs far less than replacing corroded equipment. This is general AESS practice for coastal installs rather than a measured Westchester corrosion rate; we have not seen a published figure we would quote.

Jammers, Interference and the Wi‑Fi You Cannot See

We read that burglars use Wi‑Fi jammers to knock cameras offline. Should we avoid wireless cameras entirely?

No, but you should avoid a system where Wi‑Fi is the only thing holding it together. The 2024 case News 12 Westchester reported from Florham Park described a device that knocked out both the homeowner’s cameras and their phone, and police advised hardwiring where possible. That advice fits the way we already build. Cameras on the house are wired PoE wherever cable can reasonably reach them. Outbuildings are joined by an aimed point-to-point bridge, with wired cameras at the far end, so there is one narrow link rather than many cameras on open Wi‑Fi. Cameras record locally, to the recorder or their own cards, so a camera that loses its connection keeps recording what happens in front of it.

Where a battery Wi‑Fi camera is genuinely the right choice — a rented house, a spot no cable will ever reach — we choose models that keep recording to their own storage when the network drops, and we set the system to alert you when a camera goes offline, not just when it sees motion. A camera that disappears at 2 a.m. is itself a warning. Pair that with an alarm on a cellular or wired path and the jamming trick loses most of its value.

Can the cameras run on a network separate from the one the family, guests and smart-home gadgets use?

Yes, and on a large property we recommend it. A dedicated camera network — its own wireless name or a separate segment on the router, with the bridges and recorder on it — keeps camera video from slowing down work calls and streaming, keeps guests and contractors who get the house Wi‑Fi password away from the cameras, and means that the next time someone replaces the router or changes the family password, the cameras do not all drop off at once.

The recorder still reaches the internet for remote viewing, and your phone app works the same. What changes is that the camera devices cannot be browsed from a guest’s laptop, and a cheap smart plug with weak security cannot be used as a doorway to the cameras. We document the camera network’s name, password and the recorder’s address in the handover pack, so whoever looks after your network next can see exactly what is there.

Yonkers, Mount Vernon and the Southern Cities

We own a three-family house in Mount Vernon with a rear parking pad. Can the owner and the tenants share one camera system without sharing one password?

Yes. The recorder holds every camera, and each person gets their own account with only the views they should have. The owner sees the parking pad, the side alley, the front steps and every entrance. Each tenant can be given the camera at their own entrance and the shared parking area, and nothing that looks into another unit’s door. Accounts can be removed when a tenant moves out without changing anything for anyone else, which is the practical reason not to hand out one shared login.

Mount Vernon’s housing is about 70 percent multi-unit, and on lots this tight the cameras need careful aiming. We set privacy masks so a camera covering your parking pad blacks out a neighbor’s windows or yard, and we keep cameras off the interior of anyone’s apartment. If a tenant asks for footage after a car is damaged, the owner can export the clip from the recorder without handing over access to the whole system.

Our Yonkers building’s parking court is around the back and downhill from the office where the internet comes in. Can one wireless link cover it?

Usually, provided the link starts from the right place. The office router is rarely the best launching point; it sits inside, behind brick, often on the wrong side of the building. We run cable from the office to a short bridge radio on the rear wall or roof edge that can look down into the court, and put the far radio on a light pole, the back of a garage or a fence post with power. From there, cameras at the court entrance, along the parking rows and at the rear door are wired to a small switch, so the only wireless hop is the one across open air.

Yonkers holds about 211,000 people at roughly 11,700 per square mile, and buildings sit close together, so we plan around the neighbors as much as the slope: radios aimed into your court and not across the block, cameras masked off adjacent windows, and mounting that the building’s super can reach for cleaning. Where the court is visible from the roof, a camera up there on the wired network may cover most of it without any bridge at all.

Permits, Electricians and Village Rules

Do we need a building permit to put a camera on a pole at the gate?

It depends on the village or town, and Westchester has 48 of them with their own codes. A camera screwed to an existing gate pier or house wall generally falls within ordinary maintenance in most places. A new freestanding pole with a footing, a new electrical circuit, or anything that disturbs a road shoulder is different, and the rules vary: some building departments want a permit for any new structure in a front yard setback; Mount Kisco’s permit instructions require a separate driveway, curb cut or street opening permit from the state, county or village depending on who owns the road.

We check with your building department before quoting a pole or a trench, and we tell you whether the item needs a permit, who files it and how long it is likely to take. If it does, the electrician or contractor who holds the right license files it, and we schedule the camera work around the approval. The cheapest way to avoid a permit is often a camera on an existing structure plus solar power, which is one more reason solar comes up so often on Westchester gates.

Who wires a new outlet for a camera on the garage — your crew or an electrician?

An electrician. We install, connect and power the low-voltage side: PoE cameras, bridge radios, switches, battery backup units and the cables between them. Anything at line voltage — a new outlet on the garage, a circuit to the gate, a weatherproof receptacle on a pole — goes to an electrician licensed by Westchester County. The villages make that explicit: Dobbs Ferry takes electrical permit applications only from Westchester master license holders, Irvington requires a county-licensed contractor to file electrical work, and Mount Kisco requires all electricians to hold a Westchester County license.

Often no new outlet is needed. PoE carries power and data down one cable, so a garage with a bridge and a small PoE switch can run several cameras from a single existing outlet inside. When a new circuit is unavoidable, we coordinate with your electrician or bring one in, and the estimate shows their scope as a separate line so you can compare.

Estates, Staff and Who Gets Access

House staff live in the apartment over our garage. How do we cover the garage without recording their home?

By treating the apartment as someone’s home, because it is. Cameras on the garage are aimed at the driveway, the garage doors and the approach, not at the apartment entrance, windows or the staff’s private outdoor space, and where a view unavoidably includes them we apply privacy masks so that area is blacked out in live view and recordings. New York Labor Law §203-c already bars video recording in employee restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms; we go further and keep the living quarters out of view entirely.

We suggest telling staff where the cameras are and what they see, and giving them access to the camera at their own entrance if they want it. Many owners write the camera plan into the staff agreement. It protects everyone: staff know they are not being watched at home, and the owner has a clear record of the driveway and garage if something happens.

We spend winters in Florida. Can we watch the property all season without the bridges or recorder needing a visit?

That is exactly what the system should do, and we build it with an empty house in mind. Remote viewing runs through the recorder’s app over your home internet, and alerts reach your phone wherever you are. The risk for an absentee owner is not the cameras; it is something quietly failing and nobody noticing. So we turn on device-offline alerts for every camera and bridge, set the recorder to report a failed or full drive, and put the router and network gear on battery backup with automatic restart after an outage.

We also suggest a short checklist for whoever checks the house: look at the recorder screen, confirm the time is right, and press a button on the battery unit to see that it is charged. If something does fail while you are away, many faults can be diagnosed remotely from the app, and a single service visit, with the caretaker letting us in, usually fixes it within one to two hours on site.

Taking Over What Is Already There

The previous owner left battery cameras and a base station we have no login for. Can you reuse them?

Often, yes. Most consumer base stations and cameras can be reset to factory settings with a button and re-registered to your account, and the manufacturer’s support line can sometimes release a device tied to the last owner’s account once you show proof of purchase of the house. Recordings already stored under the old account usually do not come with it, so treat a reset as a fresh start rather than a recovery.

Once we have the devices under your control, we assess each one honestly. Cameras with healthy batteries and supported firmware stay. Cameras the maker no longer updates, or that are mounted where they cannot see anything useful, come down. If the base station sits in a closet far from the outbuilding cameras, we move it or extend the network so they stop dropping. The result is one app, one owner and a list of what is installed, which is usually more than the last owner left you.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Wireless Camera Installation in Westchester County

Search for wireless cameras and an AI summary will give you confident, tidy answers. Most of them are written for an average suburban house. Here is where they part ways with Westchester properties, and what happens in the field instead.

“Wireless cameras need no wiring at all”

The summary usually means the camera sends video over Wi‑Fi. It skips the fact that every camera needs power, and power means a battery that must be charged, a solar panel that must be sized, or a cable to an outlet.

On a Westchester estate, the honest description of a “wireless” system is a wired system at each building joined by wireless links. Cameras on the house and garage run on PoE cable; the long gaps between buildings are where radios earn their keep.

Owners who expected no wiring are usually relieved once they see why: wired cameras at each building never need charging, are harder to jam and keep working when the Wi‑Fi in the house is busy.

“A Wi‑Fi extender will cover your detached garage”

An indoor extender plugged in near a back window can help a garage 50 feet away. It will not carry several cameras to a garage 300 or 400 feet away across a lawn, and it halves its own throughput by repeating everything it hears.

Across real distances the right tool is an outdoor point-to-point bridge, aimed and mounted outside, with a switch at the far end. Where several outdoor areas need coverage near the house, outdoor mesh nodes with clear paths between them do the job.

The difference shows up the first windy night: the extender-fed camera freezes and drops, while the bridged camera keeps recording at full resolution.

“Outdoor Wi‑Fi cameras reach up to 300 feet”

Range figures on boxes are measured in open air with nothing in the way. A Westchester yard has stone walls, grade changes, parked cars, hedges and mature trees, and in May the trees fill in with leaves that soak up signal.

The Town of Bedford’s 2025 wireless report makes the same point about cell towers: hills create pocket gaps even close to a facility, and coverage is irregular because terrain blocks signals. A garage-mounted camera faces the same physics at smaller scale.

We plan from a measured signal at the mounting spot, in leaf-on conditions where possible, with margin to spare. A number on a box is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.

“5 GHz Wi‑Fi is always the better choice for outdoor cameras”

The faster band sounds like the obvious pick, and indoors, close to the router, it often is. Outdoors, across a lawn or through an exterior wall, the slower 2.4 GHz band usually reaches farther and holds its connection better through siding, brick and leaves.

Many battery and budget Wi‑Fi cameras only speak 2.4 GHz anyway, so a router set to 5 GHz only can leave them unable to connect at all. The right setup on a Westchester property is often a dedicated 2.4 GHz camera network near the house and directional bridges, on their own frequencies, for the long runs.

We choose the band per location from a signal test, not a rule of thumb, and keep the cameras off the network the family uses for streaming and work.

“Add a solar panel and you never have to charge again”

Solar works well when it is sized for the worst month, not the best. Short December days, low sun behind the tree line and a week of overcast weather can drain an undersized battery even though the panel looked generous in summer.

Placement matters as much as size. A panel shaded by oaks for half the day produces a fraction of its rating, and on wooded Westchester lots the sunny spot is often not where the camera wants to be. A separate panel on a short cable solves that.

We size panel and battery for several sunless days, set the camera to use power sensibly and add a low-battery alert so you hear about a problem before the camera goes dark.

“A 4K camera solves the long-distance problem”

More pixels help when the lens is right. A wide 4K camera on a garage still shows a car at the far end of a long driveway as a small shape, because the lens spreads its pixels across the whole yard; a narrower lens aimed where cars slow down tells you far more.

On wireless systems, 4K also has a cost the summaries skip: every 4K stream takes several times the bandwidth of a lower-resolution one, and a bridge or mesh hop shared by six cameras can run out of room, which shows up as stuttering video and gaps in recording.

We match the lens to the distance, set bitrates for what the link can carry with margin, and record full resolution locally while sending lighter streams to your phone.

“National averages tell you what installation should cost here”

Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national average costs for camera installation. Those averages are built from typical jobs: a few cameras on one house near the router. They do not price a 400-foot bridge, a relay on a barn, a solar gate camera or a day of backup power.

They also leave out regional labor and travel. Our Westchester prices are our published base table times the 1.25 Westchester factor, and the wireless links, poles, solar and backup appear as separate lines after the walk-through.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Stand Where the Radio Will Stand

This is a method note, not a story. On a Westchester property the first job is not choosing cameras; it is standing where each radio will go and looking back at the house. We put a ladder against the garage or barn where the far radio would mount, climb to that height, and check whether the house is visible over the hedges, the stone wall and the parked cars. If it is not, we look for a higher spot, a different wall, or a third building that can see both ends. Then we look up and note which trees are bare that day and which will be full by June.

After the sightline, we test the signal for real: a temporary link between the two points, a speed test across it, and cellular readings at every gate and far shed for each carrier. Then we find the power — an outlet inside the garage, a circuit at the gate operator, or a sunny patch for a panel. Sightline, signal and power, written down for each building, are what turn a wish list into a system that still works the morning after a storm.

FAQ

Westchester County Wireless Camera Installation FAQ

Will deer, branches and wind set off alerts all night on cameras watching a wooded lot?
+

Not if the detection is set up properly. We use cameras and recorders that classify people and vehicles rather than reacting to any change in the picture, draw detection zones that leave out swaying branches and the road, and set a minimum size so a deer or a fox crossing the lawn does not count as a person. During the first week we review what triggered alerts and tighten the zones until the alerts you get are ones worth looking at.

Can we get an alert only when someone opens the pool gate, not every time a person walks past it?
+

Yes, in two ways. A camera can be given a line-crossing or entry zone drawn right at the gate opening, so it alerts only when someone passes through it rather than along the fence. For a firmer trigger, a gate contact can be wired to the recorder or alarm so the gate itself sends the alert and the camera bookmarks the clip at that moment. We set the schedule so it can be quiet while the family is using the pool.

Can a camera read the plate of a car coming up a long, dark driveway?
+

A dedicated plate camera can, but an ordinary wide camera will not. Plates are read where the car is slowest and closest, usually at the gate or a bend, with a narrow lens aimed at plate height and a shutter set for motion at night. We pair it with a wide overview camera so you see both the car and the plate in the same moment. Headlights shining straight into the lens are the usual problem, so we angle the camera to catch the rear plate or the car from the side.

Where should the recorder go in a large house so a burglar cannot simply carry it out?
+

Somewhere out of sight and out of the obvious path: a locked closet, a basement utility room or a cabinet in a room nobody would search first, never next to the TV or in the front hall. It needs ventilation, a network connection and a place on the battery backup. For extra protection, clips can also be copied to cloud storage or to a second device in another part of the house, so taking the recorder does not take the evidence with it.

What happens to the footage if someone pulls a camera off the wall and takes it?
+

On a recorder-based system, everything up to the moment the camera was pulled is already on the recorder inside the house, including the face of the person reaching for it. Battery cameras that store only to their own card lose whatever is on that card, which is one reason we prefer a recorder or base station for anything important. We mount cameras high enough that pulling one down takes a ladder, and a camera going offline triggers its own alert.

Can you do the installation while we are away, with our caretaker letting you in?
+

Yes, and many Westchester estate jobs are done that way. We agree the design and estimate with you first, then work with the caretaker or house manager for access, keys and gate codes. You get photos as each part goes in, and the final handover can be done by video call with your phone, so your app and logins are set up before you return. We schedule noisy work, like drilling or ladder work near bedrooms, around anyone staying in the house.

Our north-county internet is slow on upload. Will remote viewing still work?
+

Usually, with the right settings. Remote viewing depends on your upload speed, not your download speed, and rural connections are often weak there. We set each camera to send a smaller secondary stream for remote viewing while recording full resolution locally, so you can watch from your phone without saturating the line. If the line is very slow, we limit remote live view to a few cameras at a time and rely on alerts with short clips.

Police in Pelham Manor warned about intruders hunting for car key fobs. Which cameras matter most if the worry is the car in the driveway?
+

ABC7 reported Pelham Manor police describing break-ins where the intruders went inside to find a key fob and then drove off with the car. For that risk, the cameras that matter most are one covering the door people actually use at night, usually a side or back door rather than the front, one looking along the driveway where cars are parked, and one at the street end of the drive to catch the vehicle leaving. Police advice in that report was to lock cars and hide key fobs and to park in the garage; cameras add the record of who came and went.

How soon can you come out, and what do you need from us first?
+

Send the phone video, the town and a rough distance to the farthest building, and we can usually give you a range the same day and book a walk-through within the week. For the visit we need access to the modem and electrical panel, a ladder-safe path to each outbuilding, gate codes, and someone who knows where power exists at the garage, barn and gate. If you have a survey or site plan, have it on hand.
