The Neighborhood

Putnam County CCTV Begins Where the Public Road Ends

Putnam has the fewest homes of the six Hudson Valley counties we cover and some of the lowest crime counts, and residents tend to say so first when they call us. What they say second is more useful: the house sits 200 or 400 feet up from the road, the woods close in on three sides, the nearest neighbor is out of sight, and the one camera they bought online loses its Wi-Fi somewhere around the second bend in the driveway. A Putnam house is rarely seen by anyone passing by. That is pleasant to live in and convenient for anyone who wants to visit unseen.

The county itself has been building a different kind of camera network. In late summer 2026 the Putnam County Sheriff’s Office and the Carmel and Kent police chiefs published how the county’s license-plate readers work: 48 cameras at 21 fixed sites along county roads and state routes, data kept on a government server through the regional Real Time Crime Center, and searches allowed only for criminal investigations. Between January 1 and August 11, 2026, those readers generated 808 investigative alerts on 250 vehicles of interest, including 184 stolen vehicles, 27 vehicles tied to known burglary crews and 22 linked to organized retail theft rings. Nearly half of the burglary-crew alerts, 46 percent, fired at cameras on the county line, the moment those cars entered Putnam.

“When one of these vehicles shows up here, it is a strong indicator that a crew is canvassing our neighborhoods for target homes.”
Putnam County Sheriff’s Office, quoted in the Putnam County Press, August 23, 2026

Those readers stop where the public road stops. They photograph plates on Route 6, Route 22, Route 9D or Route 301; they do not see a car that turns into your driveway, idles by the garage and leaves. That last few hundred feet is the part private CCTV surveillance covers, and it is why our Putnam layouts almost always begin at the road and work back toward the house: a camera that sees each vehicle enter, one on the approach where faces become clear, then the doors, the garage and any outbuilding that holds tools, a generator or a boat.

There is a second reason Putnam owners ask for a wired recorder rather than a cloud subscription. The same months that brought those LPR numbers also brought a public argument about who gets to see camera data. At a September 9, 2026 meeting of the Legislature’s Protective Services Committee, Philipstown residents pressed the Sheriff on data sharing, and The Highlands Current covered the exchange. A system that records to a drive in your own basement, with logins only you hand out, sidesteps that question for your property entirely. We set it up that way by default.

The state’s Division of Criminal Justice Services counted 519 index crimes in the county in 2024, 456 of them property crimes: 38 burglaries, 400 larcenies and 18 motor vehicle thefts. Larceny rose from 341 in 2023 to 400 in 2024, about 17 percent. Just over half of all reported index crimes, 272, were handled by the Sheriff’s Office, which, with the State Police, covers the towns that have no police department of their own: Patterson, Putnam Valley, Southeast outside Brewster village and Philipstown outside Cold Spring village.

What Putnam adds to an ordinary camera job

  • Driveways measured in hundreds of feet. The road, the mailbox and the place a car first appears are often past what one copper run can reach without help, so the road camera gets its own plan: a midpoint switch, an extender, fiber or a short wireless link.
  • Ledge, stone walls and old trees. Much of the county sits on rock a few inches under the topsoil, threaded with fieldstone walls and mature oaks and maples. Cable routes follow what is already there instead of trenching through it.
  • Weak cell signal. In October 2015 Senator Charles Schumer asked the FCC to investigate carrier coverage maps after a RootMetrics study placed the Hudson Valley, including Putnam, last of 125 metro areas for mobile network performance. A recorder that records locally does not care how many bars you have.
  • NYSEG outages. After the May 15, 2018 storms, NYSEG had 28,043 customers in Putnam without power the next morning, with more than 4,000 each in Southeast, Putnam Valley, Patterson, Kent and Carmel. A battery under the recorder is standard on our Putnam quotes.
  • Watershed land on the property line. New York City’s West Branch, Boyd Corners and Croton Falls reservoirs sit in Kent, Carmel and Southeast, and many houses back onto city-owned buffer land. No neighbor behind you means no witness behind you.
  • Lake communities. Lake Mahopac, Lake Carmel, Lake Oscawana, Lake Peekskill, Lake Gleneida and Peach Lake ring the county with small lots, private roads, docks and cottages closed from fall to spring.
“There are more cell phone dead zones in Northern Westchester and Putnam Counties than post-winter potholes on the West Side Highway.”
U.S. Senator Charles E. Schumer, press release, October 5, 2015

Home broadband is better than the cell map suggests: the same Census survey finds 94.6 percent of Putnam households with a broadband subscription. The gap is inside and around the house, where a phone may have no signal in the kitchen and full signal at the end of the drive, and in the cottages and outbuildings that never got a line at all. That is also the honest answer to whether CCTV cameras can record without internet: every system on this page records first and connects second. The cameras talk to the recorder over cable we pull, the recorder writes to its own drive, and the internet only matters when you want to watch from somewhere else.

Carmel and Mahopac. The county’s largest town, with 12,783 housing units, the county seat in Carmel hamlet and the lake neighborhoods around Lake Mahopac and Lake Gleneida. Carmel PD reported 91 larcenies in 2024. Our Carmel work splits between split-levels and colonials on cul-de-sacs, lakefront houses with a dock and a detached garage, and the stores, banks and restaurants along Route 6 and Route 6N.

Southeast and Brewster. The most built-up part of Putnam, with the lowest detached share at 61.3 percent and the highest share of homes on utility gas. Brewster village has older two- and three-story buildings with apartments over shops, townhouse and condo communities sit off Route 312 and Route 6, and the I-84 and I-684 interchanges bring trucks, contractors and commuter parking. Business CCTV, multi-building condo systems and lot cameras are most of what we do here.

Kent. Hilly and wooded, with 83.9 percent detached houses, the Lake Carmel and Kent Lakes neighborhoods, and long stretches of Route 301 and Route 52 bordered by city reservoir land. Kent PD’s larceny count tripled from 17 to 55 between 2023 and 2024. Kent jobs often include a lake cottage with a crawl space, a camera on a steep driveway and a run to a detached garage.

Putnam Valley. Almost entirely detached houses at 95.6 percent, the highest vacancy rate in the county at 17.3 percent, and 451 seasonal-use homes, the most of any Putnam town, many around Lake Oscawana, Lake Peekskill and Roaring Brook Lake. These are the owners who watch from somewhere else most of the week, so remote viewing, health alerts and a heated spot for the recorder come first.

Philipstown, Cold Spring and Garrison. The river side of the county, with the oldest housing (26.3 percent built before 1940), Cold Spring’s Main Street historic district, Nelsonville and the estates and farms of Garrison. Village buildings need small, tidy cameras and routes that respect old plaster and trim; the large properties in Garrison need the long-run planning described below.

Patterson. Northeast Putnam along Route 22 and Route 311, with farm fields, older farmhouses, newer subdivisions and a short drive to Connecticut. Larger lots, barns and equipment buildings make Patterson the town where we most often put a recorder in the house and a small switch in an outbuilding.

Materials and mounting, house by house

Cedar shingle and clapboard. Exterior home CCTV installation on wood is its own craft. A lot of Putnam houses, especially around the lakes and in Garrison, are clad in cedar shingles or wood clapboard rather than vinyl. Wood moves and holds water at every hole. We mount on a block cut to the siding profile or on a junction box, seal the fasteners and the cable entry, and keep the camera base flat so rain runs off rather than into the wall. Cable usually drops from the attic or rises from the basement rim joist straight behind the camera, which is how home CCTV installation with hidden wiring looks on a shingled house.

Fieldstone, block and ledge. CCTV camera mounting on old stone takes judgment. Cellars and retaining walls in older houses are often dry-laid or mortared fieldstone. Drilling into a single stone works if the stone is sound; drilling into a mortar joint that is already crumbling does not. Where the stone is doubtful, we mount on a wooden sill, a deck ledger or the fascia above instead. Outside, Putnam’s ledge means a trench may hit rock after a few inches, so buried runs are planned around it and surface runs follow walls, fences and the house itself.

Log, timber-frame and contemporary houses. Exposed beams, cathedral ceilings and window walls leave few hollow cavities. We route through closets, along beams and under floors, and use painted raceway where there is no hidden path, which is still the cleanest way to install CCTV cameras without visible wires on a house that was never built for them.

Cottages on piers and crawl spaces. CCTV conduit installation earns its cost here. Many lake cottages sit on short piers or over a shallow crawl space with a vapor barrier. Cable under the house runs in conduit or is fastened up off the ground, because mice, squirrels and seasonal dampness are hard on bare cable. The recorder stays inside the heated part of the cottage, never in an unheated porch closet that freezes when the house is closed.

Rules every Putnam business camera system should follow

The points below summarize state statutes and one village rule so you can plan a layout. They are not legal advice, and a lawyer should review anything specific to your business.

  • Bathrooms and changing areas are off limits. If you employ staff, the state Labor Law (section 203-c) forbids you to video record any restroom, locker room or room where employees change clothes; only a court order changes that.
  • Audio is a separate decision. Under New York’s eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.05 and the definitions in §250.00), recording a conversation you are not part of without a participant’s consent is a crime. Our business installs ship with audio switched off.
  • Private spaces stay private. Penal Law §250.45, unlawful surveillance, covers secretly recording people undressing or in places where they reasonably expect privacy. We will not aim cameras into neighbors’ windows or yards.
  • Cold Spring’s historic district. The Village of Cold Spring’s Historic District Review Board reviews exterior changes to buildings in the district under Chapter 64 of the village code, through a Certificate of Appropriateness. A camera on a Main Street facade is an exterior change, so ask the village Building Department before the mounts go up; we can supply cut sheets and photos for the application.
  • Tell people. A sign at the door saying the premises are recorded costs a few dollars, discourages casual theft and is something many insurance carriers like to see.
Your Questions Answered

CCTV in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Putnam owners ask us on survey calls. Very few of them are about megapixels. Most are about the road, the rock, the lake, the signal and who gets to see the footage.

What does a burglary crew checking out Putnam houses look like on a driveway camera?

Usually like nothing much, which is the point. The Sheriff’s Office says burglary-crew vehicles showing up on its road cameras are a strong sign a crew is canvassing neighborhoods for target homes, and on a private driveway camera canvassing tends to look ordinary: a car that pulls in, sits for a minute and backs out; someone walking up with a clipboard or a package that is never left; a knock at the front door in the middle of a weekday, followed by a walk around to the side of the house when nobody answers. Each event alone means little. A pattern across a few days, or the same car at two houses on your road, means a lot.

The cameras that catch it are the one at the driveway entrance, set to record every vehicle with a time stamp the investigators can trust, and a face-height camera at the front door. We set vehicle alerts for the hours the house is usually empty, so a car turning in at 1 p.m. on a Tuesday reaches your phone. If something looks wrong, save the clip and call the Sheriff’s Office or your town police with the time and a description of the vehicle; that is information the county’s plate readers can work with.

Our land borders New York City watershed property. Where can our cameras point?

Point them at your own house, your own driveway and your own outbuildings, and draw the rear cameras so they cover your yard and the edge of the tree line rather than deep into the city land. In Kent, Carmel and Southeast, many lots back onto land owned by the New York City Department of Environmental Protection around the West Branch, Boyd Corners and Croton Falls reservoirs. That land may be open to permit holders for fishing and hiking, so people can pass behind your property legitimately.

What you want from those rear cameras is a clear record of anyone who crosses from the woods into your yard, not hours of video of an empty forest. We aim them slightly downward toward the lawn line, add a person-detection zone that starts at your side of the boundary, and use privacy masking on any section of the view you do not need. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.

Can I still get camera alerts at home when my phone gets no cell signal inside the house?

Yes, as long as the house has internet and your phone is on the home Wi-Fi. Camera apps send alerts over whatever data connection the phone has, and Wi-Fi counts. Plenty of Putnam houses have one bar of cell service in the kitchen and a solid cable or fiber line in the basement, and the alerts arrive normally. We test alerts on your phone both on the home Wi-Fi and on cellular before we leave, so you know which places on the property work.

If the internet itself goes down, the recorder keeps recording to its drive and you can watch live and play back on the monitor in the house, which needs no internet at all. When the line comes back, the app reconnects on its own. For owners who are often away in places with weak coverage, we keep the app’s live view set to a small substream so it loads on a slow connection instead of spinning.

Our driveway curves through the woods. How do you cover a drive no single camera can see end to end?

With two or three cameras that hand the view off to each other, each with its own job. The first sits near the road where vehicles slow to turn in, zoomed tight with a varifocal lens so the car, and ideally the plate, fills the frame. The second covers the last stretch where the drive comes out of the trees near the house, where faces and the vehicle’s side become clear. On a long S-curve, a third may cover a gate, a bridge or a parking pad halfway up. Together they show a vehicle arriving, how long it took to reach the house and whether it left the same way.

Each of those cameras is aimed across the vehicle’s path rather than straight down the drive, so oncoming headlights pass the lens instead of blinding it, and each gets its own night settings. The near-house camera is easy to wire from the house; the road camera is the one that needs a planned route and is priced after we walk it, because a drive that curves around ledge and old trees can be twice as long on the ground as it looks from above.

How do you keep mice and squirrels from chewing camera cable in a lake cottage crawl space or attic?

Rodents chew cable for the same reason they chew anything: it is there, it is soft, and in a closed cottage nobody disturbs them for months. A single chewed run can take a camera offline all winter, and you will not notice until you check the app in March. We avoid laying cable loose on the ground of a crawl space or across attic insulation where mice travel.

Instead, cable in crawl spaces and attics runs in conduit or is fastened up along joists and rafters, and the point where it leaves the house is sealed so the hole does not become a doorway. Outdoor runs near the ground go in conduit. We also turn on the recorder’s video-loss alerts, so if a camera does drop offline in an empty cottage, your phone tells you that week rather than in spring.

Can camera cable follow a stone wall or a fence line where ledge stops any digging?

Often that is the best route on a Putnam property. Old fieldstone walls and post-and-rail fences tend to run exactly where you want cable to go, along the drive or the property edge, and a trench a few feet away may hit ledge after six inches. Cable rated for outdoor use and sunlight, inside UV-rated conduit, can run along the base of a wall or be fixed to fence posts, tucked low and out of the way of mowers and plows.

Where the route crosses the driveway or a lawn that gets mowed, we cut a shallow channel for conduit or go overhead instead. The run is measured and planned on the survey, because following a wall that curves around a field can add a lot of length, and anything near the 100-meter copper limit needs a powered midpoint, an extender or fiber rather than hope.

Can camera cable be strung overhead between the house and a garage or shed?

Yes, and on rocky lots it is common for garage CCTV installation. An aerial run uses a steel support wire, called a messenger, tensioned between the two buildings, with the camera cable lashed to it, so the cable itself never carries its own weight or a load of ice. Rated outdoor cable, drip loops at both ends and sealed entries keep water from following the cable inside. The span needs enough height to clear vehicles, snowplows and anyone carrying a ladder.

The weakness of an overhead run is the same as the power lines along your road: falling limbs. We look up before we plan one and keep it out from under dead branches. If the span would pass under big trees, or if the outbuilding has its own electric service, fiber in conduit on the ground or a short point-to-point wireless link may be the better choice, and we will price both so you can compare.

Our lake community maintains a private road. Can the road association put a camera at the entrance?

Usually yes, if the association has authority over the road and the board approves it. Several Putnam lake neighborhoods are reached by private roads maintained by a road or lake association, and the entrance is the single point every vehicle passes. A camera there, mounted on an association pole or with a property owner’s written permission on their land, records every car in and out, which helps after package thefts, break-ins at closed cottages and damage to the road itself.

The details belong in writing: who owns the camera and recorder, where the recorder lives, who can view footage, how long it is kept and how a member requests a clip. We prepare a short scope and camera map for the board, keep the view on the roadway and away from members’ windows, and set up a separate login for the officer who handles footage requests.

Can a camera tell us when someone comes ashore at our lake house from the water side?

Yes, and on many Putnam lakes the shore is the least watched side of the house. Kayaks, canoes and small boats can reach a dock at night without passing any road, and in a closed cottage nobody would notice for weeks. A camera mounted on the house or a dock post, aimed down at the dock and the path up from the water, records anyone who steps ashore. The detection line is drawn across the top of the dock and the shoreline path, not over the open water, so a passing boat or a heron does not set off an alert.

Water is hard on cameras. Afternoon glare off the lake, reflected lights at night and morning fog all fool basic motion detection, so a camera with person detection and good low-light performance does much better here than a cheap motion camera. Any part of the frame that shows a neighbor’s dock or yard is blacked out with a privacy mask. When the lake freezes, the same line still catches anyone who walks across the ice to your shore.

Does a camera on a building in Cold Spring’s historic district need village review?

It may. The Village of Cold Spring’s Historic District Review Board reviews exterior changes to buildings in the historic district, and the village code sets out a Certificate of Appropriateness process for them. A camera bolted to a Main Street facade, a conduit run up the brick or a new box on the front of a building can count as an exterior change, so the right first step is a call or visit to the village Building Department at Village Hall, 85 Main Street.

From our side, we help the application along with a cut sheet for each camera, photos of the planned locations and a description of how the cable is hidden. In practice, the least visible plan is also the easiest to approve: small cameras placed under cornices or awnings, mounted into mortar joints rather than face brick, with cable run inside the building wherever possible.

This is a parking lot CCTV installation question first: give each entrance its own camera, aimed at the spot where vehicles slow down to turn, and add one wide camera that ties them together. A lot with an entrance on Route 22 and another off a side street needs both covered, because a driver who knows the area will use the one less likely to be watched. Each entrance camera is set for plates and faces through the windshield; the wide camera shows where each vehicle parked and how long it stayed.

On the recorder, we name the cameras by entrance so an owner searching footage at 11 p.m. does not have to guess which tile is which. Five install decisions that matter after the first winter

Cable choice. CCTV wiring inside the house is Cat6, the standard for PoE camera installation; on longer runs or for cameras with heaters, motors or deterrent lights we may step up to Cat6A. Outside, the cable is rated for outdoor and UV exposure, and anything underground is direct-burial rated or in conduit. Between buildings or down a long drive, fiber optic CCTV installation carries the signal with no electricity on the line, which also keeps a lightning surge at the far end from traveling back to the recorder. Every cable is labeled at both ends so the next technician does not have to guess.

Power budget. An 802.3af PoE port can supply up to 15.4 watts and an 802.3at port up to 30 watts. A plain fixed camera uses a fraction of that by day, but infrared at night, a heater in a cold housing and a deterrent light all add up on a January night. We total the worst case for every camera on the switch and leave headroom, so the coldest night of the year is not the night cameras start rebooting.

Storage and compression. How much storage do CCTV cameras need? Less than people expect when the system runs H.265, which stores a similar picture in much less space than H.264; we switch it on wherever both the camera and the recorder support it. Every system we sell keeps at least two weeks of recording; for a seasonal house or a store that wants a month or more, a CCTV storage upgrade means a larger drive or a second one and confirm the real retention after a few days of recording.

Placement. Where should CCTV cameras be installed? CCTV camera placement on a wooded lot starts at the road and moves inward: where vehicles appear, where people walk, where doors open, where valuable things are stored. Face cameras go at roughly 8 to 10 feet so they look at people rather than at the tops of their heads. Lot and road cameras go higher with tighter lenses.

Network security. CCTV remote access setup is where cheap installs cut corners. Default passwords are changed on every device, unused services are switched off, cameras on business networks sit on their own segment, and remote access goes through the manufacturer’s secure app rather than an open port on your router.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV in Putnam County

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about cameras for a house in Putnam and you get advice written for a suburb with sidewalks, streetlights and five bars of cell service. Cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr average national numbers that say nothing about ledge or a 400-foot drive. Here is where the generic answers go wrong here, and what we do instead.

“Putnam is so safe that cameras are a waste of money”

Putnam’s crime counts are low; nobody disputes that. But low is not zero, and the trend in the most camera-relevant category went the wrong way: the state counted 400 larcenies in the county in 2024, up from 341 the year before, and the Kent police reported 55 after 17. Larceny is the theft of things left outside or in reach: packages, tools, a generator, a catalytic converter, merchandise from a shelf.

The Sheriff’s own plate-reader report adds context the totals do not show. Twenty-seven vehicles tied to known burglary crews and 184 stolen vehicles were flagged on Putnam roads in just over seven months of 2026, and the Sheriff’s Office described stolen cars as typically driven into the county by people intending to commit more thefts.

A camera system does not have to stop a crime to be worth having. Its job is to show what happened, clearly enough that a deputy, an insurer or you can act on it. In a county where a patrol car may be miles away, recorded footage is often the only witness.

“The county’s cameras already watch my road, so I am covered”

The county’s 48 license-plate readers sit at 21 fixed sites on public roads. They capture plates, not faces, and they are searchable only by police during a criminal investigation. They will not show you who walked up your driveway, which door they tried or what they carried away.

They also do not cover most roads. A reader on a state route sees traffic on that route; it does not see the dead-end lane or lake road your house sits on, or anything past the end of the pavement.

The two are complementary. Your system records the event on your property and the vehicle at your driveway; the road readers can help investigators trace where that vehicle went. Neither replaces the other, and the private system is the only one you control.

“Any camera app works the same in Putnam as it does in the city”

The app may be the same, but the connection under it is not. When Senator Schumer asked the FCC to look at carrier coverage maps in 2015, he cited a RootMetrics study that put the Hudson Valley, including Putnam, last of 125 metro areas for mobile performance. Coverage has improved since, but dead spots in hollows, along the reservoirs and inside stone houses are still common.

A camera that depends on the cloud to record is only as reliable as the link from your house to that cloud. A camera that records locally to a recorder in the house does not need the link at all to do its main job.

We set up remote viewing to suit the connection you have: small substreams for live view on a phone, event clips instead of continuous uploads, and alerts that work over home Wi-Fi when the cell signal inside is weak.

“Cloud cameras are more private than a box in the basement”

This one gets repeated because cloud services encrypt their uploads, which is true. But privacy is also about who holds the footage and on what terms. With a cloud-only camera, your video sits on a company’s servers under that company’s policies, and you pay a monthly fee per camera to keep it there.

Putnam residents have spent much of 2026 debating exactly that question about public cameras: where data is stored, who can search it, and for how long. The same reasoning applies at home. A recorder on your premises, with an admin password only you hold, keeps the footage in your hands.

We still offer off-site copies for owners who want them, because a burglar can take a recorder. The answer is to hide or lock the recorder, put it on a battery, and add an off-site copy of key events on top of local recording if you choose, not instead of it.

“A small battery backup from an office store keeps cameras up all night”

A small UPS meant for a desktop computer may run a recorder and a loaded PoE switch for only a few minutes, and many are not set up to tell the recorder to shut down cleanly before the battery dies. A sudden power cut in the middle of writing is hard on hard drives.

Outages here are not hypothetical. NYSEG had about 13,000 Putnam customers out after the October 29–30, 2017 storm and 28,043 the morning after the May 15, 2018 storms. Long outages need a plan: a UPS sized to the real load, the recorder on a generator circuit if the house has one, and settings that bring every camera back on its own when power returns.

We size the battery to your equipment, connect its shutdown signal where the recorder supports it, and test a power cut before we leave. If you have a standby generator, the UPS only has to cover the seconds before it starts.

“Lay the cable on the ground and cover it with mulch”

It works for a summer. Then a mower or a string trimmer finds it, a plow catches it at the edge of the driveway, or a squirrel decides it is food. Indoor-rated cable left outside also cracks in the sun and wicks water inside the jacket.

On Putnam lots, where ledge often stops a proper trench, the alternatives are better than mulch: UV-rated conduit along a stone wall or fence line, an aerial run on a messenger wire, a shallow channel cut where the route crosses lawn or pavement, or fiber or a wireless link to skip the ground entirely.

Each of those is priced after we confirm the layout, because the right one depends on the distance, the obstacles and what is at the far end. What we will not do is leave a run where the first mowing of spring will cut it.

“Strap battery cameras to trees and you have covered a wooded lot”

Battery cameras appeal on a wooded lot because there is no cable to run. The trouble is that they sleep to save power and wake on motion, so they often catch the second half of an event. Cold Putnam winters drain batteries faster, and someone has to climb a ladder to recharge them.

Trees add their own problems. Trunks sway in wind, which shakes the picture and triggers false alerts, and a tree-mounted camera on Wi-Fi is usually at the edge of the router’s range, especially once leaves come in.

We wire cameras to the house or to a post or outbuilding where we can, and use a properly mounted pole with its own power when a camera must stand alone.

Eight Questions to Ask Any Putnam CCTV Company

Whoever you hire, a handful of questions separate an installer who has worked on Putnam lots from one who has only done townhouses. Ask them of every company and compare the answers in writing.

Will the camera app work if our only internet is Starlink or a cellular home-internet box?
+

In most cases, yes. The recorder app connects outward through the manufacturer's relay service, so it does not need a public address or an open port on your router. Live view on a slow or busy connection uses a small substream, and alerts arrive as short notifications. Recording itself never depends on the connection; it continues on the recorder's drive either way.

Can the system tell me when the power at the house goes out?
+

It can, with the right parts. When the recorder and switch run on a battery backup that supports network alerts, or when the recorder itself reports power events, we set up a notification to your phone as long as the internet equipment is also on backup. At a minimum, the app shows the cameras going offline, which tells you something is wrong at the house.

Is a yearly check-up worth paying for before a Putnam winter?
+
