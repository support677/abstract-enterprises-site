The Neighborhood

Why Crotona Park East Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Crotona Park East sits between the Cross Bronx Expressway to the north, the Bronx River to the east, East 167th Street to the south and Crotona and Prospect Avenues to the west, with Southern Boulevard running through the middle, in the description Wikipedia gives. It is in Bronx Community District 3, on ZIP codes 10459 and 10460, and it is the neighborhood that Charlotte Street made famous. President Carter stood among the burned tenements there in October 1977; President Reagan came in 1980; by the time President Clinton visited in 1995, the same blocks were lined with ranch houses and picket fences.

That history is not background for a buzzer repair. It is the reason the repair is different here. Most Bronx neighborhoods we work in have a housing stock that built up over a century, so their door systems range from pre-war bell wire to last year's video panel, building by building. Crotona Park East lost most of its pre-war stock in a decade and rebuilt much of it in two more. The result is a neighborhood where a very large share of the front doors were fitted with door hardware in the same few years, by a handful of developers, under a handful of programs, and where that hardware is now aging together.

To put numbers on it we took the city's PLUTO tax-lot file for Community District 3, kept the lots in ZIP codes 10459 and 10460, and dropped the blocks south of East 167th Street. That leaves 1,846 lots, 1,538 of them with homes on them, holding 14,448 apartments. Of those residential lots, 821 were built between 1980 and 2009, which is more than half, and the median year among them is 1992. Only three residential lots in the whole area were built between 1968 and 1979, the decade the neighborhood burned.

The rebuild did not put up apartment towers. Of those 821 rebuild-era lots, 221 are one-family houses, 386 are two-family houses and 186 are three-family houses; together that is 793 houses. Across all ages, one-, two- and three-family houses make up 1,160 of the 1,538 residential lots, about three lots in four. But because each house holds only one to three households, they hold just 2,419 of the 14,448 apartments, about one in six. The other five in six live in the 331 buildings with eight or more apartments: the surviving and rehabilitated walk-ups along Bryant, Hoe, Vyse, Longfellow and Prospect Avenues, and the large buildings put up since 2010 on Boone Avenue, West Farms Road, Southern Boulevard and Boston Road.

That split, most of the doors on one side and most of the people on the other, is the thread through this page.

That is true of households, because most households live in the big buildings, and it hides the other fact: most of the front doors are on houses, many of them owner-occupied, where the owner is also the landlord of a floor or two. Those owners are the people least likely to get a reminder from the city that the buzzer needs attention, and the ones most likely to call us when it finally stops.

Two Sets of Records

What the City Sees at Crotona Park East's Front Doors, and What It Misses

There are two public records that track broken buzzers in New York City, and in Crotona Park East both of them look almost entirely at apartment buildings.

The first is HPD's list of housing maintenance code violations. An inspector who finds a dead intercom or a door release that does not work writes it up with wording like "properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system", and it goes into the public dataset. In the Community District 3 part of ZIP codes 10459 and 10460 the dataset holds 675 such violations; 600 of them are in our study area north of East 167th Street, at 168 buildings. Of those 600, just 33 were written at one-, two- or three-family houses, about 5.5 percent, although those houses are about 75 percent of the residential lots.

The second is 311. Since 2020 the city has logged complaints under the descriptor "bell, buzzer, intercom". The Community District 3 part of the two ZIP codes produced 867 of them up to October 22, 2026. One six-story building just south of East 167th Street accounts for 263 of those on its own; in the study area there were 567 complaints from 156 buildings, and 18 of them came from houses.

None of this means the houses' buzzers work better. It means the rules and the records were built for something else. The Multiple Dwelling Law, and the HPD rules that follow from it, are written for buildings with three or more households, which is where the intercom requirement in section 50-a lives and where HPD's self-closing door and entrance rules are enforced. A one- or two-family house is outside that law altogether. A three-family house is inside it, but its owner usually lives in it and its tenants are rarely the people who call 311. So the houses do not show up, and a buzzer that has been half-working since 2015 can stay that way until it stops completely.

What the records do show about the buildings

For the apartment buildings the records are detailed and they tell a clear story. Intercom violations in the two ZIP codes have been climbing: 71 were written in 2024, 70 in 2025 and 61 in 2026 up to October 22, against yearly totals between 15 and 40 through the 2010s. Of the 675 in the two ZIP codes, 515 were Class B, 143 Class A and 17 Class C, which matters because the class decides how long the owner has to correct it.

The violations are not spread evenly by building age either. Joined to the PLUTO file, the pre-1931 walk-ups carry 354 of the 600, about 66 violations for every thousand apartments they hold. The 1931 to 1967 buildings carry 90, or 44 per thousand. Buildings from the 2000s carry 41 per thousand, those from the 1990s 27, and those finished since 2010, 18. Lots built in the 1980s, which are mostly the Charlotte Gardens houses and a few NYCHA rehabilitations, carry none at all.

The 311 complaints rose fastest in 2022, when the two ZIP codes logged 185 against 90 the year before, and have stayed above a hundred a year since. The year with the most complaints is not always the year with the most violations, because a complaint does not always lead to an inspection and an inspection does not always find the fault still there.

Self-closing doors are the other entrance item HPD writes up in the same buildings, and here the numbers are larger again: 3,809 self-closing door violations in the same two ZIP codes, 2,994 of them Class C, the immediately hazardous class. A door that will not close and latch on its own is often the reason a buzzer seems not to work, and a buzzer that does not work is often why the door gets propped. In a building, we look at both before quoting.

How It Was Built

Four Waves of Rebuilding, Four Kinds of Door System

The rebuilding came in recognizable waves, and each wave left a different kind of door system behind. Knowing which wave your house came from is the fastest way to know what is behind the button.

1985 to 1989: Charlotte Gardens and the ranch houses

The first wave was the most famous. The Mid-Bronx Desperadoes, the coalition Genevieve Brooks built from block associations and a community day care center, developed prefabricated one-family ranch houses on the cleared blocks around Charlotte Street. A People's Guide to New York puts the number at 89, sold for $50,000 to $60,000 to buyers chosen from more than 500 applicants; Gothamist counts 92 and tells how two model homes were trucked over the George Washington Bridge one night in 1983; Wikipedia says the effort started with 90. The city's records show 88 one-story houses built from 1985 to 1989 on Charlotte Street, Louis Niñé Boulevard, East 172nd Street, Seabury Place, Minford Place, Boston Road and Crotona Park East.

A one-family ranch house has the simplest door system there is: a button by the door, a bell or chime inside, a small transformer, and in some houses a release in the front door frame added later. Because the houses were built in a factory and finished on site, the bell wire may run through walls that were closed before they arrived, with the transformer in a utility space rather than a cellar. Forty years on, the common faults are the button, which has been through forty winters on vinyl siding, the transformer, and the run where it passes through a crawl space or an outside wall.

1991–92 and 1995–96: the two-family houses

The second wave was larger and less famous. In 1991 and 1992 alone, 251 residential lots in the study area were built, and two-family houses dominate the decade: 347 of the 386 rebuild-era two-family houses went up in the 1990s, lined along Vyse, Longfellow, Bryant and Hoe Avenues and Simpson and Chisholm Streets. A New York Times real estate column in December 1997 described the South Bronx revival "shifting to 2-family houses", naming Housing Partnership developments such as Longfellow Estates and Horizon Homes and their target buyers' incomes. We did not read the full article; the summary line is what we cite.

A program two-family was sold to an owner who would live on one floor and rent the other. Its door system was built for that: two buttons at the door, a bell or a simple audio station on each floor, one transformer, one release, all on one short run. It is the house version of an intercom. Thirty years later, many have been sold on, many have had their original stations replaced on one floor but not the other, and many have a video doorbell on the frame where one of the buttons used to be.

The 1990s and 2000s: the three-family houses

The third wave added a floor. Of the 186 rebuild-era three-family houses, 80 date from the 1990s and 106 from 2000 to 2009, with a burst in 2005 and 2006; they are concentrated on Hoe and Vyse Avenues, Freeman and Jennings Streets, Minford Place and Union Avenue in the city's records. With three households sharing a door, these houses were usually given a small apartment intercom rather than doorbells: a three-button audio or video station outside, a handset in each apartment, a release in the frame, and a power supply in the cellar. Many of those systems came from builders' suppliers whose product lines have since changed, which is why identifying the model is often the first job.

A three-family house is a multiple dwelling under the law, so it is the one kind of rebuild house that HPD's intercom and door rules reach. The records show it: of the 33 violations written at houses, 26 were at three-family houses, some of them from this wave and some of them older buildings converted to three apartments.

Since 2010: the large buildings

The fourth wave is not houses at all. Since 2010, 70 lots in the study area have been built with 3,492 apartments between them, nearly a quarter of all the apartments in the neighborhood, in buildings of eight to fifteen stories on Boone Avenue, West Farms Road, Southern Boulevard, Boston Road, Vyse Avenue and Crotona Park East. These came with networked door panels, fob or app access, cameras at the entrance and door controllers, and they are where the newest kinds of failure live: a panel that loses its internet connection, a directory that nobody updates, a controller that needs its software account.

Before all four waves are the survivors and the rehabilitations: 531 lots built before 1931 still stand, 177 of them walk-ups with eight or more apartments. Many of those were rebuilt inside by the same community groups in the 1990s and 2000s, so a 1910 building may have a 1995 door system behind its original stone. The records say those buildings carry most of the neighborhood's intercom violations. The next section explains why.

How Each Type Is Repaired

Methods and Materials by Building Type

Ranch houses: find the transformer first

In a one-story house without a full cellar, the hardest part of the job is often finding the transformer. It may be beside the electrical panel, on a junction box above a ceiling, in a utility closet beside the boiler or in the crawl space. Until it is found and measured, nothing else can be judged. We ask you on the phone where the panel and the boiler are, and we bring an inspection light and protective sheeting for the floors.

Once power is confirmed, the button comes off the siding and is tested on its own, and then the run is tested from the button to the chime. In factory-built houses, wire that was closed into walls before delivery can rarely be pulled out and replaced; a new run usually goes around rather than through, along a closet or the crawl space, and we show you the route before we start.

Two-family houses: test from both floors

The defining feature of a program two-family is that two households share one door and one set of wiring. The test that settles most faults is simple: have each floor press its own release while someone holds the door, and have someone press each button while each floor listens. If downstairs works and upstairs does not, the release, its power and the door are fine and the fault is in the upstairs station or its pair. If neither works, it is power or the release itself. We arrange access to both floors for the same half hour so both tests happen while we are there.

Many of these houses now have one original station and one replacement on the other floor, sometimes a different brand, sometimes a consumer video doorbell wired in where a button used to be. We check that the newer device and the older station can share the transformer without one starving the other.

Three-family houses: identify before ordering

A small three-apartment intercom from the turn of the century may carry a brand nobody stocks now. Before ordering anything, we photograph the station, read any model or board number inside, measure the supply voltage, and count the conductors at the station and in the cellar. That tells us whether a current station can be wired in its place, whether all three must change together, or whether the system can be repaired as it is. You get that answer in writing before any part is bought.

Walk-ups: the shared point first

In a walk-up with twenty or forty apartments, a fault that affects several apartments at once is nearly always at a point they share: the amplifier, a riser pair, a junction on a landing, the power supply. We find which apartments are affected first, by testing every station or by asking the super, and the pattern usually points to the shared point before any wall is opened.

In a rehabilitated tenement, the wiring is from the rehab, not from the original building. We look for the riser in the rebuilt stair hall or beside the plumbing chase, and for any conduit that would let a failed pair be replaced by pulling a new one.

New buildings: power, network, account, then hardware

In a building finished since 2010, a dead entrance is more often a lost network connection, a power supply on a tripped circuit, a lapsed software account or a door controller fault than a broken panel. We check those in that order, with the management's permission and the account details, before opening a panel. If the door itself is held by a magnetic lock, we check how its release is tied to the fire alarm and the exit device before changing anything, because that is part of the building's life safety system.

Codes and Rules

Rules That Apply at a Crotona Park East Front Door

  • Multiple dwellings and intercoms. The Attorney General's tenants' rights guide summarizes the rule from Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a: multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom with a buzz-open release; older multiple dwellings must provide the intercom if a majority of the apartments ask for it. One- and two-family houses are not multiple dwellings.
  • Doors that must close themselves. On HPD's self-closing door page, an entrance door in a multiple dwelling that fails to close and latch on its own is listed in the immediately hazardous class, C, with 14 days from the notice to correct it. Closers and latches get a look on every building call we make.
  • Line voltage. Work on the 120-volt side of a transformer or power supply, or a new circuit for one, is electrician's work under a permit where required. Our work is on the low-voltage side; where line-voltage work is needed, we say so and coordinate.
  • Magnetic locks and egress. Electrically locked doors on a way out must release under the conditions the building code sets, including on fire alarm and loss of power, with a manual means of exit. We do not change how an egress door releases without confirming what the code and the building's fire alarm require.
  • Reach and access. In places of public accommodation, controls such as call buttons should be within the 2010 ADA reach ranges, a forward reach of no more than 48 inches. We set replacement panels and buttons accordingly.
  • NYCHA property. NYCHA maintains the entrances of its buildings. Residents report entry faults to NYCHA by phone or through MyNYCHA.

This is general information about the rules we work under, not legal advice. If your building has an open violation or a dispute about who is responsible, the HPD record and the building's own documents decide it.

What the city's records can and cannot see

Nobody from the city ever inspects the buzzer in our two-family house. How do we tell whether a thirty-year-old system is wearing out or just has one fault?

One fault tends to be specific: one floor dead, one button sticking, a release that stopped after a storm or a renovation. Wear tends to be general and gradual: every bell a little quieter than it used to be, the release needing a second press, the speaker crackling on both floors, the plastic of the button plate gone chalky and loose. If you can name the day it stopped, it is probably one fault. If you cannot remember when it started being bad, it is probably wear.

That distinction matters more in Crotona Park East than in most of the Bronx because so many houses here were built within a few years of each other and have never had their door systems replaced. A worn system can still be repaired, and often should be, but it makes sense to price the repair against what is left of the rest. We say plainly after the diagnostic whether we think the failed part is the only tired one.

Because a one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, there is no HPD inspection cycle and no violation to tell you when it is time. That makes the owner the inspector. Pressing every button and every release once a season, and noting which ones are slower or quieter than last time, is the cheapest maintenance there is.

Half the houses on our block went up in the same two years. If the neighbor's buzzer was just repaired, does that tell you anything about ours?

Often, yes. Houses built in the same program year by the same developer were usually fitted with the same door hardware, run the same way: the same model of button plate, the same kind of release in the same kind of frame, the transformer in the same corner of the cellar. The city's property records show 251 of the study area's residential lots were built in 1991 and 1992 alone, so whole blocks share a birthday.

If your neighbor tells you what failed and what the technician replaced, tell us when you call. It will not replace a diagnosis of your house, because thirty years of different owners, renovations and repairs make every house different inside. It does tell us which parts to bring and where to look first, which makes a one-visit repair more likely.

It also tells you what is coming. If the neighbor's transformer gave out and yours is the same age and the same model, it is worth having ours measured at the same visit rather than waiting for it to fail on a winter evening.

Our block association wants every house on the street to deal with its buzzer at the same time. Can you price a whole block?

We can visit several houses on the same day, but each house is its own job with its own owner, its own diagnostic and its own written price. Houses that look identical from the sidewalk rarely need identical repairs after thirty years, and an owner should not be paying for a neighbor's problem. What a block arrangement does save is scheduling: one technician working down the street can reach each house within the same day.

The practical way to set it up is a list of addresses, an owner or contact for each, and a sentence about what each buzzer is doing now. We call each owner with a first read from their photos, then confirm the day. Each house is charged its own $250 service call, which includes that house's diagnostic, and anything beyond it is quoted per house.

If the block also wants a common standard, for example sealed outdoor buttons on every house or the same video doorbell model throughout, we can tell you which houses' wiring will support it before anyone buys anything.

The rebuild houses: ranch, two-family and three-family

The bell button on our vinyl-sided house keeps working loose and tilting. How should it be mounted?

Vinyl siding moves. It is hung loosely on purpose so it can expand and shrink with the temperature, and a button screwed straight through it into nothing solid will rock, crack the siding and let water into the wall around the wire. That is a common reason the button on a Crotona Park East house fails long before the rest of the system does.

The right fix is a mounting block: a small flat base made for siding that fits around the profile of the panels and is fastened through to the sheathing or framing behind, with the wire passing through its center. The button sits flat and still on the block, the siding keeps moving freely around it, and the hole for the wire is sealed. It is an inexpensive part and a short job once the wire is reached.

While the button is off, we look at the wire where it comes out of the wall. If water has been running down it for years, the last few inches are often green or brittle, and we cut back to clean copper before refitting.

Could mice or rats under the house have chewed through the doorbell wire?

Yes, it happens. Thin, unprotected bell wire stapled along joists or lying across a crawl space floor is easy for rodents to reach, and a chewed run can fail completely or, worse, short now and then so the bell rings by itself. If you have seen droppings or gnawed insulation near where the wire runs, mention it when you call.

We trace the run in sections until the damaged length is found. A short repair can be spliced and protected; a run that has been chewed in several places is usually better replaced, and where the route allows, the new wire is run in a protective sleeve or along a path rodents use less.

We repair the wiring, not the infestation. If there is an active problem under the house, deal with that first or at the same time, or the new wire will meet the same fate.

Our front door is in a hollow metal frame filled with foam. What does that change about fitting an electric release?

It changes the cutting and the fixing, not whether it can be done. Many houses built in the 1990s were given insulated steel doors in pressed metal frames, and a release has to be set into a pocket cut in that frame, with the wire brought to it inside the frame. In a foam-filled frame the foam is cleared from the pocket and the wire path rather than the frame being opened up.

The release has to suit both the frame and the lock. A release body made for metal frames, with the right faceplate for the latch it has to hold, sits flush and lasts; a wood-frame release forced into steel does not. We look at the lockset, the gap between door and frame and the condition of the hinges first, because a steel door that has dropped on its hinges will press on any release.

On our Bronx list a release replacement and alignment is $450 to $950. A first release cut into a frame that never had one sits at the upper part of that range or is quoted separately, and we tell you which before starting.

Walk-ups, senior buildings and the newest towers

Our walk-up is a 1910 building that a community group gutted and rebuilt inside in the 1990s. Does that mean the buzzer wiring runs in conduit we can pull new wire through?

Possibly, and it is worth finding out before anyone opens a wall. Gut rehabilitations of that period often ran new low-voltage wiring in the rebuilt stair hall or in a chase beside the plumbing, sometimes in conduit or raceway and sometimes simply stapled inside the new walls. The building's age on the city's records says 1910; its door system may be from the rehab, and the wiring path is from the rehab too.

We start at the panel and the cellar and look for where the riser goes up. If there is conduit with room in it, a failed riser pair can be replaced by pulling a new cable through, which is much quicker and cleaner than opening plaster on every landing. If there is not, we look for the next best route before quoting.

If the building's owner still has the rehab drawings, the electrical sheet usually shows the intercom riser. A photo of that sheet saves time.

Residents in our senior building take a while to reach their handset, and by the time the visitor pushes, the door has locked again. Can the unlock time be made longer?

Usually, yes. On most apartment systems the door release time is set at the lobby panel or the door controller, not at the handset, and it can be lengthened within limits. On older audio systems the release only lasts as long as the resident holds the button, and the change is a timer module or a different release setting. Either way, the visitor gets a longer window to push the door.

Longer is not always better. A door that stays unlocked for a long time after every buzz is easier to follow through, and a magnetic lock or a release held open too long can overheat if it was not rated for it. We set the time to what the residents need and the hardware is rated for, and we check that the door closer brings the door back and latches it before the next person comes along.

Crotona Park East has at least one building in the city's records built for older residents, and the question is a common one in buildings where many residents use walkers or canes. It is a short adjustment when the panel allows it.

Our building faces Crotona Park and the entrance is dark at night. Can the call panel light up so visitors can read the names and buttons?

Most current panels have backlit buttons and directories, and many older ones had a small lamp behind the directory card that has long since burned out or been disconnected. Restoring or adding that light is usually a small job, and it makes the panel usable after dark without anyone using a phone flashlight to find a name.

Power is the question. A lit panel draws more than an unlit one, and on an older system the extra load has to come from a supply that can carry it; we measure before we connect. If the entrance itself is dark, a light over the door helps more than a lit panel does, and that is electrician's work on the building's supply.

Buildings on the park side of Crotona Park East face the trees rather than other buildings, which is pleasant by day and dark at night. A readable panel also cuts down on visitors pressing every button to find the right one.

We live in one of the small NYCHA buildings near East 173rd Street. Is the front door system ours to have repaired, or NYCHA's?

If the city's records list the New York City Housing Authority as the owner of your building, the door and the intercom are NYCHA's to maintain, and the repair goes through NYCHA's own repair system, by phone or the MyNYCHA app, not through a private company. We do not repair entry systems on NYCHA property at a resident's request.

Crotona Park East has several small NYCHA developments rather than one large one: Wikipedia lists eight properties, including a three-story group on East 173rd Street and Vyse Avenue, a six-story building on Hoe Avenue and East 173rd Street, and rehabilitated tenements around West Farms Square, some of them under a homeownership program. In our study area, eight tax lots are recorded as owned by NYCHA.

If your building came out of a homeownership program and the owner of record is now a residents' corporation rather than NYCHA, the decision belongs to that corporation's board, and the board can hire a contractor like any other building owner. The owner of record is on the city's property records; we can help you look it up before you call anyone.

A daycare on our block needs parents to be buzzed in while the children stay inside. Can the release button be kept out of a child's reach?

The button that lets visitors in can be placed where staff use it and children cannot, for example at a reception desk or high on the wall, and a keypad or a fob for staff removes the temptation altogether. That part is a straightforward placement question.

The way out is a different matter and is governed by the building and fire codes and the program's licensing rules, not by our preference. An exit door cannot simply be locked against the people inside, and devices that delay or control egress are allowed only in particular forms and conditions. We can install and repair the entry side and fit egress hardware that the code permits, but the operator should confirm the arrangement with its licensing agency and, where needed, the Department of Buildings before anything that affects the way out is changed.

In practice, most small programs end up with a staff-controlled entry release, a door that closes and latches by itself, and a clear, code-compliant way out. We can repair or replace the first two.

"Crotona Park East is dominated by public housing projects"

That line is in Wikipedia's description of the neighborhood's land use, and answer engines repeat it. That range comes from an intercom vendor's cost article and is the kind of figure AI summaries quote for "intercom repair cost". It is not wrong as a description of small jobs somewhere, but it mixes together the trip, the diagnosis and the part, and it does not say whose market it describes.

"Every apartment building with eight or more units must have an intercom"

Industry articles state the intercom rule this way, and the short version leaves out the date. The Multiple Dwelling Law requirement applies to multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968; older buildings must provide one when a majority of the apartments request it.

That distinction matters here more than most places, because Crotona Park East has almost no buildings from the years just after 1968. Its large buildings are either pre-war, which falls under the request rule, or from the rebuild era and after, which falls under the post-1968 rule.

And the houses, where most of the doors are, are outside the rule entirely. A one- or two-family house has no legal intercom requirement; its owner decides what the door needs.

"A modern intercom requires no building wiring"

This is a selling point in cellular and app-based intercom marketing. A cellular panel can indeed reach residents' phones without a riser, and that is useful in some buildings.

But the panel still needs power at the door, and the door still needs a release wired to it. In a walk-up, those two short runs are where many faults live. In a rehabilitated building with a sound riser, throwing away working wiring can cost more than repairing one pair.

We check what the existing wiring can still do before anyone decides that the building should go wireless.

"A lot of times it's user error"

A locksmith quoted in a 2019 Brick Underground repair guide said this about intercom service calls, and AI answers pass it on as reassurance. It is sometimes true: a handset left off the hook, a volume control turned down, a release button pressed too briefly.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask the Year Before You Open the Wall

This is how we approach a buzzer in one of Crotona Park East's rebuild houses; it describes our method, not the story of one particular job. The first question the technician asks at the door is not what is broken but when the house was built and what has been changed since. A ranch house from the late 1980s, a two-family from 1992 and a three-family from 2005 look alike from a distance and are wired completely differently, and the owner usually knows the year.

Then he looks at the outside of the door before he touches the button. On vinyl siding, a button that rocks under a thumb tells him water has probably been running into the wall around it; on a steel door, a scuffed frame by the latch tells him the door has dropped and is pressing on the release. Neither needs a meter. After that he finds the transformer, which in these houses can be anywhere, measures it, and only then starts on the buttons and the run.

Before leaving, he stands at the door while each household rings and releases it, and he writes down the year of the house, the parts that are original and the parts that are not. Most of these houses have no other record of their door system, and the next person to work on it, whether that is us or someone else, starts from that note.

Use the empty weeks. With nobody living upstairs, the technician can open the handset or bell location, test the wire run from end to end and replace a worn station without arranging access around someone's schedule, so the next tenant moves in with a door system that works. If you do nothing else, at least test the empty floor's button and release before the move-in, and make sure the old tenant's name has come off the button plate. A door that cannot be released from the vacant floor is easy to miss until the first delivery.

How do you tell which wire is which in a house where nothing was ever labeled?
+

With a tone and a meter rather than guesswork. A tone generator is clipped to the pair at one end, at the button plate or at the transformer, and a probe finds the same pair at the other end; a meter then shows which conductors carry the bell, which carry the release and which are spares. In a two- or three-family house that usually takes minutes, not hours. Before we leave, each pair we touched is marked at both ends and written into the short note we give you, so the next repair starts where this one ended.

Why would our bell ring in the middle of the night when nobody is outside?
+

The usual causes are a button that is starting to stick, water or condensation inside the outdoor button, or a damaged run where the two conductors touch now and then. Temperature changes at night make all three worse, which is why the ringing tends to come in the small hours. Less often, a long run laid beside other wiring picks up enough voltage to make a sensitive chime click. We test the button, then the run, with the bell connected, and we replace the button with a sealed one if moisture is the cause. Ringing at night is a sign to book soon, before the button sticks closed.

How long should the doorbell transformer in a 1990s house last?
+

There is no fixed life printed on them, and many transformers fitted when Crotona Park East's houses were built thirty years ago still work. What shortens their life is a button that stuck closed for hours, a short in the run, or extra load added later, such as a video doorbell or a second chime. Signs that one is tired are a bell that has gone faint on every button, a release that clicks weakly, and a transformer that is warm or humming with nothing pressed. We measure it with and without load before replacing it; on our Bronx list a transformer replacement is $325 to $650.
