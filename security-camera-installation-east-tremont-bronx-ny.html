The Neighborhood

Why East Tremont Properties Need Security Camera Installation

In East Tremont, the person who decides where a security camera goes is almost always the owner of the building, not the person who lives behind the door. The Homes.com guide to the neighborhood says residences are largely in rental buildings and that most of the housing is subsidized or income-restricted. OpenIgloo counts 450 apartment buildings with rent-stabilized units in East Tremont, on streets such as Adams Place, Crotona Avenue, Crotona Parkway, Vyse Avenue, Garden Street and Southern Boulevard, and tracks violations across about a thousand buildings in total. Behind those numbers is a simple fact about camera work here: the street door, the vestibule, the stair, the halls, the cellar and the rear yard are common spaces controlled by a landlord, a managing agent, a nonprofit sponsor or an HDFC board. Brick Underground puts the tenant's side plainly: you need the landlord's permission to put any equipment in the building's common areas, including the hallway outside your own apartment. So most of the camera plans we write in East Tremont are written for the people who run buildings, and most of the questions we answer are theirs.

The doors in those buildings carry more weight than they used to. After the January 2022 fire at Twin Parks North West, a 19-story building in the scatter-site development that spans Fordham, Tremont and East Tremont, investigators found that smoke spread because two self-closing doors did not close. The laws that followed, as Gothamist reported, give owners 14 days to correct a self-closing door violation, with fines of $250 a day after that, and require HPD to come back within 20 days to reinspect. Weverit's record for 1230 East Tremont Avenue, a 60-apartment building from 1932, shows an open Class C violation for defective self-closing doors among 87 open HPD violations. We bring this up on a camera page for two practical reasons. A camera bracket, a cable clip or a sensor fixed to a door or its closer can stop that door from closing fully, so we never mount anything that touches door hardware. And a camera aimed at a stair door is one of the few ways an owner can see, after the fact, whether a door is being wedged open by someone in the building.

The stores add their own reasons. Wikipedia remembers East Tremont Avenue as a busy strip of bakeries, kosher butcher shops, candy stores, delicatessens and clothing stores, and the Homes.com guide describes it today with Venezuelan, African and Dominican restaurants and Frank's Sports Shop, which has been there for decades. CityNeighborhoods describes automotive shops and warehouses along Webster Avenue giving way to commercial strips, and Southern Boulevard with its bodegas, churches and bus routes. In September 2026, the half-mile Tremont Avenue Busway began enforcing its rules: eastbound from Third Avenue to Southern Boulevard and westbound from Southern Boulevard to Belmont Avenue, policed by stationary cameras, bus-mounted cameras and the NYPD. NY1 and News 12 reported that businesses on the avenue raised concerns about losing a lane of car traffic. For a store camera that changes what the outside view sees all day, which we come back to below.

East Tremont facts that shape a camera plan

  • Rentals, mostly regulated: 450 apartment buildings with rent-stabilized units, per OpenIgloo, and housing that Homes.com describes as largely subsidized or income-restricted, so common-area cameras are an owner's decision.
  • Prewar brick and one stair: five- and six-story brick apartment buildings put up between 1900 and 1940, which means plaster halls, masonry walls and a single stair to cover floor by floor.
  • Doors under a 14-day clock: since the 2022 Twin Parks fire, self-closing door violations must be fixed within 14 days, with $250-a-day fines after, and HPD reinspects within 20 days.

What owners often pair with cameras. When an East Tremont owner calls about cameras, the conversation usually turns to the street door within a few minutes: whether the lock and closer are working, whether the intercom does its job, and whether the camera in the vestibule should be able to show who was buzzed in. The lawsuit filed after the Twin Parks fire listed the intercom among the building's alleged failures, and owners here know an entry door is judged as a whole. We plan the vestibule camera around the door and the intercom panel that are already there, and we tell you plainly when the door hardware needs attention before a camera is worth adding.

The owner's building: the common spaces are yours to cover, carefully

An owner of an East Tremont walk-up or elevator building may put cameras in the common areas, and nothing in city law requires them to; a compliance guide from Seneca Security notes there is no general New York City law mandating cameras in apartment buildings. What the owner does have to respect is the line between common space and the apartments. Connextivity's summary of New York practice is that hallway cameras are fine when they cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open, and Brick Underground says the same about cameras placed by neighbors. In a walk-up with apartment doors facing each other across a narrow landing, that line is tighter than it sounds. We set the camera height and angle on each landing so the frame shows the stair and the landing floor, and when a door opens, it shows the person in the doorway, not the room behind them. We check that by opening each door that faces a camera, with the tenant's permission, and looking at the picture on the monitor.

The tenant: inside your apartment, and a conversation with the landlord

Tenants call too, usually after something went missing in the hall or after a frightening night on the stairs. Inside your own apartment, a small camera on a shelf facing your door is your decision, and we can set one up that runs on an outlet and your own Wi-Fi. Anything outside your door, on the frame, in the hall or in the vestibule, needs the landlord's permission, and in a rent-stabilized building we would not install it without that permission in writing. Where tenants want better coverage of the whole building, the most effective route is often a written request to the owner or managing agent, ideally from a tenant association, pointing to specific doors and times. We are happy to give the owner a quote on that request; we will not put equipment in common space on a tenant's order alone.

The ground-floor store: your lease inside, the owner's facade outside

Many of the buildings on East Tremont Avenue are store buildings with apartments upstairs, and a store tenant there has a camera job with two owners in it. Inside the store, the door, the counter, the aisles and the stock room are yours to cover, subject to the lease. Outside, the facade, the sign band and anything that would look along the sidewalk belongs to the building owner, who may already have a camera over the residential entrance next door. We ask for the owner's written answer before any bracket goes on the storefront, and we keep the store's system separate from the building's, with its own recorder, so a change of tenant or a change of owner never leaves someone holding footage that is not theirs.

Five- and six-story buildings from the early 1900s, like the 1911 walk-up at 868 East Tremont Avenue: one stair, plaster halls on masonry, a cellar under the whole building, and apartments that open right onto each landing.

Houses on Rebuilt Lots

Two- and three-family houses put up on cleared lots, most of them between 1990 and 2009, often with a fenced parking pad, a side gate and a back yard.

Low buildings with shops on the ground floor and a few apartments above, lining East Tremont Avenue, where the store tenant and the building owner each have their own camera interests.

Brick, plaster and a single stair in a prewar walk-up

A prewar walk-up is the building we expect to see most in East Tremont, and its shape decides the camera count more than any brand does. The stair usually rises in short flights with a turn at every landing, so a single camera at the bottom sees the first flight and nothing after it. On most of these stairs, one camera per landing, aimed down the flight below and across the landing floor, gives continuous coverage from the vestibule to the roof door without blind turns. The walls are generally plaster over brick or block, and plaster can crack if a bracket is screwed in carelessly. We locate the solid masonry behind the plaster, drill through the plaster with a sharp bit at low speed before switching to a masonry bit, and use anchors sized for the wall rather than for the camera's weight alone. Cable is the bigger question. In most walk-ups, the cleanest path is along the cellar ceiling to a point under the stair, then up through whatever vertical route the building offers: an old dumbwaiter shaft, a chase left by a plumber, or, when there is nothing else, a painted surface raceway tight to the corner of the stair wall. We never run cable on the outside of a stair door or through its frame, and we keep every clip clear of the closer and the latch.

Long corridors and inherited recorders in elevator buildings

The larger elevator buildings put up from the 1930s onward bring long corridors, more than one stair, a lobby with a mail area and, often, a camera system someone else installed. Before we suggest anything new, we find out what is already there: where the recorder sits, whether anyone has the password, which cameras still give a picture and which cables run where. In a building like 1230 East Tremont Avenue, with dozens of open HPD violations on record, we also ask for the violation list, because a camera plan should not draw money away from a door or a light that has to be fixed first. A long corridor needs a camera at each end looking toward the middle, not one in the middle trying to see both ways, and the lobby needs an identification camera at the inner door where faces are close, separate from the overview of the room. Cable between floors travels through existing risers or closets wherever possible, and every opening through a floor or a rated wall is sealed with listed fire-stopping before we leave the floor.

Towers: floors, closets and the limits of a cable run

In a 1970s tower, distance is the constraint. A network camera cable can only carry power and picture so far from the switch that feeds it, and on a building of twenty or thirty floors, a lobby recorder cannot reach the top floors on direct runs. So a tower system is built in pieces: switches in telecom closets or locked cabinets on intermediate floors, each serving the cameras within reach, and a backbone cable or fiber back to the recorder downstairs. That makes the location and the security of those closets part of the camera plan, along with their power and ventilation. Concrete ceilings and block walls in these buildings hold mounts firmly, but they need core drilling where cable passes between spaces, and we plan each opening in advance so there is one clean hole, sealed, rather than several attempts. Towers also tend to have staffed or semi-staffed entrances, community rooms and offices, and we agree with the manager which of those spaces are on camera and which, like an office where residents discuss private matters, are left out.

Rebuilt lots: fenced parking and newer framing

The Homes.com guide calls multifamily homes with parking and fences a special commodity in East Tremont, and many of the two- and three-family houses built on rebuilt lots since 1990 have exactly that: a gate, a short parking pad, a side passage and a yard. These are the easiest East Tremont buildings to cable, because there is usually a basement or attic and the walls are newer framing, but they raise their own questions. The camera that watches the parking pad has to see a person beside the car, not just the roof of the car, which usually means mounting it lower and closer than owners expect. The side passage is often only a few feet wide and leads straight to the back door, so it needs its own camera. And with houses close together, every outdoor view gets checked against the neighbor's windows and yard before the camera is tightened down. Where a tenant lives upstairs, we agree with the owner which views the tenant should see on their phone, usually the front door and the gate, and keep the rest on the owner's account.

What the busway changes for a storefront camera

A camera that looks out of a store on East Tremont Avenue now looks at a street that behaves differently from the one it was set up for. Buses stop and pull away closer to the curb, delivery vehicles that used to pull up in front now stop on side streets or at the times the rules allow, and the curb lane is no longer a row of parked cars that blocks the view for hours. Two practical things follow. First, if your camera sends motion alerts, a busway produces far more movement in the outer part of the frame, so we draw the motion zone tight around the doorway and the window, where people actually approach the store, and leave the bus lane out of it. Second, a camera that once saw a parked car for most of the day may now see straight across the street, which means more glare from passing headlights and from the windows opposite in the evening. We check the outside view at the hours your store is open late, adjust the exposure for that light, and aim the camera down onto the sidewalk in front of the door rather than out across the avenue. The busway's own cameras are there to enforce the bus lane; we could not find any public process for a shop to obtain that footage, so we do not plan your coverage around it.

Getting a usable face at a narrow shop door

Most East Tremont storefronts are narrow, with a single door straight off the sidewalk, and that is good news for identification, because everyone who comes in passes the same spot. The trick is putting a camera where that spot fills the frame. We mount an identification camera just inside the door, at about head height or a little above, aimed so people walk toward it as they enter, and we set it to a narrow view of the doorway alone. A second camera over the counter sees the customer's face across the counter along with hands, cash and the register. The wide ceiling camera in the corner stays, because it shows the order of events across the whole store, but it is not the one that will name anyone. On the walk-through we hold a phone at each proposed spot so you can see, before we drill, whether a face at the door and at the counter would be recognizable, including when the door is open and daylight is pouring in behind the person.

The recorder, the staff and the network

The recorder is the part a thief looks for, so it goes in the back office or a locked cabinet, out of sight from the sales floor, on a battery backup so a short power cut does not stop it recording. The owner holds the only account that can change settings or delete footage. Managers who open and close get their own logins with the cameras they need, and staff who only need to see the front door from the back room get a view-only user. If the store's internet also carries the card terminal and an office computer, the camera system goes on its own network segment or switch, every factory password is changed, firmware is updated, and remote viewing uses the manufacturer's app rather than an open port on the router.

Rules and practical limits for East Tremont business cameras

  • The building owner decides the facade. A store tenant in a building with apartments above needs the owner's written answer before any camera, bracket or cable goes on the storefront or the sign band.
  • Cameras are a choice, not a mandate. Security compliance guidance on NYC buildings notes there is no general city law requiring cameras, so the scope is set by the owner or tenant and their insurer, not by a rule.
  • Low-voltage work and the Department of Buildings. Under NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025, wiring below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data does not need an electrical permit when done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person. Seneca Security notes that work penetrating fire-rated walls or ceilings may still need a filing, and we check each job against that.
  • Fire-rated openings stay rated. Every hole we make through a fire-rated wall, floor or ceiling, in a store or a residential building, is sealed with listed fire-stopping before we leave.
  • Doors and exits come first. Nothing we mount touches a door closer, a latch, an exit device, a sprinkler head or an exit sign, and nothing hangs low enough to narrow a means of egress.
  • New circuits are an electrician's job. Our cameras are powered over low-voltage cable from the recorder or a switch. If the recorder location has no outlet, the quote says so and names that as an electrician's work.
  • Private places stay off camera. No cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere people reasonably expect privacy, and we tell you plainly when a requested view crosses that line.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Opening and Releasing Every Door Before We Talk About Cameras

We do not publish stories about particular East Tremont buildings or the people in them, so this is our routine rather than an anecdote. It is how the first half hour goes on a walk-up survey, before anyone mentions a camera model.

We start at the sidewalk with the street door. We open it fully, let go, and watch it close: does it swing shut by itself, does it latch, does it bounce off the frame. Then the inner vestibule door, the same way. Then every stair door on the way up, if the building has them, and the cellar and yard doors on the way down. Each one gets a note: closes and latches, closes but does not latch, does not close, or held open by something. We do this because a camera plan built around a door that does not work will either record the same problem every night or end up blamed for it. Next comes the light. We ask the super what each landing, the cellar and the yard look like after dark, and which fixtures are out, because a camera can only record what the light lets it see. Then the paths: where the cellar ceiling is clear enough for cable, where a shaft or chase goes up, where the recorder could live behind a lock with air around it. Then the old equipment, traced and tested. Only after that do we sit down with the owner or manager and talk about how many cameras, where, and who gets to see them.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in East Tremont: Questions Answered

Doors, HPD and the rules that followed the Twin Parks fire

HPD now gives us 14 days to fix a self-closing door violation. Can a camera show us which stair door keeps getting wedged open, and by whom?

Yes, if the camera is placed to see the door itself and the space in front of it. A camera on the landing, aimed so the stair door and the floor in front of it fill part of the frame, records the moment someone props it with a chair, a brick or a folded cardboard, and the time stamp tells you when it happens. That lets an owner deal with a pattern rather than guess. What the camera cannot do is fix the closer. If the door does not swing shut and latch on its own, that is the repair to make first, within the deadline, and we will say so on the walk-through. We mount the camera on the wall or ceiling nearby, never on the door or its closer, so nothing we install can stop it closing.

After we repair a door, can our camera footage take the place of HPD's reinspection?

No. Under the rules adopted after the 2022 fire, HPD reinspects within 20 days, and the city restricted landlords' ability to simply certify a correction themselves. Footage of the door closing properly is a useful record for your own files and for any dispute later, but it does not replace the inspector's visit. Keep the repair invoice, take dated photos of the closer and the latch, and let the camera be a long-term check that the door stays fixed after the inspector has gone.

We were cited for the intercom. Will adding a camera in the lobby take care of that violation?

No. A camera and an intercom are separate systems under the law. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a deals with the devices that let a resident talk to a visitor and release the door, and an HPD intercom violation is cleared by repairing that equipment. A lobby camera does not satisfy it. Where a building has both problems, we can plan the camera so it sits clear of the intercom panel and does not interfere with the door release, and the intercom repair is quoted as its own line of work. Some owners later add a video panel at the door, but that is an intercom decision, not a camera one.

Our building has a long list of open HPD violations. Should cameras wait until those are cleared?

It depends on what is on the list. A camera does not help a building that has no heat or a stair door that will not close, and money spent on cameras ahead of those repairs is money in the wrong place. But many violation lists are long because of small items, and cameras can be planned alongside the repairs rather than after them. Send us the current list with your request. We will tell you which items affect where cameras can go, for example doors, lighting and hallway fixtures, and quote the camera work so it can follow the repairs floor by floor instead of waiting for the last one.

Walk-ups, elevator buildings and towers

Our walk-up stair turns at every landing. How many cameras does it take to see the whole stair without blind spots?

On most East Tremont walk-ups, one camera per landing, mounted high on the wall at the turn and aimed down the flight below, gives continuous coverage from the vestibule to the top floor. A six-story building therefore usually needs five or six stair cameras, plus the vestibule, the cellar and the yard. Some stairs have long straight runs or open wells where one camera can see two flights, which lowers the count; others have deep corners that need a second camera. We confirm the number on the walk-through by holding a phone at each turn and checking what it can and cannot see.

Half the hallway cameras in our building went dark years ago. Can new cable go in beside the old run instead of tearing the old run out?

Usually, yes. In a prewar walk-up the old cable often sits in a conduit, a chase or a raceway that is not worth disturbing, and pulling it out risks damage to plaster, fixtures or other wiring sharing the space. Running fresh cable alongside it, then testing and labeling each new run, is often quicker and cleaner. What we will not do is leave dead cable hanging loose or tied to live wiring. We trace the old runs first, remove what is exposed and abandoned where we can reach it, and mark on the handover record anything that stays in the wall, so the next person knows it is not live.

Our walk-up was gut-rehabbed in the early 1980s. Does that make running camera cable easier or harder?

It can go either way. Several East Tremont walk-ups owned by nonprofits and HDFC boards were rebuilt inside their brick shells around 1978 to 1983, and those rehabs sometimes left usable chases next to the stair or the plumbing stack that make vertical runs easy. Other rehabs closed everything up with new partitions and fire-rated ceilings, so cable has to go up the stair in surface raceway or through openings that must be sealed properly. The fastest way to know is to look in the cellar where the stair and plumbing come down, and at the top-floor ceiling. If there are original drawings from the rehab, bring them to the survey; they save time.

In a 1970s tower, can one recorder in the lobby handle cameras on thirty floors, or does each floor need its own?

One recorder can handle the whole building, but the cameras cannot all be cabled straight to it. A single network camera run can only go so far from the switch that powers it, roughly a hundred meters, so a tall building gets switches in closets or locked cabinets on intermediate floors, each serving the cameras within reach, linked back to the recorder by a backbone cable or fiber. The recorder is then sized for the total number of cameras and the number of days you want kept. Each floor does not need its own recorder, which would be harder to manage and easier to tamper with.

Our building has an outer street door and an inner vestibule door. Can one camera cover both?

Sometimes, but rarely well. A camera inside the vestibule can see the inner door and, through the glass, part of the outer one, but a face coming through the outer door is usually backlit by daylight and small in the frame. The better plan is a camera inside the vestibule facing the outer door, which catches faces as people enter, and a second camera in the lobby or hall facing the inner door, which catches them again as they come through. If the budget allows only one, the vestibule camera facing the street door is the one that matters most, because that is where everyone who enters, invited or not, has to pass.

Lobbies, stairs and what happens in them

People gather in our lobby late at night, and tenants have complained about drug use. What can cameras realistically do about that?

Cameras give you a record and a way to see the lobby without walking into it, and that matters, but they do not end the problem by themselves. News 12 Bronx reported tenants at a building on Walton Avenue in Tremont describing exactly this in April 2026, and the fixes that work usually combine several things: a street door that closes and locks, lighting in the vestibule and lobby, a camera at the inner door that records faces clearly, and an owner or manager who reviews footage and acts on it. We place the lobby camera so it identifies people at the door rather than just showing a crowd, and we set alerts for the hours when the lobby should be empty, so the super or manager knows when something is happening.

Garbage keeps getting left in our public stairwell and HPD has written it up. Will a camera on the stair help us find who is doing it?

It can, if the camera covers the landing where the bags appear and the light is good enough to see faces. Weverit's record for one East Tremont Avenue walk-up shows an open violation for garbage in a public stairwell, so this is not a rare complaint. A camera aimed down the flight toward that landing, with motion recording and the clip saved, will usually show which floor the bags come from within a few days. Use the footage to talk to the household directly or through the managing agent, and keep the clip in case it needs to go further. Make sure the trash room or the curb arrangement is clear to tenants too, because sometimes the stair is simply the easiest place left.

Storefronts on the avenue

Our store's roll-down gate covers the window at night. Where should the camera go so it still sees something after we close?

Two places, depending on what you are worried about. Inside, a camera on the ceiling facing the door and the gate from behind records anyone who forces the gate and comes through, and it keeps working whether the gate is up or down. Outside, if the building owner allows it, a camera mounted above the gate box, aimed down at the gate and the sidewalk in front of it, records anyone who tampers with the gate, the lock or the window before they get in. A camera that sits behind the glass looking out will see nothing but the inside of the gate once it is down, so we do not rely on it for night coverage.

The store tenant and the building owner both want cameras on the same facade. One system or two?

Two, in almost every case. The store's cameras serve the store, follow the store's lease and leave with the store tenant; the building's cameras serve the residential entrance and stay with the owner. Combining them on one recorder means one party can see, and sometimes delete, the other's footage, and it causes trouble the day either side changes. What we do is plan both together so the cameras do not duplicate each other or point into each other's space: the building camera covers the residential door and the sidewalk in front of it, the store camera covers the shop door, and each recorder sits in its own locked space with its own accounts.

The busway has its own cameras on our block. Can we use their footage, and should our camera watch the bus lane?

The busway's stationary and bus-mounted cameras are there to enforce bus-lane rules for the city and the NYPD, and we could not find any published way for a store to request that footage, so do not plan around it. If something happens in front of your store, report it and tell investigators the time and place; they can look for footage themselves. Your own camera should watch your door, your window and the sidewalk directly in front of them, not the bus lane. Pointing it at the lane fills it with motion, floods your phone with alerts and pushes the important part of the picture, the person at your door, to the edge of the frame.

FAQ

East Tremont Security Camera Installation FAQ

Our managing agent runs several buildings in East Tremont. Can one login see all of them while each super sees only his own building?
+

Yes. Each building keeps its own recorder, which is cleaner and safer than one recorder for several addresses, and the managing agent's account is given access to every recorder in the app. Each super then gets a user on his own building's recorder only, usually with rights to view and export clips but not to change settings or delete footage. When a super leaves or a building is sold, that one user or that one recorder is handed over without touching the others.

We read that some Bronx buildings use facial recognition at the door. Should our East Tremont building consider it?
+

The Guardian reported in 2019 that the Morris Avenue Apartments in the Bronx had installed facial recognition, but it is far from standard, and it raises questions about tenant consent and personal data that belong with a lawyer before any installer. We do not install facial recognition entry. For a regulated rental building, recording cameras at the entrance and in the common areas, combined with a working lock and intercom, cover what most owners actually need.

Can our store's cameras send alerts only after we close, and stay quiet while we are open?
+

Yes, most recorders and camera apps let you set an alert schedule. We set the cameras to record all the time, but send motion alerts to the owner's phone only between closing and opening, and only for the zones that matter after hours, such as the inside of the door and the stock room. During the day the cameras still record everything, so you can go back to any moment, without your phone buzzing every time a customer walks in.

Our hallways are narrow and painted with glossy paint. Will the night picture just be glare?
+

It can be, if the camera's own infrared light bounces off a nearby wall or ceiling straight back into the lens. In narrow walk-up halls we keep the camera off the wall closest to it, angle it down the length of the corridor rather than across it, and turn down or switch off the built-in infrared where the hall light is enough. Where the hall is dark at night, a better light fixture often helps the picture more than any camera setting.

Tenants keep bikes and strollers under the ground-floor stair. Can a camera watch that spot without catching anyone's apartment door?
+

Usually, yes. We mount the camera on the opposite wall or the ceiling of the hall and aim it at the space under the stair, framing the bikes and strollers and the floor in front of them. Before we tighten it, we open the nearest apartment doors and check the monitor, so that when a door opens the camera sees the person in the hall, not the room behind them. If the layout makes that impossible, we tell you and suggest a different spot for the storage.

Why do you ask for our building's HPD violation list before quoting cameras?
+

Because some violations change where cameras can go or whether they are worth adding yet. An open self-closing door violation means a door must be repaired before anything is mounted near it, missing hall lights mean a camera will see little at night, and some items involve the same walls and ceilings our cable would pass through. Seeing the list lets us plan the cameras around the repairs and tell you honestly what should come first.

The cellar ceiling under our walk-up is packed with heating pipes. Where does the camera cable travel?
+

We never tie camera cable to heating pipes or rest it on them, because the heat ages the jacket and the next plumber may cut it. In a crowded cellar we find a clear line along a beam or wall a little away from the pipes and hang the cable on J-hooks or straps, keeping it clear of the boiler and the flue. Where there is no clear line, a short section of conduit or raceway along the wall carries it past the worst stretch.

Our walk-up's hall walls are old plaster over brick. Will mounting cameras crack them?
+

Not if the holes are made carefully. We find the solid masonry behind the plaster, start through the plaster with a sharp bit at low speed and no hammer action, then switch to a masonry bit for the brick, and set anchors in the brick rather than relying on the plaster to hold anything. Small chips around a hole are covered by the camera's base. Loose or hollow plaster is something we point out before drilling, so you can decide whether to repair it first.

I saw that the NYPD is installing cameras on East Tremont Avenue. Is that our part of the avenue?
+

No. The Bronx Times report on those NYPD cameras covers the Throggs Neck stretch of East Tremont Avenue, between Bruckner Boulevard and Harding Avenue, at the far eastern end of the avenue. In the East Tremont neighborhood, the cameras that arrived in 2026 belong to the Tremont Avenue Busway and enforce bus-lane rules. Neither program gives a building or store a view of its own doors.

A tenant has complained that a hallway camera points at her apartment door. How should the owner handle it?
+

Take it seriously and check it the same day. Open her door with her present and look at the monitor: if the camera sees into the apartment when the door is open, it needs to be moved or re-aimed so it covers the hall without looking inside. If it only shows the hall and the doorway, explain that to her and show her the picture. We can come back to adjust the angle or set a privacy mask on that part of the frame.

Our building is due for a major renovation next year. Should we put cameras in now or wait?
+

If you need coverage now, install a small system at the entrance and the stair that can stay through the work, and plan the full system with the renovation. When walls and ceilings are open, cable can go in hidden paths that would otherwise need surface raceway, and the cost of running it is lower. Tell your architect or contractor early so they leave pathways and outlets where the cameras and recorder will go, and we can work from their drawings.
