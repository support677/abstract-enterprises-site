The Neighborhood

Why Fordham Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Fordham is two neighborhoods sharing the same blocks: a shopping district that never seems to empty, and an apartment neighborhood whose halls go silent after midnight. StreetEasy draws Fordham from Burnside Avenue north to East 196th Street and from Jerome Avenue east to Webster Avenue. The name goes back to a 1671 manor grant and a shallow ford across the Bronx River, and the railroad in the 1840s and the subway in the early 1900s turned the farm town into a city district. Between 1900 and 1930, according to CityNeighborhoods, Fordham Heights went from farmland to one of the densest parts of the Bronx. The city's needs assessment counted about 118,000 residents in the study area at roughly 86,000 people per square mile. That density is the first thing a camera plan has to deal with. On almost any block, a single camera sees more people in an hour than a camera on a quiet house sees in a month.

The shopping side is enormous. The Department of Small Business Services, working with the Fordham Road Business Improvement District, surveyed 657 storefronts along Fordham Road, Jerome Avenue, the Grand Concourse between East 184th Street and Kingsbridge Road, Webster Avenue, East 188th Street and Third Avenue. It counted 65 clothing and shoe stores, 52 beauty and barber shops, 47 quick-service restaurants, 46 phone and electronics shops, 40 medical and dental offices, 27 jewelry, watch repair and pawn shops, 12 banks and 11 check cashers, plus 188 street vendors, the largest vending scene in the Bronx. A Department of Transportation pedestrian count on East Fordham Road between Valentine and Tiebout Avenues recorded 7,296 people on a weekday afternoon. In front of stores like these, a ceiling camera that shows the whole sales floor is useful for seeing what happened. It is close to useless for showing who did it, unless another camera at the door was framed to catch each face as it came in.

Merchants know this. When the Fordham Road BID surveyed vendors, merchants and stakeholders in 2021, public safety came out as the top priority, and the BID put five patrol ambassadors, all licensed New York State security guards, on the street during peak shopping hours. Business owners told the Bronx Times that theft and high rents are hard on the corridor's smaller shops, and the needs assessment found that 43 percent of the merchants it surveyed had two or fewer full-time employees. A shop run by one or two people cannot watch the counter, the door, the fitting rooms and the stock room at once. Cameras are how a small store sees the parts of itself the owner is not standing in, and the recordings are what a store gives the police when the patrol has moved on.

The apartment side has a different problem. Wikipedia describes Fordham as dominated by five- and six-story apartment buildings, often set flush to the sidewalk, and much of that housing went up in the 1920s. When we filtered city lot records for eight census tracts around Fordham Road for another of our Fordham pages, the count came to 189 five-story walk-ups, 70 six-story elevator buildings, 14 six-story walk-ups, 22 taller buildings (most built since 2007), 12 co-ops and 248 house lots, holding close to 13,000 apartments between them. These buildings have a street door, a vestibule, a lobby, one or two stairs, a cellar door, a roof door and sometimes a rear yard, and almost all the trouble that reaches the news happens at those points, at hours when no one is watching.

The local reports show it. News 12 Bronx reported that residents of 2654 Valentine Avenue said their entryway was filled with loiterers and drug use, and the station obtained surveillance video showing several people using drugs and people unconscious in the lobby. In a Fordham Heights building, neighbors told News 12 that people had been trespassing for months and that nobody could tell how they were getting in; some suspected a resident was letting them in. In the West Bronx nearby, CBS New York reported that several men went into buildings on Davidson Avenue and damaged many cameras inside and outside before running off, and in another Bronx case a man used a large object to push cameras aside before stealing from a building basement on Morris Avenue. Those reports are not about every Fordham block, and we will not pretend to judge how safe yours is. What they show is that lobby cameras are now part of how tenants, owners and police find out what happened, and that cameras placed carelessly get moved, broken or simply fail to see the door that mattered.

Two rules shape where cameras can go in these buildings. The first is fire safety. In January 2022, the fire at Twin Parks North West, 333 East 181st Street in Fordham Heights, killed 17 people, and investigators found smoke spread because self-closing doors failed. Under Local Law 111, a broken self-closing door is a class C immediately hazardous violation, and the door must close and latch on its own. Nothing a camera installer does may get in the way of that: no camera on the door leaf, no cable through the closer, no device that holds the door. The second rule is permission. Brick Underground spells out that a renter needs the landlord's permission to put anything in common areas, including the hallway outside the apartment. So in a Fordham building, the owner or managing agent decides on lobby, stair and cellar cameras, and a tenant's plan stays inside the apartment unless the owner agrees in writing.

Fordham facts that shape a camera plan

  • A crowd at the door: 7,296 pedestrians were counted on a weekday afternoon on one stretch of East Fordham Road, so store cameras must be framed for faces at chokepoints, not wide shots of the sidewalk.
  • Small shops, few hands: 43 percent of surveyed merchants had two or fewer full-time staff, which is why the counter, the door and the stock room each need their own view.
  • Buildings that go quiet: the area is mostly five- and six-story apartment houses from the 1920s through the 1940s, whose lobbies, stairs and cellars empty out overnight.
  • Doors that must close: after the Twin Parks fire, self-closing doors carry class C violations when they fail, so camera hardware stays off door leaves and closers.
  • Turnover above the shops: 40 medical and dental offices, 20 schools and 26 agencies, nonprofits and houses of worship were counted along the corridor, many on upper floors that change tenants.
  • Thin home internet: the State Comptroller reported that 47.8 percent of households in the Fordham, Bedford Park and Norwood area had no broadband subscription by its definition, which affects remote viewing for renters.

What Fordham owners often add next. Building owners who start with lobby cameras usually ask, a few weeks later, whether the camera at the street door can be watched from the intercom or whether a fob reader can log who used the door at the hour something happened. Storekeepers on Fordham Road tend to ask about an after-hours alarm on the back door. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port for that kind of addition, so the next job does not mean opening the ceiling twice.

The Busy Hour and the Empty Hour

Crowd by Day, Empty Lobby by Night: How a Fordham Camera Plan Is Built

Most camera advice is written for a house with a yard. Fordham is the opposite: a place where the hard part is not darkness or distance, but too many people at once during the day and nobody at all at three in the morning. A good plan here asks two questions about every spot. When it is busy, can the camera pick out one person from the crowd? When it is empty, will anyone know that somebody came through? Identification at the chokepoints, overview everywhere else

There is a simple reason one well-placed camera often does more than five scattered ones. A camera can only show a face clearly if the face fills a fair share of the frame, which means the camera must be close to where people pass and aimed so they walk toward it. On Fordham Road, every shopper eventually funnels through the store's front door; in an apartment house, every visitor crosses the vestibule. Those funnels are where we put the cameras that identify people. Wide cameras on the ceiling then show what happened on the sales floor or in the lobby, and the two are matched by the time stamp. When a store owner asks why the overview camera shows a blur of heads, the answer is that it was never meant to identify anyone; the door camera does that.

Alerts that wake someone only when it matters

A lobby on a busy block will set off motion alerts all day long, and an owner who gets two hundred notifications a day soon turns them off entirely. So we schedule alerts by place and hour. The cellar door, the roof bulkhead and the store's back door are the cameras that should see nobody outside working hours, and those are the ones that send alerts overnight. The lobby and the sales floor keep recording around the clock without buzzing anyone. Most recorders and camera apps let alerts be limited to people rather than every moving shadow, which cuts out reflections from passing buses and headlights. The super, the managing agent and the owner can each receive different alerts, so the person on site hears about the cellar door and the person in another borough does not.

Who owns the system, and who can see it

In Fordham buildings the question of who sees the cameras matters as much as where they point. The owner or the co-op holds the main administrator account, the only one that can change settings or delete footage. A managing agent may get full viewing; a super may get the lobby, cellar and stairs; a store manager may get the sales floor but not the office. Tenants do not get access to building cameras unless the owner decides to share the street door view, and even then it is view-only. If tenants or a reporter ask for footage, the owner decides, and police requests are handled by exporting a copy and keeping the original. We set these accounts before we leave and write down who has what, because the trouble with an old system is almost always that nobody remembers who still has a login.

Walk-ups: the cellar first, then up the stair

In a five-story walk-up the recorder almost always belongs in the cellar or a locked closet near the super's room, because that is where power, the internet provider's equipment and the building's other low-voltage lines already live. From there, cable for the street door and vestibule cameras runs a short distance up through the floor or along the cellar ceiling to the front wall. Hallway and landing cameras are harder. The halls are often plaster over brick, and opening them is messy and slow, so we look first for an unused shaft, an old dumbwaiter, a pipe chase or a closet stacked floor above floor. Where none exists, a slim painted raceway along the stair wall carries the cable up, fixed into the plaster with the right anchors so it does not crack. At each floor the cable passes through, the opening is sealed with rated firestop. We do not run camera cable through the stair door frame, along the door closer or across the latch side of any door that must close by itself.

Six-story buildings: two doors, one elevator, longer runs

The six-story elevator buildings along the Concourse side streets usually have a two-door vestibule, a bigger lobby, an elevator and sometimes a second stair at the back. A single camera inside the inner door, aimed at the vestibule, shows each person who is buzzed in or who follows someone through, and that one view answers most questions a building gets asked. A second camera covers the lobby and elevator landing, and a third covers the mailboxes, which are a common target. Because the runs to upper floors are longer, the cable must be the right kind for the path it travels, and if it climbs between floors it must be rated for that use. Where the lobby still has older tile, mosaic or a stone base, we mount cameras on the ceiling or the plain wall above, never through the decorative finish.

Co-ops and gated complexes: the board's plan, not one shareholder's

Fordham has co-ops from the 1940s through the 1960s, some in gated complexes like Fordham Hill above Sedgwick Avenue, with nine high-rises and 1,130 apartments. In buildings like these, nothing goes on the common areas or the outside walls unless the board agrees, and the managing agent usually wants the system to fit with whatever cameras the complex already has. A camera plan for a co-op starts with the board's question, which is usually about the gate, the parking, the paths between buildings and the lobbies at night. We put the proposal in writing for the board meeting, name every camera position and who will be able to see it, and say where the recorder will be kept and who holds the administrator account. Individual shareholders who want something for their own apartment get an indoor plan that stays within the house rules.

Store rows: the dropped ceiling, the block wall and the gate

The one- and two-story commercial buildings along Fordham Road and Jerome Avenue are mostly block or brick with a dropped ceiling inside and a metal roll-down gate in front. That combination is good for cameras inside the store and awkward for cameras outside. Inside, cable rides above the ceiling on hangers, drops to each camera through a neat hole in the tile, and gathers in the back office where the recorder sits on a shelf with battery backup. Between the sales floor and the back office there is usually a block wall, and one planned, sealed opening carries every camera cable through it. Outside, a camera above the gate needs the landlord's written agreement, because the façade is the landlord's, and it needs a mount high enough to be out of easy reach. A camera behind the window glass works during the day, but once the gate comes down it sees nothing but the back of the gate.

The sales floor that never empties

On Fordham Road a store can be crowded from opening to close, and that changes how cameras should be laid out. We start at the door. A camera mounted just inside the entrance, around head height and set back far enough that people walk toward it, gives one clear face for each person entering; on a busy day that single camera is the most valuable one in the store. Next comes the counter, where a camera above and slightly in front of the register shows the customer's face, their hands and the drawer. Then the places the owner cannot see from the counter: the far end of the aisles, the fitting-room entrance, the stairs to a basement stock room and the back door. Overviews from the ceiling tie these together. Because many of these stores keep the sales floor lit brightly against a bright street, the door camera must handle strong backlight, and we test it at midday when the window glare is worst, not first thing in the morning.

Upper floors: clinics, schools and agencies

Above the shops, the needs assessment found many of the district's 40 medical and dental offices, 20 schools and training centers and 26 agencies, nonprofits and houses of worship, and 46 percent of jobs located in Fordham are in education, health care and social assistance. Cameras in these suites follow different rules. A waiting room can be covered for safety, but the camera must not show computer screens, paperwork or anyone in an exam or treatment room. A school's entrance and hallways can be covered, but never restrooms or changing areas. A social-service agency usually wants its reception and suite door covered and its counseling rooms left alone. The shared floor lobby, the elevator and the stairs belong to the building owner, so any camera there needs the landlord's written agreement. Work in these suites often happens while clients are in the space, and quiet ceiling work can go on during opening hours while drilling waits for an evening or weekend.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Fordham

  • Face-matching software needs a sign. City rules require covered businesses that collect biometric identifier information, such as face templates, to post a notice of at least 8.5 by 11 inches at every customer entrance. Ordinary video that is not run through identification software and is not sold or shared, except with police, is outside that rule.
  • Private places stay off camera. Recording where people expect privacy, including fitting rooms, restrooms and changing areas, is a crime under New York Penal Law 250.45, and we will not aim a camera into one.
  • The landlord decides outside the unit. The façade, the sidewalk canopy, a shared floor lobby and the stairs belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement first.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is mounted on a self-closing door, its closer or its frame in a way that stops it closing and latching.
  • Openings stay rated. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Landmarks need their sign-off. In designated buildings, including those with designated interiors, camera positions and cable paths are approved before work begins.
  • The installer must be licensed. The NYS Department of State licenses businesses that install, service or maintain security systems; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords, boards and insurers usually ask to see first.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Standing in a Fordham Lobby at Two Different Hours

We do not publish stories about a particular building or customer, so this is the routine rather than an anecdote. It is how we look at a Fordham apartment house or store before we suggest a single camera.

If we can, we see the place twice. The first visit is at the busy hour: mid-afternoon in a store, or the late-afternoon rush in an apartment lobby when people come home, aides arrive and couriers stack boxes by the mailboxes. We stand where a camera would go and watch how people actually move, which door they use, where they pause, where the light comes from and where the glare falls. In a store we note where the crowd bunches and where the owner cannot see from the counter. The second look is at the empty hour, either in person or from photos and video the super or owner sends. We check what the vestibule looks like with only the hall light on, how dark the cellar stair gets, whether the roof door shuts by itself, and whether there is a corner of the lobby nobody can see from the street door. Then we go down to the cellar or the back office, find the power, the internet equipment and a dry, locked spot for the recorder, and look for a path for cable that does not involve opening plaster. Only after both visits do we write the plan.

Security Camera Installation in Fordham: Questions Answered

Apartment houses between Jerome and Webster

Tenants say people keep getting into our building and nobody can tell how. Which cameras would show the way in?

One camera at each way into the building, each set to record and time-stamp every person who passes: the street door, the inner vestibule door, the cellar or service door, the rear yard door if there is one, and the roof bulkhead. News 12 reported exactly this puzzle in a Fordham Heights building, where some neighbors suspected a resident was letting people in. With every entry covered, a single night's recording usually answers it: either someone is let in through the front, or a door that should lock is not latching. Once you know which, the fix is often a door repair rather than more cameras.

Can a camera be mounted on the stair door itself, or on the door closer, so it sees people coming through?

No. Stair and corridor doors in these buildings must close and latch on their own, and under Local Law 111 a failed self-closing door is a class C immediately hazardous violation. Adding weight to the door, running cable along the closer arm or fixing anything to the latch side can stop the door from shutting properly. We mount the camera on the ceiling or the wall beside the door, aimed through the opening, which gives a better view anyway because it sees people approaching rather than only the moment they pass.

A news crew asked our building for its lobby video. Who decides whether it gets handed over?

The owner of the system does, which in a rental building is the landlord or managing agent and in a co-op is the board. Tenants and outsiders, including reporters, have no automatic right to it. A police request is different: export a copy of the clip, give it to the detective and keep the original on the recorder. Before sharing footage with anyone else, owners should think about the residents who appear in it. We set up accounts so only the people the owner names can export, which keeps those decisions with the right person.

After the Twin Parks fire, will the holes your crew makes between our floors meet the fire rules?

Every opening we make through a fire-rated floor, wall or shaft is sealed with a rated firestop product sized for the opening and the cable, so smoke cannot use it as a path. That fire, at 333 East 181st Street in January 2022, showed how quickly smoke moves through a building when barriers fail. Where cable has to pass several floors, we prefer an existing shaft or chase so we make as few new openings as possible, and we leave the owner a note of every place we sealed so an inspector can find them.

Co-ops and gated complexes

Our complex has a staffed gate. Should the booth get a live monitor, or should the footage stay with management?

Usually both, with different access. A monitor at the booth showing the gate, the nearest paths and the parking entrance lets the guard see what is coming without walking out, and those views can be live-only, so the booth cannot export or delete anything. Recording, exports and the full set of cameras stay with the managing agent and whoever the board names. That split stops footage leaving the complex without a decision behind it. We put it in writing for the board, including which cameras the booth sees and what happens when guards change.

Fordham Road and the side-street shops

Our clothing store has fitting rooms. Can a camera watch the fitting-room door to count what goes in and comes out?

A camera can watch the entrance to the fitting-room area from the sales floor, showing who goes in carrying what and who comes out. It must never see into a cubicle, over a curtain or into a mirror that faces one, because recording there is a crime under Penal Law 250.45. We aim the camera so its frame ends at the entrance, test it with the curtains open and closed, and check reflections. Many stores pair this with a staff member at the entrance handing out tags, which the camera then backs up.

Our phone repair shop takes in customers' devices at the counter. Can a camera record each phone's condition at drop-off?

Yes. A camera above the intake counter, close enough to show a cracked screen or a dent, records each device as it is handed over and the face of the person handing it in. With the recorder's clock set correctly, the ticket time and the clip time line up, which settles most disputes about what condition a phone was in. Phone and electronics shops are among the most common businesses on the corridor, 46 in the city's count, and the intake counter is the view they ask about most. We keep that camera off customers' screens once the device is unlocked.

Our check-cashing window sits behind thick bullet-resistant glass. Which side of the glass does the camera go on?

For the customer's face, the camera goes on the staff side, aimed through the glass at a slight angle so its own infrared light and the counter lights do not bounce straight back into the lens. Thick laminated glass can soften the picture and add reflections, so we test the position with the counter lights as they are during business hours. A second camera on the customer side, high on the wall, records the lobby and the line, which is where trouble usually starts. Nothing is drilled through the protective glass or its frame.

Vendor carts set up in front of our window most days. Will a camera see past them?

An outside or window camera may not, because a cart or umbrella can fill the frame. That is one reason the most useful store camera is the one just inside the door, which sees every customer whatever is happening on the sidewalk. If you want a view of the sidewalk itself, a camera higher up, above the window or the gate with the landlord's agreement, looks down over the carts rather than into them. Fordham has the Bronx's largest vending scene, 188 vendors in the city's count, so we plan for carts as a normal part of the view.

Our store sits under the elevated 4 train on Jerome Avenue. Does the shaking and the shadow of the tracks spoil the picture?

It can if the camera is mounted badly. Vibration from passing trains travels through a building, and a camera on a thin bracket or loose mount will shake on every train, blurring the clip and setting off motion alerts. We fix outside cameras firmly into solid masonry and keep inside cameras on ceiling structure rather than flimsy tiles. The tracks also throw strong stripes of light and shadow across the sidewalk during the day, so we use cameras that handle mixed light and test them when the sun is low enough to cast the shadows.

A vendor offered face-matching software that flags repeat shoplifters. What does city law ask before we turn it on?

City rules require businesses that collect biometric identifier information from customers, such as face templates, to post a clear notice at every customer entrance, at least 8.5 by 11 inches. Selling or trading that data is barred. Ordinary video recording that does not use identification software is not covered. Because the rules have details and possible penalties, talk to a lawyer before switching on face matching, and read the software's terms on where the data goes. We install the cameras; we do not decide whether a store should use face matching.

Upper floors, campuses and landmark rooms

Our dental office is on an upper floor above Fordham Road. Can the waiting-room camera record patients?

A waiting-room camera aimed at the door and seating is common for safety, as long as it cannot read screens, forms or the front-desk computer, and it never sees into treatment or X-ray rooms. We frame it on the entrance and the room, not the reception counter's paperwork. A visible notice that the waiting room is recorded is good practice. We would not set it to record audio. Who can view and export footage should be limited to the practice owner or office manager, and we set the accounts that way.

Our training school shares a floor lobby with other tenants. Who decides whether a camera goes there?

The building owner. A shared floor lobby, the elevator landing and the stairs are common areas, and each tenant controls only its own suite. If the landlord agrees, the camera can feed your recorder, but other tenants may object to being recorded at their own doors, so the landlord may want the view limited. Inside your suite, you can cover your entrance, reception and hallways, keeping restrooms and any changing areas off camera. We write up the request so the landlord can answer it quickly.

Our business occupies a designated interior landmark. How is camera cable run without touching the protected finishes?

The Dollar Savings Bank building at the Grand Concourse and Fordham Road is one example: the city designated both its exterior and its banking hall interior as landmarks in 1994, and the hall now holds a supermarket. Work inside a designated interior needs approval from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. We look for camera positions on surfaces that are not part of the protected design, and for cable paths through basements, back rooms or later ceilings, then submit those for approval before drilling. Small, plain cameras in approved spots usually do the job without touching ornament.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Fordham

These are claims that turn up again and again in quick online answers, cost guides and installer pages about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about Fordham. Cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they give a rough sense of scale for New York City, but none of them has stood in a Fordham lobby.

"Fordham means Fordham University."

Search for Fordham and the university fills the screen, so quick answers often describe the area as a campus neighborhood. The Rose Hill campus is real and large, 85 acres founded in 1841, but it is one part of the picture.

Most of Fordham is dense apartment houses and one of the biggest shopping districts in the city. The city's survey counted 657 storefronts and about 118,000 residents, and the housing is mostly five- and six-story buildings from the 1920s through the 1940s.

For cameras, that means the typical job is a lobby, a stair and a cellar, or a storefront with a crowded door, not a campus quad. The university runs its own security; our customers are the buildings and shops around it.

"Wired camera installation in New York City requires a licensed electrician."

At least one installer's blog says this flatly. The state sources we found say something different: a license from the NYS Department of State is required to install, service or maintain security systems, and a trade association says that covers cameras, CCTV and access control.

Camera cable that carries low voltage from a recorder or switch is not the same as new electrical wiring. Where a job needs a new outlet or circuit, that is electrical work, and our quote says so and names it as an electrician's job.

The question to ask any installer is whether they hold the state security license. AESS does, under NYS license #12000287431, and we will show it to a landlord, board or insurer before work starts.

"Public cameras already watch Fordham Road, so a store doesn't need its own."

Fordham Road has cameras of many kinds, and the BID added licensed security guards as patrol ambassadors during peak shopping hours. Quick answers sometimes take that to mean a shop is covered.

Street cameras and patrols watch the street. None of them is aimed at a store's register, fitting-room entrance, stock-room door or back door, and none of them sends the owner a clip when something goes missing at closing time.

A store's own cameras answer the questions that matter to that store, on a recorder the owner controls. When police or prosecutors look for video after an incident, the private camera at the door is often the one with the clear face.

"Every camera costs about the same to install."

Online calculators and cost guides give a per-camera average for New York City, and AI answers repeat it as if every camera were the same amount of work.

In a Fordham walk-up, the camera at the street door may need a short run from the cellar, while the one on the fifth-floor landing needs a path up four floors with firestop at each one. In a store, one camera drops through a ceiling tile and the next goes outside above the gate with the landlord's approval.

So we do not print one camera price for the neighborhood. Each Fordham job is priced on our Bronx rate, quoted line by line after photos or a visit, and booked within 2 hours.

"Wi-Fi cameras are fine for any apartment building."

Product pages show wireless cameras working everywhere, and for a single camera inside a renter's apartment near the router, that can be true.

Fordham's older buildings have brick, block and thick plaster walls that weaken Wi-Fi quickly, and a lobby camera in a walk-up may be four floors and several walls from any router. A camera that keeps dropping off is worse than none, because nobody notices until the night it was needed.

For building and store systems we run cable to each camera, which also carries its power. Wi-Fi stays for the small indoor cameras renters and students set on a shelf.

"A 4K camera will identify anyone in the crowd."

Camera listings promise that more pixels will show every face. AI summaries tend to repeat that a higher-resolution camera covering a whole sales floor solves identification.

On a crowded floor, a wide camera spreads its pixels across dozens of people, and faces turned away or partly hidden show up as heads. Resolution helps, but placement helps more: a camera at the door that people walk straight toward will identify more people than a sharper camera on the far ceiling.

That is why a Fordham store plan pairs a door camera framed for faces with overviews framed for what happened, and why we test each view at the busiest time of day.

"Putting up cameras stops trespassers."

Quick answers often claim cameras deter intruders, as though a camera in the lobby keeps people out.

Local reporting shows the limits. News 12 obtained lobby video from a Valentine Avenue building that showed drug use and people unconscious, and CBS New York reported cameras being damaged or pushed aside in other Bronx buildings. The cameras recorded what happened; they did not prevent it.

What cameras do well is show how people get in and when, so the owner can repair a door, change who has keys or bring in the police with evidence. We place them so they answer that question and so they are hard to knock out of aim.

FAQ

Fordham Security Camera Installation FAQ

My roommates and I share an off-campus apartment near Fordham University. Whose name should the camera account be in?
+

Put it in the name of the person who expects to stay longest, ideally whoever holds the lease, and share access with the others as separate users rather than one shared password. When someone moves out, remove their user and change the main password. Keep the camera inside your own apartment, facing your own door or living room, and agree as a group what it covers, since shared rooms are shared. Nothing goes in a hallway or on the building's walls without the landlord's permission.

Can I set up a camera in my residence hall room when I have a roommate?
+

Check the residence hall's rules first, because many halls limit or ban cameras in rooms, and your roommate has a right to privacy in the space you share. A camera aimed only at your own desk or belongings, switched off when your roommate is there, is the most that is usually reasonable, and only with their agreement. We would not install a camera in a dorm room that the school or your roommate has not approved.

In another Bronx building someone shoved a camera aside before a theft. Can our cameras warn us when one is pushed off its view?
+

Many business-grade cameras and recorders have a tamper or scene-change alert that fires when a camera is covered, sprayed, knocked or turned away from what it normally sees. We turn it on for cameras in cellars, stairs and back rooms, where the CBS New York report on Morris Avenue showed this happening, and send the alert to the super or owner. Mounting the camera higher, in a hard dome and with no loose bracket, makes it harder to move in the first place.

We own one of the houses tucked between apartment buildings on a side street. Can cameras do much on a lot that narrow?
+

Yes, but with fewer, better-aimed cameras. A camera at the front door framed for faces, one on the front gate or steps, and one at the back door or yard usually covers everything that matters. The tall buildings on each side mean your cameras must be aimed down your own lot, not up at your neighbors' windows. Close walls can also bounce infrared light back into a side camera, so we check those views after dark and adjust the angle or the light.

Since the bus lane redesign, our deliveries stop at a loading zone a few doors down. Can our camera still see the drop?
+

Often not from inside the store, because a door camera looks at your entrance, not along the block. The Department of Transportation reported that many Fordham Road businesses take deliveries at the curb and planned new loading zones as part of the offset bus lanes. The surest record is a camera inside your door that shows each box coming in and who carried it, matched to the delivery time. An outside camera looking down the sidewalk needs the landlord's agreement and still may be blocked by buses or trucks.

Shoppers stream past our window all day. Why does our ceiling camera never show a clear face?
+

A ceiling camera looking down over a crowded floor sees the tops of heads, and each face takes up only a few pixels. It was built to show what happened, not who did it. To get faces you need a camera near head height just inside the entrance, set back so people walk toward it, with settings that handle the bright window behind them. That one camera, matched by time stamp to the ceiling view, usually solves the problem.

How quickly can someone come look at a Fordham shop that has to stay open?
+

We book every request within 2 hours and set the visit for a time that suits the store, often before opening or during a quieter part of the day. Many quotes can be started from photos of the door, counter, ceiling and back office, so the visit only confirms details. Because our Bronx office is on East Fordham Road, a short survey does not need a whole morning. The installation itself is scheduled so drilling happens before customers arrive.

Our hall near the Grand Concourse fills up for events a few hours at a time. How should cameras be set for those nights?
+

Cover the entrances and exits with cameras framed for faces, because everyone passes them, and use wide cameras for the floor so you can see crowd movement and any incident. Keep enough recording days so a complaint raised a week later can still be checked. Set alerts for the hours the hall is empty, not during events. If the hall uses ticket scanning or a coat check, a camera over each of those points is worth adding. Restrooms and changing rooms stay off camera.

Should our store's recorder sit in the same back room as the safe?
+

It is better kept somewhere separate and less obvious, such as a locked cabinet, a ceiling space or another room, so anyone going for the safe cannot carry off the footage too. A camera should still cover the safe and the door to that room. The recorder needs ventilation and a battery backup wherever it goes. Some owners also send a copy of key cameras to a second location, which we can set up if your internet line allows it.

Is it worth aiming one of our store cameras at the bus stop outside?
+

Sometimes. A camera that shows the sidewalk and the stop can show which way someone went after leaving your store, which helps police follow them on other cameras. It will not show faces at that distance, and buses will block it often. If the landlord agrees to an outside camera, aim it along your own frontage and the curb in front of it rather than across the street, and keep the identification job for the camera inside your door.

Which camera shows a customer's hands and the piece when a jewelry case is opened?
+

A small camera directly above the case or behind the counter, angled down, shows the staff member's hands, the piece and the customer leaning in. A second camera facing the customer from behind the counter catches the face. Display lighting is bright and can wash out the picture, so we test with the case lights on. The city's survey counted 27 jewelry, watch repair and pawn shops on the corridor, and most need this pair at each case they open for customers.

Can a camera cover the produce stands our grocery sets out on the sidewalk?
+

Yes, with a camera mounted high above the entrance or window, aimed down at the stands, with the landlord's agreement for the outside wall. It will show people taking items and which way they go, though faces will be small. Set its motion alerts to stay off during the day, when there is constant movement, and to come on after the stands are brought in. A camera inside the door still does the job of identifying who came in and out.
