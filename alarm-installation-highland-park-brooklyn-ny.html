The Neighborhood

Why Highland Park Properties Need Alarm Installation

Start with the ground. Highland Park sits on the terminal moraine, the ridge of glacial debris that runs along the top of Brooklyn and Queens, and that ridge decides almost everything about how houses here are built and how they are entered. Brownstoner’s architectural writer Suzanne Spellen, describing the row of one-family houses at 149–181 Sunnyside Avenue, notes that the hills forced steep streets: the houses on the north side of that block stand above the street on steep stairs and graded lawns, while their twins across the road sit at street level. Kevin Walsh of Forgotten New York calls Miller Avenue possibly the steepest street in Brooklyn, a two-block climb. On a slope like that, a house has a high side and a low side, and the low side often has a full-height cellar wall with a door and windows of its own. That is the first thing a Highland Park alarm has to account for.

Then the park. Highland Park itself covers 141.28 acres on the Brooklyn–Queens border, according to Wikipedia’s article on the park, and NYC Parks counts about 72 acres of natural area across the park and the old reservoir, including roughly 41 acres of forest and 8 acres of freshwater wetland. The Ridgewood Reservoir, bounded by Highland Boulevard on the south and Vermont Place on the west, operated as part of Brooklyn’s water supply from the 1850s, was drained in 1989 and handed to NYC Parks in 2004. For the houses along Highland Boulevard and the blocks just below it, this means the view across the street is woodland and lawn rather than a row of windows. A Reddit poster weighing a house purchase here put “Next to Park” first on the list of pros, and that is a fair reason; it also means that after dark, nobody on the park side of the street is looking at your house.

Then the houses themselves. The housing stock here is older and more varied than most of eastern Brooklyn. Spellen describes Highland Boulevard as the wealthiest street in Highland Park and Cypress Hills, built up in the early 1900s mostly by German-American families, with large mansions next to smaller one-family houses: an Arts and Crafts house of river rock and shingles under a steep tiled roof at 278 Highland Boulevard, a limestone mansion across the street, Stick style Victorians nearby. On Sunnyside Avenue, Richards Real Homes built about 90 houses between 1900 and 1920 to designs by William C. Winters, in red or buff brick with tile roofs, deep porches and bay windows. On Arlington Avenue, a pair of brick single-family houses from 1908 to 1912 have a Dutch gambrel roof and Colonial Revival detail. Deep porches, bay windows, casements, transoms and outbuildings are everywhere, and each of those openings needs a different kind of sensor.

Finally, the edges. A few blocks of Highland Park are reached by private dead-end courts, such as Schenck Court and Van Siclen Court off Jamaica Avenue,, and a few rows have lanes or slopes behind them rather than other yards. Below the hill, Jamaica Avenue carries the J and Z trains on an elevated structure completed in 1893, and its storefronts sit in the shade of the tracks. Those details are what make an alarm here different from one on a flat block of attached houses: the openings you have to cover are more numerous, the places where someone can work unseen are different, and the people who would normally notice a problem are fewer on some sides of the house than others.

What we do not claim matters as much. We found no published burglary figures for Highland Park as a separate area, because the city does not report crime by that name; the nearest public reporting is for the 75th Precinct, which Wikipedia’s East New York article says patrols East New York and the surrounding area. So this page does not tell you the neighborhood is dangerous or safe. It tells you how a house built into a hill and facing a park is put together, and where an alarm earns its place on that kind of house.

Highland Park in six facts that shape an alarm

  • 141.28 acres of park on the county line. The park sits in Brooklyn on the border with Queens, with Cypress Hills to the south and Glendale and Ridgewood, Queens, to the north (Wikipedia, Highland Park).
  • About 72 acres of natural area. NYC Parks counts 40.9 acres of forest, 8.1 acres of freshwater wetland and 2.8 acres of grassland across the park and the drained reservoir basins, with a paved loop of about three miles on the reservoir walls.
  • A reservoir drained in 1989. The Ridgewood Reservoir’s third basin was used as a backup until it was decommissioned and drained in 1989; the state designated it a Class I freshwater wetland in 2018, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places on February 2, 2018 (Wikipedia, Ridgewood Reservoir).
  • Houses above the street. On Sunnyside Avenue the north-side houses stand on steep stairs and graded lawns while identical houses opposite sit at street level (Brownstoner, 149–181 Sunnyside Avenue).
  • Built mostly from 1900 to 1920. Richards Real Homes put up about 90 houses on Sunnyside Avenue in those two decades; the Highland Boulevard houses date from the same early-1900s period (Brownstoner).
  • The J on Jamaica Avenue since 1893. The elevated line that now carries the J and Z opened in 1893; Wikipedia lists Cleveland Street, Norwood Avenue, Crescent Street and Cypress Hills among its stations along Jamaica Avenue (Forgotten New York; Wikipedia, East New York).

Where Highland Park begins and ends is not settled. The name covers two things. One is the park, which NYC Parks and Wikipedia both describe, with the reservoir on its Queens side. The other is the residential section on the slope below it, which Brownstoner calls “the Highland Park section of Cypress Hills” and which Wikipedia’s East New York article places north of East New York. No city map draws a line around it. On this page, the houses we mean are the ones on the hill between Jamaica Avenue and Highland Boulevard, roughly from the Evergreens cemetery side in the west toward the Queens line in the east, including Sunnyside Avenue, Highland Place, Miller Avenue, Barbey Street, Hendrix Street, Vermont Street, the stretch of Arlington Avenue around Barbey Street and Schenck Avenue, and the courts. If your address is a few blocks south, our Cypress Hills alarm page or East New York alarm page may fit you better; the prices are identical.

Most Highland Park owners who call about an alarm also ask about a camera that looks across the porch or down the side yard. Those are handled together on one visit, and our Highland Park security camera installation page explains the camera side.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Highland Park’s Architecture

Highland Park was built up over a short period, mostly between 1900 and 1920, but by many different builders on lots of very different sizes. Five types cover most of what we see, and each has its own weak points.

The Sunnyside Avenue porch rows

Brick one-family houses with tile roofs, deep porches on Craftsman or Colonial columns, and bracketed bay roofs, built by Richards Real Homes to William C. Winters’ designs. On the north side they sit high above the street. The weak points are the porch, which shelters the front door from view, the bay, and the cellar at the foot of the stair.

Highland Boulevard’s large houses

Detached houses on wide lots, from Arts and Crafts designs in river rock and shingle to limestone and Stick style Victorians. Windows on all sides, outbuildings, and the park across the road. The work is mostly in deciding which of many openings are truly reachable, and in covering the side that faces trees.

Brick singles with gambrel roofs

Houses like 124 and 130 Arlington Avenue, built 1908 to 1912, with Dutch gambrel roofs and Colonial Revival trim. Solid masonry, original windows where they survive, and upper floors under the roof slope. The masonry decides how sensors and the panel are fixed.

Houses on the private courts

Row houses on short private dead ends like Schenck Court and Van Siclen Court, many from the early 1900s. Little through traffic, shared ownership of the roadway, and rear yards that meet other yards. Good for privacy, which is exactly why the back of the house needs attention.

Re-sided and altered houses

Spellen notes that only a few houses on that Arlington Avenue block keep their original brick; many nearby have been re-sided or altered. Siding over old walls, replacement windows mixed with originals, and porches closed in at different times all change what each opening needs.

Newer infill houses

Walsh noted new construction by United Homes on Sunnyside Avenue between Vermont Street and Miller Avenue in 2008, among detached houses. Newer houses usually have pre-run wiring or easy wall access, uniform windows, and an attached garage or a lower-level entrance on the slope.

The Hill

Houses Built Into the Moraine: Which Floor Is the Ground Floor?

On a flat block, an alarm starts at the ground floor and works upward: the front door, the back door, the first-floor windows, then whatever upper windows a ladder, a fence or a porch roof can reach. In Highland Park that order breaks down, because the ground floor is a matter of which side of the house you are standing on. A house on the north side of Sunnyside Avenue may have its front door a full storey above the sidewalk, reached by a long flight of steps through a graded lawn, while its cellar windows are at eye level from the sidewalk. A house on a cross street that climbs the hill may have a cellar fully buried at the uphill end and a full-height door and windows at the downhill end, opening onto the side yard. On the uphill side, the first-floor windows can be within arm’s reach of the yard.

So the first job on a survey is to walk all four sides and note, for each opening, the real height above the ground directly outside it. We mark each one on a sketch of the house: openings at standing height, openings reachable from a step, wall, fence or porch roof, and openings that truly need a ladder. That sketch, not the floor the window happens to be on, decides which openings get a contact or glass-break detector. On a typical Highland Park house it moves two or three openings onto the list that a standard “first floor plus basement” design would miss, and occasionally takes one off.

The cellar deserves its own note. Many houses here have a cellar door or old exterior bulkhead on the low side, and some have a separate entrance used by a tenant or for storage. Those doors are usually the least visible on the property: below the line of sight from the street, below the windows of the house itself, and often below the neighbor’s fence. A contact on each one is the minimum. Where a cellar has windows at grade on the low side, we look at whether those windows are fixed, barred or openable, and protect the openable ones with contacts and the large fixed ones with glass-break detection. Because a cellar can be the dampest room in the house, the panel and its battery go upstairs, never at the foot of the cellar stairs.

The front stair itself is less of a barrier than it looks. Anyone climbing it is in full view of the street until they reach the porch, where the roof, columns and railings hide them. A house high above the sidewalk is often well watched at the bottom of the lot and poorly watched at the top, and the alarm should be thickest where the watching stops.

The Park Side

Living Across From 141 Acres of Park: Alarms for the Side Nobody Overlooks

Most of Brooklyn is protected, without anyone deciding it, by the windows across the street. Someone is usually home, awake, and looking out. On the blocks that face Highland Park and the Ridgewood Reservoir, half of that is missing. NYC Parks describes the reservoir basins as forest and seasonally flooded wetland, with red maple, gray birch and gray willow, and a paved greenway of about three miles running around the top of the old walls. During the day the park is busy: NYC Parks lists three playgrounds, and walkers, cyclists and birders use the reservoir loop, which Wikipedia notes is part of the 40-mile Brooklyn–Queens Greenway. In the evening it empties, and the houses facing it lose their audience.

That changes three things in an alarm design: when the perimeter is armed, who is told when something trips, and how the front of the house is treated. The questions section below answers the first two in detail. The third is simple to state. Front windows that face the park get the protection we would give rear windows on an ordinary block, because functionally that is what they are: openings nobody is watching.

The rear edge works the same way. Walsh’s survey describes Robert Street as an unpaved path behind the Highland Boulevard houses, and Heath Place and Robert Place as vestigial ends of streets that were laid out and abandoned. Elsewhere, back yards end at a slope, a retaining wall or a fence that meets parkland. None of that is a reason for alarm in itself; plenty of houses back onto paths and parks without trouble. But a lane or slope behind a house means the back of the property can be approached without passing anyone’s front door, and that is the kind of approach an alarm is built for. On those houses the back door, any rear cellar entrance and the rear windows of the main floor are the first priority, and glass-break detection on large rear windows is worth more than on a house hemmed in by neighbors.

Original Openings

Porches, Bays, Casements and Transoms: Fitting Sensors to 1900–1920 Houses

Alarm equipment is designed around the modern window: a vinyl double-hung or slider with a flat frame, a straight gap between sash and jamb, and nothing decorative in the way. Highland Park’s houses were built a century before that, and many still have their original openings. Each type gets its own treatment, and getting it right is the difference between an alarm that works quietly for years and one that faults every humid August.

Five openings come up on almost every survey here. Deep porches, which Spellen describes on the Sunnyside Avenue houses as resting on Craftsman or Colonial columns under deep eaves, shelter the front door from view. Bay windows put three or more sashes on angled walls. Outward-swinging casements, common on Arts and Crafts houses, open from the latch side rather than sliding. Hinged transoms sit over doors and some windows. And cellars on the low side of the lot often have an exterior bulkhead. Each has its own question in the section below, with the sensor we choose and where it goes; the diagram shows the positions side by side.

One simpler rule completes the picture: French doors onto a side porch and double cellar doors get a contact on each leaf unless one leaf is permanently bolted.

Diagram: where contacts go on the openings most common in Highland Park houses. On a casement the latch side, not the hinge side; on a bay one contact per opening sash plus one glass-break for all the glass. AESS practice, schematic.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Highland Park

Highland Park is mostly houses, and most of its businesses sit on its southern edge, along Jamaica Avenue, where the J and Z trains run overhead. A storefront under an elevated line has particular habits. The steel columns and the shadow of the structure break up the view of the shop from across the avenue. The train itself runs every few minutes for most of the day and much of the night, and its rumble makes any sensor that reacts to vibration a poor choice for the front glass. Many have apartments above, a cellar below and a rear door onto a side street that climbs the hill.

On a typical Jamaica Avenue storefront we put a contact on the front door and on the rear door, a contact on the roll-down gate where the owner wants to know the moment it lifts, glass-break detection behind the display windows tuned to ignore train noise, a motion detector covering the sales floor from the back wall, a contact on the cellar door or hatch, and a panic button within reach of the register. The panel runs on its own cellular communicator so a cut phone or internet line does not silence it, and the app sends opening and closing reports so an owner knows the shop was locked at night and opened by the right person in the morning. If the apartments above share a hallway with the shop, we keep the business alarm entirely separate from the residential side.

Away from the avenue, the non-residential buildings we are asked about are mostly home offices, small trades and religious or community buildings. On Highland Boulevard, Walsh describes the Discalced Carmelite Sisters’ monastery there, in a building put up in 1974 as a Lithuanian community center. A building with public hours and private areas is alarmed in separate areas, each armed on its own schedule.

Jamaica Avenue storefronts

Shops and restaurants under the elevated J line, from the Cypress Hills end eastward. Gate contacts, glass-break that ignores the train, cellar and rear-door coverage, and a panic button.

Corner stores on the side streets

Small groceries and services at cross streets climbing the hill, often with an owner’s apartment above. Separate partitions for the shop and the home.

Home offices and small trades

Contractors, consultants and craftspeople working from a house or a garage on the slope. Tool storage on its own zone, with an alert to the phone rather than the house siren.

Religious and community buildings

Halls, chapels and offices with public hours. Separate areas, scheduled arming, and user codes for volunteers that can be added and removed easily.

Compliance notes for Highland Park buildings

  • Siren time limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building burglar alarm to stop sounding within fifteen minutes of activation. We program every panel we install with a siren cutoff inside that limit, which matters most on a shop below apartments.
  • Licensed installers. New York State licenses businesses that install security and fire alarms through the Department of State, which requires qualifying training, an examination and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431; ask any company you are considering for theirs.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors. In rental housing, HPD rules set out what owners must install and what tenants must maintain. Smoke and CO detectors that report through a burglar alarm are a welcome extra, but they do not replace the detectors the law requires in a two- or three-family house.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A commercial or institutional building that needs a code fire alarm gets one from a fire alarm contractor, filed with the city as required; the burglar alarm can share a communicator but is never a substitute. Our Highland Park fire alarm installation page covers that work.
  • No city alarm permit found. We looked for a citywide permit or registration requirement for residential burglar alarms in New York City and did not find one, so this page does not quote a permit fee.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Highland Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Highland Park owners put to us that come from the hill, the park and the age of the houses. Questions about price, timing and monitoring in general are in the FAQ further down.

A house built into the slope

Our house sits on a bank above the sidewalk, and on the downhill side the cellar wall is fully exposed, with windows at head height from the yard. Which level of a hillside house is really the one to protect first?

Usually the exposed cellar level on the downhill side, and then whichever first-floor windows can be reached from the uphill yard. The front door at the top of the stair matters too, but it is the one opening on the property that the whole street can see. The cellar wall on the low side is the reverse: it is below the sightline of the street, below the windows of your own house, and often hidden from the neighbor by a fence or a change in grade. Anyone working at a window there is out of view.

We start every hillside design by walking all four sides and noting the height of the ground outside each opening. Openable cellar windows on the exposed side get contacts; large fixed panes get glass-break coverage from inside; the cellar door gets a heavy-duty contact. On the uphill side, a first-floor window with its sill a few feet above the yard is treated as a ground-floor window, whatever the floor plan calls it. The panel and its battery go upstairs, away from cellar damp, and a motion detector at the top of the cellar stairs covers the route up into the house.

Our cellar has an old steel bulkhead door set into the slope of the side yard. Can a door like that be put on the alarm?

Yes. Exterior bulkhead doors, the slanted pairs of steel or wooden leaves over a set of outside cellar steps, are common on houses of this age and are among the least watched openings on a property. Each leaf that can be opened gets its own heavy-duty contact, usually a wide-gap type mounted on the inside of the frame, because bulkhead leaves rarely close to a tight, consistent gap after decades of weather. If one leaf is bolted from inside and never used, it can stay off the alarm.

Just as important is the door at the bottom of the steps, the one into the cellar itself. If it exists, it gets a contact as well, so the alarm still trips if someone lifts the bulkhead quietly and works on the inner door out of sight. In winter, ice and snow can shift a bulkhead leaf enough to open a contact; we set the gap with that in mind and show you how to bypass that one zone for a night if a storm has jammed it.

Facing the park and the reservoir

After dark the park across from our house is empty, and some stretches are unlit. Does living opposite a park change when the alarm should be armed and who should hear about it?

It changes both. On an ordinary block, people across the street are an unofficial early warning, so many families arm only when they leave or go to bed. Opposite a park, that warning disappears at dusk. We suggest a schedule that arms the perimeter, every door and the reachable windows, automatically at sunset, with the interior motion detectors left off so the family can move around normally. It disarms with your code or your phone like any other arming, and it costs nothing to set up.

As for who hears about it, a siren on the park side of the house has few listeners at night. We set up alerts to every adult in the household, add a trusted neighbor on the house side of the street if you want one, and, if you choose monitoring, give the central station a call list that starts with someone likely to answer. The siren still sounds inside, where it does the most good: it tells an intruder they have been noticed.

Summer gatherings, music and Fourth of July fireworks around the park get loud. Will a glass-break detector on the park side of the house mistake that noise for breaking glass?

Not if it is the right kind and properly placed. Current acoustic glass-break detectors listen for a particular two-part pattern: the low thud of something striking glass followed within a fraction of a second by the high-frequency sound of glass shattering. Fireworks, shouting and music do not normally produce that pair, which is why a good detector ignores them. Cheap single-frequency detectors are another story, and we do not install them.

Placement matters as much as type. The detector goes inside the room, on a wall or ceiling with a clear line to the windows it covers, never pointed out through an open window, and within the distance its maker specifies. We test each one with a glass-break simulator before we leave, which plays the real pattern at the detector, and we turn sensitivity down a step on park-facing rooms if your house is unusually close to an event area. If you ever get a false trip on a festival night, the panel logs which detector it was, and we adjust that one.

Our house is in Brooklyn, but the reservoir across Highland Boulevard is on the Queens side of the line. When the alarm goes off, is there any chance help is sent to the wrong borough?

The risk is real enough to plan for, and easy to remove. Wikipedia places the Ridgewood Reservoir in Glendale, Queens, within Highland Park, with Highland Boulevard as its southern boundary, so the county line runs very close to some Brooklyn houses. Emergency dispatch works from the street address, and a correct, complete address is what puts a call in the right hands. The weak point is not the city; it is a monitoring account that holds a vague address, a missing apartment letter, or a street name that exists in both boroughs.

When we register a monitored system, we enter the full address with the borough as Brooklyn and the ZIP code, add the nearest cross street, and note anything that confuses visitors, such as a house number on a corner that faces a different street from its front door, or a home on a private court that some maps do not show. Then we run a test signal and ask the central station to read the address back to you.

Our house faces the park and has a steep red tile roof with deep eaves. Where can an outdoor siren and strobe actually do some good, and how is it mounted without anyone walking on the tiles?

An outdoor siren is not required, and on some Highland Park houses it is not worth having. Facing the park, it mostly tells the trees. Where it helps is on the side of the house that faces neighbors, where a flashing strobe also tells a responding officer or a neighbor which house is in alarm. On a detached house that is usually a side wall, and on a row house the rear.

Nothing goes on the roof. Old clay tiles break under a foot and are expensive to match, so an exterior siren goes under the eaves on a gable wall or on the fascia, reached from a ladder, with the cable run through the attic if the attic is safe to enter, or along an existing downpipe line if it is not. Many owners choose instead to keep all sounders inside and rely on alerts, which is a perfectly good choice opposite a park. Whatever you choose, the siren cutoff is programmed inside the fifteen-minute limit in New York City Administrative Code § 24-238.

Original openings on houses from 1900 to 1920

Our deep front porch gives anyone at the door a sheltered spot out of the rain and out of sight of the street. Should the porch itself be covered by a sensor, or only the door?

Usually only the door, plus the inside of the hall behind it. An outdoor motion sensor on a porch sees everyone who rings the bell, delivers a package or shelters from rain, so it cannot be allowed to trigger the alarm. What it can do, if you want it, is announce people: a porch camera or video doorbell sends a notification with a picture when someone lingers, without involving the siren at all.

The alarm itself goes where a trigger means someone is coming in. The main door gets a heavy-duty contact. Any glass in the door, sidelights beside it or a porch window into the front room is covered by a glass-break detector inside. A motion detector in the front hall watches the door from inside, so even someone who opens it without disturbing the contact is seen within a step or two. If your porch has a second door into a front room, that door gets its own contact; porches on houses of this age often do.

Our Arts and Crafts house has casement windows that swing outward on cranks. Where does a contact go on a window that opens out instead of sliding up?

On the latch side of the sash, where it moves away from the frame first and farthest when opened. The magnet goes on the sash and the sensor on the frame, or the reverse if the sash is too narrow, positioned near the top or bottom corner on the latch side so the gap closes completely when the window is latched. On the hinge side the sash barely moves for the first few degrees of opening, so a contact there can miss a window that has been pried open just far enough to reach inside.

Old casements have two habits we check for. Some no longer close square after a century of paint and settling, leaving a gap that varies with the weather; we test the contact at several points and choose one where it reads closed every time. And some have stays or crank arms in the way of the obvious mounting spot; small surface contacts can go beside them. If a casement simply will not close properly, we tell you, and suggest glass-break coverage for that room until a carpenter has seen it.

The windows on our bay are three separate sashes on angled walls. Is one sensor enough for the whole bay, or does each sash need its own?

Each operable sash needs its own contact, because a magnetic contact only knows whether one particular sash is closed against one particular frame. A bay on a Sunnyside Avenue house, for example, often has a center sash that opens and narrower side sashes that may or may not. We test each one. Sashes painted or screwed shut get nothing; sashes that open, even a few inches, get a small contact each.

Glass is a separate question. The panes in a bay are usually the largest in the house, and a contact does not notice broken glass. One acoustic glass-break detector on the wall facing the bay, or on the ceiling in front of it, listens to all three planes at once, so a single device covers the glass for the whole bay. That pairing, small contacts on the opening sashes and one glass-break across the room, is what we fit on most bays here.

Over many of our doors and a few windows there are small hinged transom lights that tilt open for air. Do transoms need sensors?

It depends on whether they open, how big they are, and what they are next to. A transom painted shut, as many are, needs nothing. One that opens and is large enough for an adult to climb through is treated like any other window and gets a contact. Most transoms are smaller than that, and the risk is not someone climbing through but someone reaching through: a transom over a door can let an arm reach down to a thumb latch or bolt.

For those, a contact on the transom itself is often fiddly on old hinged frames, and a glass-break detector covering the door, plus the contact on the door below, protects better. Over interior doors, transoms are irrelevant to the alarm. We mark every transom painted, small or passable on the survey sketch.

Shops on Jamaica Avenue

Our store is on Jamaica Avenue right under the J line, and the steel columns block the view of our gate from passing patrol cars. Does that change what the alarm should do when it trips?

It argues for knowing sooner and confirming faster. If passers-by and patrols cannot easily see your gate, the alarm has to be the one that notices. We put a contact on the roll-down gate so you are alerted the moment it starts to lift, rather than when someone finally reaches the door behind it, and glass-break behind the display window for anyone who goes through the glass instead. Shock sensors on the window are avoided under the el, because the trains would set them off.

Confirmation is the second part. A camera inside, facing the gate and the register, lets you or a central station look at the moment of an alarm and see whether it is a person or a sign blown over. Many monitoring services treat a visually confirmed break-in as a higher priority when they call the police. If the store is yours and the apartments above are not, we also make sure the siren is programmed to stop within the fifteen minutes the city allows, so a night alarm does not run long under people’s bedrooms.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Highland Park

We could not open Google’s AI Overview from where this page was written, so the claims below are not quotations. They are the generic statements that AI summaries and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr commonly make about home alarms, set against what we see on a hillside of hundred-year-old houses beside a park.

“A typical home needs one sensor per exterior door and a few first-floor windows.”

That rule assumes a house on flat ground, where “first floor” and “ground level” mean the same thing. On Highland Park’s slope they often do not. A house on the north side of Sunnyside Avenue can have its first floor a full storey above the sidewalk and its cellar windows at eye level, while a house on a climbing cross street can have a buried cellar at one end and a walk-out cellar at the other.

Counting by floor gives the wrong list. Counting by the height of the ground outside each opening gives the right one, and on a typical house here that adds a cellar door, one or two cellar windows and an uphill first-floor window that a floor-based rule would skip.

So the total is often larger than national averages imply. That is the shape of the house, not upselling.

“Professional installation costs a few hundred dollars on top of the equipment.”

National cost guides often quote installation as a small add-on to a boxed kit. That can be true for a four-room apartment with modern windows. It is rarely true for a detached Highland Park house with thirty-odd windows, original casements and a walk-out cellar, where the time goes into survey, placement and adjustment rather than sticking sensors on flat frames.

Our prices are published as complete Brooklyn figures: a basic residential system from $600, complete home protection from $2,500, and a custom hardwired system for a whole property between $2,000 and $6,000, all before sales tax. Labor-only installation of a kit you already own is $200 to $400.

“Outdoor motion sensors are the best early warning for a house with a yard.”

For a fenced yard surrounded by other yards, outdoor detection can work. For a house facing the park or backing onto a wooded slope, an outdoor detector looks straight at moving branches, animals, wind-blown leaves and people walking the park after work.

The result is a system that cries wolf, and a system that cries wolf gets switched off. We have seen it in older installations where the outdoor zone was simply bypassed for years.

Our approach on park-facing houses is to keep the alarm on doors, windows and the interior, where a trigger means entry, and to use a camera with person detection outside if the owner wants warning, sending a picture instead of sounding a siren.

“Wireless sensors stick on anywhere with adhesive.”

Adhesive pads hold well on smooth modern frames. On a century of paint over wood, on brick, on river rock or on old plaster, they let go, usually in the first humid summer, and a fallen sensor reports an open window that is actually shut.

On Highland Park houses we fix most contacts with small screws into the frame or stop, use anchors in mortar joints on masonry, and never drill decorative stone. Each fixing is agreed with the owner, and the holes are small enough to fill invisibly if the system is ever removed.

Wireless is still the right choice in most of these houses, because it avoids opening plaster walls. It is the adhesive that is the problem, not the radio.

“ADT, Ring or SimpliSafe will cover any home the same way.”

National brands each do something well. ADT offers large-scale professional monitoring; Ring and SimpliSafe sell easy self-install kits with low monthly options. None of them can look at your bay window or your bulkhead door from a website, and a boxed kit contains the sensors a typical modern house needs, not the ones a 1910 house on a slope needs.

Where a family already owns one of those kits, we are happy to install it, add the pieces it lacks, and tell you plainly where it falls short. Where they are starting fresh, we design around the house first and choose equipment second, with monitoring optional and month to month.

The real choice is who walks the house before the parts are chosen.

“An alarm makes a house safe on its own.”

An alarm detects and reports. It does not lock doors, it does not stop someone determined from breaking glass, and it does not replace good locks, lighting on the side yard and the habit of locking the cellar. Summaries that present an alarm as complete protection oversell it.

What an alarm does well is shorten the time between entry and someone knowing about it, and make that knowledge reach the right person. On a park-facing house, where fewer people are watching, that is worth a great deal.

A perfect sensor that alerts nobody useful protects nothing.

“You need a city permit before installing a home alarm.”

Some AI answers and generic cost guides tell New Yorkers to budget for an alarm permit. We looked for a citywide permit or registration requirement for residential burglar alarms in New York City and did not find one, so we do not charge for one or quote a fee.

What the city does regulate is how long a building’s burglar alarm may sound: Administrative Code § 24-238 requires it to stop within fifteen minutes. The state, separately, requires that alarm installation businesses be licensed through the Department of State.

If a co-op, a lender or an insurer asks for paperwork, the usual request is a certificate of installation or of insurance, which we provide. If any agency’s rules change, we will say so on this page rather than guess.

Field Notes: Measure the Ground, Not the Floor Plan

This describes how our technicians survey on a hill, not a story about a particular house. The technician starts at the sidewalk and looks up: how high the front door sits, where the porch hides it, which windows can be seen from the street and which cannot. Then he walks the side of the lot downhill, noting where the cellar wall comes out of the ground, where any door or bulkhead opens, and how far the nearest neighbor’s windows look onto it. At the back he notes what the yard ends in and whether anything there brings a window within reach.

Only then does he go inside. Every opening is tried by hand: does it open, does it latch, is it painted shut, does the sash close square. Each one is marked on a sketch with the height of the ground outside it. The quote that follows lists openings by that sketch, so the owner can see why the uphill dining-room window is on the alarm and the third-floor dormer is not, and can argue with any line of it before a single sensor goes up.

Our front hall has an open staircase rising two floors with a landing window halfway up. Where can one motion detector cover all of that?
+

Usually on the wall of the front hall facing the bottom of the stair, at the height the manufacturer specifies, aimed so its pattern crosses the foot of the stair, the front door and the first flight. Anyone coming down from the landing window or up from the front door walks across it. The landing window itself gets a contact if it opens, because a motion detector should back up the openings, not replace them. In very tall halls we check the detector’s coverage pattern against the actual stair before fixing it.

Can the alarm be armed and disarmed by a voice assistant like Alexa or Google?
+

Many current panels and alarm apps link to voice assistants, and arming by voice is convenient. Disarming by voice is a different matter: we set it up only with a spoken PIN, or leave it off entirely, so that nobody can call through a window or play a recording to switch the system off. We show you the settings during handover so you can decide for yourselves.

The wires from our old alarm disappear into the plaster and nobody has a diagram. Can you work out where they go before deciding whether to reuse them?
+

Usually, yes. With the old panel open, we test each pair of wires to see which are intact and which are broken, then trip doors and windows one at a time to match wires to openings. Where a wire leads to a sensor that still works, it can often be kept; where it is broken inside a wall, we replace that one zone with a wireless sensor rather than open the plaster. You get a list of what was kept and what was replaced, and the price is fixed before we start.

Will your technician need to get into our attic or a crawl space, and what if ours has no safe floor?

Only if you want a hardwired device on the top floor or an exterior siren under the eaves, and only where it is safe. Many attics in houses of this age have no flooring between the joists and old insulation underfoot; in those, we do not walk the attic at all. Instead we use wireless devices on the top floor and keep any exterior siren to a spot reachable from a ladder outside. Nobody is sent into a space that is not safe to stand in.

Do you offer a yearly check-up of the alarm after installation?

Yes, if you would like one. Where the Highland Park Facts Come From

Every source below was opened in October 2026, and the page uses only what it attributes to each. Two kinds of statement carry no citation because they are ours: the prices, which are AESS’s published Brooklyn alarm list, and the descriptions of how our technicians survey, mount and program, which describe our practice rather than any particular job.

  • Wikipedia, “Highland Park (Brooklyn)” — 141.28 acres; Brooklyn on the Queens border; neighbors Cypress Hills, Glendale and Ridgewood; 1891 purchase, 1901 establishment, 1905 Schenck estate, 1906 final expansion; The Dawn of Glory 1925; Vito P. Battista Playground 1991; reservoir in service to 1959, third basin drained 1989, transferred to Parks 2004; renovation completed October 2013
  • NYC Parks, Highland Park natural areas — about 72 acres of natural area: 40.9 acres of forest, 8.1 of wetland, 2.8 of grassland; basin sizes; red maple, gray birch and gray willow; paved loop of about three miles; National Register 2018 and Class I wetland
  • NYC Parks, Highland Park highlights — three playgrounds; the name’s origin in the high plateau and its views; reservoir land acquired 1856 and park purchases of 1891, 1905 and 1906–08; 1935 reconstruction; 2020 playground renovation
  • Wikipedia, “Ridgewood Reservoir” — about 50 acres in Glendale, Queens; bounded by the Jackie Robinson Parkway, Highland Boulevard, Vermont Place and Cypress Hills National Cemetery; groundbreaking July 11, 1856, water raised November 18, 1858; drained 1989; Class I wetland February 2018; National Register February 2, 2018; part of the Brooklyn–Queens Greenway
  • Forgotten New York, Kevin Walsh, “Highland Park, Brooklyn, Part 1” — the Town of New Lots and the 26th Ward; the reservoir built 1856–59; the 1893 elevated line over Jamaica Avenue; Miller Avenue’s two-block climb; the courts between Jamaica and Sunnyside; Robert Street’s unpaved path and glass-topped wall; Richards’ row houses on Sunnyside; Vito Battista co-naming
  • Forgotten New York, Kevin Walsh, “Highland Park, Brooklyn, Part 2” (2008) — Robert Place, Heath Place, Vermont Court and Place; United Homes construction on Sunnyside Avenue; the Carmelite monastery on Highland Boulevard, built 1974 as a Lithuanian community center; the 1897 Vermont Street viaduct; the Interboro (now Jackie Robinson) Parkway built 1933–35
  • Brownstoner, Suzanne Spellen, “Building of the Day: 278 Highland Boulevard” (2013) — Arts and Crafts house of river rock and shingles with a steep tiled roof and matching garage; Highland Boulevard as the area’s wealthiest street; early-1900s German-American families; limestone and Stick style houses nearby
  • Brownstoner, “Building of the Day: 149–181 Sunnyside Avenue” — about 90 houses by Richards Real Homes, 1900–1920, architect William C. Winters; brick, tile roofs, deep porches, bays; north-side houses on steep stairs and graded lawns; the terminal moraine
  • Brownstoner, “Building of the Day: 124 and 130 Arlington Avenue” — brick single-family houses of 1908–1912 with a Dutch gambrel roof; many nearby houses re-sided or altered; nearness to Broadway Junction
  • Wikipedia, “East New York” — Highland Park north of East New York; J and Z stations along Jamaica Avenue; patrol by the 75th Precinct; Community District 5
  • Reddit r/AskNYC, “Considering buying a home in Highland Park/Cypress Hill” — a buyer’s list of pros beginning “Next to Park” (search excerpt only; the thread itself could not be opened)
  • NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 — building burglar alarms must stop sounding within fifteen minutes of activation
  • New York Department of State, security or fire alarm installer license — licensing of alarm installation businesses, qualifying education, examination and fingerprinting
  • NYC HPD, smoke and carbon monoxide detector requirements — owner and tenant responsibilities in rental housing

Some sources disagree. NYC Parks’ highlights page says the Ridgewood Reservoir operated until 1985, its natural-areas page says it supplied water from 1858 to 1959, and Wikipedia says it left regular service in 1959 with the third basin kept as a backup until it was drained in 1989; this page uses the Wikipedia dates and notes the difference here. The boundary of Highland Park as a residential area is not fixed by any city map, as explained in the neighborhood section. Kevin Walsh’s Part 2 dates from 2008, so its description of new construction on Sunnyside Avenue is historical.
