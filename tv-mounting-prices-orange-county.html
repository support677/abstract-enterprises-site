Drywall over wood studs (Chester, Monroe and newer Montgomery homes)

We locate both studs, confirm each with a small probe hole, and drive 5/16″ lag bolts into the center of the wood so the wall plate can't creep. When a window or hearth leaves one usable stud, we screw an $85 plywood panel across three or four of them and hang the bracket on that. No wall add applies.

Plaster on wood lath (Middletown, Port Jervis and East End partitions)

Lath fools electronic stud finders, so the tech traces the nails with a magnet and drills small test holes. Plaster is scored and pre-drilled so it doesn't blow out, then the lag bolts bite the framing behind. The add is $45, and old keyed plaster can chip at the edge; patching isn't included.

Plaster directly on brick (Newburgh party walls)

There's no wood behind these walls. We confirm brick with a test hole, then set sleeve or wedge anchors into solid brick faces, not into the mortar line alone. Because the anchors hold in masonry, the tech prices it as brick, +$65, and shows you that number before drilling.

Fieldstone chimneys and stone-veneer fireplaces

Real fieldstone gets masonry anchors set in sound stone. Manufactured veneer on a new build is only an inch or two thick over a framed chase, so we drill through it into the wood behind. Stone adds $65, above-fireplace placement $130, or $160 for a MantelMount.

Cinder block and poured concrete basements

Finished basements in raised ranches and splits often have one block wall left exposed or furred. Block is hollow inside, so pilot holes tell us where the solid webs are before any anchor goes in. Block or concrete adds $75; a $59 paintable cover handles the cord on a wall we can't open.

Metal studs and tile (offices, warehouses and kitchens)

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in Orange County

Ask an AI tool what mounting a TV costs in Orange County, NY, and the answer often blends out-of-state results, national averages and lead-generation pages. Some of it is close. Much of it misses how walls here are actually built, what the job includes, and who should be doing which part. We picked the seven we hear most and set each against what Orange County walls actually show.

1. AI says: “Near West Point, mounting a TV averages $400 to $535.”

That range comes from a cost page built for lead generation, which also lists a typical spread of $206 to $962. It folds brackets, wiring, electrical and sometimes drywall repair into one number, so the average looks far higher than a plain mount costs.

With us, a 65″ on drywall in Highland Falls is $259.99 in labor. Even a 75″ above a stone fireplace is $484.99. You'd need a big set, masonry, a fireplace and hidden wires before you approach the top of that quoted range.

The point isn't that the page is dishonest; it's that averages hide the variables. Price your own wall, size and bracket in the calculator, and you'll know the number before anyone arrives. If your job does land near the top of that range, you'll at least see which line items put it there.

2. AI says: “TV mounting with hidden wires is a $139 job.”

Marketplace pages often show sample prices like $119 for a mount and $139 with wires inside the wall. Those are examples one provider posted, usually for a small TV on drywall, with the bracket and the in-wall hardware not included.

A legal concealment needs a listed power-and-cable kit, because a TV's power cord isn't rated to run inside a wall. Our kit is $169 on top of the mounting labor, and the result is a recessed outlet box behind the screen fed from your existing outlet.

If an offer seems to include concealment for almost nothing, ask how the power gets behind the TV. If the answer is the TV's own cord pushed through the drywall, that's a code problem and a fire risk. A recessed box fed by a short power lead behind the wall plate is the safe version.

3. AI says: “New construction is the easiest place to mount a TV.”

Drywall over studs in a new Chester or Montgomery house is easy to anchor into, and that's why it carries no wall add. The tricky part is everything around the wall: two-story ceilings, gas fireplaces and outlets placed for a TV stand.

Builders often center a single outlet low on the fireplace wall and frame the chase above it, leaving one stud where you want two. That's where an $85 plywood backer, a $99 cable run, or the $169 in-wall kit comes in.

Height is the other trap. In a great room, the empty wall above a mantel looks inviting, but a fixed TV up there strains your neck. A tilt bracket or a MantelMount (+$160) usually solves it better than mounting higher.

4. AI says: “If you're renting near a base, keep the TV on a stand.”

A stand is a fine choice, but it isn't your only one. Plenty of landlords around Highland Falls, Cornwall and New Windsor allow a TV mount when asked, especially if you agree to leave the bracket or fill the holes at move-out.

Get the permission in writing, either from the owner or the housing office, and keep it on your phone for the appointment. Then a 55″ on drywall is $229.99. When orders come, take-down is $85 and bracket removal $45, and the bracket can go with you to the next duty station.

If the answer is no, we'll still set the TV on a stand, connect your devices and configure it for $153.99. Either way, nothing gets drilled without the owner's OK, and patching stays your responsibility. A stand also packs faster when the moving company shows up.

5. AI says: “A lake cottage wall is the same as any other wall.”

Greenwood Lake's houses began as seasonal cottages and were expanded over generations. Behind paneling you may find studs at odd spacing, a former exterior wall now inside, or sheathing boards with no stud where you'd expect one. Some walls were furred out twice as the cottage grew.

We find framing by measuring from corners and openings, confirming with small probe holes, and bridging mismatched studs with an $85 plywood panel. Knotty pine shows every mistake, so every probe hole goes where the bracket plate will cover it later.

Lake houses also have damp crawl spaces and decks that move with the seasons. For an outdoor screen we anchor to the house framing, not a railing, and add $90 for the outdoor mount. Moisture is the other enemy, so the TV, bracket and any outdoor cord need to be rated for it.

6. AI says: “Old row houses all have plaster on wood lath.”

Inside walls in Newburgh's East End are often plaster on lath, but the long party walls between row houses are typically solid brick with plaster applied directly to it. A stud finder shows nothing, and a drywall anchor finds no grip.

So the tech drills one test hole before deciding. Lath means lag bolts into framing at the $45 plaster add. Brick means sleeve or wedge anchors set into the brick face, priced as brick at $65. You see the number first.

Middletown and Port Jervis houses from the same era add another wrinkle: walls that bow and ceilings that sag. We level the TV to the room's lines, not to a bubble on a crooked wall, so it looks straight from the couch.

7. AI says: “Any TV over 75 inches needs a two-person crew.”

Not with us. One technician handles screens up to 85″, including 77″ and 83″ OLEDs, with no crew surcharge. The 76″–85″ tier is a flat $329.99 on drywall, which is the cost to mount an 85″ TV in most Orange County family rooms.

We send two technicians from 86″ up, because those panels are heavy and awkward to lift onto a bracket safely. The 86″–97″ tier is $404.99, and 98″ and larger is $449.99, both including the second person. Nothing else about those jobs changes: same wall adds, same bracket list.

What matters more than the screen size is the path in. Split-level stairs, tight row-house hallways and narrow basement doors decide whether a big box fits. Measure turns before you buy, and mention any tight spots in your booking notes so the techs come prepared.
