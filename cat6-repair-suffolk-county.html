The Neighborhood

Why Suffolk County Properties Need Cat6 Repair That Reads the Building's History

Suffolk is eighty-six miles long, it has ten towns and a long list of incorporated villages, and very few of its buildings are still used the way they were when their data cable went in. That is the single most useful thing to know about Cat6 repair in this county. In a Manhattan tower the problem is usually getting to the cable. In Suffolk the cable is usually easy enough to reach, but it was laid out for a different tenant, a different floor plan, a lower house, a different business or a different season, and the building has moved on without it. A link that tested perfectly in 2012 can fail today not because the copper aged but because somebody split the space, raised the floor, rerouted the duct, moved the closet or added three feet of cable with a coupler to reach a desk that used to be elsewhere.

Commercial space shows it most clearly. CoStar described the industrial cluster around Hauppauge, Central Islip and Bay Shore in August 2024 as Long Island's largest, with more than 26.1 million square feet, and said owners there were contending with the most available space on the Island because of a mismatch between the size of the buildings and what tenants now want — smaller bays. When a large single-tenant building is carved into smaller units to meet that demand, the cabling is rarely carved up with it. We find jacks in one tenant's office that are punched down on a panel in another tenant's closet, runs that cross the demising wall above the ceiling, a single switch serving two businesses, and long stretches of cable whose far end has been abandoned behind new drywall. Each of those can produce a failing link, and each one also means the repair has to decide whose cable it is before anyone touches it.

Houses have their own version. After Superstorm Sandy, FEMA's tally reported by Newsday counted 20,798 Suffolk structures flooded, damaged or destroyed, and along the South Shore — in Lindenhurst, Babylon and Amityville, where the National Weather Service recorded two to five feet of water south of Montauk Highway — owners took state money to raise their houses or sell them for demolition. CBS New York reported in 2015 that NY Rising elevation grants for Long Island shoreline homeowners had averaged over $100,000, and that towns and villages were requiring closed foundations with water vents rather than open pilings. Every raised house had its low-voltage wiring disturbed: runs that once dropped into a basement or a slab were cut, extended or rerouted through a new crawl space or the enclosure beneath the living floor. FEMA's own guidance limits that enclosure to parking, building access and storage. Cat6 that was spliced to reach a router left down there is among the most common failing runs we see on the South Shore, and it fails for reasons that have nothing to do with the cable's age.

Then there is the East End, where buildings change use as well as owners. Suffolk had 578 farms and 79 wineries in 2022, according to the state comptroller's agricultural profile as summarised by the Shelter Island Reporter, and a good share of North and South Fork commerce now happens inside buildings built for something else — barns turned tasting rooms, packing sheds turned offices, garages turned guest cottages, houses turned rentals with a new family on the Wi-Fi every week of the summer. Cabling in those buildings was often added in stages by whoever was there, through spaces never meant to hold it, and it gets moved, unplugged and re-patched by people who are not thinking about category ratings.

Finally, a great deal of new Cat6 is going into Suffolk and much of it is never tested by anyone after handover. Station Yards at Ronkonkoma alone is planned for 1,450 homes when complete, with 489 delivered in its first phase in 2020. New apartments, new medical buildings and new tenant fit-outs all hand over jacks that may have been terminated in a hurry, pinched by a drywall screw or never connected at the panel. When those fail, the owner is usually told it is a network problem. Often it is a cabling problem with a very ordinary cause, and a certification tester finds it in minutes.

How the repair changes by building type

Subdivided flex buildings. Before we repair a single link in a divided building, we map it: which jacks in your bay land on which panel, which panel sits in which tenant's space, and which runs pass over the demising wall. That usually takes a toner, a tester and an hour with the ceiling tiles out. The repair that follows is often less about re-terminating and more about ownership — moving your links to a panel you control, capping the stubs that wander next door, and leaving you a port map that matches the space you lease. Where the old closet is now inside someone else's unit and cannot be moved, we document which ports belong to you so the landlord can sort out access.

Raised houses. A house lifted on a new foundation usually had its low-voltage wiring cut at the old floor line and reconnected afterwards. Power wiring is reconnected and inspected as part of the job; low-voltage cable is often an afterthought and gets a coupler, a crimped joint or a box of splices in the crawl space so the existing runs reach the router again. Those joints are the weak point. We trace each run, take the joints out, replace the short lengths with continuous cable from the room to a new equipment location above the design flood elevation, and certify each link from end to end so it is a single permanent link again rather than a chain of pieces.

Barrier-island cottages. On Fire Island the repair is planned before anyone leaves the mainland. We confirm how the technician reaches the community — ferry, water taxi or a contractor's driving permit where the National Park Service allows one — and pack every part the likely repair could need, because there is no running back to the van. Terminations get sealed or moved indoors, and anything on a deck or under a house gets cable rated for the exposure.

Farm and winery buildings. In production spaces we keep terminations away from wash-down areas and equipment paths, mount boxes high and sealed, protect runs crossing floors or beams where forklifts and bins move, and check that the cable is rated for a building that may not be heated. Outdoor or between-building runs get outdoor-rated cable and proper entry protection.

Hospitals, colleges and new construction. In institutional buildings we work to the facilities office's rules, schedule around patients or classes, and hand back saved certification results for every link touched. In new buildings we test everything the owner reports dead and the jacks around it, because fit-out faults come in clusters.

Licensing, permits and code callouts for Suffolk Cat6 repairs

  • Suffolk County Chapter 563. The county code licenses home improvement contractors under Article II and master and restricted electricians under Article XI, through the Department of Consumer Affairs. RiverheadLOCAL reported in 2019 that the home improvement licence requires a written test plus liability and workers' compensation insurance, that working without one can bring civil penalties of up to $750 for a first violation and $1,500 after that, and that 9,107 such licences were held countywide in 2018. Which licence a given job needs depends on the work and the address, and we confirm it before starting.
  • Town permit desks. Building and electrical permits in Suffolk are issued by the ten towns and the villages, not by the county. The Town of Southampton, for example, runs its own electrical e-permit portal. A routine Cat6 repair rarely needs a permit, but new exterior penetrations, trenching or work in a rated assembly can, and we check with the municipality before quoting that work.
  • Utility marking before digging. New York 811 serves Nassau and Suffolk, and asks that it be called at least 48 hours and no more than 10 working days before any digging. A failed buried Cat6 run is not dug up until the marks are in.
  • Flood-zone enclosures. FEMA's Technical Bulletin 5 limits the enclosure under an elevated building to parking, building access and storage, and FEMA guidance expects building utility components below the required elevation to be elevated or designed for flood loads. On raised houses we move network equipment and terminations above the flood line rather than repair them in the enclosure.
  • Central Pine Barrens. In the Core Preservation Area, clearing or materially altering vegetation counts as development under the Long Island Pine Barrens Protection Act, which the Central Pine Barrens Commission says requires a hardship waiver. Buried repairs in the pine barrens of eastern Brookhaven, western Southampton and southern Riverhead are planned around that.
  • Abandoned cable and entry protection. When a run is replaced, the accessible part of the old communications cable comes out unless it is tagged for future use, as the National Electrical Code requires, and outdoor runs entering a building are fitted with a listed primary protector close to the entry point.

Businesses whose failing links feed doors and readers often fold that work into the same visit; our access control installation team in Suffolk County handles the controllers and readers once the cabling underneath them is sound.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Suffolk County: Questions Answered

These are the questions tenants, homeowners, property managers, farm and winery owners and IT providers in Suffolk ask once a Cat6 link has started failing. They are grouped by what changed around the cable: the tenancy, the house, the land and the island, and new buildings. Scheduling and smaller practical points are in the FAQ further down.

When the space was divided or re-let

Our bay in a subdivided Hauppauge flex building has jacks that still run back to a closet on the other tenant's side. Can the repair bring them to our own panel?

Yes, and in most subdivided buildings it is the repair that stops the failures from coming back. When a large building is split, the original closet stays wherever it was, and the jacks in the new bays keep their old runs to it. Your links then depend on a patch panel, a switch and sometimes a power strip in a space you cannot enter, which the neighbouring tenant can unplug, re-patch or lock. They also tend to be long, because the closet was placed for the old floor plan rather than yours.

We first map the bay: which jacks are yours, where each one lands, and whether the run passes over the demising wall. Where your space has a sensible location for a small rack or wall cabinet, we pull new runs or reroute the existing ones to it, terminate them on a panel you control, and certify each link. The stubs left on the old panel are disconnected and labelled so nobody patches into them by mistake. If the landlord wants the old closet left as it is, we document which ports used to be yours so the building's records are accurate.

Our lease says the landlord hands over working data cabling. What test results should we ask for before accepting the space?

Ask for certification results, not a continuity check. A simple tester that lights all eight wires proves only that each conductor is connected end to end; it does not show whether the link meets Category 6 for crosstalk, return loss, insertion loss and length. A certification report shows each link's result against the category limit and its margin, so you can see which links passed comfortably, which barely passed and which failed. It is the only evidence that will settle a dispute later about whether the cabling was working at handover.

If the landlord cannot provide it, we can certify the space for you before you sign off. We test every jack, compare it with the floor plan you are leasing, and give you a summary that separates passing links, thin-margin links, failed links and jacks that land somewhere other than your space. That summary is what you take back to the landlord. We repair the failures on your instruction or the landlord's, whichever the lease makes responsible.

Floor jacks in our slab-on-grade office keep failing after the floors are washed or after heavy rain. What is happening under there?

Water is reaching the terminations. Floor boxes and jacks fed through a slab sit at the lowest point of the room, and a floor box with a worn gasket, an unsealed cover or a conduit that drains toward it collects water from mopping, from tracked-in rain or from groundwater pushing up through the conduit. Once the contacts in the keystone have been wet a few times they corrode, and the link becomes intermittent before it fails outright. If the conduit holds water, the cable jacket can also wick it along the run.

We open the box, dry and inspect it, and test the run disconnected to see whether the problem stops at the termination or extends along the cable. Corroded keystones are cut back and replaced, the box gasket and cover are renewed, and where the conduit is wet we pull the run, dry or clear the conduit if possible, and replace the cable with one rated for a damp location. Where the box keeps flooding, the better answer is often to move the jack to a raised furniture feed or the nearest wall.

The building was reroofed and now the Cat6 runs to the rooftop cameras and units fail. Who fixes what?

Usually the roofer fixes the roof and we fix the cable, but the order matters. Reroofing a flat-roofed commercial building means tearing off membrane and insulation around every penetration, and Cat6 runs that went up to rooftop cameras, antennas or equipment are cut, pulled tight, pinched under new flashing or left with an open hole where the old penetration was. Links that still work may have been kinked or crushed enough to fail return loss.

We test each rooftop link from the closet to find which are cut, which are damaged and where. Where the run is cut at the penetration, we re-terminate it in a sealed outdoor enclosure or replace the exposed section with outdoor-rated cable through a proper weatherhead or sealed penetration, which the roofer then flashes. We give you a list of what the reroofing damaged and photographs of it, which is useful if the cost is going back to the roofing contractor.

When the house was raised or renovated

Our South Shore house was lifted after Sandy and the rooms fed through the new crawl space keep dropping. Why?

Because the runs feeding those rooms are probably no longer single cables. When a house is lifted, the wiring that went down into the old basement or slab has to be disconnected, and after the house is set on its new foundation the low-voltage cable is often reconnected with whatever reaches: inline couplers, crimped plugs joined by a barrel, or a box of punched-down splices sitting in the crawl space. Each joint adds crosstalk and return loss, and in a damp, unheated crawl space with flood vents open to the outside air, the contacts corrode. The result is links that drop out in wet weather and come back when it dries.

The fix is to make each link continuous again. We find the joints with the tester, which places them by distance, then replace each patched run with one length of cable from the room to the network equipment, routed above the flood line and through the conditioned part of the house where possible. Every repaired link is certified end to end. Where the house will be lived in during the work, we do it room by room so the family keeps a working connection somewhere.

The network equipment used to sit in the enclosure under our raised house. Where can it go, and how do the runs get rerouted?

It belongs above the design flood elevation, and usually inside the living space. FEMA's guidance for elevated buildings in flood zones limits the enclosure underneath to parking, building access and storage, and expects building utility equipment below the required elevation to be raised or protected. A router, switch, camera recorder and patch panel on a shelf in the enclosure will be underwater in the next surge, and even between storms it lives in salt air and condensation.

We pick a location upstairs — a closet, a utility room or a ventilated cabinet near where the internet service enters the living floor — and bring every run to it. Runs that came up from the enclosure are replaced with continuous cable through interior walls or a chase; where cable has to pass through the enclosure to reach a camera or a dock, it is rated for the location and has no terminations below the flood line. The internet provider may need to move its own equipment as well, and we tell you what to ask them for.

Farms, rentals and the island

Our winery's Cat6 runs through the same building as the wash-down area. How is it kept working when the floor is hosed?

By keeping the terminations, and as much of the cable as possible, out of the wet zone. Production buildings on the North Fork often combine a crush pad or barrel room, storage and a tasting or retail space under one roof, and the Cat6 for the registers, cameras and office was added later along whatever wall or beam was handy. Floor-level jacks, boxes on walls that get sprayed, and cable lying across the floor or down a drain channel are what fail, usually at the busiest time of year.

We reroute runs high, along beams or in raceway above the splash line, and move terminations into sealed enclosures or into the dry rooms the links serve. Where cable has to cross a wet area, it goes overhead or in sealed conduit, and anything exposed to spray is rated for it. We also protect runs from forklifts and bins at doorways. Then each link is certified, so you know it will hold through harvest.

Summer guests at our rental re-patched the network cabinet and now half the house is offline. How do you put it back and stop it happening again?

We put it back from the cabling up, then make it hard to undo. Guests who lose Wi-Fi in one room often unplug and replug things in the cabinet, swap cords between ports, plug a travel router into a jack or move the switch. The cabling itself is usually fine; what is lost is the map of which jack goes to which port, and sometimes a port or a cord that was damaged in the shuffle. Occasionally a guest pulls a jack out of the wall trying to reach the cable behind it.

We test each jack, re-establish which room lands on which panel port, replace damaged cords and jacks, and patch the cabinet so every room works. Then we label both ends, leave a printed map for you or your property manager, and, if you want, fit a locking door or a lockable panel cover so guests can reach the internet equipment's reset button but not the patching. Owners who rent all season often book a quick check-up between bookings at the start of summer.

Can a Cat6 repair be done in a Fire Island community where contractors need a driving permit or a ferry?

Yes, with more planning. The National Park Service issues off-road driving permits on Fire Island only to year-round residents and to contractors and businesses with work in a given community, and in winter access is by ferry, water taxi or private boat. Our failed buried Cat6 crosses woodland inside the Pine Barrens. Can it be dug up and replaced?

Possibly, but not by simply cutting a new trench through the trees. Does anyone have to mark utilities before a failed buried Cat6 run is dug up?

Yes. New York 811, which serves Nassau and Suffolk, asks for a call at least 48 hours and no more than 10 working days before digging, excluding weekends and holidays, so the utilities can mark their lines. On a Suffolk property that can include electric, gas, the internet provider's cable, and sometimes a water service, and on farms and estates the buried lines can run a long way from the house. New buildings

We bought a new apartment at a Suffolk development and some jacks have never worked. Is that a builder issue or a repair?

Often both, and a test settles which. Jacks that have never worked in new construction usually fail for a handful of ordinary reasons: a run that was never punched down at the panel, pairs that were split or reversed at the jack, a drywall screw or nail driven through the cable in the wall, a cable pulled so hard during installation that a conductor broke, or a jack connected to a panel in a different unit. We certify every jack in the unit, place each fault by distance, and give you a written result for each one. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Suffolk County

A word on sources: we were unable to view Google's AI Overview while researching this page, so we do not quote it. The seven claims below are ones that cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, store listings, DIY articles and chat-style answer tools tend to repeat about Ethernet wiring. After each claim comes the Suffolk reality.

“Cat6 lasts for decades, so if it worked once it still works”

Properly installed Cat6 in a stable building does last a very long time, and that is a fair general point.

The catch in Suffolk is the word “stable.” Cable outlives the building it was installed for. The flex building gets split into bays, the house gets lifted, the barn becomes a shop, and each change moves, lengthens, crushes or disconnects runs that were never touched by age.

So a link that worked once has to be retested after any change to the building around it. The question is not how old the cable is but what has happened to it since.

“A barrel connector is a fine way to lengthen a short run”

Inline couplers are sold everywhere and recommended in countless forum answers as a quick way to lengthen a run.

For a temporary patch cord on a desk, fine. Inside a wall, a crawl space or a ceiling, a coupler is a hidden joint that adds crosstalk and return loss, and Fluke Networks' troubleshooting guidance says female couplers should not be used in a data installation. They are exactly what we find in Suffolk houses that were raised after Sandy and in offices that were re-fitted.

The repair is to replace the joined pieces with one continuous run and certify it, rather than add another coupler.

“Running a new cable is about $125 to $250 a drop”

Fixr's national cost guide, updated January 2025, quotes roughly that range per drop for Cat6 installation, and many answers repeat it as the cost of a fix.

That figure describes new drops in an open, ordinary building. It does not describe repairing a link that crosses another tenant's ceiling, rerouting runs out of a flood enclosure, or getting a technician and parts to a Fire Island community by boat. Repair cost in Suffolk depends on what changed around the cable.

Our own Suffolk figures are published below: $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum for repair time, and replacement runs from $175 per drop when a run cannot be saved.

“A green link light proves the wiring is good”

A link light on a switch or laptop is often treated as proof that the wiring is good, and simple testers that light eight LEDs reinforce the idea.

A link light only shows that the two ends negotiated something. A link can light up while falling back to 100 megabits on a broken pair, or pass a continuity test while failing Category 6 crosstalk because of a coupler or a split pair. That is the difference between the landlord's “it works” and a link that meets its category.

Ask for certification results when you take over a space or pay for a repair. They show margin, not just a light.

“Any handyman can re-punch a jack”

Re-punching a keystone is presented as an easy DIY job, and the tools are cheap.

The physical act is easy. Doing it so the link still meets Category 6 is not: too much untwist at the contacts, pairs in the wrong order or a nicked conductor all leave a link that lights up and then fails a crosstalk test. In rental houses and re-tenanted bays we regularly find jacks that several people have re-punched.

Licensing in Suffolk is also not a single county answer. Chapter 563 licenses home improvement contractors and electricians, the towns and villages run their own permits, and which applies depends on the job and the address. Ask whoever does the work to certify it and leave you the result.

“Put the router wherever the internet comes into the house”

That is common advice, and in most houses it is sensible, because it keeps the provider's line short.

In a raised South Shore house the internet service may now come in at the enclosure below the living floor, which FEMA guidance limits to parking, access and storage. Equipment and terminations left there sit in the flood zone and in damp air.

The right place is above the flood line, and the Cat6 should run to it in continuous lengths. Ask the provider to bring its line up to that point rather than building your network around where they left it.

“Wi-Fi extenders make wired runs unnecessary in a big house”

Mesh systems and extenders are widely recommended as an alternative to wiring large houses, and they do help phones and laptops.

The East End houses we work in tend to be large, with pool houses, guest cottages and cameras at the gate, and the devices that matter most there — cameras, access points, recorders and the mesh nodes themselves — work best wired and often need power over the cable. A mesh node with a failed wired backhaul quietly drops to a slower wireless hop.

Repairing the runs to the far rooms and buildings is usually cheaper and steadier than layering more wireless hardware on top of a broken wired network.

South Shore Flood Line

Cat6 Below the Flood Line on Suffolk's South Shore

On the bay side of Babylon and Islip, water is not a once-in-a-generation event for low-voltage cabling. The National Weather Service's impact table for the Lindenhurst gauge records Sandy's peak at 7.7 feet on the evening of October 29, 2012, with much of the area south of Montauk Highway in Lindenhurst, Amityville and Babylon under two to five feet of water. The same table lists repeated moderate flooding at around four feet in 2016, 2017 and 2018. A house that was raised after Sandy is protected from most of that; the cable that still runs through the space underneath it is not.

Not every house damaged in 2012 was lifted. The Long Island Advocate reported in July 2026 that NY Rising either bought flood-zone houses for demolition or paid to raise them, and that Lindenhurst is now full of empty lots. The houses that remain on those blocks are, for the most part, the ones that went up — and with them, networks rebuilt around a new floor height.

The policy background explains what we find under those houses. FEMA's Technical Bulletin 5 limits the enclosure below an elevated building to parking, building access and storage, and FEMA's guidance on protecting building utilities expects components below the required elevation to be raised or built to resist flood loads. CBS New York reported in 2015 that Long Island towns and villages were requiring closed foundations with water vents rather than open pilings, and quoted Suffolk Legislator Kevin McCaffrey saying closed foundations protect “the mechanical structure of the house; the pipes from freezing.” Those vents are designed to let floodwater in and out. Any Cat6 termination left in that space sits in air that floods, drains and stays damp.

So on the South Shore we treat the flood line as a boundary in the cabling, not just in the house. Before repairing a link that passes beneath a raised floor, we check three things:

  • Where the joints are. A coupler, barrel or splice box below the floor is replaced with continuous cable, because a joint is where corrosion starts.
  • Where the equipment is. A router, switch, recorder or patch panel in the enclosure is moved to the living floor, and every run is brought up to it.
  • What has to stay below. Runs that must cross the enclosure to reach a dock camera, a gate or a garage use cable rated for wet locations, with no termination until the cable is back above the flood line or inside a sealed enclosure at the device.

The same thinking applies to ground-floor offices and shops on the bay-side streets that flood without being raised: floor boxes and low jacks are the first things to fail, and the lasting repair is often to move the termination up the wall rather than to replace it where it was. After a flood, we certify every link that was in the water, not only the ones that stopped, because wet terminations often keep working for weeks before they fail.

What to Gather Before a Suffolk Cat6 Repair Visit

Because Suffolk visits are planned to finish in one trip, a little information before we come saves a great deal on site. None of this involves opening anything.

  • Anything outdoors or underground. Runs to outbuildings, docks, gates, roofs or poles, and whether they are buried.

FAQ

Suffolk County Cat6 Repair FAQ

Will you send photos of what you found inside the wall or ceiling?
+

Yes. When we open a ceiling, a crawl space, a floor box or a wall, we photograph what caused the fault — a coupler, a crushed section, a corroded keystone, a screw through the cable — along with the repair. The photos go with the saved test results, which helps when the cost belongs to a landlord, a builder, a roofer or an insurer rather than to you.

Can one Cat6 run be split to feed two devices instead of pulling a second cable?
+

Not at gigabit speed. Gigabit Ethernet uses all four pairs in the cable, so splitting the pairs between two devices drops both to 100 megabits at best and leaves a non-standard link. The proper options are a small switch at the far end, which turns one run into several ports, or a second run. We tell you which is cheaper for the location before doing either.

Can you fix a Cat6 jack that was painted over or caulked during a repaint?
+

Yes. Paint or caulk that gets into a keystone jack or the plug opening can keep a cord from seating fully and coat the contacts, so the link drops or never comes up. We remove the plate, replace the keystone and plate rather than trying to clean paint off the contacts, check the cable end behind it, and certify the link. If several jacks in the same room were painted, we test each one before you put furniture back.

Will a Cat6 repair interrupt our alarm or camera monitoring?
+

It can, briefly, if the alarm communicator or camera recorder uses the network being repaired. Before we disconnect anything, we ask which systems report over the network, and we suggest you tell your monitoring company that the system may go offline for a short time. We work on those links last, or one at a time, and check that they report again before we leave.

Can you repair the Cat6 that connects an EV charger in a garage or carport to the network?
+

Yes, the data link to the charger. Chargers that report over Ethernet are often on long runs to a garage wall, a carport post or the end of a driveway, and those runs fail the same ways other outdoor and garage links do. We repair and certify the Cat6 up to the charger's network port; the charger itself stays with its installer or electrician, who should be the one to open it.

Does someone need to be at the property for the whole visit?
+

Someone needs to let us in, show us the closet or cabinet and approve the repair list once testing is done. After that, a caretaker, a property manager or an office contact is enough, and for rentals and second homes many owners approve the list by phone. We call before work that goes beyond what was agreed, and you receive the results and photos when we finish.

Can a Cat6 repair be done while a house elevation or renovation crew is still on site?
+

Often it is the best time, because walls and floors are open and the new routes are easy to reach. We coordinate with the general contractor so new runs go in before insulation and drywall close the walls, and so nobody drills through them afterwards. What we avoid is terminating jacks and certifying links before the dusty and heavy work is finished; those are done at the end, when they will stay undisturbed.

Can you repair Cat6 in a unit we rent out year-round while the tenant is living there?
+

Yes. We need you, as the owner, to arrange access with the tenant and agree a time, and we tell you how long the network will be down while we work. We keep the work to the affected jacks and the panel, clean up as we go, and send the results to you rather than the tenant unless you ask otherwise.
