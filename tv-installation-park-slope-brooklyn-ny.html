The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Properties Need TV Installation

Park Slope runs downhill from Prospect Park West to Fourth Avenue, and from Flatbush Avenue at the north end to the Prospect Expressway at the south. Most of it was built as row houses in the last decades of the nineteenth century, with the grandest of them, the mansions people still call the Gold Coast, going up near the park in the 1880s and 1890s. Wikipedia describes a neighborhood of row houses with six- to eight-story apartment buildings mixed in, and a historic district of about 2,575 buildings across roughly forty blocks that it calls the largest landmark neighborhood in the city. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the first district in 1973 and extended it in 2012 and again in 2016. For a TV installer, the important part of all that history is simple: the walls you will want to hang a screen on were mostly built well over a century ago, by plasterers and masons, not drywall crews.

Those walls behave in ways that surprise people who have only mounted TVs in newer apartments. Brownstoner's forum has been full of Park Slope and nearby owners describing them for twenty years. In a 2005 thread, one owner asked for an installer who understood "the delicate qualities of hundred year old plaster walls," and a commenter whose installer lived in Park Slope reported that hiding the wires meant breaking through the plaster and a serious vacuuming afterward, but that the result "looks fantastic." In a 2011 thread, an owner in a brownstone that had been gutted and turned into a co-op in 1986 found that his stud finder gave false readings on the street wall between two windows, and others warned that plaster can sit directly on brick and that knocking on it tells you little because "it is so hard." In another thread about mounting over a decorative fireplace, a long-time owner wrote, "In my house the fireplaces are on the party walls with no studs, just plaster over brick." Those three comments describe most of the TV walls we see in the neighborhood.

The rooms are just as particular. A Park Slope parlor floor is often two or three connected rooms with tall windows at the front and back, a marble mantel on the party wall, and very little plain wall left over. Brownstoner's 2021 feature on 257 6th Avenue, an Italianate two-family, describes a marble mantel and an "ornate pier mirror" in the front parlor and "Pocket doors open to the rear parlor," with built-in storage under the rear windows and along one wall. The 2019 feature on 427a 8th Street, a Neo-Grec brownstone from about 1884, counts "five handsome incised white marble mantels," with the rear parlor fireplace flanked by built-in bookcases and cupboards. And the 2015 feature on a four-story limestone at 600 2nd Street describes a triple parlor running about 65 feet front to back, with "a wall of original wood built-in cabinets" in the middle room and a giant pier mirror in the front. Every one of those features is beautiful and every one of them is a place a TV either cannot go or has to be planned around. Deciding where the screen lives is usually a bigger question in Park Slope than how it is held up.

Then there is the way the houses are divided. Wikipedia notes that many Park Slope brownstones were cut up into apartments and rooming houses in the twentieth century and that, from the 1960s and 1970s, buyers who got them cheaply converted many back into one- and two-family homes. Today a single house might hold an owner's triplex and a rental on the top floor, as 257 6th Avenue does, or a garden duplex with two apartments above it, as 600 2nd Street did. Each division left its own layer in the walls: a 1950s partition in plaster on metal lath, a 1980s gut renovation in drywall over the old brick, a 2010s kitchen wall on steel studs. Two rooms on the same floor can need two different anchors, and an apartment's TV wall may be one the owner downstairs considers his. That is why our first question on any Park Slope call is not the TV size but who lives where in the house, and which floor the screen is on.

The newer buildings are a different job entirely. The 2003 rezoning of Fourth Avenue opened the western edge to taller apartment buildings, and the last few years have added more: New York YIMBY reported in June 2026 that Paseo on Fifth, with 180 rental apartments on Fifth Avenue at Baltic Street and Gregory Place, had finished construction, and Brooklyn Paper reported in October 2026 that a five-story, 144-apartment building on the former Grand Prospect Hall site on Prospect Avenue was nearly complete. Buildings like these are more likely to put drywall on light steel studs behind your screen, which is a smaller add-on but needs entirely different fasteners, and they come with management offices that set hours and want insurance paperwork before a contractor arrives. Commercially, the Park Slope Fifth Avenue BID counts 521 ground-floor businesses along 62 block faces, and Seventh Avenue has its own long strip of shops and restaurants, many of them in old buildings with brick walls and apartments upstairs.

Five Park Slope facts that shape a TV job before anyone picks a bracket

  • Most buildings are landmarked. The historic district and its 2012 and 2016 extensions cover thousands of row houses and flats. Work inside a landmarked building usually needs no Landmarks permit, but anything that shows on the outside, like a new cable up the front or a dish on the roof, can.
  • Party walls are masonry. In row houses, the long side walls are shared with the neighbors and are typically brick under plaster, often with the fireplaces and flues built into them. We price that wall at the $65 brick or stone rate.
  • The mantel wall is the obvious wall. Many parlors have one wall with a mantel and nothing else large enough for a TV, which is why the $130 above-fireplace add-on comes up so often here.
  • Houses are shared. Two-family and multi-unit brownstones mean a neighbor, a tenant or an owner may sit on the other side of the wall or directly below the drill.
  • Families with young children are a large share of the neighborhood. The city's 2018 community health profile for Community District 6 counted 18 percent of residents under 18, which is why we ask about anchoring dressers and keeping cables out of reach as a matter of course.

Same-Day Repair in Park Slope

Need Repair in Park Slope? A Screen Tilting Off the Chimney Breast, Anchors Spinning in Plaster, or a Cord Pushed Through the Wall

The urgent calls from Park Slope houses come in three shapes. The first is a TV over a mantel that has begun to lean forward. Almost always, the bracket was screwed into the plaster face of a chimney breast with anchors meant for drywall, and the soft brown coat of plaster behind the hard finish has slowly crushed around them. The Brownstoner owner who said his long lead shields had held shelves for about forty years while short anchors failed was describing exactly this: on plaster over brick, the fixing has to reach the brick. The second is a full-motion arm on a party wall that has started to droop when pulled out. That usually means the top anchors went into mortar joints rather than brick, and every pull levers them a little further loose. The third is the leftover of a past installation: a bracket nobody trusts, a TV power cord fed into a hole above the screen and out of another near the floor, or a strip of tape holding a cable along a ceiling cornice.

If a screen is moving, take the weight off it. Two people can usually lift a TV off its bracket by raising it a little and pulling it forward; unplug it first and set it face-out against a sofa, not flat on the floor. Then call or text (347) 934-8335 with a photo of the bracket and the wall. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the screen is going back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, the job is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the take-down added only if the old TV has to come down separately. A cord inside a wall gets pulled out and replaced with an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) where the wall is hollow, or a paintable cord cover ($59) where it is brick. If what we find is a problem with the wall itself, such as a crack running across a chimney breast or loose brick behind the plaster, we say so plainly and stop, so the house can be repaired before anything heavy goes back on it.

The brownstone parlor floor

The prewar co-op near the park

The brownstone parlor floor: deciding which wall before deciding which bracket

The parlor floor is where most Park Slope owners want the main TV, and it is the room that leaves the fewest choices. The front parlor usually has two or three tall windows on the street wall, often with a pier mirror standing between them. The long side walls are party walls shared with the houses on either side, and one of them carries the fireplace and mantel. The back wall of the front parlor is often an opening, with pocket doors that slide into the wall on either side. When you subtract the windows, the mirror, the mantel and the door openings, there may be only one stretch of wall wide enough for a 55- or 65-inch screen, and it is often the chimney breast itself.

That party wall is the strongest wall in the house. Under the plaster it is usually solid brick, laid when the row was built, and a bracket anchored into that brick will hold a heavy screen indefinitely. The work is in getting through the plaster without cracking it and into the brick at the right depth, keeping clear of the mortar joints and of the flue that runs up inside the chimney breast. We drill one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, read the dust (white for plaster, red for brick, gray grit for a later block repair), and set the rest of the anchors to suit. Brick or stone is $65 on our list, and above-fireplace mounting adds $130 because of the height, the flue, the mantel clearance and the extra time. On a chimney breast there is no hollow cavity for an in-wall kit, so the cables usually come down the side of the breast in a paintable cord cover ($59) that disappears once it is painted the wall color, or down behind a piece of furniture beside the hearth.

The other walls in the parlor are worth checking before you settle on the mantel. In many houses the partition between the front and rear parlors, beside the pocket door pockets, is framed in wood with plaster on lath. It is hollow, which means an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) can carry the wiring out of sight, but the pocket door itself slides into that wall, and a bracket screw or a kit box in the wrong place will stop the door. We find the pocket with a scanner and a tape measure before we mark anything. If the door has not moved in decades, we still treat the pocket as live, because the next owner may want it working.

The limestone triple parlor: one long room, one screen, many seats

The limestone rows of the North and Center Slope often have longer parlor floors than the brownstones, with three rooms in a line. Brownstoner's 2015 feature on 600 2nd Street described one about 65 feet from front to back, with carved dark wood mantels, parquet floors and a wall of original wood built-in cabinets in the middle room. In a room that long, the first question is where people actually sit. A TV on the middle room's free wall can serve a sofa in the middle room and nobody else. A TV at the far end of the front room, on the street side, faces down the whole length of the floor but puts the windows behind the screen. We usually walk the room with the household, put painter's tape outlines on two or three candidate walls, and look at each one from every chair before deciding.

The divided brownstone: several renovations in one house

Many Park Slope houses are shared. Wikipedia records how brownstones were split into apartments and rooming houses in the twentieth century and then, from the 1960s and 1970s, often turned back into one- and two-family homes by new buyers. The result is a house in which the original party walls and chimney breasts are still nineteenth-century brick, but the partitions may be from any decade since. A 1950s wall can be plaster on expanded metal lath, which confuses a scanner because it reads as metal everywhere. A 1980s gut renovation like the one described in the 2011 Brownstoner thread can leave drywall screwed onto furring strips over the old brick, so the wall sounds hollow but has masonry an inch behind it. A recent kitchen or bathroom wall may be on light steel studs. We price each wall by what is actually behind its surface, which is why the pilot hole matters more than the paint.

The prewar co-op near the park: gypsum block, a board and a service elevator

The apartment buildings along Prospect Park West and its side streets, such as the 1929 Emery Roth building at 35 Prospect Park West that Wikipedia mentions, are a different kind of wall. Interior partitions in many prewar elevator buildings were built of gypsum block or hollow clay tile and then plastered, which is the "cement-like wall" a StreetEasy poster described when he said he could mount a TV but could not see how to hide the wires. One reply in that thread put it well: trenching those walls is messy, and "It's the price you pay for solid walls." Hollow block holds a TV well if the anchor is made for hollow masonry and placed so it bears on the solid web rather than breaking through the thin face into the void. A plain plastic plug simply spins. Because hollow block is not on our list as its own line, we confirm the wall from your photos and price it before you book.

Co-ops bring rules as well as walls. Many boards require contractors to carry insurance naming the building and its managing agent, limit work to weekday hours, and want the service elevator reserved. Hanging a TV is normally treated as minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall kit can count as an alteration in some buildings. Ask the managing agent what the building requires, send us whatever they send you, and we will send the insurance paperwork they want before the visit. If the building will not allow any opening, the wires go into a paintable cord cover, which needs only a few small screws.

The walk-up above a store: stairs, kitchens and the noise of the avenue

Along Fifth and Seventh Avenues, many buildings have a shop or restaurant on the ground floor and two or three floors of apartments above. The stairs are narrow and steep, with a tight turn at each landing, and a 65-inch TV in its carton is long and awkward on them. Keep the screen in its original box until we arrive, because a boxed TV can go up on its edge safely while an unboxed one wrapped in a blanket cannot. Inside, the apartments are usually railroad or floor-through layouts with brick side walls and a kitchen at the back that often shares a wall with the living room. A TV on the kitchen side may have a gas line, a range hood duct or a sink drain behind it, all of which we look for before drilling.

The new rental or condo: steel studs and a management office

The newer buildings along Fourth Avenue, and recent ones such as Paseo on Fifth and the building rising on the former Grand Prospect Hall site, are built differently. Apartment partitions are commonly drywall on light-gauge steel studs, and the walls along corridors and between apartments may be fire-rated assemblies that management does not want opened. A lag bolt that would bite into a wood stud just strips a thin steel one, so we use toggle fasteners rated for steel and spread the load across two studs, with a plywood backer behind the bracket where a full-motion arm needs something solid to pull against.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks on a Park Slope Parlor Floor Before the Drill Comes Out

These notes describe how our technicians work in Park Slope's older houses. They are our standing practice, not a story about any one address.

Walk the floor first. The technician walks the whole parlor floor before looking at the wall you chose: where the windows are, where the mantel sits, which walls are party walls, where the pocket doors slide, and whether the pier mirror is fixed to the wall or freestanding. Many TV positions get decided in that first walk, because the wall you pictured turns out to be the one with the door pocket in it.

Measure the mantel, not just the wall. On a chimney breast, the technician measures the mantel shelf's height and depth, the width of the breast, and the distance from the shelf to the ceiling cornice. A deep marble shelf pushes the bottom edge of the screen higher than people expect, and a cornice can stop a tilting bracket from tilting. Those three numbers decide the bracket.

Knock, scan, then drill once. A knock along the wall at screen height gives a rough idea, but on hard old plaster it misleads, as the Brownstoner commenters said. The scanner finds framing, metal lath and pipes. Then one small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it, settles the question. If the result changes the price from what you booked, you hear it right then, before the second hole.

Protect the floor and the stair. Blankets go down from the front door to the work area, over the stair runner and the parquet. A drop cloth goes under the wall, and the vacuum runs at the drill bit while we work, because old plaster and brick make fine dust that finds its way into everything.

Wall by Wall

Plaster on Brick, Plaster on Lath, Block or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Park Slope Wall

Our Brooklyn base price, $179.99, assumes drywall on wood studs, the wall of most newer American houses. Very little of a Park Slope row house is built that way, so the wall add-ons are the part of the price most worth understanding. Each exists because that wall takes different bits, different anchors and more time to get right, and each is shown to you before you book.

Plaster laid directly on brick (+$65, priced as brick). This is the classic party wall and chimney breast of a brownstone: a hard white finish coat over a thicker, softer brown coat, applied straight onto the brick with no air gap. It is the wall the Brownstoner owner meant when he wrote that his fireplaces sat on party walls with "no studs, just plaster over brick." There is nothing to find with a stud finder because there are no studs. The fixing has to pass through the plaster and seat fully in the brick, using a masonry anchor long enough that the plaster carries no load. Short anchors that only reach the plaster are the most common cause of the sagging over-mantel TVs we are called to fix. Because what holds the bracket is brick, we price this wall at the brick or stone rate.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). Interior partitions in row houses, between the front and rear rooms or along the stair hall, are often wood studs with thin wooden lath strips nailed across them and two or three coats of plaster on top. The plaster is strong when pressed and weak when pulled, so the fixing has to go through it and into the studs. Studs in a nineteenth-century house are rarely on modern spacing, and the 2011 Brownstoner poster found his by drilling a very fine bit until it hit wood, which is close to what we do. If the bracket cannot reach two studs, we spread the load with a wood strip or plywood backer fixed to the studs that are there. This wall is hollow between the studs, so it is the one wall in an older house where an in-wall power and cable kit can usually go.

Plaster on metal lath (priced by what is behind it). Mid-twentieth-century partitions and repairs often used expanded steel mesh instead of wood lath. Scanners read metal everywhere and become useless, and a knock sounds like any other plaster. The pilot hole tells us whether there are studs, block or brick behind the mesh, and the wall is priced by that material: $45 when it is framed, $65 when it is brick.

Solid brick with no plaster (+$65). Exposed brick walls are common in garden floors that were opened up in later renovations, as in the garden level at 257 6th Avenue that Brownstoner described with "a wall of exposed brick, recessed lighting." Brick holds a TV very well as long as every anchor goes into the brick itself and not into the mortar, which in old walls can be soft enough to scrape with a key. We keep holes away from the edges of each brick and, on softer old brick, use more anchors at lower load each.

Drywall on furring over brick, or on steel studs. Gut renovations from the 1980s on often fixed drywall to wood furring strips over the old brick. It sounds hollow but has masonry an inch behind it, and the best fixing goes through the drywall into the brick, priced at the brick rate. New buildings often use drywall on light steel studs, which need toggles rated for steel; that wall is quoted from your photos.

Hiding the wires in a house where most walls are solid

"Make the wires disappear" is the request after "mount it," and in Park Slope it has two parts. The first is a rule: the TV's own power cord may not be pushed inside a wall. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings, and a cord fed through two holes can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and never be inspected again. A StreetEasy poster summed up the common advice correctly: it is fine to bury speaker wire and HDMI, but not power cords. The right way to put power behind a TV is an in-wall power and cable kit, which plugs into the existing outlet low on the wall, carries power inside the wall in its own rated cable, and gives you a recessed outlet behind the screen with a separate pass-through for HDMI. On our list it is $169 including the kit.

The second part is the wall itself. An in-wall kit needs a hollow cavity between the low opening and the high one, which a lath-and-plaster partition or a drywall wall has and a brick party wall or a chimney breast does not. Cutting a channel into brick and replastering over the cables can be done, but it is plastering work, not TV work, it is messy, and in a shared house it can mean the neighbors hear chisels for an afternoon. For masonry walls, the clean answers are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs from the TV down to the outlet in a slim channel that disappears once it is painted the wall color, or a console directly under the screen so the cables drop straight down behind it. On an over-mantel TV, the cover usually runs down the side of the chimney breast, in the corner where it meets the wall, where it is hardest to see.

Landmarks, Boards and Leases

Who Gets a Say Before a TV Goes on a Park Slope Wall

A TV bracket is about as small as wall work gets, but in Park Slope three different sets of rules can touch it: the Landmarks Preservation Commission for anything that shows outside, a co-op board or condo office for apartments, and a lease for renters. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the public guidance says and what we see on jobs, so you can ask the right person the right question before the visit.

Landmarks: inside is usually yours, outside is not. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says a permit for interior work is needed when the work requires a Department of Buildings permit, when work on the interior affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. Mounting a TV on an inside wall of a row house normally does none of those things. What can affect the exterior is the wiring around it: a new cable line stapled up the front of the house to reach the parlor, a dish or antenna on the roof or the facade, or a new opening in a front wall. Those are the things to ask Landmarks about before anyone does them, and LPC's own guidance says its preservation staff can tell you whether a permit is needed. We keep everything we do inside the house, and if the plan needs anything outside, we tell you before we book so you can ask.

Dishes and antennas: a federal rule with limits. The Federal Communications Commission's rule on over-the-air reception devices protects a homeowner's or renter's right to install a satellite dish one meter or less across, or a TV antenna, in an area they have exclusive use of, such as a terrace, balcony or patio. The FCC guide is explicit that it does not cover common property such as "the roof or exterior walls of a multiple dwelling unit," and that restrictions are allowed where a historic site is involved. In a landmarked Park Slope house or a co-op, that means a dish on the shared roof or the front facade is not automatically yours to install. Most households here use streaming services or cable instead; if you do want an indoor antenna for local channels, it can usually sit behind or beside the TV with no outside work at all.

Co-ops and condos: minor work, with paperwork. Prospect Park West co-ops and the newer condo buildings usually have house rules for contractors: insurance naming the building and managing agent, weekday hours, a reserved service elevator, and sometimes an alteration agreement for anything that opens a wall. A TV on a bracket is normally treated as minor work, while an in-wall kit may be treated as an alteration in a strict building. Ask the managing agent which applies, and send us the building's requirements so the paperwork reaches them before we do.

Renters: read the alteration clause. If you rent a floor of a brownstone, the lease decides whether you may drill. Look for the clause on alterations or wall fixtures, ask the owner in writing if it restricts them, and keep the reply. A bracket leaves four to six holes behind the screen and a cord cover leaves a few small screw holes, all easy to fill and paint when you move. If the lease is strict or you expect to move soon, a TV set up on its own stand with the cables tidied ($119.99) and a soundbar placed in front of it ($79) gives you most of the result with nothing to patch.

Compliance notes for Park Slope businesses

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, wall-mounted objects whose leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may not project more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm pulled out in a narrow dining room can break that rule, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls or ceilings. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings. Power behind a commercial screen goes through an in-wall power kit or a surface cover, and anything beyond that is a licensed electrician's job.
  • Exits, sprinklers and alarms stay clear. We do not place screens where they block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a smoke detector or a door swing, and no cable crosses the floor of an exit route.
  • Insurance before work. Commercial landlords and managed buildings usually want a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent. We send ours ahead of the visit.
  • Outdoor screens are a different product. A patio TV in a bar's back yard faces rain, sun and cold. An ordinary indoor TV is not built for that, and we will say so before mounting one outside.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Park Slope: Questions Answered

These are the situations Park Slope owners, renters and business owners describe when they call: rooms full of original features, walls that turn out to be something other than they look, old wiring from past tenants, buildings with boards and rooftops with rules, and bars that need every seat to see the game. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Mirrors, mantels and connected parlors

Our front parlor has a tall pier mirror between the two street windows. Can the TV go on that wall, or should the mirror stay where it is?

Usually the mirror should stay, and the TV should go somewhere else. A pier mirror is often fixed to the wall, sometimes with a marble shelf or base below it, and taking one down risks the glass, the gilded frame and the plaster behind it. Even if it is freestanding, the strip of wall between two front windows is a poor TV wall: the screen sits between two bright openings, so in the daytime you are looking at a dark rectangle framed by glare, and the wall is often the house's front masonry with radiators below the windows. The better spots are usually the party wall across from the mantel, if there is one, or the chimney breast itself. If the pier wall really is the only option, we would still leave the mirror up, measure the space beside it, and show you with painter's tape what a screen there would look like before anything is drilled. We do not take down or rehang original mirrors.

With the pocket doors open, our double parlor is one long room. Is there a TV position that works from both halves?

There often is, but it is rarely the obvious wall. A screen on the chimney breast in the front parlor faces across the room, not down it, so people in the rear parlor see it from a sharp angle. A screen on the short wall at the far end of the front parlor, against the street, faces down the whole floor but has the windows behind it. The arrangement that works best in many double parlors is a TV on the party wall near the opening between the rooms, on a full-motion arm, so it can face the front sofa most evenings and turn toward the rear room when guests are spread out. On a brick party wall that arm needs anchors well into the brick, and it needs clearance so the pocket doors can still close when the arm is folded flat. The alternative is two screens, one in each room, which costs two mounts but avoids compromising either. We tape both options and sit in both rooms with you before choosing.

A Brownstoner commenter framed his TV as an extension of the mantel so it looked like a mirror when it was off. Can you build something like that?

We can mount the TV so a frame like that is possible, but the frame itself is carpentry. The 2005 Brownstoner thread describes a Bed-Stuy owner who had a wood frame built that extended the original oak mantel, so the switched-off screen read as an over-mantel mirror. In a Park Slope parlor that means three trades working in order: a carpenter or millworker designs and builds the frame to match the mantel, we fix the TV on a low-profile bracket into the brick of the chimney breast so the screen sits at the depth the frame needs, and the painter finishes the wood. The frame has to leave a gap around the TV for ventilation and access to its buttons and ports, and the cables still need a legal path down, usually inside the frame's side to the outlet. If you go this route, have the carpenter talk to us before building, so the bracket depth and the frame depth agree.

What is really behind the wall

Our garden floor has a long wall of exposed brick that was uncovered in a renovation, and the mortar crumbles a little when touched. Is it still safe for a 65-inch TV?

Usually yes, because the anchors go into the brick, not the mortar. Old lime mortar is softer than modern cement mortar by design, and on an exposed wall it often dusts at the surface. The bricks themselves are generally sound. We place every anchor in the middle of a brick, well away from its edges and the joints, use sleeve or screw anchors sized for the bracket, and on softer old brick use more anchors at lower load each. Before drilling we check two things: that the bricks around the bracket are not loose (a tap test and a careful look at the joints), and that the joints behind the bracket are not so far gone that the wall needs repointing first. If they are, we will tell you, and a mason should repoint before anything heavy goes on it. A 65-inch TV on that wall is priced at the brick or stone rate, $65, on top of the base, and the screen size itself is priced from the model number before you book.

In our Prospect Park West co-op, the drill skated off the wall when we tried to hang a picture, and the bit barely went in. What is that wall, and will it take a TV?

It is probably plaster on gypsum block or hollow clay tile, and yes, it will take a TV with the right anchor. Prewar elevator buildings often used block or tile for partitions, finished with hard plaster, which is why an ordinary bit skids and then barely bites. The StreetEasy thread on prewar walls described the same thing as "cement-like." The trick is a sharp masonry bit, a slow start without hammer action until the bit is through the plaster face, and an anchor designed for hollow masonry, placed so it grips the solid web of the block rather than breaking through into a void. On a wall like that we also avoid full-motion arms unless the layout really needs one, because an arm pulled out levers the top anchors. Because that wall type is quoted from photos rather than listed separately, send us a picture and tell us about the drill skating, and we will price it before you book.

Old wires, rooftops and the rules of the building

A previous tenant ran a cable along the top of our parlor wall, stapled just under the plaster cornice. Can it come down without damaging the molding?

Yes, if it is removed slowly. Old coax and phone cable stapled along a cornice is common in houses that were rented for decades, and the staples are often driven into the plaster just below the molding. Pulling the cable off in one go can tear out small chips of plaster. We cut the cable into short lengths, lift each staple separately, and fill the small holes so they can be painted. Before removing it, we find out where it goes, because sometimes a cable that looks abandoned still feeds a jack in another room. Tracing it with a tone generator at one end and a probe along its length tells us which one it is in a few minutes. If it is live and you still need that line, we can reroute it to a cleaner path when we mount the TV. If it is dead, it comes down and stays down.

Our top-floor apartment has a private roof deck. Does the federal rule on dishes let us put a small satellite dish up there?

Possibly, and the details matter. The FCC rule protects the right of owners and renters to install a dish one meter or less across, or a TV antenna, in an area they have exclusive use of, and the FCC guide names terraces, balconies and patios as examples. A roof deck that only your apartment can reach may count. The same guide says the rule does not cover common property such as the roof or exterior walls of a multiple dwelling, and that restrictions are allowed where a historic site is involved. In a landmarked Park Slope building, a dish visible from the street can also raise a Landmarks question, because work that affects the exterior can need a permit. So check three things before buying a dish: whether the lease or the building treats the deck as yours alone, whether the dish would be visible from the street, and what Landmarks staff say about it. Our part starts inside, connecting the receiver to the TV.

Our co-op's alteration agreement covers "any work that opens a wall." Does an in-wall power and cable kit count?

By that wording, very likely yes, so ask the managing agent before booking it. An in-wall kit means two openings in the wall, one behind the TV and one near the outlet, each a few inches across, with the kit's own rated power cable and a pass-through for HDMI running between them. Nothing is connected to the building's electrical wiring; the kit plugs into your existing outlet. Some boards treat that as minor work with a simple notice, some want the full alteration paperwork, and some do not allow it at all on walls shared with a corridor or another apartment. If the agent says it needs an application, we can describe the kit in writing for the board and send our insurance certificate. If the building says no, a paintable cord cover ($59) on the surface gives a clean finish with only small screw holes, and the in-wall kit ($169) can wait for a renovation.

Bars, patios and storefronts

Our pub wants a screen on the brick wall of the back yard for summer evenings. Will an ordinary TV survive out there?

An ordinary indoor TV is not built for it, and we would advise against leaving one outside. A Park Slope back yard gets rain blowing in, direct afternoon sun, summer humidity and winter cold, and indoor TVs are made for none of those. Screens built for outdoor use have sealed housings and brighter panels for daylight, and they cost more for that reason. The wall itself is usually fine: a brick yard wall or the back wall of the building takes masonry anchors like any party wall, and we keep the screen under an overhang where there is one. Power outside needs a weather-protected outlet installed by an electrician, which is not part of our work. If the patio is only used in the evenings and the screen can come in, a removable arrangement is another choice. Tell us what the yard looks like and how the screen will be used, and we will tell you what makes sense before you buy anything.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Park Slope

These are claims that show up in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Park Slope's houses and buildings actually show.

"Use a stud finder, find two studs, and screw the bracket in"

This is the first line of nearly every how-to answer, and it assumes a wall of drywall on wood studs spaced sixteen inches apart.

In a Park Slope row house, the walls most people want for a TV are party walls and chimney breasts of brick under plaster, which have no studs at all. A long-time brownstone owner on Brownstoner put it plainly: his fireplaces sat on party walls with "no studs, just plaster over brick." The partitions that do have studs are often lath and plaster, where scanners read poorly and studs sit wherever the original carpenter put them, and a mid-century repair in metal lath makes a scanner beep everywhere. A stud finder that works perfectly in a new apartment can be useless in a brownstone.

We confirm the wall with one small pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate and pick the anchor for what is really there. On brick, that is a masonry anchor long enough to seat in the brick, not the plaster.

"TV mounting costs about the national average"

Cost guides publish averages and ranges drawn from many jobs. Angi's Brooklyn page, for example, gives an average of $280, a typical range of $172 to $376, and a low-to-high spread of $100 to $670.

Those numbers are real, but they cannot tell you what your wall needs. A range that wide covers everything from a small TV on drywall to a big one over a stone fireplace, and it does not say which add-ons were in which job. In Park Slope, the difference between the low and high end is almost always the wall, the mantel and the cable plan, not the size of the screen alone.

"Feed the power cord through the wall to hide it"

Many answers suggest drilling a hole behind the TV and another near the floor and pushing the TV's power cord through the wall between them.

The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls. A cord inside a wall can be pinched by a fixing, can overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected. Hidden power needs an in-wall kit with its own rated cable and a recessed outlet behind the screen. And on a brownstone's brick party wall or chimney breast there is no hollow space to feed anything through at all.

On masonry we use a paintable cord cover or a console under the screen; on hollow lath-and-plaster or drywall walls we use an in-wall kit. Low-voltage cables such as HDMI may go inside a wall; power cords may not.

"A historic district means you need permission to hang a TV"

Some answers suggest that living in a landmarked house means any work, inside or out, needs city approval.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own guidance says an interior permit is needed when the work requires a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark. A bracket on an inside wall of a Park Slope row house normally does none of those. What can need approval is the work around it that shows outside: a new cable run up the facade, a dish on the roof, a new opening in a front wall.

We keep TV work inside the house. If your plan needs anything outside, we tell you before booking, so you can check with Landmarks staff first.

"Mount the TV over the fireplace, it's the natural focal point"

Design sites often present the mantel as the default place for a TV.

In many Park Slope parlors the mantel wall really is the best wall, but not always. A working fireplace sends heat up the chimney breast toward the screen, and the Brownstoner thread on mounting over a fireplace warned about exactly that, along with the neck strain of a screen set high over a mantel. Deep marble shelves push the screen higher still, and a ceiling cornice can stop a tilting bracket from tilting enough.

We measure the shelf, the breast and the cornice, mark the screen with tape and have you sit in your usual seat before deciding. If the mantel is not the right wall, we say so, even though it is the wall with the add-on.

"Renters can't mount a TV" or "it never matters"

Some answers say a renter may never drill; others say landlords never notice.

Neither is a rule. What decides it is the lease, especially any clause about alterations or wall fixtures, and in a Park Slope two-family the owner may live one floor away and care a great deal about a party wall. A bracket leaves a few holes that are easy to fill when you move; an in-wall kit leaves two openings that need patching.

Read the lease, ask the owner in writing if it restricts fixtures, and keep the answer. If the lease is strict, a TV on its own stand with a soundbar in front of it, $119.99 plus $79, needs no holes at all.

"One bracket height works for every room"

Generic advice gives a single height for the center of the screen.

Park Slope rooms range from a garden-floor den with an eight-foot ceiling to a parlor with a much taller one, and from a sofa six feet from the wall to a triple parlor where people sit forty feet away. A single number cannot be right for both. Over a mantel, the shelf decides the minimum height, not the eye line.

We mark the screen with painter's tape, you sit where you watch, and we move the tape until it is right. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket on old plaster costs a patch.

Park Slope TV Installation FAQ

Does the above-fireplace charge apply to a chimney breast in an upstairs bedroom that has no mantel shelf?
+

It depends on the height. The $130 add-on covers the extra work of mounting high over a hearth, around a flue and a mantel. A TV at normal height on a bedroom chimney breast with no fireplace opening below is priced as a brick wall, the $65 add-on, without the fireplace charge.

Can a TV go on the wall of a stair hall or a landing in a brownstone?
+

Sometimes, but stair halls are narrow, and a screen there sticks out into the path people use to go up and down, often in the dark. We only mount on a stair wall if the screen sits flat on a low-profile bracket, well clear of the handrail and the line of travel.

Will the technician move our pier mirror or a heavy bookcase to reach the wall?
+

No. We do not move original mirrors, antique furniture or loaded bookcases, because the risk to them is not worth it. Please clear about six feet in front of the TV wall before the visit and take fragile pieces off nearby shelves.

Could the cable box and router sit inside the firebox of a fireplace we never use?
+

Only if the fireplace is sealed and will never be lit, and even then the box needs airflow and a clear line for the remote. A firebox can be damp and sooty, so we usually suggest a shelf or console beside the hearth instead, with the cables in a cover.

Can you set up the TV in a child's room so it only opens the apps we approve?
+

Yes, as part of setup we can turn on the TV's and the streaming device's parental controls, hide the apps you do not want, and set a PIN that you choose. A setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour if the TV is already mounted.

Our sofa sits about ten feet from the parlor wall. How large a screen makes sense there?
+

Most people are comfortable with a 55- to 65-inch screen at that distance, but the wall decides too. Measure the width between the mantel edges or window frames and send it with your photos, and we will tell you which sizes fit before you buy.

Can you put a small TV on a bathroom wall in a renovated brownstone?
+

Possibly, but bathrooms are humid, ordinary TVs are not built for steam, and the wall is usually tile. Tile is not a standard line on our list, so we price it from photos, and we will tell you honestly if the room is too wet for the screen you have.

Our over-mantel TV was hung too high and gives us a stiff neck. Can it be lowered on the same wall?
+

Often, if the mantel shelf leaves room. We take the TV down, move the bracket lower into sound brick, and fill the old holes. Where the shelf stops it going lower, a tilting bracket angled toward the sofa helps. We price it as a new mount on that wall.

The front wall of our garden floor sits partly below the sidewalk and feels cool and slightly damp. Is it a good TV wall?
+

Usually not. Below-grade masonry can carry moisture, which is bad for electronics and for anchors over time. An interior partition or a party wall above grade is a better choice, and we will check both before you decide.

Can a TV hang from the ceiling of a high-ceilinged parlor instead of a wall?
+

It is possible but rarely wise in a period parlor. A ceiling mount has to reach the joists through old plaster, often near a cornice or medallion, and it leaves the screen floating in the room. We price ceiling mounts from photos and usually suggest a wall first.

Our plasterer just patched the parlor wall. How long before a TV can be mounted on it?
+

Ask the plasterer, because drying time depends on the material and the thickness. A skim coat may be ready in days; a deep patch can take longer. We would rather wait than drill into soft plaster that has not cured.

Could drilling our party wall knock pictures off the neighbors' side?
+

It is unlikely with short, sharp drilling, but a hammer drill does send vibration through brick. A quick note to the neighbors saying when the drilling will happen lets them lift anything fragile off that wall for ten minutes, and we keep the drilling to one short session.
