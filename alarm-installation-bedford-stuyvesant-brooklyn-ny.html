The Neighborhood

Why Bedford Stuyvesant Properties Need Alarm Installation

Bedford-Stuyvesant is a neighborhood of tall, attached houses. Wikipedia counts roughly 8,800 buildings here put up before 1900 and calls them the largest collection of intact Victorian architecture in the United States. For an alarm, the age of those houses matters less than their height. A typical brownstone or brick rowhouse here has a garden floor half below the sidewalk, a parlor floor at the top of the stoop, two more floors above, and a roof reached by a ladder and a hatch. Every one of those levels has its own way in, and the highest ones are the ones owners forget.

The record of break-ins here shows how often the top and the back of the house are involved. In 2014 the 79th Precinct, which covers the western half of the neighborhood, told residents through BK Reader that doors, windows, cellar doors and roof hatches were the most common points of entry and asked owners to consider an alarm system. In 2015 the same precinct told DNAinfo that burglars were pushing window air conditioners into apartments, sometimes after coming over from the building next door and down the fire escape. In 2019 two owners writing the Brownstone Boys column for Brownstoner described hearing footsteps in the empty upper duplex of their Bed-Stuy brownstone at night; they never learned how the intruder got in, and the roof hatch was one of three possibilities they listed.

Renovation is the other thing that sets Bed-Stuy apart. A large share of the housing stock has been bought, gutted and rebuilt over the past twenty years, and empty houses full of new appliances and tools are an easy target. In 2016 the 79th Precinct reported a run of construction-site burglaries in which refrigerators, stoves, washers and dryers were carried out, often at night or on weekends when work had stopped and soon after deliveries. The same year, the 81st Precinct on the eastern side said it was tracking about 95 vacant properties and that copper pipe was being stripped from houses under renovation. An alarm that goes in while the walls are still open protects the house during its most exposed months and is far easier to wire.

The commercial strips have their own pattern. Between September and November 2025, FOX 5 New York reported, one man broke into four Brooklyn businesses through the roof and tried a fifth, three of them on Fulton Street, taking about $48,000 in cash, merchandise, gold and jewelry. News 12 Brooklyn showed video of a man climbing a wall and going through the ceiling of Fulton Pawnbroker on Fulton Street in Bed-Stuy just after midnight on October 14, 2025, with about $20,000 in merchandise taken. A store alarm that only watches the front gate and the glass misses that route entirely.

None of this means Bedford-Stuyvesant is unusually dangerous, and the current numbers say so. Burglary in both precincts is far below its levels of the early 1990s. What it does mean is that a good alarm plan here starts at the roof and works down, takes empty floors seriously, and treats a store's ceiling as a door.

Six facts about Bed-Stuy that shape an alarm

  • Two precincts, one neighborhood. The 79th Precinct covers roughly Classon Avenue to Marcus Garvey Boulevard between Flushing and Atlantic avenues, according to DNAinfo, and the 81st covers the eastern side out to Broadway. Monitoring dispatch goes through 911 either way, but the precinct numbers below are reported separately.
  • Burglary in the 79th is down this year. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the week of September 14–20, 2026 shows 102 burglaries year to date against 114 a year earlier (-10.5%), 172 in all of 2025 and 1,706 in 1990.
  • The 81st is lower than last year but above two years ago. Its report for November 2–8, 2026 shows 94 burglaries year to date against 100 (-6.0%), but a two-year change of +32.4%, against 981 in 1990.
  • The roof is a real route. FOX 5 counted four business burglaries through the roof in about two months of 2025, three of them on Fulton Street.
  • Renovations attract thieves. DNAinfo reported 12 burglaries in a single week of October 2016 in the 79th Precinct during a run of thefts of appliances and tools from houses under construction.
  • Most houses are protected landmarks or close to one. The Stuyvesant Heights, Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights and Bedford historic districts cover many hundreds of buildings, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission limits what can be fixed to their fronts.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Bedford Stuyvesant’s Architecture

Bed-Stuy was built mostly in one long burst. Masonry row houses began going up in the 1870s, the first row on MacDonough Street in 1872, and brownstones for upper-middle-class buyers followed from the 1890s to the late 1910s. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s report on the expanded Stuyvesant Heights district describes the features that still define the blocks: row houses with a shared first-floor entrance through a vestibule into a hall, round-headed double-leaf doors, areaways and raised basements, and four- and five-story French flats from the 1890s. Each of those shapes an alarm in a particular way, and so do the public housing towers and the newer buildings that came later.

The four-story brownstone

Garden floor below the stoop, parlor floor above it, two bedroom floors and a roof hatch. The front is the least likely way in; the rear yard, the cellar, the reachable back windows and the roof are where the plan starts.

The brownstone with a rental floor

Usually an owner’s duplex over a garden apartment, or the reverse. Two households, one vestibule and one roof, so the panel needs areas and the shared openings need an owner.

The house in the middle of a gut renovation

Open walls, no locks on half the doors, contractors in and out and new appliances in boxes. The easiest house to wire and the most exposed one on the block.

The French flat

Four or five stories of apartments over a shared hall, built in the 1890s, with a fire escape on the back or front. Most alarms here go inside a single apartment.

Public housing and newer buildings

Marcy Houses’ 27 six-story buildings and 1,705 apartments, Sumner Houses’ 13 buildings, and the newer condos and rentals near the avenues: an apartment-only alarm, light to install and removable.

The storefront with apartments above

Two- and three-story mixed buildings on Fulton, Nostrand, Tompkins and the other avenues, often with a cellar under the store and a flat roof shared along the row.

The four-story brownstone: plan it from the roof down

Most alarm plans start at the front door, because that is where people stand when they think about an alarm. On a Bed-Stuy brownstone the front door is the most visible opening on the house, set at the top of a stoop in full view of the street, and it is usually behind a second door in the vestibule. That makes it the least attractive way in. The roof, by contrast, is reached from the roofs of every other house in the row, which often run level from corner to corner, and nobody on the street can see someone standing there. A hatch with a worn latch, or a skylight over the top-floor stair, is an opening into the bedrooms with no witnesses at all.

So our walkthrough on a brownstone starts upstairs. The technician climbs to the hatch or bulkhead, checks how it closes and whether it is the building’s only way out to the roof in a fire, and looks at any skylight from inside and, where it is safe, from above. Then he works down floor by floor: the rear windows, especially any that sit over an extension roof or a fire escape; the front windows that can be reached from the stoop; the parlor door and the vestibule; the garden door under the stoop; the rear door to the yard; and finally the cellar, its door, its coal chute or old delivery hatch if one survives, and the stairs up into the house.

The list that comes out of that walk is usually longer at the top and the back than at the front. A typical one-family brownstone ends up with contacts on the roof hatch and every exterior door, contacts or glass-break detection on the reachable windows, one or two motion detectors on the stairs where every route through the house meets, and a keypad at the door the family actually uses, which on many blocks is the garden door rather than the parlor door. Upper front windows that nobody can reach without a ladder in plain view of the street usually stay off the list.

The brownstone with a rental floor: who owns the stairs and the roof

A great many Bed-Stuy brownstones are now two homes, most often an owner’s duplex over a garden rental or an owner on the garden and parlor floors with the top floors rented. The alarm question is less technical than it is social. The owner wants the whole house protected; the tenant wants privacy and the freedom to come and go; and the vestibule, the stairs, the cellar and the roof hatch belong to both and neither. The answer is a panel divided into areas. The owner’s floors arm on the owner’s codes, the rental arms on the tenant’s, and the shared openings sit in the owner’s area with the sensors placed so a tenant walking to the front door never trips anything.

The Brownstone Boys’ experience shows why the empty floor matters. They were living in the finished garden rental while the upper duplex was being renovated, so the floors above them were empty, and someone walked through those floors at night. A motion detector on the upper stairs, armed while the floors are empty and while the household below is home, would have reported the first step. That is a routine setting on the panels we install: the downstairs household can be at home and disarmed while the upstairs area stays armed.

The house in the middle of a gut renovation: wire it while you can

An owner who is gutting a brownstone has a once-in-a-generation chance to run alarm cable where it will never be seen, and a few months during which the house is most at risk. We usually split the job in two. While the walls are open we run cable to the doors, the roof hatch and the stair landings, and set boxes where the keypads and detectors will finally go. At the same time we put a temporary wireless layer on the openings that matter during the work: the roof, the rear door, the cellar and the room where appliances and tools are stored. When the walls close, the wired devices take over, the temporary pieces move to windows or are removed, and the panel stays.

Contractors complicate codes but do not prevent an alarm. Each trade gets its own user code, the codes can be limited to working hours, and the app shows the owner who disarmed the house and when. The DNAinfo reports from 2016 described thieves who seemed to know when appliances were about to be delivered, which is a reason to keep delivery dates off the street and to have the alarm armed the night they arrive.

The French flat: your door, your fire-escape windows, your app

The four- and five-story apartment buildings of the 1890s, the French flats in the Landmarks Commission’s description, are the most common place for a tenant to ask us for an alarm. The street door and the hall are the landlord’s responsibility, so a tenant’s system starts at the apartment door. A contact on that door, contacts on the windows that open onto the fire escape, and a motion detector in the room those windows open into cover the routes the 79th Precinct described in 2015, when burglars came over from the next building and down the fire escape or pushed in window air conditioners.

The equipment is wireless and battery powered and fixed lightly to the inside of the apartment, so it comes off without damage when the lease ends. Window contacts on a fire-escape window never lock the window or slow anyone climbing out; they only report that it opened.

Marcy, Sumner and the newer buildings: inside the apartment only

Marcy Houses, opened in 1949, has 27 six-story buildings and 1,705 apartments; Sumner Houses has 13 buildings. Residents there, and tenants in the newer rental and condo buildings near the avenues, can have an apartment alarm on the same principle as a French flat: wireless devices inside the unit, nothing on the building’s doors, halls or roof. Where a lease or a management office requires written consent before anything is fixed inside, we provide a short description of each device and how it is removed.

Up the Stairs First

Why a Bed-Stuy Alarm Plan Starts at the Roof Hatch

The precinct’s 2014 advice listed four openings, and two of them are ones a suburban alarm salesman rarely mentions: cellar doors and roof hatches. In a row of attached houses the roofs form a continuous walkway, broken only by low parapets, and a person can reach your hatch from any house on the block, including one that is vacant or under renovation. A Brownstoner forum thread on roof hatch security drew replies from owners of brownstones in Bed-Stuy and Bushwick, and the Brownstone Boys listed the roof hatch as one of three ways their intruder might have come in.

The hatch is also a fire exit, which limits what can be done to it. Forum posters asking how to secure a hatch worried that a padlock would trap someone inside, and that is the right worry. An alarm contact solves the detection problem without touching the egress problem: it reports that the hatch has opened, and it never stops anyone opening it. The latch and the hatch itself are a roofer’s job, and we will tell you if what we find is too worn for any sensor to be meaningful.

Skylights need a different device. A contact on a skylight that does not open is pointless, and a burglar who breaks the glass may drop straight onto the stairs. The choice is between a glass-break detector in the stairwell, which hears the glass, and a motion detector on the top landing, which sees the person. In a narrow brownstone stair the motion detector usually wins, because one well-placed detector also covers the hatch, the top-floor hall and the bedroom doors.

The cellar is the other end of the same thought. Many cellars here have a separate door to the areaway under the stoop or to the yard, and in buildings with several apartments the cellar is shared. CBS New York reported in 2019 that a burglar entered the basement of a multi-unit building near Kosciuszko Street and Marcus Garvey Boulevard through a cellar door at about 10 p.m., came back at about 1 a.m. and forced his way into an apartment next to the basement while two residents slept. A contact on the cellar door, and a second on the door from the cellar into the house or the apartment, turns that route into the first thing the alarm reports.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant

Bed-Stuy’s shops are spread along a handful of long streets rather than gathered in one district. Fulton Street is the main commercial street, and the Bed-Stuy Gateway Business Improvement District covers it from Classon Avenue to Troy Avenue, which BK Reader described in 2014 as hundreds of businesses. The north–south avenues that cross it, Bedford, Nostrand, Tompkins, Lewis and Malcolm X Boulevard, carry cafés, restaurants, salons and corner stores, and Broadway and Myrtle Avenue frame the northern and eastern edges. Most of these stores are in two- and three-story buildings with apartments above, a cellar below and a flat roof shared with the neighbors.

Fulton Street

The neighborhood’s main shopping street, with the A and C trains below it. Pawnbrokers, gold buyers, phone and clothing stores, pharmacies and food businesses, several of them hit through the roof or ceiling in 2025.

Nostrand Avenue

A long avenue of corner stores, restaurants and services. In 2015 DNAinfo counted five burglaries in one month around Nostrand and Jefferson avenues alone.

Bedford Avenue

Cafés and restaurants among the houses. In late 2016 and early 2017 a pair of burglars hit Stonefruit Espresso at 1058 Bedford Avenue twice, taking a safe, and Joloff Restaurant at 1168 Bedford.

Tompkins and Lewis Avenues

Smaller storefronts in the heart of Stuyvesant Heights and the Bedford district, often in landmarked buildings where nothing new should go on the front.

Malcolm X Boulevard and Ralph Avenue

Shops and services on the eastern side, in the 81st Precinct, many on corners with side entrances and cellar hatches on two streets.

Broadway and Myrtle Avenue

Stores under the J, M and Z elevated on Broadway and along Myrtle Avenue to the north, with rear doors onto service areas and roofs that run along the row.

When the burglar comes through the roof

The 2025 break-ins on Fulton Street followed a route that most store alarms are not built for. FOX 5 reported that the suspect entered four businesses through the roof, and that on November 15 he came in through the ceiling at about 4:30 a.m.; News 12’s video of the Fulton Pawnbroker burglary showed a man climbing a wall before going through the ceiling. A store protected only by a contact on the roll-down gate and a glass-break detector on the window has nothing between the roof and the stock.

Protecting a store from above takes three things. First, any roof hatch or skylight the store controls gets a contact or a detector of its own. Second, the motion detectors inside are placed so that they look across the ceiling as well as along the aisles, which in a narrow Fulton Street store usually means one mounted high at the back looking toward the front, so a person dropping from a drop ceiling is in view the moment he lands. Third, the room where the most valuable stock is kept, whether a back office, a cage or a safe, gets its own detector so that it reports even if the burglar starts somewhere else.

Pawnshops, gold buyers and jewelers: the safe and the back room

Stores that hold gold, jewelry or cash after hours are the ones the 2025 burglar went for, and the amounts reported, about $20,000 in a single night more than once, show why. For these stores the alarm should report the first second of an attack on the stock rather than the moment the burglar leaves. That means a shock or vibration sensor on the safe, a contact on the door of the room it sits in, motion detection covering the ceiling of that room, and a panic button under the counter for the hours when the store is open.

The panel and its cellular communicator go in a locked, out-of-sight place away from the front, so the first thing a burglar finds is not the box that calls for help. A short entry delay applies only at the door staff use to open the store; every other zone reports instantly.

Cafés and restaurants: the evening after closing

The Bedford Avenue break-ins that DNAinfo covered in January 2017 mostly happened between about 7:30 and 11:30 in the evening, shortly after small cafés had closed, with one restaurant hit at about 3:45 a.m. One café was entered twice in three weeks. For a business that closes early, the risk starts the moment the last person locks up, so the alarm has to be armed every time, by whoever closes, without a manager present.

We set each employee up with a personal code, so the app shows who armed and disarmed and when, and we add a closing reminder that alerts the owner if the store has not been armed by a set time. A second burglary at the same address usually comes through the same weak point as the first, so after any break-in we look first at the route that was used and make sure the alarm now reports it instantly rather than after an entry delay.

Corner stores and buildings with a cellar under the shop

Many corner stores here keep stock in a cellar reached by a sidewalk hatch or an inside stair, and the cellar often runs under the building’s apartments too. The cellar door or hatch gets a heavy-duty contact rated for metal doors, and any door from the cellar into the store gets its own. Where the cellar is shared with the apartments above, the store’s alarm covers only the store’s part of it, and the landlord’s system or the building’s rules govern the rest.

Compliance notes for Bed-Stuy businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of shutting off, and to shut off automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. We set store and house sirens well inside that, which matters on blocks where a store sits under apartments.
  • Licensing. Alarm installation businesses in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, which requires 81 hours of qualifying education. AESS installs under license #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs before work starts.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm is not a code fire alarm system. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on a burglar alarm add warning and notification, but they do not replace a fire alarm a building is required to have.
  • Detectors in rental housing. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room.
  • Landmarked fronts. In the historic districts, the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s guidance keeps equipment off primary facades unless no other option exists, limits visible conduit to about ten inches, and limits cameras on small buildings to two per 25 feet of street frontage. Alarm devices go inside, so most alarms need nothing from the Commission at all.

Empty floors and renovations

We live on the garden floor while the duplex above us is gutted, and one night we heard footsteps up there. What would have told us sooner?

A motion detector on the upper stair, in an area of the panel that stays armed while you are home and disarmed downstairs. That is the exact situation the Brownstone Boys described in 2019: they were in the garden rental, the floors above were empty, and the first sign of an intruder was the sound of someone walking overhead. With the upper area armed, the first step on the stairs sends an alert to your phone and, if you have monitoring, to the central station, while the siren can be set to sound downstairs so you know before you go up. Add contacts on the roof hatch and on any upper rear window that can be reached, and the empty floors are covered from every direction an intruder can use.

Our contractor wants the new refrigerator, range and washer delivered weeks before we move in. Can the house be alarmed while the walls are still open?

Yes, and it should be. In 2016 the 79th Precinct reported thieves carrying refrigerators, stoves, washers and dryers out of houses under construction, often at night or on weekends and soon after deliveries. A temporary wireless layer goes on the openings that matter during the work, the rear door, the cellar, the roof and the room where the appliances are stored, with a motion detector covering that room. The panel runs on its own cellular line and battery, so it does not depend on the site’s temporary power or anyone’s Wi-Fi. Arm it every night and every weekend, and keep the delivery date between you and the contractor.

Will the sensors we pay for during the renovation carry over into the finished house, or is that money lost?

Very little is lost. The panel, the keypad, the communicator and the siren are the finished house’s equipment from day one. Wireless contacts used on temporary doors move to permanent ones or to windows when the work is done, and the motion detector watching the stored appliances moves to a stair landing. Cable run while the walls are open is permanent by nature. What is usually extra is a short return visit to re-zone the system once the floors are finished and the final doors are hung, which is billed as a service visit if it falls outside the original job.

Roofs, hatches and skylights

After a break-in nobody could explain, the roof hatch was our best guess. Should the sensor go on the hatch, on the stair door below it, or both?

On the hatch itself first, because that is the opening that reports the earliest. A contact on the hatch frame tells you the moment it lifts, before anyone is inside the house. If there is a bulkhead with a door at the top of the stair, that door gets a second contact, and in a house with a rental floor at the top the two may belong to different areas of the panel. Neither sensor locks anything. A hatch is often a fire exit, and Brownstoner forum posters rightly worried about padlocks that would trap someone inside; a contact only reports that the hatch opened and never stops anyone opening it.

The top-floor stair in our brownstone is lit by an old skylight that does not open. Contact, glass-break or motion?

Not a contact, because a skylight that does not open will never trip one. The real risk is the glass being broken and someone dropping onto the landing, so the choice is between a glass-break detector, which hears the glass, and a motion detector on the top landing, which sees the person. In a narrow brownstone stairwell we usually choose the motion detector, because one detector on the top floor also covers the hatch, the hall and the bedroom doors. A Brownstoner poster in 2007 listed “Wire glass, bars, armored roof hatch, alarm” as the full answer; the alarm is the part that tells you something has happened.

The precinct says to secure cellar doors and roof hatches. If the first budget only covers one of them, which should be on the alarm?

It depends on which one an outsider can reach unseen. On most blocks that is the roof, because the roofs run level from house to house and nobody on the street can see them, while a cellar door in the front areaway is in view of the sidewalk. If your cellar opens to the rear yard instead, it is just as hidden as the roof and the choice is closer. Either way, both openings are cheap to add to a basic system, often only a contact each, so we would rather fit both than leave one out.

Windows, fire escapes and extensions

Burglars here have come over from the next building and down the fire escape. In our five-story walk-up, which fire-escape windows get sensors?

Every window in your apartment that opens onto the fire escape, because once someone is on the escape, height does not protect you. The 79th Precinct told DNAinfo in 2015 that burglars were entering buildings or coming over from a neighboring building and descending fire escapes, then pushing window air conditioners in. Each fire-escape window gets a contact, any window that holds an air conditioner gets a contact on the sash so it reports if the unit is pushed in, and a motion detector in the room those windows open into backs them up. None of it locks a window or slows your way out in a fire.

Our house has a one-story rear extension whose flat roof sits just under the second-floor bedroom windows. Do those windows count as ground floor?

For an alarm, yes. A burglar standing on an extension roof is effectively at ground level, and the extension is usually reached from the yard or from the next house’s extension. Every window that opens onto that roof gets a contact, and if the bedroom is used every night, the zone can be armed in the night mode that leaves the interior motion detectors off while the windows stay protected. The same applies to windows beside a rear deck, a shed roof or a neighbor’s lower building.

Shared basements

I rent the apartment beside our building’s basement, and someone came in through the cellar door one night. Can my own alarm cover the door between the cellar and my unit?

Your own alarm can cover your own door, and that is the one that matters to you. In the 2019 burglary near Kosciuszko Street and Marcus Garvey Boulevard that CBS New York reported, the intruder went through the cellar door and later forced the apartment next to the basement. A contact on your apartment door, a contact on any of your windows into the cellar or the areaway, and a motion detector inside give you an instant alert if anyone comes through. The building’s cellar door belongs to the landlord, so ask in writing for it to be repaired and kept locked; if the landlord wants it alarmed as well, that is a separate system we can quote to them.

Stores on Fulton Street and the avenues

After last year’s roof break-ins on Fulton Street, should our store’s motion detectors watch the ceiling instead of the front door?

They should watch both, and the placement does it. The burglar FOX 5 described came in through the roof of four businesses and, on one night, through the ceiling. A detector mounted high at the back of the store, looking forward, sees the whole length of the floor and anyone dropping from a drop ceiling, while the gate and front door keep their own contacts. Any roof hatch or skylight the store controls gets a sensor, and if the space above your ceiling runs under the neighbors’ stores, the back room where the stock is kept gets a separate detector so it reports even if the entry starts next door.

We buy and sell gold on Fulton Street. What protects the stock once the gate is down, beyond a siren?

Sensors that report the attack on the stock itself, and a panel the burglar cannot silence. A vibration or shock sensor on the safe, a contact on the door to the room it is in, and a motion detector covering that room’s ceiling report the moment someone starts working on the valuables, not when they leave. The panel sits hidden away from the front on its own cellular line with battery backup, so cutting a cable outside does nothing. Monitoring with dispatch is worth having here; News 12 reported about $20,000 in merchandise taken from Fulton Pawnbroker in a single night in October 2025.

A burglar who hit our Bedford Avenue café came back three weeks later. What should change after a first break-in?

Start with the route that was used the first time, because a repeat burglary usually comes the same way. DNAinfo reported in 2017 that Stonefruit Espresso on Bedford Avenue was hit on December 22 and again on January 13, with a safe taken the first time. After a break-in we make sure the entry point reports instantly rather than after an entry delay, add a detector in the room where cash or the safe is kept, check that the panel cannot be reached and smashed before it reports, and set a closing alert so the owner knows if the store was left unarmed. The door or window that was forced has to be properly repaired before any sensor on it means anything.

Landmarked blocks

We want a camera on the front of our landmarked rowhouse alongside the alarm. How many will the Landmarks Commission allow, and where can the wire go?

The Commission’s permit guidebook allows staff-level permits for security cameras on a small residential building up to two per 25 feet of street frontage, well spaced, on plain masonry or mortar joints rather than decorative surfaces, with conduit concealed and no more than about ten inches visible, painted to match. Equipment should stay off the primary facade unless no other option exists, so a camera under the stoop or at the garden door, wired from inside, is the usual answer. The alarm itself needs none of this, because its devices are all inside the house.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant

These are claims that turn up again and again in generic online advice, directory pages from Angi and HomeAdvisor, and machine-written answers about home alarms. Each is reasonable for a detached suburban house. Each needs correcting for a Bed-Stuy brownstone, French flat or Fulton Street store.

“Most burglars come through the front door, so protect it first.”

National advice starts at the front door because in a detached house it is often the easiest opening to reach. The 79th Precinct did once report the front door as the point of entry in a run of burglaries, which is a reason to keep a contact there.

But a Bed-Stuy front door sits at the top of a stoop, behind a vestibule, in full view of the street. The openings no one can see are the roof hatch reached from the next roof, the skylight, the rear windows over an extension and the cellar door to the yard, and those are the ones the precinct, Brownstoner posters and the 2025 Fulton Street burglaries point to.

The front door still gets a contact. It simply is not where the plan or the budget starts.

“A basic package of three door sensors and one motion detector covers a home.”

Starter packages are designed around a one- or two-story house where one motion detector in the living room sees most of the ground floor.

A four-story brownstone has a garden floor, a parlor floor, two bedroom floors, a roof and a cellar, and a single motion detector on any one of them sees almost nothing of the others. The stair is the one place every route meets, and even there a house usually needs one detector low and one high.

The right count follows the openings floor by floor, which is why our walkthrough starts at the roof and ends in the cellar, and why the price is written after the visit rather than read off a package.

“Wait until the renovation is finished before installing an alarm.”

This is common advice because an alarm installed in a finished house never has to be moved, and contractors setting off sirens are a nuisance.

In Bed-Stuy the months of renovation are the riskiest months a house has. The 79th Precinct’s 2016 reports of appliances and tools carried out of houses under construction, and the 81st Precinct’s count of about 95 vacant properties that same year, describe exactly the houses this advice leaves unprotected.

Open walls are also the cheapest moment to wire. A temporary wireless layer with codes for each trade solves the nuisance problem, and the panel stays when the work is done.

“Every alarm needs a loud outdoor siren so the neighbors hear it.”

In a detached suburb an outdoor bell may be the only way anyone hears an alarm, and alarm websites often treat one as standard.

On a landmarked Bed-Stuy block the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s guidance keeps equipment off primary facades unless no other option exists, and New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off within fifteen minutes. In attached houses an interior siren is heard through the party walls anyway.

We fit interior sirens, keep the facade clear, set a short cutoff, and rely on the app and the monitoring station to bring help, not the neighbors.

“Renters can’t have an alarm because they don’t own the building.”

Many guides assume an alarm is wired into the building and therefore only an owner can install one.

A tenant in a French flat, a walk-up, a brownstone rental floor, or an apartment in Marcy or Sumner Houses can have a wireless system that sits entirely inside the apartment: a contact on the apartment door, contacts on the fire-escape windows and a motion detector, fixed lightly and removed at the end of the lease.

The lease and the landlord still have their say, and we put the plan in writing for them. What a tenant cannot do is alarm the building’s cellar, roof or street door, which belong to the owner.

“A store alarm stops break-ins.”

Alarm marketing often implies that the siren itself prevents a burglary.

The burglar who went through the roofs of Fulton Street stores in 2025 was not deterred by the stores’ front gates or glass; he came in from above and was gone with tens of thousands of dollars in goods. An alarm cannot make a roof stronger. What it can do is report the first second of the entry, from the ceiling, the back room or the safe, to a monitoring station and to the owner’s phone.

Prevention is physical: a sound roof hatch, a bolted safe, stock locked away overnight. The alarm makes sure that when those fail, someone knows at once.

“Professional installation always means a long monitoring contract.”

Forum users on Reddit describe national dealers financing equipment through monitoring agreements of three to five years, and AI summaries often repeat that as how professional alarms work.

We sell the equipment at our published Brooklyn list price. Monitoring through our partners is optional, month to month, from $30 a month, and many owners start with phone alerts only, as the Brownstone Boys did with their app-based system.

If you later move, sell or decide you do not want monitoring, the equipment is still yours and keeps working with the app.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start on the Top Landing, Finish at the Cellar Door

This describes how our technicians survey a Bed-Stuy brownstone, not a story about one particular job. The visit starts with a question at the front door: which floors are lived in, which are rented, and which are empty or being worked on. Then the technician goes straight to the top of the house. He opens the hatch or the bulkhead door, checks how it latches and whether anything on the roof would let a person stand there unseen, and looks at the skylight from the landing. He notes the rear windows on the top floor and whether any of them open onto a fire escape or sit over a neighbor’s lower roof.

On the way down he stops on every landing, because the stair is where every route through the house meets and where the motion detectors will finally go. On the parlor floor he looks at the front windows beside the stoop and at the vestibule doors. On the garden floor he checks the door under the stoop and the rear door to the yard, then walks the yard itself to see what the back fences and the extension roofs look like. The cellar comes last: its door, any old delivery hatch, the boiler and water heater, and the stair up into the house. By the time he leaves he has a list of openings floor by floor, and that list, not a package, is what the written price is built from.
