The Neighborhood

Why Sunset Park Properties Need Security Camera Installation

In Sunset Park, the camera job is decided at the ground floor. The neighborhood runs from the harbor up the hill to Eighth and Ninth Avenues, with Green-Wood Cemetery and Greenwood Heights to the north and Bay Ridge to the south. Its housing is some of the oldest in the city; Community Board 7 calls it the "second oldest housing stock in NYC," and the Fifth Avenue Committee's 2020 housing report found that more than half of the district's 45,903 homes were built before 1939. Most of the blocks between Fourth and Seventh Avenues are lined with brick row houses of two stories over a basement, many with a rounded or three-sided bay, most of them built between 1892 and 1910 and most of them originally for two families. Those houses have one stoop, one street door and often one shared vestibule for everybody who lives there. That one doorway is where nearly every camera question on the residential blocks begins.

The avenues are a second world. When NYC Small Business Services funded a needs assessment for the Sunset Park Business Improvement District, its surveyors walked Third, Fourth, Fifth, Seventh and Eighth Avenues from 39th to 65th Street, plus 39th and 65th Streets between Third and Eighth, and counted 1,580 storefronts with an 11.3 percent vacancy rate. Eighth Avenue had the most, at 455, followed by Fifth Avenue at 445, Fourth Avenue at 232 and Seventh Avenue at 230. The same study surveyed 887 merchants, and 62 percent said that ensuring community safety would bring more shoppers; 76 percent said their sales had fallen over the past year. A store owner losing sales wants the door, the counter and the back of the shop covered properly, once, at a price known in advance.

The local reports show why those three spots matter. In February 2025 News 12 reported that a man walked into the church at 5623 Seventh Avenue through an unlocked side door, took $350 and left around 1:10 in the afternoon; the NYPD released video of him afterward. In April 2024 several people entered a store on 65th Street around 8:30 in the evening, and one pulled a gun on the clerk and took cash from the register. In May 2025 police said a man with a knife robbed a 63-year-old on Eighth Avenue around 10 p.m., and the image News 12 obtained of the suspect was a surveillance still. In June 2024 two men forced their way into a business on Second Avenue near 41st Street and took $8,000 from a desk. A side door in daylight, a counter in the evening, a sidewalk at night and a desk in a waterfront unit: four different jobs for a camera, on four different kinds of property, within a few blocks of each other.

Then there are the packages. In April 2025 the city's NYCLocker program opened Sunset Park's first secure package locker on Sixth Avenue, near City Fresh Market, after residents reported thefts, including a string on that block the same week. One resident told News 12, "We saw packages were missing in our building group chat." The Department of Transportation puts the citywide figure at more than 90,000 packages misplaced or stolen every day. In a row house where the courier leaves boxes inside the street door, or in a walk-up where they pile up under the mailboxes, a camera that sees the faces of people coming in and out of the vestibule is often the first one a family asks us for.

West of Third Avenue the job changes again. Industry City alone is about sixteen buildings and roughly six million square feet between the 30s and the low 40s, Bush Terminal continues south of it, and the Brooklyn Army Terminal sits further down the waterfront. Tenants there lease suites inside buildings the landlord runs, often with the landlord's own cameras in the courtyards and corridors. Third Avenue itself runs under the Gowanus Expressway, and the SBS study says that stretch has insufficient lighting along with heavy noise and air pollution. A waterfront tenant's cameras have to work in long concrete corridors, at loading doors and under an elevated highway, and the decisions about where they can go often belong to someone else. For neighbors up the hill, our Brooklyn security camera installation page covers the rest of the borough.

For a store, the most common pairing is an alarm on the back door, which reports the door opening even when nobody is looking at a phone.

Seven Corridors

Third Avenue to Eighth Avenue: How a Storefront Camera Plan Changes From One Sunset Park Corridor to the Next

The SBS needs assessment is the most useful document we know of for planning cameras on Sunset Park's shopping streets, even though it never mentions cameras. It describes each corridor's storefronts, their condition and the merchants' complaints, and those details decide where a camera can be mounted, what light it will have to work with and which door matters most. Below is how we read each corridor, using the study's own numbers. None of this is a substitute for standing in front of your store, but it explains why two shops ten blocks apart can get very different quotes.

Eighth Avenue: 455 storefronts and the busiest counters

Eighth Avenue is the heart of Brooklyn's largest Chinatown, roughly from the low 40s to the high 60s, and the study counted more storefronts here than on any other corridor. It also found 16 percent of them vacant, the highest share of the five avenues. For a camera plan that means two different customers on the same block: an established bakery, grocery or restaurant with a crowded counter from early morning, and a new tenant fitting out an empty storefront. The established shop needs a face camera inside the door that catches people as they step in out of the daylight, a camera above the register that shows hands, money and the drawer rather than the tops of heads, and a camera on the kitchen or back door, which in older buildings often opens onto a yard or a passage. The tenant fitting out a vacant space has the easiest job of anyone on the avenue, because the ceiling is open and cable can go straight to every camera position before the finishes go in.

Fifth Avenue: 445 storefronts, a BID and the question of the gate

Fifth Avenue is the Sunset Park BID's own street, from 38th to 64th Street. The study rated 56 percent of its storefronts in poor condition, which on the ground often means an aging awning, a sign band that has been repainted many times, and a solid roll-down gate. Gates matter more for cameras than most owners expect. With a solid gate down, a camera inside the window sees only the back of the gate all night, so the hours when the store is most at risk are recorded by nothing. Back in 2016 the BID urged Fifth Avenue merchants to drop their full-front metal gates, and a 2009 city law required many businesses to switch to gates at least 70 percent see-through by July 1, 2026; in July 2026 the City Council voted unanimously to repeal that deadline, so a compliant gate is now required only when a gate is replaced for other reasons. Either way, a Fifth Avenue store needs one camera plan for the hours the gate is up and another for the hours it is down, and we design both.

Fourth Avenue: 232 storefronts on a wide, fast street

Fourth Avenue is the widest of the shopping streets, with the subway underneath and heavy traffic on top. The study found only 5 percent of its storefronts vacant, the lowest rate on any corridor. Stores here tend to sit in larger buildings with apartments or offices above, and the facade usually belongs to a landlord who has opinions about what is fixed to it. A camera over a Fourth Avenue shop door has to see across a deep sidewalk without staring into the headlights of the traffic beyond, so we frame it tightly on the door and the sidewalk in front of it, set the exposure for faces rather than for the street, and keep the cable inside the building so the landlord's facade carries nothing but the camera.

Seventh Avenue: 230 storefronts with houses and churches between them

Seventh Avenue is a mixed street, with shops, row houses, community organizations and houses of worship side by side, and the study found it in the best condition of the corridors, with 38 percent of storefronts rated good. The church theft at 5623 Seventh Avenue in February 2025 is a reminder that the doors at risk here are not always shop doors. A church, a day care or a community office often has a front door that is locked and watched and a side door that volunteers use and leave open. The camera that matters most on those buildings is the one on the side door, set to record faces as people enter and to send an alert during the hours when that door should be closed.

Third Avenue: 97 storefronts in the shadow of the expressway

Third Avenue runs under the Gowanus Expressway, and the study names its problems plainly: insufficient lighting, noise and heavy air pollution. Its 97 storefronts were rated 68 percent fair. For a camera, the expressway turns midday into dusk and night into near-darkness, and the grime that settles out of the traffic films a lens faster than on any other corridor. A Third Avenue shop or auto business needs cameras chosen for low light, mounted where they can be wiped without a ladder if possible, and often a fixed light over the door that the camera can rely on. We cover how we choose between a better camera and a better light in the questions below.

Bayed two-family row houses: one doorway for two households

The classic Sunset Park house was built as an owner's duplex with a rental floor on top. Brownstoner's description of 29 two-family houses on 44th Street, built in 1903, is typical: an owner's home on the lower floors and a separate apartment above, sharing one stoop and one street door. For a camera that shared doorway is the whole puzzle. The best view of a visitor's face is just inside the street door, looking out, where people pause to find a key or ring a bell; a camera on the outside wall high above the stoop mostly records hats. We usually put the main face camera in the vestibule ceiling or high on the inside wall, add a second camera covering the stoop and the sidewalk in front of it, and give each household a separate account, so the tenant sees the door but not the owner's back yard camera, and the owner's recordings stay with the owner.

Landmarked rows: cameras the Commission can approve at staff level

The four districts the Commission designated in 2019 include about 285 buildings in Sunset Park South between 54th and 59th Streets, 148 in Sunset Park Central around 47th and 48th Streets, 56 in Sunset Park North on the south side of 44th Street, and 50 houses in the 50th Street district. On those blocks, a front camera is Landmarks work, and the Commission's permit guidebook sets out what its staff can approve without a hearing. We cover those rules in detail in the next section. The short version for a bayed front: keep cameras off the face of the house where another spot works, keep them small and colored to match, attach them only at mortar joints or plain masonry, and hide the cable in the wall rather than on it.

Walk-ups and early co-ops: the stair hall and the mailboxes

Sunset Park's older apartment houses have a street door, a vestibule with mailboxes, a stair hall and sometimes a basement laundry or storage area. Common-area cameras in those buildings belong to the owner or, in a co-op, the board, and Habitat magazine's reporting on hallway cameras is clear that a shareholder cannot simply mount one in a common hall. Attorney Debra Guzov told Habitat, "If you're living in a co-op, you can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways." For a building, we plan the vestibule and mailbox camera first, then the stair landings, the basement door and the roof door, with accounts for the super, the managing agent and the board, and a recorder in a locked closet the building controls.

Stores with apartments above: two occupants, one facade

On Fifth, Seventh and Eighth Avenues, the typical building has a shop on the ground floor and two or three apartments above, reached by a separate street door beside the store. The shop and the residential entrance need different cameras with different owners. The store wants its counter, its door and its back door covered; the landlord wants the residential door and the mailboxes covered; and the tenants upstairs do not want a camera that sees into their windows. We plan the two systems side by side, often on separate recorders, and we aim every outdoor camera so its view and its infrared light stay below the second-floor windows. Where one owner holds both the store and the building, one recorder with separate accounts can serve both, as long as each person sees only their part.

Waterfront factory buildings: long corridors and the landlord's network

The buildings of Industry City, Bush Terminal and the Brooklyn Army Terminal were built for manufacturing and shipping, and their suites are reached through courtyards, freight elevators and long concrete corridors. Network camera cable has a practical distance limit of about 100 meters between a camera and its switch, so a camera at a dock door far from a suite needs either a switch closer to it or a fiber link. Many campus landlords also control what can be drilled, where cable can run and how a tenant connects to the building network. We ask for the landlord's contractor rules before we quote, use surface raceway or the building's own cable trays where cores are not allowed, and keep the tenant's cameras and recorder on the tenant's own equipment so they keep working when the landlord changes something.

Landmarks Rules

Two Cameras per 25 Feet: What the Landmarks Commission Allows on a Sunset Park District House

If your house is on one of the blocks the Commission designated in June 2019, any camera on its street front needs a Landmarks permit. That sounds slower than it usually is, because the Commission's Permit Guidebook lists criteria its staff can approve without sending the job to the Commissioners. A camera plan that meets those criteria from the start is the fastest way through. Here is what the guidebook says, and how each rule plays out on a bayed Sunset Park front.

How many cameras: two for every 25 feet of frontage

For small residential or commercial buildings, the guidebook allows "no more than two security cameras" for every 25 feet of street frontage, and three per 25 feet on large residential, commercial or institutional buildings. It also says cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance." A typical Sunset Park row house is narrower than 25 feet, so in practice its street front can carry two cameras at most under the staff-level criteria. That is enough for one camera at the stoop and the sidewalk and one at the areaway or the basement door, as long as the face camera for visitors goes inside the vestibule, where the Commission's facade rules do not reach.

Where they go: off the primary facade, unless there is no other option

The guidebook says equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," and when it must be, it should sit somewhere discreet: "beneath a band course, under a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, or behind plantings." Sunset Park fronts are full of exactly those places. The underside of the stoop gives a dry, hidden spot that looks along the areaway and up the first steps. A short post or the inside face of an areaway wall or fence can carry a small camera that watches the gate. And on houses with a band course between the parlor and upper floors, a small camera tucked under that ledge can see the stoop without standing out against the brick.

What they look like: small, and colored to blend

The guidebook asks that equipment be "as small as possible and painted to blend with surrounding building material, if possible," and its example shows dome cameras finished to match the wall. We choose compact turrets or mini domes for district fronts, in a housing color close to the brick or stone, and avoid large bullet cameras with sunshields on the face of a landmarked house. If the color cannot be matched from the factory, the mount and housing can be painted before installation so the job does not leave a bright white object on a brown front.

How they attach: mortar joints, never cast iron or ornament

Equipment "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork, or concrete paving" at the areaway or sidewalk, and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." That rules out the decorative ironwork on many Sunset Park stoops and railings, carved lintels and cornices, and any terra-cotta or pressed ornament. It favors anchoring into the mortar joints of the brick, which can be repointed later, rather than drilling the face of a brick or brownstone block. It is also simply good practice on any old masonry house, landmarked or not.

Where the cable goes: inside the wall, or ten inches at most

The guidebook wants conduit concealed by running it through plain masonry or cladding behind the camera. Where that is not possible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit along a wall or architectural element, painted to match and secured into mortar joints rather than onto ornament. On a row house, that usually means drilling straight through the front wall behind the camera into the vestibule or the basement, then running the cable inside. A camera under the stoop can almost always be fed from the basement through a short, hidden penetration.

What the application needs: cut sheets, and no drawings for a like-for-like swap

The guidebook asks for manufacturer cut sheets for the cameras with the application, and it notes that elevation drawings are not needed for an in-kind replacement of existing cameras. We provide the cut sheets for every camera we propose, along with photos of each position, so the owner or their filer can submit a complete application the first time. Cameras on the back of the house, inside the vestibule and indoors are separate questions from the front, and we plan them so they do not wait on the facade permit unless the owner prefers to do everything at once.

Bakeries, groceries and restaurants: the counter, the door and the kitchen

The SBS study counted 455 storefronts on Eighth Avenue alone, and many of them are food businesses whose day starts before sunrise. Their camera needs are the same in outline and very different in detail. The door camera has to handle a doorway that is dark inside and bright outside, so we use cameras with strong wide dynamic range and mount them inside, looking out at the threshold at about head height. The register camera goes above and slightly in front of the counter, so it sees the cashier's hands, the drawer and the customer's hands, not the screen of a card terminal where a PIN is typed. The kitchen or back door camera is set to record continuously and to alert only after closing, because deliveries come and go through it all day. Steam and grease from a kitchen film a lens quickly, so we keep cameras out of the cooking line's air and leave a cleaning schedule.

Small retail and service shops: one clerk, late hours

The April 2024 robbery on 65th Street happened around 8:30 in the evening, with one employee struck at the register. A small store open into the evening needs three things from its cameras. First, a clear face of every person who walks in, recorded at the door, not across the room. Second, a view of the counter that shows what happened there in enough detail to describe it. Third, a recorder that the people who rob a store cannot easily take with them, which means mounted out of sight in a back room or basement, not on a shelf behind the register.

Houses of worship and community groups: the side door in daylight

The February 2025 theft at the church at 5623 Seventh Avenue is the pattern we plan for in these buildings: an unlocked side door, the middle of the day, and someone who walks in, takes cash and leaves before anyone notices. Most congregations want their doors open for the people who use the building, and they should be. What a camera can add is a recorded face at every side and back door, alerts during the hours when those doors should be closed, and a recorder whose login does not depend on a single volunteer. Inside, cameras watch hallways, offices and any room where money is counted, never restrooms or private counseling rooms.

Studios and makers in Industry City: inside a landlord's campus

Industry City counts more than 650 businesses on its campus, and nearly all of them are tenants in suites inside buildings the landlord manages, with the landlord's own security in the courtyards and lobbies. A tenant's camera system starts at the suite door and works inward: the door, the freight elevator landing if it opens into the suite, the area where expensive equipment or finished work is kept, and any loading door the tenant controls. Before quoting, we ask for the campus's contractor rules, insurance requirements and any limits on drilling or on connecting to the building network, and we keep the tenant's recorder inside the suite on its own internet connection where possible.

Warehouses and distribution: before dawn at the dock

The waterfront between Second Avenue and the piers is where Sunset Park's warehouses, distributors and food processors work, and their risky hours are the early ones, when trucks arrive before the office opens. The June 2024 burglary at a business on Second Avenue near 41st Street, where two men forced their way in and took $8,000 from a desk, shows that the target is not always the stock. We plan dock cameras for faces at the personnel door and for the dock plates and trailer doors, yard cameras for vehicles at the gate, and an office camera for the desk and the safe, all on a recorder in a locked room with a battery unit.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras in Sunset Park

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance; ask any installer for both before work starts. Landmarks. A business in one of the 2019 historic districts follows the same camera rules as a house, with three cameras per 25 feet allowed on large commercial or institutional buildings at staff level. Landlords and campuses. Most avenue facades and every campus building belong to a landlord, so get written permission for any outside mount or wall penetration before the visit. Gates. The 2026 repeal of the gate deadline means a see-through gate is required only when you replace your gate for other reasons, and the Department of Buildings says permit requirements for grilles have not changed. Sound. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations under Penal Law sections 250.00 and 250.05, so a camera that records the talk of customers when no one in the conversation has agreed is a risk; the cameras we install record video, and audio stays off unless the owner has taken advice on it. Private rooms. No camera views a restroom, changing area or staff room used for changing, and we will not install one that does.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Starting a Sunset Park Walk-Through on the Sidewalk, Not Inside

This describes how we approach properties here, not one particular job. On a Sunset Park walk-through we start on the sidewalk, across the street if traffic allows, and look at the building the way a stranger would. Where is the street door, and is there a second door under the stoop or beside the shop? Where do couriers leave packages? Is there a gate, and when it is down, what does it hide? Then we walk to the door and stand where a visitor stands, and look for the spot that would see that person's face: the vestibule ceiling, the transom over a shop door, the underside of the stoop. Only then do we go inside and walk to the back door, the yard, the kitchen or the dock.

We ask three questions most installers skip. Who else has a say: a landlord, a co-op board, a campus manager or the Landmarks Commission? Who needs to see which camera: the owner, a tenant upstairs, a manager, a pastor, a volunteer? And what are the hours when nobody should be there? The first decides where cameras can go, the second decides how many accounts the recorder needs, and the third decides when alerts should fire. Then we write the quote, one line per camera, each with its job beside it.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Sunset Park: Questions Answered

Stoops, vestibules and shared houses

Packages keep disappearing from the shared vestibule of our three-family between the delivery and when we get home. Should the camera go outside the street door or inside the vestibule?

Inside, in most cases. A camera on the outside wall high above the stoop records the tops of heads and the backs of couriers, while a camera in the vestibule ceiling or high on the inside wall, looking toward the street door, records each person's face as they step in and the spot where the boxes sit. If the vestibule has an inner door, aim the camera so it sees both the package spot and the outer door. Outside, a second camera on the stoop or under it adds the sidewalk and the direction people leave in, which is useful but rarely decisive.

NYCLocker opened a locker on Sixth Avenue in 2025 so people can send deliveries there instead. If our building switches to it, is a vestibule camera still worth having?

A locker solves the parcel problem for the people who use it, and it is a good idea for anyone who is never home during the day. It does not cover the visitors, food deliveries, contractors and strangers who still pass through your vestibule, or the packages that relatives and stores send to your street address anyway. Most of the incidents a vestibule camera records are not package thefts at all. We would still put a face camera at the street door; the locker just makes it less likely that its first recording is of someone walking off with your order.

Our building's group chat shares a clip every time something goes missing. How do we send neighbors one clip without giving anyone access to our cameras?

Export the clip, do not share the account. Every recorder we install can copy a chosen stretch of time from one camera to a phone or a USB stick, and that file can be sent like any other video. Sharing a login, even for a minute, gives the other person every camera and every recording, and it is easy to forget to change the password afterward. We show owners how to export at handover, and we set the recorder's clock to a time server so the time on your clip matches what neighbors see on theirs.

The avenues before opening and after closing

When our Fifth Avenue store finally replaces its solid gate with a see-through one, will a camera behind the window be able to see the sidewalk at night through the grille and the glass?

It can, if it is set up for it. The usual problem is not the grille but the glass: a camera's own infrared light bounces off the inside of the window and turns the night picture white. The fix is to press the camera close to the glass with a hood or foam gasket, or to turn its infrared off and rely on the store's night lighting or a light outside. A see-through gate then becomes a pattern across the bottom of the picture that the recorder can be told to ignore. Until your gate is replaced, a small camera outside, under the awning or in the transom, is the only thing that records the closed hours.

Our Eighth Avenue bakery's counter faces the front window, and by mid-morning the customers at the register are dark shapes against the light. What fixes that?

A camera with real wide dynamic range, and a better angle. When a camera looks toward a bright window, it exposes for the window and leaves faces in shadow. A camera rated for true wide dynamic range, rather than a digital setting, combines bright and dark parts of the scene so faces show. Moving the register camera so it looks from the window side toward the cashier, rather than from behind the cashier toward the window, helps even more. The door camera has the same problem and the same fix: inside the door, close to the glass, aimed in at people as they enter.

Our restaurant's back door opens onto the yard where staff take breaks and deliveries come in all day. How do we watch that door without recording the staff on break for hours?

Give the camera one job: the door. We frame it on the doorway and a short stretch of yard in front of it, mask any bench or corner where staff sit, and set alerts only for after closing. It still records every person who comes in or goes out, because that is what the door camera is for, but it does not become a camera on the break area. Tell your staff where it points and why; a short notice by the door saves misunderstandings, and in our experience most staff are glad of a camera on a door they lock at night.

Our church on Seventh Avenue keeps a side door unlocked for volunteers on weekdays. Can a camera tell us right away when someone comes through it?

Yes. A camera inside the side door, aimed at faces as people enter, can send a phone alert the moment a person crosses a line drawn across the doorway, and the alert can be limited to the hours when no one is expected. During busy hours it simply records. For a building where nobody watches a phone all day, a small chime or a door contact tied to the same schedule tells whoever is in the office that the door opened. What happened at 5623 Seventh Avenue in February 2025 took minutes, so the point is a face on record and a person alerted, not a guard at the door.

West of Third Avenue

Our shop is on Third Avenue under the Gowanus Expressway, where even the city's study says the lighting falls short. Is it better to add our own light or buy a stronger night camera?

Usually both, but the light matters more. A camera in permanent shade switches to infrared for long stretches of the day, which gives a black-and-white picture and loses the color of clothing and vehicles. A fixed, steady light over the door and the sidewalk in front of it lets the camera stay in color and see faces clearly, and it does not flicker on and off with passing traffic the way a motion light does. Then we choose a camera with good low-light sensitivity and a flat lens that wipes clean, because the grime from the highway films a lens faster than anywhere else in the neighborhood.

Our Industry City landlord already has cameras in the courtyards and corridors. Can we get their footage of the area outside our suite, or do we need our own camera there?

You can ask, but you cannot count on it. Brick Underground's reporting on landlord footage says a landlord generally "isn't obligated to share surveillance footage" with a tenant unless police request it or a court orders it. The landlord's cameras also serve the landlord's purposes, so their view of your door may be distant, low resolution or recorded for a short time. If the corridor outside your suite matters to you, a camera inside your door looking out, or one mounted at your entry with the landlord's written permission, gives you footage you control.

The freight elevator in our waterfront building opens straight into our unit. Should a camera watch the elevator door?

Yes, and it is often the most important camera in the suite. In converted factory buildings a freight elevator that opens into a tenant's space is effectively a second front door, used by movers, building staff and other tenants' deliveries, sometimes outside your hours. We put a camera inside the unit facing the elevator doors, framed so it records faces as they open, and set alerts for any opening outside your working hours. If the landlord controls the elevator's access, ask whether it keeps a log; a camera on your side and the building's log together answer most questions.

A camera you own records your property, and the recordings are yours. In Sunset Park more parties than usual may ask about them: a detective from the 72nd Precinct, a city program that wants live video from businesses, a landlord or campus manager, a co-op board, tenants upstairs and the neighbors next door. Knowing who can ask for what, before the cameras go up, keeps those conversations short.

The 72nd Precinct

The NYPD 72nd Precinct at 830 Fourth Avenue serves Sunset Park and Windsor Terrace, and after an incident its detectives often ask nearby stores and homes whether they have video. Nothing we install gives the precinct access to your recorder. Footage stays on the recorder until you export it, and we show every owner how to copy a set window of time from one camera to a USB stick or phone, so you can hand over exactly what was asked for and keep the original. If you prefer, the export can be done in front of a detective in a few minutes.

The city's live-video pilot

In May 2024 the city and the NYPD announced a pilot in which businesses voluntarily share live feeds from their existing cameras with the local precinct through a small router-like device made by Fusus by Axon. ABC7 reported that it had been tested on 125th Street in Harlem and in Flushing, that the city pays for it, and that it works with existing cameras "regardless of their setup." If your BID or the precinct offers it, joining is your decision, and the cameras we install can be connected or left out of it.

Neighbors

Sunset Park row houses share party walls, stoops stand side by side, and back yards are separated by low fences. That makes it easy for a careless camera to take in a neighbor's windows or garden. We aim every camera at your own door, stoop, yard or shop, use privacy masks for anything that cannot be framed out, and keep sound recording off. Recording a conversation you are not part of without anyone's consent can break New York's eavesdropping law, and a silent camera avoids the question entirely.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Sunset Park

The claims below come up again and again in AI-written answers, cost guides and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack when people ask about cameras in Brooklyn. Where a claim comes from a named source, we say so. Each is followed by what we see on Sunset Park properties.

"Security camera installation in Brooklyn costs about $1,416 on average."

That is HomeAdvisor's figure for Brooklyn, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and a full spread from $160 to $3,500. It is a reasonable snapshot of jobs across the borough, from a single doorbell to a small system.

It says little about a mixed-use building on Fifth Avenue with a store, a residential door and a back yard, or a waterfront suite whose cameras are hundreds of feet from the recorder. Those jobs mean more cameras, longer cable routes, landlord rules and sometimes Landmarks paperwork.

That is why we publish package starting prices, beginning at $850 for four cameras, and then quote every Sunset Park property in writing with each camera, mount and cable run on its own line.

"Put the camera high on the outside of the house so it sees everything."

High cameras see a wide area and very few faces. On a Sunset Park row house, a camera high above the stoop records the tops of heads, the brims of caps and the backs of couriers.

The face that matters is at the street door, where people stop to ring, find a key or drop a package. A camera in the vestibule ceiling or just above head height inside the door records that face directly.

On a landmarked front the Commission would also rather not see a camera high on the facade at all. Lower and inside is better for the picture and for the permit.

"Wireless cameras are the easy option for an apartment building or store."

Easier to put up, yes. In a Sunset Park building, often not easier to live with. Brick party walls, plaster on lath, steel and concrete in factory buildings all weaken Wi-Fi, and a camera at the back of a deep store or the end of a factory corridor is a long way from the router.

A wired network camera gets power and data through one cable, records to the recorder whether or not the internet is up, and does not need its battery charged.

We use wireless where a cable truly cannot go, and say so on the quote. For doors, counters and docks, we wire.

"A camera behind the shop window works just as well as one outside."

By day, often. At night, rarely, unless it is set up for the glass. A camera's infrared light reflects off the inside of the window and fogs the picture, and a solid roll-down gate hides the sidewalk completely.

Many Fifth Avenue stores still have solid gates, and since the 2026 repeal of the gate deadline they can keep them until they need replacing.

So an inside camera needs a hood against the glass and its infrared turned off, and a store with a solid gate needs a camera outside, under the awning or in the transom, to record the closed hours.

"Cameras are a low-voltage job, so the Landmarks rules don't apply."

The voltage has nothing to do with it. The Landmarks Commission regulates what is fixed to the outside of a building in a historic district, and its Permit Guidebook has a section specifically about security cameras.

On Sunset Park's district blocks that means at most two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on a small building at staff level, placement off the primary facade where possible, small housings painted to blend, attachment at mortar joints, and concealed conduit.

Interior cameras, including a vestibule camera behind the street door, are not facade work. That is one more reason the face camera usually goes inside.

"Cloud cameras mean the footage is safe even if the recorder is stolen."

A cloud copy does protect against a stolen recorder, which is a real risk in a store. But cloud cameras upload over the building's internet, and in a small store or two-family that connection is shared with card terminals, phones and televisions; continuous video from several cameras can strain it.

A local recorder hidden in a back room or basement, out of reach from the sales floor, records everything at full quality and keeps going when the internet drops.

For stores that want both, we can add an off-site copy of selected clips. If a service carries a fee, it is named on the quote.

"Point your camera anywhere you like on your own property."

Your camera, your property, yes, but not your neighbor's windows and not their conversations. Row houses in Sunset Park stand wall to wall, with bays and yards a few feet apart, and a wide camera on one house easily takes in the next.

New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, so a camera that records sound from a neighbor's stoop or a customer's phone call, with no one in the conversation agreeing, is a legal risk.

We aim every camera at your own doors and yard, use privacy masks for the rest, and keep audio off unless you have taken advice on it.
