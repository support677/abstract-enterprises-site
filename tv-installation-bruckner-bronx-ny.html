The Neighborhood

Why Bruckner Properties Need TV Installation

Bruckner is a neighborhood people find on a map by its highway, but they live in it by its buildings. NabeWise, in its guide to Soundview-Bruckner, describes the area as the northern part of Soundview, cut off from the rest of it by the Bruckner Expressway, with tree-lined streets, very large apartment complexes and its own shopping district along Westchester Avenue, all served by the 6 train. Bronx Community Board 9 lists Bruckner among its neighborhoods alongside Castle Hill, Parkchester, Soundview, Harding Park, Bronx River, Clason Point and Unionport. The post office splits the area in two: ZIP code 10472 lies north of the expressway and 10473 to the south, so towers on the same boulevard can carry different ZIP codes. You will also find at least one website that puts "Bruckner" down by Mott Haven in the South Bronx, because a stretch of Bruckner Boulevard runs through there too. That is not the Bruckner this page covers. Here, Bruckner means the Soundview side, between the Bronx River and the 6 train's stops at Elder Avenue, Morrison Avenue and St. Lawrence Avenue.

The reason that matters for a TV mount is that the neighborhood was built in distinct layers, and each layer left a different kind of wall. Wikipedia notes that the land east of the Bronx River belonged to Westchester County until New York City annexed it in 1895. A local history summary on ActiveRain says the area stayed relatively undeveloped until the 1940s, with most of its early housing, multi-unit rowhouses and tenement-style apartment buildings, clustered near the elevated train on Westchester Avenue. The 6 train's Morrison Avenue–Soundview station opened on May 30, 1920, then called Sound View Avenue. NYCHA developments followed from the 1950s into the 1970s. In the 1970s large high-rise rental and co-op complexes went up under the Mitchell-Lama program, and the Bruckner Expressway, which Robert Moses steered through Soundview, was finished in 1973. Walk a few blocks and you pass all of those eras. A TV in a 1920s rowhouse near the El, a TV in a 1950s public housing apartment and a TV in a 1960s co-op tower can look identical once they are hung, but getting each one safely onto its wall is a different job.

Bruckner Boulevard itself is lined with the mid-century towers that give the area its skyline. StreetEasy lists 1760 Bruckner Boulevard as a 15-story co-op built in 1958 with 408 units, and 1569 Bruckner Boulevard as a 14-story building from 1962 with 104 units, classed as an elevator cooperative. Openigloo records 1770 Bruckner Boulevard as a 14-floor building from 1958 with 102 units. CityRealty describes 2010 Bruckner Boulevard as a post-war rental completed in 1966, with 330 apartments across 14 stories, and lists the 2010–2040 Bruckner Boulevard complex under Soundview-Bruckner rentals. Buildings of this size and age bring two things to a TV job. The first is rules: co-op boards and managing agents commonly want a certificate of insurance before a contractor drills, set the hours when noisy work is allowed, and want the super to know who is in the building. The second is walls that are not what they appear to be. A mid-century tower can mix masonry outer walls, plaster partitions and later drywall from a renovation, sometimes in one room. We do not guess which one you have from the age of the building alone. We test it.

Then there is public housing, which is a large part of daily life here. A Wikipedia summary of Soundview counts nine NYCHA developments in the neighborhood and calls it one of the highest concentrations of NYCHA housing in the Bronx. Sotomayor Houses alone, bordered by Bruckner Boulevard and Watson, Soundview and Leland Avenues, has 28 buildings and nearly 1,500 apartments on 30.77 acres. In a City-Data forum thread about what NYCHA walls are made of, one user wrote that they are not drywall but heavy plaster, and another described someone who hired a man to hang a 65-inch TV on a NYCHA wall and watched him struggle to get the drill bit through. That matches what tenants tell us on the phone: the stud finder is useless, the drill skids, and the screws in the TV box are the wrong thing entirely. The NYCHA House Rules posted on nyc.gov add their own limits. Residents may install TV antennas only inside their apartments, video doorbells must be wireless, and tenants may not make holes in or alterations to the entry door, its frame, or the walls above and around it. We work inside those rules, and for tenants who would rather not drill at all, a TV on its stand is a line on our list.

The newest layer is going up right now. New York YIMBY reported in March 2026 that the facade was taking shape on 1499 Bruckner Boulevard, a seven-story, 135-unit building from J Frankl Architects and Bayrock Capital, on the block bounded by Wheeler Avenue, Bruckner Boulevard and Evergreen Avenue. The same outlet reports that permits have been filed for a 13-story mixed-use building at 831 Rosedale Avenue. And the Bronx Times reported on October 26, 2026 that construction is under way on an affordable and supportive housing building with 228 rental units at 1920 Turnbull Avenue, expected to be finished in March 2029. Many of the people moving into buildings like these will arrive through the city's Housing Connect lottery, and a City-Data thread asks exactly the question they ask us: can a large TV go on the wall of a Housing Connect apartment at all? The answer depends on the wall, which in a new building is usually drywall over some kind of framing, and on the lease and the management office, which may have rules of their own.

Who lives here shapes the calls, too. A Weichert community profile lists 37,915 residents in 12,094 households with a median age of about 29.6, and Wikipedia gives a 2020 population of 74,342 for the wider Soundview–Bruckner–Bronx River area. That is a young neighborhood of large households, which in practice means game consoles, streaming apps, a TV in the living room and another in a bedroom, and parents who want the screen up where small hands cannot pull it over. It is also a neighborhood that pays a lot to stay connected. The New York State Comptroller's 2025 broadband report found that the Bronx had the highest weighted average monthly price for broadband service in the state's analysis, and an aggregator, ISPReports, says Verizon offers fiber to 98.2 percent of Soundview homes and Optimum offers cable to 99.8 percent. When the internet bill is that high, people want every dollar of it to reach the TV, and they often ask whether they can stream everything and hand the cable box back. Local news is local here in the most literal sense: News 12 The Bronx, which began in June 1998, is based in Soundview, and Wikipedia notes that Verizon Fios began carrying News 12 on November 4, 2019.

Living beside an interstate affects the room, not just the commute. The Bruckner Expressway carries I-278 and I-95, and along much of its length Bruckner Boulevard is its service road. One resident quoted by NabeWise put it bluntly: the Bruckner makes things loud and dirty and not very good smelling. For a TV, that translates into practical choices: whether the screen faces a window full of glare and road grime, whether the soundbar can rise above traffic noise without shaking the neighbors, and whether the vents on the back of the set are going to fill with dust. Redfin rates Soundview-Bruckner as having a moderate risk of flooding, and StreetEasy lists the small 2002 building at 1520 Bruckner Boulevard in hurricane evacuation zone 4. For a ground-floor apartment, that is a reason to keep the TV on the wall and the cable box, router and power strip off the floor.

We searched for Reddit threads in r/bronx and r/AskNYC about TV mounting in Bruckner or Soundview and found nothing specific, only a Reddit quote reposted on a vendor's blog. Patch has no coverage of this topic for the area, and BKReader covers Brooklyn, so it does not apply. We found no published survey of how the interior walls of Bruckner's towers and NYCHA buildings were built. Where this page describes plaster on masonry or block, it is drawing on what residents say in the City-Data threads, on the age and type of the buildings in public records, and on what we find in buildings of those eras generally. The only reliable way to know your wall is to look at it, and that is the first thing we do.

Apartments near the El on Westchester Avenue

The oldest housing in Bruckner sits closest to the elevated tracks, in the rowhouses and tenement-style buildings the neighborhood's early history describes. In buildings of that age, the long side walls are often brick, sometimes shared with the building next door, and the interior walls between rooms are usually wood-framed and plastered. That split matters. A TV on a brick side wall goes up on masonry anchors set into the face of the brick, not the mortar joint, after test holes show whether the brick is hard or crumbly. A TV on an interior partition goes into wood studs, which in an old plastered wall are hard to find with an electronic stud finder because the lath confuses it. So we use the stud finder as a hint, drill one small probe hole behind where the bracket plate will sit, and feel what the bit meets. In multi-unit houses the wall you want may also carry pipes or wiring for the apartment above or below. We look at what is on the other side and on the floors above before we pick a spot.

Public housing apartments with heavy plaster

The City-Data descriptions of NYCHA walls, heavy plaster rather than drywall and a drill bit that barely goes in, are the clearest account we have of what a Bruckner public housing tenant is up against. In practice, a wall like that cannot be treated like drywall at all. There are no wooden studs to aim for, the plaster is dense and hard, and whatever lies behind it is likely to be masonry or block. Drywall anchors, the plastic or self-drilling kind that come in bracket boxes, do not grip in material like that; they spin or crush the plaster. What holds is a proper masonry anchor in a hole drilled with a hammer drill and a sharp carbide bit, sized to the bracket and the weight of the screen. We drill slowly at first so the plaster face does not spall around the hole, then clear the dust before setting the anchor. The plaster line on our list, $45, covers working on plaster; if the probe hole shows the anchors are going into brick behind it, we tell you which line applies before we continue. If your development's rules or your own preference rule out drilling, the TV on its stand at $137.99 is the honest alternative.

Co-op and rental towers on the boulevard

The towers at 1760, 1770, 1569 and 2010 Bruckner Boulevard were built between 1958 and 1966, and apartments in buildings of that kind often combine three materials. The outside walls, the ones with windows facing the boulevard or the side streets, are typically masonry. Walls between apartments and along corridors may be block or heavy plaster. And walls inside the apartment may have been opened, moved or covered with drywall by an owner who renovated, which in a co-op can mean one room is original and the next is new. A wall can even sound solid at one end and hollow a foot away, where an old partition meets a newer one. That is why the probe hole matters more in these buildings than anywhere else. Once we know the material, the fixing follows: masonry anchors in solid masonry, rated toggle bolts or a spread of fixings into framing in hollow drywall, and never a fixing that sits half in one material and half in another. Co-op owners should also check whether their board has rules about drilling the exterior wall at all.

New construction and lottery apartments

Buildings like 1499 Bruckner Boulevard and the 2002 building at 1520 are the ones most likely to have drywall, which is the base line on our list at $209.99 for a TV up to 55 inches over wood studs. But new mid-rise buildings are frequently framed in light-gauge metal studs rather than wood, and a metal stud only carries a TV well with fixings designed for it. We scan for the stud type, check its spacing, and choose toggles or specialty fasteners rated for the load. New apartments also come with new rules: a management office that wants to know what is going into its walls, a lease that may restrict holes, and sometimes a period when the building is still finishing common areas. Ask your management office before you book, and if they want details, we can describe exactly what will be drilled and where.

Measure from your seat

The right height for a TV is set by your eyes, not by the wall. Sit where you usually watch, look straight ahead and have someone measure from the floor to your eye level. For most people the middle of the screen should land close to that height or a little above it. Then measure from the seat to the wall. In the compact living rooms of many tower apartments, a very large screen can end up closer than is comfortable, and in a bedroom, the view from the pillow is lower than the view from a sofa. Send us both numbers and the screen size you are considering, and we will tell you what we see in rooms like yours.

Look at the other side of the wall

In any building, the wall you want might carry a pipe, a wire or a chimney flue. Look at what is on the other side. A kitchen or bathroom behind or above the TV wall means plumbing may run through it. An outlet or switch nearby means wiring. In public housing and tower apartments, the wall between your apartment and the corridor or a neighbor may be a fire-separation wall, and building rules may treat it differently from walls inside your apartment. We scan before drilling, but your notes about what is next door tell us where to be most careful, and in some buildings they tell us to choose a different wall entirely.

Clear a path and a landing spot

A TV over 55 inches comes in a large, awkward box, and it has to stay upright on the way in. Clear a path from the door to the room, move breakable things off the console, and leave a space on the floor in front of the wall about as wide as the TV where we can lay blankets. If you have pets, a closed room for them helps more than anything. If the building wants the elevator padded or booked, let us know the rule and the time, and we will plan around it.

On masonry anchors in old plaster. The most common mistake we see in Bruckner apartments is an anchor set into plaster alone, without reaching the solid material behind it. Plaster on masonry can be half an inch to more than an inch thick, and a short anchor that stops in the plaster holds only as well as the plaster's grip on the wall, which in an old building is not much. We measure the plaster depth from the probe hole and use anchors long enough to seat fully in the masonry, with the bracket clamped against the wall face so the plaster is not crushed. When the probe hole shows the masonry behind is hollow block rather than brick or concrete, we place anchors where they meet the solid webs of the block, or use fixings designed for hollow masonry. That decision cannot be made from a photo; it is made with the drill in hand.

On renovated co-op walls. Owners in Bruckner Boulevard co-ops sometimes renovate a kitchen or open up a living room, and the new walls may be drywall on metal studs while the original walls beside them are plaster on masonry. Where the two meet, there can be a hollow gap that a knock test picks up as a change in sound. A bracket straddling that joint is fixed into two different materials, and one side will always be weaker. We find the joint, keep the bracket entirely on one side of it, and if the TV has to sit across it, we use a bracket wide enough to reach solid fixing on both sides or a backing approach that spreads the load. If you are renovating and the walls are open, tell your contractor where the TV will go so solid blocking and a recessed box for the in-wall kit can go in while it is easy.

On public housing and the door wall. The NYCHA House Rules say tenants may not make holes in or alterations to the entry door, its frame, or the walls above and around it. A TV rarely belongs there anyway, but in small living rooms where the entry opens straight into the main room, the wall beside the door can look like the obvious spot. We avoid it. We also avoid any wall where a hole might interfere with building systems, and we keep the job inside your apartment. If you are unsure what your development allows, the management office is the authority, not us, and a TV on its stand avoids the question altogether.

Bruckner TV Installation FAQ

Our internet bill is already high. Can the new TV run on streaming apps alone so we can hand the cable box back?
+

Often, yes, as long as the channels you watch are available through apps and your internet is strong where the TV sits. The NYS Comptroller found the Bronx had the highest weighted average monthly broadband price in its analysis, so many households want to get full value from the connection they already pay for. A setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks, and covers signing in to the TV, connecting WiFi and setting up the apps. Check what your provider charges or credits before returning any equipment.

Can you make sure News 12 The Bronx and our other local channels come up on the new TV before you leave?
+

Yes. Checking the channels you actually watch is part of the handover. News 12 The Bronx is based in Soundview, and Wikipedia notes that Verizon Fios began carrying it on November 4, 2019, so whether it comes through a set-top box or an app depends on your provider and plan. We connect the box or app, find the channel, and show you how to get back to it with your remote.

Our first-floor window faces Bruckner Boulevard. Can the TV go where people on the sidewalk cannot see it?
+

Usually. A screen on the wall that the street window looks straight at is visible from outside at night, so we look for a side wall or the wall beside the window, where the screen faces into the room instead. That spot also avoids glare from passing headlights and daylight. We tape the outline first so you can check it from the sidewalk before anything is drilled.

Can a screen hang in our storefront window on Westchester Avenue so people walking under the El see it?
+

Yes, though a window screen is a different job from a wall screen. It may hang from the ceiling or a header above the glass rather than a wall, it faces direct sunlight and the flicker of light through the El, and its cables need a tidy route back to a power point and the media player. We look at what is above the window, how bright the afternoon is, and the sight line from the sidewalk before quoting it.

The only outlet in our bedroom is down by the floor. Does the TV need a new outlet up high?
+

Not usually. On a drywall wall, a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit for $169 carries power from near the low outlet up to a recessed plate behind the TV, which is the only proper way to hide power inside a wall. On masonry or in a rental where the wall cannot be opened, a paintable cord cover for $59 runs the cord down the surface. A brand-new circuit would be an electrician's job.

Is it safe to mount the TV on the inside of an outside wall in a brick tower apartment?
+

Generally yes, and a solid masonry outer wall can be one of the strongest walls in the apartment once anchors are seated properly in it. Two checks come first. Co-op owners should confirm their board has no rule against drilling the exterior wall, and we look for signs of water coming in, such as stains or soft plaster, because a damp wall holds anchors poorly and is hard on electronics.

Our soundbar sits on the console now. Is it worth mounting it under the TV?
+

It depends on the console. If the soundbar blocks the bottom of the screen or the TV's remote sensor, or the console is low and the sound is aimed at your knees, mounting it under the screen helps. Placing it is $79 and mounting it is $129, because a mounted bar gets its own anchors in the wall instead of hanging from the TV. In a rental where every hole counts, placing it is the simpler choice.

If the plaster in our apartment turns out to have brick behind it, which wall charge applies?
+

We price the wall we actually fix into, and we tell you before continuing. The plaster line is $45 and the brick or stone line is $65. The probe hole behind the bracket plate shows what is under the plaster, and if the anchors are going into brick, the technician shows you and names the line before drilling further. If the material is block or concrete, that is a separate line on the full Bronx list, also named before any work continues.

Do we have to be home for the whole visit, or can the super let you into our co-op?
+

Someone who can make decisions needs to be there at least for the start, when we agree the wall, the height and the position, because that cannot be undone once drilled. After that, if your building allows the super to give access and you are comfortable with it, we can finish and test without you in the room. Follow your building's rules on contractor access and tell us the arrangement when booking.

What happens to the small test hole if we decide on a different spot after all?
+

The test hole is small, about the size of a pencil, and we drill it where the bracket plate would cover it. If you move the TV elsewhere, it is left as a neat hole that fills easily with a dab of setting compound and a touch of paint. That is one reason we tape the outline on the wall and agree it with you before the first full-size hole goes in.

Can you turn on captions and bigger menus for a relative who has trouble hearing the TV?
+

Yes. Most smart TVs and set-top boxes have closed captions, larger text, high-contrast menus and dialogue settings buried in their accessibility menus, and turning them on is part of a setup visit. If the TV is already installed, a setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks. We show your relative how to switch captions on and off with their own remote.

We want a TV in a back bedroom with no cable jack. Will WiFi reach it through these thick walls?
+

Sometimes not well. Heavy plaster, block and masonry walls weaken WiFi more than drywall, so a bedroom two walls away from the router can buffer even on a fast plan. We check the signal where the TV will sit, and if it is weak we tell you the options, such as moving the router or running a wired connection, before the TV goes up.
