The Neighborhood

Why Rockland County Condo Communities Need Security Built for Townhouse Courts, River Buildings and Constant Resales

Rockland is a condo county that rarely looks like one. Drive Route 304 north of Nanuet or the back roads of Valley Cottage and Congers and the condominiums are rows of two-story townhouses on private loops, each with its own front door, a shared pool behind the clubhouse and sometimes a pair of tennis courts at the far end. Go east to the Hudson and the picture flips: elevator buildings in Nyack, Piermont and the Village of Haverstraw with garages, lobbies and river views. A third group sits in between, the 55-and-over developments around Stony Point and Pearl River, where an easy entrance matters as much as a locked one. Condominium security in Rockland County has to work for all three.

The numbers explain why boards are thinking about it now. OneKey MLS counted 543 condo sales in Rockland County in the twelve months through October 2026, at a 12-month median price of $475,000, up 8.2 percent from a year earlier. The same report showed 193 condos for sale in October, against 114 a year before. In plain terms, units change hands constantly, prices are climbing and listings are piling up. Every sale is a set of fobs, remotes, app accounts and garage tags that has to be shut off, and every listing brings agents and buyers through the gates and lobbies. A condo security system that cannot keep up with turnover becomes a list of strangers who can still get in.

Three kinds of condo property in one small county

Rockland covers just under 200 square miles and is the smallest county by area in the state outside New York City, yet its condo stock is varied. The most common type is the townhouse or garden condominium from the 1970s through the 2000s: wood-frame units in clusters, owners entering through their own doors, the association owning the roads, lawns, pool, clubhouse and courts. Listings across the county on the day this page was written described community pools, clubhouses and tennis courts in Valley Cottage and New City complexes, a gated community in Nanuet and gated parking with garages in Piermont. For property managers covering more than one Rockland County association, see property management security in Rockland County.

The second type is the riverfront elevator condo. Hudson Valley Magazine described one Village of Haverstraw development in 2019 as single-level residences with river views, where the first phase had two buildings and sixteen units and "at completion, the final phases will result in 40 total units in five buildings." Amenities listed included two swimming pools, a fitness center and an indoor basketball court. That is a typical Rockland river condo: several mid-size buildings built in phases, each with a lobby and garage, sharing amenities. The third type is the age-restricted community, where listings in Stony Point and Pearl River mention 55-plus rules, billiards rooms, barbecue areas and private elevators.

What resale turnover does to a credential list

In a building where owners stay for twenty years, a fob list can be maintained by memory. In a Rockland complex where dozens of units sell every year, it cannot. Sellers leave the closing with their fobs in a kitchen drawer, renters get copies from owners, and the cleaning service has a remote that nobody remembers issuing. When the board finally audits the list, it usually finds more active credentials than there are residents. The fix is not complicated: a system that ties every credential to a unit, lets the managing agent shut off a unit's whole set in one step, and keeps a record of who issued what. Most of our condo security installation work in Rockland County starts with that list, not with the hardware.

Five towns, eighteen villages and no cities

Rockland has five towns, Clarkstown, Haverstraw, Orangetown, Ramapo and Stony Point, and eighteen incorporated villages, but no cities. That shapes which rules a condo board deals with. New York City's Multiple Dwelling Law, with its intercom requirements for larger apartment buildings, applies to cities of 325,000 or more; the state's Multiple Residence Law covers "all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population and to all towns and villages," which includes every community in the county. Day to day, a condo in a village such as Nyack, Suffern or Haverstraw answers to that village's building department, and a condo in an unincorporated hamlet such as Nanuet, Congers or Valley Cottage answers to its town. Since February 2022 the county's own Office of Buildings and Codes has handled code enforcement in the Village of Spring Valley, under a state order, News 12 reported. We confirm the jurisdiction at the first visit so the plan fits the right office.

Storms, outages and the river

Weather is part of every Rockland proposal. Orange & Rockland Utilities reported in July 2026 that storms beginning July 1 had affected more than 47,000 of its customers, and the Rockland County Times counted 6,466 county customers still dark late on July 4, with one New City neighborhood out for over 36 hours. Along the river, Rockland Daily reported in January 2024 that Piermont saw its worst tidal flooding since Hurricane Sandy, and in 2012 the Rockland County Times wrote that residents near the Hudson "got the worst of Sandy." For a condo, that means battery backup sized for long outages, controllers and recorders kept off garage floors that take water, and readers that come back on their own when the power does.

What Rockland condo boards tell us goes wrong

The calls repeat. A pool gate reader stops accepting fobs in June, a week before opening day. The original lobby intercom in a river building dials a phone line the association dropped years ago. Packages disappear from shared vestibules; Daily Voice reported in April 2026 that packages taken from several buildings within a Haverstraw complex were later recovered in a search. The clubhouse door is propped open by whoever booked the party room. Real estate agents ask for a code to show a unit, and the code they get opens every door in the complex. A recorder that the developer installed has been overwriting a failed drive for a year. None of these needs a whole new system on day one, but each is a sign the board should have a plan.

Rockland County condo facts that shape the work

  • Turnover: OneKey MLS logged 543 closed condo sales in the county over the year ending October 2026; the median for that year was $475,000, an 8.2 percent rise, and October's count of condos on the market reached 193, up from 114.
  • Where the listings are: condo listings on Redfin on November 26, 2026 were spread across Pomona, Valley Cottage, Spring Valley, Nanuet, Monsey, Suffern, New City, Congers, Piermont, Pearl River, Stony Point, Haverstraw, Tappan, West Nyack and Bardonia, at a median list price of about $449,000.
  • Housing: 109,999 housing units and an owner-occupancy rate of 67.9 percent (U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts, Rockland County).
  • Who owns the equipment: unless a declaration says otherwise, New York counts "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" among a condo's common elements, alongside its entrances, community facilities and parking (Real Property Law §339-e); bylaws may set aside reserves for "major and minor maintenance, repairs, additions, improvements, replacements" (§339-v).
  • Pools: state law expects the gate in a swimming pool enclosure to close and latch by itself, and to be secured with a key, combination or other child-proof lock whenever nobody is using or supervising the pool (Executive Law §378).
  • Owner alarms in Clarkstown: the town's Burglar Alarm Device Control Law requires alarm users to obtain an alarm user permit from the Police Department (Town of Clarkstown Code, Chapter 91).
  • Who does what: the county defines electrical contracting as wiring for light, heat or power, or for signaling systems operating on 50 volts or more, under a master electrician (Rockland County Code, Chapter 250); security and fire alarm installers are licensed by the state (General Business Law §69-m).

Building intercoms: repair first, then a replacement plan

The condo intercom is where most Rockland calls start. In a river building it is the lobby entry panel that calls each apartment; in a garden complex it may be a separate audio panel at every building door, installed by the developer and patched by three different companies since. When one goes quiet, our first visit is a repair, and our intercom repair service in Rockland covers the panel, the apartment stations, the power supply and the door release. We tell the board honestly whether the fault is a part or a pattern.

When it is a pattern, the conversation turns to condo intercom replacement. The usual signs are parts that are no longer made, repairs every few months, stations that hum or cut out, and an older system that depends on a landline the association wants to cancel. The options run from a like-for-like video upgrade that reuses the existing two-wire or multi-wire cable, to an IP panel that calls owners' phones through an app, to a hybrid where owners choose an in-unit screen or the app. Some modernization projects never enter a unit; others need access to every apartment on a line. We say which one applies after the survey, not before.

Replacement is a board decision, and boards need paper. We write a scope that a condo board can put out as an RFP or compare against other bids line by line: every entrance, every station, the cable plan, the programming, the training and the warranty. Budget figures for building-wide work come from the published ranges on our Rockland County intercom installation page, which lists video intercom for a 4-to-8-unit building at $1,200 to $3,500, multi-unit buildings at $1,200 to $5,000 and IP smartphone systems at $2,500 to $8,000 and up for larger buildings. Boards and HOAs can also read our separate condo intercom replacement guide.

Community entrances on private roads

Many Rockland townhouse condos own the road that runs past the units, and some have a call box or keypad at the turn-in from the town road. These are often the oldest pieces of equipment on the property: telephone-entry boxes that dial out over a copper line and a directory nobody has updated since the developer left. A modern call box can ring owners' cell phones or an app over a cellular or network connection, keep the visitor directory current from the owner list, and log every gate opening by unit. Where the entrance has a gate operator that still works, we connect the new entry equipment to it rather than replace the motor; the gate company keeps responsibility for the operator itself.

Not every community wants a gate, and we do not push one. A camera and call box at the entrance, with fobbed doors at the buildings and amenities, is often the better fit for a complex that sits on a busy road, gets frequent deliveries and has owners who would rather not wait behind visitors.

Door buzzers and entry door hardware

Smaller condos, including converted houses and two- and three-story buildings in the river villages, still run on audio buzzers. When a buzzer stops releasing the door, a station goes silent or the transformer burns out, our door buzzer repair crews in Rockland County handle it, starting with the $250 visit. That page lists typical part ranges, such as $250 to $525 for an apartment station and $450 to $950 for an electric strike with door alignment. A buzzer system is a good candidate for an upgrade only when the board wants video, app calling or a record of entries; otherwise a repaired buzzer is often the right call.

Fobs, readers and a single owner list

The most useful thing a Rockland condo can buy is often not a camera but a credential list that matches reality. Our access control installation work in Rockland County puts readers on building doors, the clubhouse, the pool gate, the tennis or pickleball courts, storage rooms and the maintenance shed, and ties them to one list organized by unit. When a unit sells, the managing agent turns off that unit's fobs, remotes and app access together. Encrypted credentials replace the older 125 kHz fobs that can be copied at a kiosk, and the board decides who can issue new ones. Published Rockland figures on that page put a single door or gate at $1,700 to $5,000 and a multi-door property system at $4,500 to $15,000.

Pools, clubhouses, courts and guest suites

Amenities are where condo rules meet hardware. A pool gate reader can follow the season the board sets and the hours posted at the gate, while the gate keeps the self-closing, self-latching hardware state law requires. The clubhouse can open on a schedule for the fitness room and only for the owner who booked the party room. Court gates can open at dawn and lock at dusk. Guest passes can expire on their own. Behind all of it is a record of who used each door and when, which settles most disputes before they reach a board meeting.

Garages and elevators in the river buildings

Rockland's riverfront condos are where residents arrive by car and walk straight from the garage into the building. The garage door, the door from the garage into the building and the elevator are the security points that matter most. A reader at each, tied to the same owner list, means a person who drives in behind a resident still cannot reach the residential floors. Where a building wants floor-by-floor control, our elevator access control installation in Rockland County covers it; that page lists Level 1 fob, card, code or intercom-station readers from $1,950 per car and Level 2 face, fingerprint or iris readers from $3,250 per car, with the elevator maintenance company handling its own connection.

Cameras, briefly

Cameras matter, but they are a supporting piece of a condo plan. Most Rockland associations want coverage of the entrance, the parking courts, the mail kiosk or package area, the pool gate and the clubhouse door, plus the garage ramps in a river building. Our dedicated condo security camera installation page for Rockland County covers condo cameras, and the camera side of multifamily buildings is covered on our apartment building security camera installation page for Rockland County. What we ask every board before quoting cameras is simple: who will watch them, how long should recordings be kept, and who is allowed to export a clip?

Packages and mail areas

Package handling is a real security question in Rockland condos, not a convenience issue. River buildings receive deliveries in shared vestibules and mailrooms, and townhouse communities often have a central mail kiosk or a clubhouse alcove where carriers leave boxes too big for the slots. The Haverstraw case Daily Voice reported in April 2026, where packages were taken from several buildings in one complex, is the pattern boards worry about. The practical steps are a controlled door into whatever room holds packages, a camera that sees faces at that door rather than the tops of heads, and a delivery credential for carriers that works only in the vestibule. Where a board wants smart package lockers, we install the access control and network they need and integrate with the locker provider; the lockers and their service come from that provider.

Alarms for owners and for the clubhouse

Individual owners often ask us for an alarm in their own townhouse or apartment, and associations sometimes want one on the clubhouse or management office. We install those through our alarm installation service in Rockland County, with central-station monitoring in two tiers: $30 a month for keypad-only monitoring or $45 a month with the phone app. In Clarkstown, the town code requires alarm users to get an alarm user permit from the police department, and we explain that step when we set up the monitoring account.

Fire alarm work, within our scope

Rockland is within our fire alarm service area. Condo buildings that are required to have a fire alarm system follow a sequence of their own: drawings from a design professional, filing with the town, village or county office that enforces the state Uniform Code, installation by a licensed installer and a witnessed acceptance test. We cover that process, without pricing, on our fire alarm installation page for Rockland County. On a security project, the practical overlap is that electrically locked doors must release when the fire alarm activates, which we design for on every job.

Cable, networks and backup power

Townhouse complexes are long and low, so cable travels through attics, crawl spaces, buried conduit and sometimes wireless links between buildings. We keep the security equipment on its own network, separate from any resident or clubhouse Wi-Fi, and size battery backup for the controllers, locks and recorders to ride out the kind of multi-day outages the county saw in July 2026. We do not do electrical work: low-voltage wiring and door hardware are ours, and any new high-voltage circuit or outlet is the association's electrician.

Guards, patrols and "virtual doorman" offers

We do not supply guards, run a patrol or operate a virtual doorman or remote concierge service. What we do is install and maintain the intercoms, cameras and readers that a guard company or a remote-monitoring provider would use, and connect our equipment to a provider the board already has. If an association is considering one of those services, we can tell them what equipment it needs and whether what they have will work with it.

Service calls, maintenance and support

After installation, phone support is free. A visit for a problem is the $250 service call. Boards that want regular inspections can schedule them, usually in spring before the pool opens and in fall before the first storms, when we check batteries, clean camera housings, test drives and update firmware. Workmanship is covered for the 30 days stated on our warranty page; longer coverage of a year or more is a paid option offered when the system is sold, and manufacturers cover their own products on their own terms.

Who calls us

In Rockland we hear from volunteer board presidents in self-managed townhouse communities, from managing agents who handle several associations along Route 59, from developers finishing the last phase of a river project and from individual owners who want something done at their own door. We work with all of them. For decisions that belong to the association, we deal with whoever the board names as its contact, and we keep owners' requests separate from the association's account.

Building Specialists

Condo Security for Rockland County's Architecture: Six Property Types

The same fob reader behaves differently on a vinyl-sided townhouse in Congers and on a concrete garage wall in Haverstraw. These are the six kinds of condominium property we work in most across the county, and what each one asks of a security system.

1

Townhouse loops with private roads

Owner front doors, association-owned roads, a clubhouse, pool and courts.

2

Garden-style condo clusters

Two- and three-story buildings with shared stair halls and several entry doors.

3

Riverfront elevator buildings

Lobbies, garages and amenity rooms along the Hudson, often built in phases.

4

55-and-over communities

Age-restricted sections, active clubhouses and residents who need simple entry.

5

Golf and country-club settings

Condo units beside shared recreation that non-residents may also use.

6

Small village conversions

Converted houses and walk-ups with a handful of owners and one front door.

Townhouse loops with private roads: the association owns the edges

In a townhouse condo, each owner's front door is usually the owner's business, and the association's security job lives at the edges: the entrance, the roads, the mail kiosk, the clubhouse, the pool fence and the courts. That changes the shape of the system. Instead of one lobby panel with a long directory, the board needs a handful of controlled points spread over a large site, often hundreds of feet apart, and one list of owners that works at all of them. Cable runs are long, buildings are wood-frame and there is rarely a central equipment room, so controllers end up in the clubhouse utility closet or a weatherproof cabinet. We plan the cable or wireless path first, because it drives most of the cost.

Garden-style condo clusters: many doors, finished halls

Garden condos share stair halls between four to twelve units per building, each hall with its own entry door. Some still have the original audio intercom at each door, wired back through the basement or crawl space to apartment stations. The decision for the board is usually whether to keep a separate panel at every building or move to fobbed doors with one video panel at a central point. Running new cable without opening finished hallway walls is possible in most of these buildings through basements, attics and existing chases, but we confirm it building by building, because two buildings in the same complex can be framed differently.

Riverfront elevator buildings: phases, garages and water

The river condos in Nyack, Piermont and Haverstraw tend to be groups of mid-size buildings rather than single towers, and several were built in phases over years. That makes them half city building and half campus. Each building has a lobby panel and a garage, and the complex shares pools, a fitness room and outdoor spaces. The board's best move is to put every building on the same platform as it is added, so one credential works everywhere and one person manages it. The river adds a second concern: garage levels and low lobbies near the water can take flooding, so we mount controllers, power supplies and recorders high, in rooms above the flood line where the building allows it.

55-and-over communities: easy to use, hard to misuse

Age-restricted condos in Rockland usually have busy clubhouses and residents with a wide range of comfort with technology. Some owners will use an app happily; others want a large-button station in the kitchen and a fob on the key ring, and nothing else. Caregivers, aides and family members come and go on schedules. A good system here offers more than one way to answer the door, readable displays at the entrance, and visitor and caregiver credentials that expire when the visit or the arrangement ends. We avoid anything that asks a resident to learn a new routine every year.

Golf and country-club settings: shared grounds, separate rules

Some condo communities share their setting with recreation they do not fully control, such as a golf club or a clubhouse run by a separate entity; one Pomona listing we reviewed sat on a country-club road with community clubhouse amenities. Where that is the case, the residential buildings and roads still belong to the condo, but outsiders may have a legitimate reason to be on part of the property. The security plan draws a line: open where the public is welcome, controlled at the residential buildings, the condo-only pool and the parking courts. Cameras at the boundary points help the board and the club sort out incidents without either one blaming the other.

Small village conversions: few owners, one door

Nyack, Piermont, Suffern and Haverstraw have small condos in converted houses and older walk-ups, sometimes with only three to eight units and no managing agent. Here the right system is modest: a reliable entry panel or video doorbell station, a strike that releases cleanly, and perhaps a fob reader so owners can stop copying keys. We take these jobs. The owners usually handle the paperwork themselves, and we keep the proposal short enough to approve in one meeting.

What changes with materials and methods

Vinyl and wood siding need mounting blocks so readers and cameras sit flat and the cable entry is sealed. Stone or brick entrance walls get anchors rated for masonry and conduit that does not collect water. Concrete garages need surface conduit and fixtures rated for damp, cold and salt brought in on car tires in winter. Older garden buildings sometimes have plaster on lath, where we drill carefully and patch our own holes; painting is left to the association. Wherever we bury cable across lawns or under walkways, it goes in conduit, and the utility mark-out comes first.

Commercial Condo Security

Clubhouses, Mixed-Use Buildings and Shared Grounds: Business-Side Condo Security in Rockland County

Some of what a Rockland condo association runs is closer to a small business than a home. The clubhouse has staff and outside vendors, a mixed-use building has a commercial owner downstairs, and a developer may still be selling units from an office on site. Each of those needs its own access rules inside the same system.

Mixed-use condos in the village centers

Village downtowns such as Nyack's Main Street and the area around Pearl River's station have buildings with shops or offices on the ground floor and condo units above. The commercial owner needs customers to come and go during business hours, while residents need their entrance, mailboxes and stairs closed to the public. The solution is separate entrances or, where they share a door, a schedule that keeps the shared door controlled outside business hours and a second reader between the commercial space and the residential stair. Who pays for which hardware depends on the declaration, and we price the pieces so the board and the commercial owner can split them.

The clubhouse as a workplace

A busy clubhouse has a part-time manager, cleaners, fitness equipment technicians, caterers for events and the occasional contractor. Each group gets credentials that match their schedule, so the cleaner's fob works Tuesday and Friday mornings and the caterer's code works only during the booked event. The office where owner files and keys are kept gets its own reader and its own record. If the clubhouse has a front desk, its staff can answer the community call box during staffed hours.

Developers still selling units

In phased river projects and newer townhouse sections, the developer often keeps a sales office and models open while owners move in nearby. Owners want the buildings they live in controlled; the developer needs agents and buyers to reach the models. We set up the developer's credentials as a separate group the board can see and, at turnover, remove in one step. Boards should insist that every administrator password and account moves to the association at turnover, and we document them so it happens.

Landscapers, plow crews and service vendors

Rockland condos spend the year with outside crews on site: landscapers from spring to fall, plow contractors through winter, pool companies in summer and pest, elevator and fire alarm inspectors on schedules of their own. Vendor credentials tied to the season and the days of service keep them from becoming permanent keys. Where vendors need the maintenance shed or the pool equipment room, those doors get readers too, so the board knows when they were opened.

Parking courts, carports and detached garages

Many townhouse condos park cars in open courts or rows of detached garages owned by individual units. Cameras here should cover the drive aisles and the entry into the court, not the inside of anyone's garage. Lighting often matters more than the camera, and we will say so. For detached garage blocks with a shared side door, a reader on that door keeps out people who would otherwise walk in behind an open overhead door.

  • Local approvals: a new pedestal, pole or entrance sign may need review from the village or town building department; we identify the items that might, and the board confirms with the office that has jurisdiction.
  • Electrically locked doors: locked egress doors must release on fire alarm activation and loss of power where the Uniform Code requires it; the 2025 New York State Uniform Code took effect on December 31, 2025.
  • Pool gates: keep the self-closing, self-latching hardware; a reader and electric lock are added alongside it (Executive Law §378).
  • Privacy: no cameras inside restrooms, showers or similar rooms (General Business Law §395-b); no audio recording without legal advice, because recording conversations can raise eavesdropping issues under the Penal Law.
  • Not New York City: the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law for smart-access buildings is a New York City local law and does not govern Rockland condos, though its principles are a sensible guide for a board writing its own policy.
  • Licensing: we work under New York State license #12000287431. Low-voltage security work is ours; new circuits of 50 volts or more belong to a licensed electrician under Rockland County Code Chapter 250.
  • Insurance: proof of coverage is produced when the association awards us the work, never with a bid or quote.

Sales, renovations and management changes

With so many units listed for sale, how do we let showing agents into the building and amenities without giving out a master code?

Give each listing its own temporary access instead of a shared code. Most current access and intercom platforms let the managing agent create a code or mobile credential tied to one unit, valid only for set days and hours, and limited to the doors a showing needs: the entrance, the building door and perhaps the clubhouse. When the listing closes or expires, the credential stops working by itself. Every use is logged against that listing, so if something goes missing after an open house the board knows which access was used and when. With 193 condos on the market in Rockland in October 2026, that kind of control is worth setting up before the spring season.

An owner is renovating for three months. How should the contractor's crew get in each day?

Issue the contractor a credential that matches the job, not a copy of the owner's fob. The board or agent can set it to work on weekdays during the hours the house rules allow for construction, only at the doors the crew needs, and to end on the date the alteration agreement gives for completion. If the work runs long, the agent extends it; if the crew leaves the door propped or works outside hours, the log shows it. This also keeps the owner from being blamed for a crew that comes in on a Sunday.

We're switching management companies. What has to be handed over so the new agent can run the access system?

Four things: the administrator logins for the access, intercom and camera software; the current owner and credential list, exported in a format the new agent can import; the account details for any cloud subscription, including who pays it; and the documentation of what is installed where. The association should own every one of those accounts, not the outgoing agent. When we install a system, the board gets all of it in writing. When we take over a system someone else installed, recovering these items is the first thing we do, and it is easier while the outgoing agent is still cooperating.

Prospective buyers keep asking what security the community has. What can the board safely share?

Share what helps a buyer decide and nothing that helps someone get in. A short summary is fine: the entrances are controlled by fob, visitors use a video intercom, the pool and clubhouse are on schedules, cameras cover common areas and the recordings are kept for a set period. Leave out door codes, camera positions in detail, recorder locations, the names of administrators and any weak spots the board is still fixing. Many associations put that summary in the resale package so the managing agent gives the same answer every time.

Staff, parking and the grounds

Our longtime superintendent is retiring, and only he knows how the system works. What should we capture before he leaves?

Walk the property with him and write it down. The essentials are where every controller, power supply, recorder and network switch is located, which keys open those closets, the logins he uses, which cable runs he has repaired and where, which devices give trouble, and any workarounds he has been doing without telling anyone. We can join that walk, label the equipment as we go and turn his knowledge into a document the board keeps. It is far cheaper than rediscovering it during the next failure.

Non-residents have been using the electric vehicle chargers in our parking area. Can access control stop that?

Usually. Many chargers have their own card or app authorization, and the simplest fix is turning that on and issuing cards only to owners. Where the chargers sit inside a garage, the garage reader already limits who reaches them; in an open lot, a camera at the charging spaces helps the board enforce its rules. Installing or rewiring the chargers themselves is electrical work for the association's electrician, but we handle the access and camera side and make sure owners' charger cards match the unit list.

Before you trench cable across our lawn, who checks for buried gas, water and irrigation lines?

The excavator does, through the state one-call system. New York's Code Rule 753 requires notice to the one-call center at least two but not more than ten working days before digging, and the utilities then mark their lines. Lines that belong to the association, such as irrigation, site lighting or a feed to the pool house, are not the utilities' to mark, so we ask the board or its landscaper for any drawings or knowledge of those before we dig. Where a trench is not worth the disruption, a wireless link between buildings may be the better answer.

Is a wireless link between two of our buildings reliable through heavy rain and snow?

A properly designed point-to-point link is reliable in Rockland weather. The keys are a clear line of sight between the two antennas, mounting that does not move in wind, equipment rated for outdoor temperatures, and enough signal margin that rain fade does not drop the connection. Trees are the usual problem, because a path that is clear in March can be blocked by leaves in June. We survey the path in person, and if it is marginal we say so and price the trench as the alternative.

Can the pool's chemical storage room have its own lock and record, separate from the pool gate?

Yes, and it should. The chemical room is a safety space that only the pool company and maintenance staff need, while the pool gate serves every owner during the season. A reader on that door with its own short list of credentials keeps owners and children out and gives the board a record of each entry. If the pool company changes, their credentials are removed without touching anyone else's.

Budgets, owners and the board

Can your proposal give each device an expected service life we can drop into our reserve study?

Yes. Our proposals list each entrance and device on its own line, and on request we add the manufacturer's warranty term and a typical service life based on our experience with that type of equipment, along with notes on what tends to fail first, such as batteries, drives and outdoor readers. That gives the board's reserve specialist a component list instead of one lump sum. Service life is an estimate, not a promise, and weather exposure in Rockland shortens it for outdoor gear.

Some owners avoid operating electronic devices on the Sabbath. Are there entry options that work for them?

There are several, and the board chooses. An entry door can be scheduled to unlock or stay on a timed setting for set hours, a mechanical key override can stay on the door, and some communities keep one door on a manual lock during those hours. Each option trades convenience against security, so the board decides which doors, which hours and how they are announced to owners. We program what the board approves and document it so the setting is not changed by accident.

Can the freight or passenger elevator be held for a move-in without someone riding it all day?

Partly. In buildings with elevator access control, a mover's credential can be set to reach only the moving owner's floor and the lobby or garage, for the hours of the move. Holding a car on independent service is part of the elevator's own controls and is handled with the elevator maintenance company, not our equipment. We can work with them so the move schedule, the floor access and the padding all line up on the same day.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems in Rockland County

Chatbot answers and the big cost-guide sites, Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr among them, tend to describe condo security with numbers and habits taken from somewhere else. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, and what we actually find on Rockland County condo properties.

1. "Suburban condos just need a video doorbell on each unit"

This advice treats a condo like a row of single-family homes. In a Rockland townhouse community each owner may well add a doorbell, but the association's exposure is somewhere else: the clubhouse, the pool, the mail kiosk, the parking courts and the entrance.

Doorbells also create a patchwork the board cannot manage. Each owner's device reports to that owner's phone, records to that owner's account and disappears when the unit sells. None of it tells the board who used the pool gate after hours or which fobs are still live.

What works is a common-area system the association owns, with owners free to add their own devices if the declaration and house rules allow it. The two can coexist; one cannot replace the other.

2. "Your installer needs a county electrical license"

Some summaries tell boards to hire an electrician for intercom and access work. Rockland County Code Chapter 250 defines electrical contracting as wiring for light, heat or power, or for signaling systems operating on 50 volts or more, under the supervision of a master electrician.

Intercoms, readers, cameras and alarm devices run at low voltage, and the state licenses security and fire alarm installers under General Business Law §69-m. We hold New York State license #12000287431 for that work.

Where a project needs a new 120-volt circuit, for example for a gate operator or a recorder closet with no outlet, that piece goes to the association's licensed electrician. Good proposals say which is which, so nobody is surprised.

3. "A building intercom costs about the same everywhere"

HomeAdvisor gives a national "normal range" of $1,200 to $8,500 for intercom installation. A range that wide is not wrong, but it does not help a board budget, because it mixes a single-family doorbell with a fifty-unit building.

Our Rockland County intercom installation page breaks it down: $1,200 to $3,500 for video intercom in a 4-to-8-unit building, $1,200 to $5,000 for multi-unit buildings and $2,500 to $8,000 and up for IP smartphone systems in larger buildings. The actual number depends on entrances, units, wiring and whether owners want stations or the app.

For a townhouse community, the cost driver is distance. A clubhouse four hundred feet from the entrance call box costs more to connect than the call box itself. That is why we walk the property before quoting.

4. "Battery and Wi-Fi cameras are storm-proof"

AI answers often recommend wireless cameras because they keep working when the power goes out. That holds only until the router loses power, the battery runs down or the cellular backup fails, which in a long outage is a matter of hours.

Rockland's July 2026 storms left more than 47,000 Orange & Rockland customers affected, and some neighborhoods went more than a day and a half without power. Battery cameras in a common area also need someone to recharge them, which in a condo usually means nobody.

For a community system, wired cameras on a recorder and switch with properly sized battery backup, or on a generator circuit, keep recording through an outage. Wireless cameras still have a place at a remote pool house or court where cable is impractical.

5. "Waterproof equipment is fine near the river"

An outdoor rating such as IP65 or IP66 means a device resists rain and jets of water. It does not mean the device survives sitting in floodwater, and a power supply or controller at garage-floor level certainly will not.

Piermont's tidal flooding in January 2024 and the damage along the river during Sandy are reminders that some Rockland condo garages and lobbies take water. Placement matters more than the rating on the box.

We mount controllers, power supplies and recorders as high as the building allows, keep cable entries sealed and protected, and plan an inspection after any flood before the board relies on the system again.

6. "A contractor based in the city won't serve Rockland reliably"

The concern is fair in general: a crew that has to cross the Hudson may be tempted to treat a Rockland call as an afterthought. Distance is real, and the Mario M. Cuomo Bridge is not always fast.

We book every Rockland request within 2 hours, schedule seven days a week, and favor early-day visits so the crew is on site before traffic builds. Work is first come, first served once the job is paid, and every appointment comes with a two-hour arrival window that the crew keeps.

We also avoid promising what nobody can deliver. We do not promise same-day work or round-the-clock service, and we would rather give a board an honest date than a fast one that slips.

7. "Common-area cameras can record audio as long as there's a sign"

Some chatbot answers suggest a posted notice covers any camera feature. Recording conversations is a different matter from recording video, and audio recording in common areas can raise eavesdropping issues under New York's Penal Law.

Video in hallways, lobbies, garages and grounds is ordinary condo practice, with no cameras inside restrooms, showers or similar rooms under General Business Law §395-b. Most boards publish a short camera policy so owners know what is recorded and who can see it.

We install condo cameras with audio recording turned off unless the board's attorney approves otherwise. That is not legal advice; it is the cautious default we recommend to every association.
