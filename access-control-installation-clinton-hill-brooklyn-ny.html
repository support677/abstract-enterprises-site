The Neighborhood

Why Clinton Hill Properties Need Access Control

Clinton Hill’s front doors are the reason this page exists. The Historic Districts Council describes the Clinton Hill Historic District, designated on November 10, 1981, as a mix of row houses, mansions, stables, apartment houses and institutional buildings, in styles that run from Greek Revival and Italianate to Romanesque Revival and Art Moderne. Almost every one of those building types came with a front entrance designed in the nineteenth or early twentieth century, for a household that answered its own door. Today the same doors serve a family upstairs and a renter on the garden floor, eight condo owners in a divided mansion, a co-op of a hundred apartments, or a restaurant with a kitchen in the cellar. Access control is how an old door learns to tell those people apart.

The most common call we get is about a brownstone’s parlor entrance: a pair of tall wood-and-glass doors at the top of the stoop, a vestibule behind them, and a single inner door into the hall. Brownstoner’s own forums are full of owners asking the same things we are asked. One owner was told that an electric strike “can’t be done” on rowhouse double doors; a door rebuilder replied that about half the double doors his firm works on get an electric latch, and that it is easier with the leaves on the bench and the glass out, but it “can be done.” Another owner of a three-family brownstone with a bi-fold outer door and a single inner door asked whether to lock the outer doors, the inner door or both, and how the mail carrier would get in. Those two threads are, in practice, the start of most Clinton Hill access projects, and we answer both in detail below.

The second thing that makes Clinton Hill different is how carefully its fronts are treated. The doors themselves are often original, or faithfully copied. Brownstoner’s December 2024 tour of a gut-renovated 1870s Italianate row house on the Hill notes that the house had once been “separated into weird apartments” and that, when it was taken back to one family, the entry and vestibule doors were among the very few things saved; two pairs of original entry doors were restored and repainted, and the inner set got new reeded glass. In June 2025 the same publication described another Clinton Hill brownstone whose entry doors were “nearly all glass” and had to be replicated for a thinner inner set. Owners who have paid to keep doors like these do not want a strike bodged into the edge of a leaf or a keypad screwed through carved stone. They want hardware that disappears into the frame and a cable route nobody sees.

Third, the neighborhood rents. Pratt Institute’s housing office says that only about 40 percent of its roughly 5,000 students live on campus, and the rest find rooms and apartments nearby. Small buildings like 43 Putnam Avenue, three apartments on three floors built in 1895, are typical of the rental stock. Every September brings new names to doors that were keyed decades ago. A brass key copied for a tenant in one decade can still be in a drawer in another state, and the owner has no way to know. Individual codes and fobs, each of which can be cancelled on its own, are the plainest fix for that, and they are why so many divided brownstones here move to access control at the same time as a change of tenants.

Fourth, the Hill has real apartment towers. The Clinton Hill Coops at 210 Clinton Avenue, built in 1945, is a 13-story elevator building of 104 apartments with a full-time doorman, 24-hour security, package rooms, bike storage and common gardens, and it is unusual enough to generate much of its own electricity. A few blocks away, Willoughby Walk is a two-building co-op at 195 Willoughby Avenue and 185 Hall Street; the Willoughby building is a 16-story slab erected in 1958, and the listing gives each building 287 co-operative apartments, with a full-time doorman, round-the-clock security at the front entrances and a live-in superintendent. A doorman sees the front door. He does not see the garden gate, the bike room, the package room or the service entrance on the other avenue, and those are the doors a co-op asks us about.

Fifth, the commercial edges have a record that is specifically about doors. In April 2016, DNAinfo reported that burglars broke into Five Spot Soul Food at 459 Myrtle Avenue through the restaurant’s side door at 4:20 a.m. and, about twenty minutes later, into Wray’s Caribbean and Seafood Cuisine at 503 Myrtle Avenue through a cellar door. BK Reader reported in February 2022 that two men forced their way into Wray’s again, near Myrtle Avenue and Ryerson Street, at about 2:45 a.m. and left with a small safe. And in the summer of 2016 a burglar crawled into Speedy Romeo at 376 Classon Avenue through a back window left open and took the cash from a safe in the basement. Side doors, cellar doors and back openings are exactly where a restaurant’s keys get handed around, and where a door that reports its own opening after closing time is worth more than another lock.

None of this is written to frighten anyone. Most Clinton Hill front doors are opened only by the people who live behind them; the job is simply to keep it that way as the doors age and the households behind them change.

What the materials mean for the install

Brownstone is a soft sandstone facing over brick, and it does not forgive mistakes. We never fix a reader or a station through the carved surround of a doorway. Where something must sit on the face of the building, it goes on a plain stretch of stone or in a mortar joint close to the opening, and the cable reaches it through one small hole drilled in plain masonry rather than through the carving. Very often nothing needs to go on the stone at all: a slim reader fits on the wood door frame or on the face of the fixed leaf, and the strike itself lives entirely inside the frame, invisible from the street.

The wood is the other half of the job. Original parlor frames in Clinton Hill have usually been bored for three or four generations of locks, and the jamb may be soft where earlier strikes were cut. We fill or splice those sections with sound wood before cutting a pocket for the strike. Pairs of doors bring a further question, whether the fixed leaf is truly fixed. Many are held only by worn flush bolts at the top and bottom, so the pair flexes when the active leaf is pulled. A strike on a pair that flexes will not hold, so the bolts are renewed first.

Iron is the third material. Under-stoop gates and areaway railings are often original or historic, and the Landmarks guidebook says an existing historic gate below a stoop must be kept. We clamp gate locks and shields to the gate’s frame rather than welding or drilling ornamental work, and we run the cable in a small sealed conduit along the plain face of the stoop wall or below grade, not across the railing. On the co-op towers the materials are simpler, metal-and-glass entrances and painted block or plaster inside, and the hard part there is coordination, not stone.

Two Leaves, a Lot of Glass: Putting a Strike on a Clinton Hill Pair of Parlor Doors

The belief that a brownstone’s double doors cannot take an electric strike is common enough that owners repeat it to us on the phone. It is not true, but it is easy to see where it comes from. On a single door, a strike is simple: the latch in the door’s edge drops into a strike in the frame, and when the strike is powered its keeper swings free so the door can be pushed open. On a pair, the active leaf usually latches into the fixed leaf, not into the frame, and the fixed leaf is held only by bolts at the top and bottom. There is no frame to cut on the latch side. Put a strike in the wrong place, or on a fixed leaf that is not really fixed, and the door either will not release or will not hold.

The door makers on Brownstoner’s forum describe the same three ways we use. The first, and usually the best, is a strike set into the edge of the fixed leaf where the active leaf’s latch meets it, with the power carried into the fixed leaf through a hinge or a short, protected loop at the hinge side. One fabricator wrote that for most of the doors his shop makes they recommend a strike on the exterior passive leaf. The second is a strike at the head of the frame, working with a vertical latch at the top of the active leaf. The third, where neither of those is practical, is an electrified mortise lock in the active leaf itself, so the lever on the outside is locked or freed electrically and no strike is needed at all. Each has a different cost in carpentry, and the choice depends on what the leaves are made of and how much original hardware you want to keep.

Glass is the part owners worry about most, and with reason. Many parlor doors here are mostly glass in a thin wood frame, and the reeded or etched panes are irreplaceable or expensive to copy. A strike itself does not stress the glass; what cracks old glass is a door that slams because its closer is missing or worn, or a leaf that twists because its bolts are loose. So the first thing we do on a glass pair is make the door close gently and evenly: a closer adjusted to bring the leaf in slowly over the last few inches, flush bolts renewed so the fixed leaf is truly rigid, and, where needed, a small adjustment at the hinges so the latch lands without force. Only then is a strike cut in. The brownstone door rebuilder on the forum made the point that the work is easiest with the leaves on the bench and the glass out, which is why, if your doors are going out for restoration anyway, that is the moment to prepare them.

If the doors are being restored

One of the fabricators on the forum explained that when access is planned before a door is built or rebuilt, wiring can be run through the door, the hinge and the jamb rather than added on the surface later. That is exactly what we ask a restorer for: a channel bored through the fixed leaf from the hinge side to the strike pocket, a hinge position prepared for a power transfer hinge, the strike pocket itself cut to the template of the strike we have chosen, and the glass reinstalled afterward. It adds little to the restorer’s bill and saves visible work on the finished doors. We send the template and the model number to the shop directly if you want.

The Vestibule Between: Keys, Mail and Who Opens Which Door

A Clinton Hill vestibule is a small room with two doors and, often, the mailboxes. How those two doors are locked decides how the whole house works. The Brownstoner thread about a three-family brownstone sets out the choices honestly. Lock both doors, and the mail carrier and every courier need a way through the first. Lock only the inner door, and the vestibule becomes an open porch that anyone can step into from the street. Lock only the outer doors, and every household inside shares one barrier with nothing behind it. There is no single right answer; there is a right answer for the way your house is used.

For a one-family house we usually put the strike and the reader on the outer pair and leave the inner door on its key. The family comes and goes with codes or fobs, parcels can be left in a locked vestibule by releasing the outer door from a phone, and the inner door is a second, mechanical barrier at night. For a divided house where the mailboxes sit in the vestibule, the forum replies point to two workable routes: a strike on the outer passive leaf tied to a postal lock, so the carrier can open it briefly with the Postal Service’s own key, or a key keeper for the carrier if the outer door stays on keys. We prefer the first, because it keeps the outer pair locked the rest of the day.

There is one thing we will not do without a conversation. A 2008 Brownstoner thread includes an installer’s advice that he would “highly recommend not tying a remote release to the inner door,” and another poster described being robbed in their own vestibule, between the two doors. A remote release on the inner door means that anyone who talks their way past the outer door is buzzed straight into the house. In a divided house, where tenants release the street door for visitors they cannot always see, we keep the inner door on keys or on its own credentials, and we give each household a video call from the station outside so they see who is at the stoop before anything opens.

Keyed alike or keyed differently

The same forum thread argues both sides of a question every owner of a divided house faces. One door maker suggests keying the outer and inner doors differently, so that if a key is lost or a tenant leaves on bad terms, only one lock needs changing; another reply prefers keying them alike for simplicity. Once the outer pair is on codes and fobs, the question mostly answers itself. The outer pair is managed electronically, so its key is now only an emergency override held by the owner, and the inner door keeps an ordinary key that tenants carry. If that inner key goes astray, one cylinder is rekeyed, and nobody has to change a code. The forum thread also recommends dead latches over deadbolts on these doors, because a dead latch locks every time the door closes, which matches the way a strike behaves on the outer pair.

The Gate Under the Stoop

Below the stoop of most Clinton Hill brownstones is a second entrance: the garden-level door, set back in a small areaway behind a wrought-iron gate. In a one-family house it is the everyday door to the kitchen; in a divided house it is often the front door of the garden apartment. The gate in front of it is usually the weak point of the whole house, because its lock sits on an open grille. A Brownstoner owner put the problem simply: anyone can “reach through the bars to open the lock.” The replies suggested a double-cylinder lock that needs a key on both sides, or a welded iron box around the inside of the lock so an arm through the bars cannot reach it. One reply added that double-cylinder locks “aren’t allowed in NYC” and could be dangerous.

That last point is the one we build around. A gate or door that needs a key to open from inside can trap people in a fire, and on the way out of a garden apartment the areaway gate is part of the exit. So we do not fit locks that need a key, a code or a card to leave. What we fit instead is an electric gate lock with a shield plate behind it, so that nothing on the inside can be turned or pressed by an arm through the grille, and an inside release that a person on the inside can operate with one simple movement, positioned and shielded so it is reachable from the areaway but not from the sidewalk. Outside, a slim keypad or fob reader goes on the gate’s frame or on the plain wall of the stoop beside it, which is one of the places the Landmarks guidebook lists for equipment at an entrance under a stoop.

The gate and the garden door then become one entrance with two parts. A code at the gate releases it; the garden door behind it keeps its own lock, either a key or a second strike on the same system. If the garden floor is rented, the tenant gets their own credentials for both, and the owner keeps only an emergency method for the gate. If the house is one family, the gate can be set to stay locked at night and to open on the family’s codes, a dog walker’s code on weekdays, and a courier release from the phone. Cable reaches the gate through a small sealed conduit along the base of the stoop wall or under the areaway paving, never draped across the ironwork.

Historic gates stay

The Landmarks guidebook says that an existing historic gate below a stoop must be retained, and many owners would keep theirs anyway. Everything we add is clamped to the gate’s frame, not welded to the scrollwork, and can be removed later without leaving a mark on the iron beyond a few small holes in the frame. Where a gate has sagged so badly that it will not close against its post, a blacksmith rehangs it before we start; a lock cannot fix a gate that does not shut.

Keys Before Codes: Why Clinton Hill Doors Keep a Working Key

Some keyless products are sold on the idea that keys are obsolete. We do not install them that way in Clinton Hill or anywhere else. In 2019, amNY reported that city rules still require a traditional key-based way into residential buildings, and that the Department of Buildings knew of no buildings that had improperly relied only on new technology. The same report described tenants in a Manhattan loft who sued after their landlord installed a phone-based lock and stopped handing out mechanical keys to the lobby, their only route to the elevator, and tenants in an East New York complex who fought a landlord’s plan for biometric entry. A New York Civil Liberties Union policy director told the paper: “It’s fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data.”

Two practical lessons follow. The first is that every door we put on access control keeps a key path: a cylinder in the original lock of a brownstone’s parlor doors, a keyed override on a lobby reader, or an emergency key cylinder of the kind Habitat Magazine described co-ops keeping because fobs do not work in a blackout. The second is that tenants in a multiple dwelling must be able to choose a credential that does not hand over more data than the law allows. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires owners who use smart access to get express consent, collect only the minimum, give tenants a written privacy and retention policy, and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. A plain fob issued to a name, with short log retention, satisfies all of that.

Keys also explain why switching a building over has to be planned. A vendor’s account of a fob roll-out at the Queensbridge South houses, citing CBS New York, describes residents locked out by fobs that worked inconsistently and doors that jammed, until some propped entrances open to get in. We read that as a warning about process, not about fobs. A new system should run side by side with the old keys for a set period, every resident should have tested their own credential at the door before the keys stop working, and there should be a person to call on the first mornings. Nothing in a Clinton Hill brownstone needs a big-bang switch-over.

A key count before anything is bought

Before we price a door in a house that has been rented for years, we ask the owner to write down everyone who has, or ever had, a key: tenants past and present, a super, a cleaner, a contractor from the last renovation, a relative, a neighbor who watered the plants. The list is almost always longer than anyone expected. It is the clearest case for individual codes and fobs, and it tells us which cylinders should be rekeyed on the day the system goes live, so the old keys stop working at the same time the new credentials start.

Doorman Towers and the Doors the Doorman Cannot See

The co-op towers on Clinton Avenue and Willoughby Avenue look like the easy case: there is already a doorman, 24-hour security and a live-in super. Boards call us anyway, and the reason is geography. What the Myrtle Avenue break-ins say about doors

The April 2016 burglaries on Myrtle Avenue are worth reading closely, because they describe the two doors restaurants most often forget. At Five Spot Soul Food the burglars came in through the side door before dawn and took four cash drawers; at Wray’s, about twenty minutes later and a block away, they came in through a cellar door. Neither was the front door, which in most restaurants is behind a roll-down gate, lit, and the door everyone thinks about. Side doors are used by staff for breaks, trash and deliveries; cellar doors are used by delivery drivers and the kitchen. Both get propped, both have keys that travel, and both are out of sight from the dining room.

Access control does three useful things on those doors. It ends shared keys: each person who needs the side or cellar door gets a code or fob that works only during their shifts and stops working when they leave the job. It records: the log shows which credential opened the door and when, so the morning after a problem you know whether the door was opened by someone who belonged there. And it reports: a contact on the door sends the owner a phone alert if the door is opened, forced or left open after the last shift has gone. What it does not do is stop someone who breaks the door itself, which is why we pair it with a strong strike, a closer that actually closes the door, and, where the owner wants it, a camera aimed at the same opening.

The 2016 Speedy Romeo burglary on Classon Avenue is the reminder that doors are not the only openings. That burglar came through a back window left open and found the safe in the basement. A door system cannot close a window, but it can include a contact on a rear window or a basement hatch so the closing manager sees, on the same app, every opening that is still open before setting the alarm.

Rules that apply to Clinton Hill business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit must release when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Doors out of a dining room, kitchen or shop always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above the shop. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked unless an attendant is on duty, and a building with eight or more apartments needs an intercom with voice and remote release. A restaurant cannot put the residential street door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires a written policy, consent, limits on what is collected and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A restaurant’s own staff door is a business door, but a shared residential door is not.
  • Landmarks. Inside the historic district, anything visible on the front, a reader, a station or conduit, follows the LPC Permit Guidebook: kept small, next to the opening, conduit concealed or limited, and not fixed to cast iron or decorative surfaces. LPC staff can issue permits for intercom panels; we prepare the drawing and product sheet.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Clinton Hill: Questions Answered

Brownstone doors, gates and keys

Once a strike goes on the outer pair of our three-family brownstone, should the outer and inner doors be keyed alike or keyed differently?

Keyed differently, in most divided houses. After the strike goes in, everyday entry through the outer pair is by code or fob, so its key becomes an emergency override that only the owner holds. The inner door keeps an ordinary key that each household carries. If an inner key is lost or a tenant leaves on bad terms, one cylinder is rekeyed and nobody’s code changes; if the owner’s override key is lost, only that cylinder is changed.

Keying alike makes sense only in a one-family house where the same people hold every key anyway. Either way, we suggest dead latches rather than deadbolts on these doors, because a dead latch locks every time the door shuts, which is how the strike on the outer pair behaves too.

Our parlor doors are almost entirely original glass. Will the jolt of the door latching onto an electric strike crack a pane?

The strike is not what cracks glass; slamming and twisting are. A leaf that swings shut fast, or a fixed leaf that flexes because its top and bottom bolts are worn, puts shock and torsion through the panes every time the door closes. Before we cut a strike into a glass pair, we fit or adjust a closer so the leaf comes in slowly over the last few inches, renew the flush bolts so the fixed leaf is rigid, and set the strike so the latch drops in without being forced.

If a pane is already cracked or loose, we say so before work starts.

Someone could reach through the bars of the iron gate under our stoop and turn the lock from the inside. What fixes that without fitting a lock that could trap us?

A shielded electric lock with a protected inside release. The lock body and anything that could be turned or pressed sit behind a solid plate on the inside of the gate, so an arm through the bars meets flat steel. Leaving is done with a release on the areaway side that opens the gate with one simple movement, placed where it can be reached from the areaway but not from the sidewalk.

We do not use a lock that needs a key to get out, because that gate is part of the way out of the garden floor in a fire. Coming in, a code or fob at a slim station on the gate frame or the plain stoop wall releases it, and the garden door behind keeps its own lock.

We read about someone being robbed in their own vestibule, between the street door and the inner door. How should our two doors be set up so the vestibule is never a place to be cornered?

Keep the two doors on separate decisions. The outer pair gets the strike and the video station, so whoever answers sees and speaks to the visitor before it opens. The inner door does not get a remote release in a divided house; it opens with the resident’s own key or credential, so nobody buzzed past the outer pair is buzzed straight into the hall. A light inside the vestibule that comes on when the outer door opens means you are never stepping into a dark box.

Residents should also be able to see the vestibule on the station’s screen or their phone before coming down to collect a visitor or a parcel. If someone is waiting there who should not be, you do not open the inner door.

Owners, tenants and guests

Our landlord says the building is going app-only and he will stop handing out keys. Can he do that?

Not as a requirement, as far as the city has reported. amNY wrote in 2019 that city rules still require a traditional key-based way into residential buildings, and the Department of Buildings said it knew of no buildings that had improperly relied only on new technology. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law separately requires express consent before smart access is used and limits what data the system may collect.

An app can be offered alongside keys or fobs, and many tenants like it. Ask the landlord in writing what the non-app option is. When we install for owners, every system keeps a key path and a plain fob option.

We host short-term guests in our brownstone and stay home while they are here, as the city now requires. How do we let guests through the street door without giving them the tenant’s floor upstairs?

Separate the doors that belong to your home from the doors that belong to someone else’s. News 12 explained in 2023 that under Local Law 18 hosts must register, must live in the unit and be present while guests stay, and guests must have full access to the host’s home. That access stops at your front door: a guest gets a code for the shared street door and your own entrance, with a start and end time, and nothing that opens a tenant’s apartment, the cellar or the owner’s storage.

Because each guest’s code is separate, it can be ended early if a stay goes wrong, and the log shows only when the shared door was used, not anything about your tenant.

A fob roll-out at a big city complex left residents locked out and propping doors. How do we move our building off keys without that happening?

Run the two side by side. The new readers go live while the old keys still work, every resident tests their own fob at the door with someone from our team or the super nearby, and only when the last household has a working credential are the cylinders rekeyed. Spare programmed fobs stay with the super for the first weeks, and the door has a key override throughout.

The account we read of the Queensbridge South problem, a vendor’s summary of a CBS New York report, described fobs that worked inconsistently and jammed doors. That is a failure of hardware adjustment and of process, and both are checked before any key is retired.

Co-ops and apartment houses

Our co-op makes much of its own electricity. What happens to the doors when the building switches between its plant and the utility?

Nothing a resident should notice, if the door power is set up for it. A switch between sources can cause a short dip that resets electronics and, on a badly set up system, momentarily releases a lock. We put each controller and lock power supply on a battery-backed supply that carries the doors through the changeover, choose fail-secure strikes for entrances that should stay locked, and keep the fire alarm release wired so it still frees exit doors whatever the power source.

Before handover we run the changeover with the super watching the doors, so the board knows exactly what happens.

Our co-op has entrances on Clinton, Willoughby and Vanderbilt Avenues. How do we keep staff and residents clear about which door is which in the system?

Name every door by its street and use, not by a number: “Willoughby lobby,” “Vanderbilt service,” “Clinton garden gate.” The same names go on the readers’ labels inside, on the controller wiring, in the app and in the log, so a doorman looking up an event and a resident reporting a problem are talking about the same door. Each entrance can then keep its own schedule.

The board gets a one-page map of every controlled door and its name.

The co-op’s common gardens sit behind the buildings. Should the garden gates go on fobs, and what about shareholders who still have the old gate key?

Yes, if the gardens are for residents only, because the old gate key has almost certainly multiplied over the years. A fob reader on each gate, opening on residents’ credentials and on a schedule that matches garden hours, replaces the shared key. The gates still open freely from the garden side so nobody can be shut in at dusk.

Retire the old key by rekeying the gate cylinders on the day the readers go live, after notice to shareholders. Keep one keyed override with the super.

Shops, restaurants and institutions

We own a Myrtle Avenue building, run the restaurant on the ground floor, and the apartments above share our cellar. How do we split the cellar so tenants reach their storage but not our stock?

Divide it into zones and give each zone its own door. The stair or hatch the kitchen uses opens on staff credentials during service hours; a separate door or partition into the tenants’ storage opens on tenants’ fobs; and the door between the two stays locked to both groups except for the owner. A contact on the restaurant’s cellar door alerts the owner if it opens after closing, which is the hour the 2016 Myrtle Avenue cellar-door burglary happened.

Every cellar door must still open freely from the inside, and the boiler, meters and shut-offs stay reachable for the super and the utilities.

Our institution’s grounds are open to neighbors in the daytime and closed at night. Can the gates lock on a schedule without shutting anyone inside?

Yes. Gates can unlock at opening time and relock at closing on a schedule, with a warning period, and always open from the inside by hand, so anyone still on the grounds can leave. Staff and residents keep fob access after hours. Pratt Institute’s 2009 decision to enforce its rule against non-students lingering on campus, reported by Brownstoner, shows how sensitive these choices are; the schedule is the institution’s decision, and the hardware simply makes it consistent.

We add a contact on each gate so security knows if one is left open after closing.

“Double doors can’t take an electric strike.”

Field Notes

Field Notes: Push the Fixed Leaf Before Anything Else

This describes how we approach a brownstone pair, not a particular customer. The first thing a technician does at a Clinton Hill parlor entrance is close the active leaf and push hard on the fixed one. If it moves, the flush bolts are worn or their keepers in the sill and head have loosened, and no strike will hold that door. Then we open and release the active leaf from about a foot to see how it lands, and look at the glass for loose putty.

Only then do we go down to the cellar under the stoop and look for a dry wall for the controller and a route up to the door. On most houses all three checks pass with a little carpentry, and the electronics become the easy part.

FAQ

Clinton Hill Access Control FAQ

Are double-cylinder deadbolts allowed on a Clinton Hill house door or under-stoop gate?
+

We treat them as unsafe on any door or gate that is part of the way out, and we do not install them there. A lock that needs a key to leave can trap people in a fire, and on a brownstone the parlor doors and the areaway gate are both exits. Owners usually want them to stop a hand reaching through glass or bars; a shield plate and a protected inside release solve that without the risk.

Can a Clinton Hill parlor door with an electric strike still be slipped open with a card?
+

Not if the latch is set up properly. Old spring latches can sometimes be pushed back with a thin card, which is one reason we pair the strike with a latch that has a deadlocking guard, so the latch cannot be pressed in once the door is shut. We also fit a latch guard plate where the gap between the leaves is wide. Both are checked on the site visit.

What should we tell the restorer before our Clinton Hill entry doors are rehung?
+

Ask for a wire channel bored through the fixed leaf from the hinge side to the strike position, a hinge prepared for a power transfer hinge, and the strike pocket cut to the template of the strike we choose. We send the model number and template to the shop. It adds little to the restoration and avoids cutting the finished doors later.

Do you need to get into the cellar of a Clinton Hill brownstone for a parlor door job?

Usually, yes. The cellar under the stoop is where the controller and its battery normally go, off the floor on a dry wall, and where the cable starts its short route up to the doors. If the garden floor is a rented apartment and the cellar is only reachable through it, we arrange the visit with the tenant’s agreement and keep the time inside to a minimum.

Can anyone on the street tell that a Clinton Hill door has an electric strike?
+

No. The strike sits inside the door frame or the edge of the fixed leaf, and the cable runs inside the frame and down to the cellar. The only things visible from the sidewalk are the reader or station beside the doors and any lit ring on it, which is also all the Landmarks guidebook is concerned with at a historic front.
