Need Repair in Borough Park? Two Locked Doors, a Release That Hums, or a Bell That Rings Itself

Borough Park buzzer calls tend to come in a handful of recognizable shapes. The first is the walk-up where the street door clicks open but the visitor is then stuck in the vestibule, facing an inner door that will not release or will not latch. The second is the walk-up or enlarged house where the lower floors still ring and the top floors have gone quiet, which nearly always points to a joint or a splice somewhere between the two. The third is the attached two-family where the owner downstairs hears the bell and the tenant upstairs does not, which tells us the transformer is alive and one branch has failed. The fourth is the bell that will not stop: a button on the plate sticks in after a delivery driver has leaned on it, and an apartment rings until somebody pulls the wire or the button frees itself. The fifth is the door that will not lock at all.

That last one is the call nobody should sit on. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a building of three or more apartments as a class C, immediately hazardous condition, and its guidance gives the owner 14 days to correct it after a notice. When you call (347) 934-8335, tell us first if a door is standing open or will not lock. We deal with that door before anything else on the visit, and if a part for the rest of the system has to be ordered, we leave both doors closing and latching before we go.

We want to be straightforward about timing, because a lot of repair sites are not. We do not promise same-day repairs. Requests are booked within 2 hours, and visits are scheduled on weekdays, early in the day, in a two-hour arrival window. Many faults are found and fixed on that first visit, because strikes, keepers, closer parts, push buttons, transformers and common audio stations travel on the van. When the fault is a discontinued station or a panel part that has to be ordered, we tell you on the day, price it in writing and come back once it arrives.

The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Borough Park is a neighborhood of many homes behind a small number of doors, and most of those doors are old. LucidRents' report card for ZIP code 11219, the neighborhood's main ZIP, tracks 10,552 buildings and counts 73 percent of households as renters. Its map of the area follows the familiar outline: Sunset Park to the west, Kensington and Green-Wood to the north, Bensonhurst and Dyker Heights to the south, Flatbush and Mapleton to the east, with the shopping streets of 13th, 16th and 18th Avenues running through the middle. Across those blocks sit attached brick two-family houses, four- and five-story walk-ups from the 1920s and 1930s, houses that have been enlarged upward and backward, and apartments stacked over stores. Each of those has a street door that is supposed to call upstairs, let a visitor in, and then shut and lock again by itself. When any of those three things stops, someone in the building calls it "the buzzer."

The public record shows where the trouble usually lands. LucidRents counts 56,896 HPD violations and 68,302 complaints across the 11219 buildings it tracks, about 5.4 violations and 6.5 complaints per building, and it grades the area C for maintenance and D for responsiveness. Those are violations of every kind, not buzzer faults. But when we look at individual Borough Park walk-ups, the category that keeps appearing near the top is not electrical at all. At 5510 13 Avenue, a four-story building from 1923, LucidRents lists 201 HPD violations in the "Door/Lock" category, second only to paint and plaster. At 6623 Ft Hamilton Parkway, a five-story building from 1935, the same category holds 166 violations over seven years. Neither page shows a single violation worded about an intercom or buzzer, and we draw no conclusion about either building's entry system. What the numbers do show is that the doors themselves, their latches, locks and closers, are what the city writes up most often in buildings like these. A buzzer can only open a door that latches properly to begin with.

The wording of those records is worth reading closely. In August 2026, HPD wrote class C violations at 5510 13 Avenue to "REPLACE OR REPAIR THE SELF-CLOSING DOORS THAT IS MISSING OR DEFECTIVE LATCH AT VESTIBULE DOOR, 1st STORY." In June 2026, it wrote another class C item to "PROPERLY REPAIR OR REPLACE THE BROKEN OR DEFECTIVE MORTISE LOCK AT VESTIBULE DOOR." Those are the inner doors many Borough Park walk-ups were built with, behind the street door, and they are where a visitor gets stuck when the release only works on one of the two. We repair them as part of the same entrance, because a tenant pressing release upstairs does not care which of the two doors failed.

Ownership adds a second layer. The same LucidRents report lists the largest "landlord" in 11219 as "UNAVAILABLE OWNER," with 152 buildings under that label, and names several limited liability companies among the owners with the most violations. In practice that means a tenant who finds the buzzer dead often cannot simply knock on the owner's door. Many buildings are run by a managing agent, a relative of the owner, or an LLC with a phone number on the lobby notice. Part of every Borough Park buzzer job, before any tool comes out, is working out who can approve the repair and who has the key to the cellar or the meter room where the transformer hangs. We ask that on the first call so the visit is not wasted.

Then there is the sheer amount of traffic through each door. Borough Park households are often large, and Wikipedia's Thirteenth Avenue article notes that nearly every store on the avenue closes for the Sabbath and Jewish holidays, which concentrates shopping and deliveries into the other days of the week. A door that a small household might open a handful of times a day can open dozens of times in a building with many children, visiting relatives and a steady run of couriers. In an r/AskNYC thread about apartment bells, one reply observed that Amazon and UPS drivers ring every unit anyway, and another said people are "way too comfortable ringing random doorbells to be let in." Every one of those presses works the button plate, the release relay and the strike. Worn buttons, strikes that have been hammered out of line, and release circuits that stick are ordinary findings in buildings this busy.

The housing stock keeps changing too. Borough Park's special zoning, adopted in 1992 according to Wikipedia, allows higher lot coverage and smaller yards than much of the city, and the neighborhood has been one of Brooklyn's busiest for private construction since. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in February 2016 that DOB filings for 1311 44th Street described a two-story two-family house being enlarged to a five-story four-family building of 5,633 square feet, part of what the paper called "constant demand for additional housing." When a house grows like that, the bell wiring usually grows with it: a few new runs spliced onto the old ones, sometimes inside a wall that was later closed up. Those splices are among the most common reasons the newest apartments in an old Borough Park building are the first to go quiet.

Safety is the reason people care. Boro Park 24 reported in November 2020 that a thief stole residents' packages from a building at Caton Avenue and McDonald Avenue "every night for a year and a half," entering through an open window in the early morning hours, until the superintendent watched the building's camera feed and Shomrim volunteers waited outside for the NYPD to make an arrest. In December 2025, Boro Park Shomrim reported an arrest after a man was seen on exterior camera footage entering a multi-family building and then found inside an apartment. Neither report says a broken buzzer was involved, and we do not suggest it was. They are reminders of why a street door that stays locked, and a vestibule door that latches behind every visitor, matter in this neighborhood.

We also want to be clear about what our research did not find. We could not pull 311 complaint counts for the bell, buzzer and intercom category in Borough Park's ZIP codes, we found no Borough Park Reddit thread or local news story about a particular broken intercom, and we found no Yelp reviews about buzzer repair here beyond directory listings. So this page does not claim Borough Park has more or fewer buzzer problems than anywhere else. It describes the buildings that are here, the records that are public, the rules that apply, and how a repair actually goes.

Six Borough Park facts that change how a buzzer repair goes

  • Two doors at many entrances: HPD records at a 1923 walk-up on 13th Avenue cite the "VESTIBULE DOOR" for a defective latch and a broken mortise lock in 2026, so the inner door is part of the repair, not an afterthought.
  • Door and lock faults dominate: LucidRents lists 201 "Door/Lock" violations at 5510 13 Avenue and 166 at 6623 Ft Hamilton Parkway, more than the buildings' plumbing or leak categories.
  • Mostly renters, often absent owners: 73 percent of 11219 households rent, and 152 buildings in the ZIP carry the owner label "UNAVAILABLE OWNER" on LucidRents.
  • Prewar stock: the building records we reviewed date from 1910 (a semi-attached house on 11th Avenue), 1923 and 1924 (walk-ups on 13th Avenue) and 1935 (a walk-up on Fort Hamilton Parkway).
  • Houses that grow: special zoning since 1992 and steady enlargements, like the two-family on 44th Street filed to become a five-story four-family, mean bell wiring is often extended rather than replaced.
  • A six-day week at the door: stores on 13th Avenue close for the Sabbath and holidays, and many residents do not use electric releases then, so the mechanical side of the door, keys, latches and closers, has to work on its own every week.

What owners sometimes ask about once the door works. After both doors call, release and lock again, some Borough Park owners ask about a camera over the entrance, or about replacing the whole entry system with a video panel. Those are separate jobs with their own quotes, and many owners pair a working buzzer with a camera because of the package thefts Shomrim keeps reporting. We will tell you whether the transformer and wiring we just tested could carry more, but a repair visit stays a repair visit unless you ask for more in writing.

The 1920s walk-up: two doors, one visitor

A walk-up from the early 1920s on or near 13th Avenue was typically built with a pair of doors at the entrance: an outer street door and an inner door into the stair hall, with a small vestibule between them where the mailboxes and the button plate often live. Over the decades, whoever added the buzzer had to decide which of those two doors to put the strike on, and the answer was not always the same from building to building, or even from one repair to the next. Some buildings have the strike on the street door and a plain latch on the vestibule door. Others have it on the vestibule door, with the street door left on the latch during the day. A few have strikes on both, run from the same release. Before we touch anything, we find out which arrangement is on your entrance and whether it is the one the building actually wants.

The vestibule door is also where the mortise lock lives in many of these buildings: a heavy lock body set into a pocket in the edge of the door, with a latch, often a deadbolt, and a cylinder for the residents' keys. HPD's 2026 records at 5510 13 Avenue cite exactly that part on the vestibule door. A mortise lock that has been worked for a century can wear in ways that look like a buzzer fault. The latch may retract slowly. It may stop a few millimeters short of the keeper, so the door rests closed without being locked. The springs inside may weaken so the latch never fully extends. And if a resident throws the deadbolt at night, no strike in the world will release that door. We check each of those by hand before we measure a volt.

Once the doors are right, the electrical side of a 1920s walk-up is usually simple and old: a bell transformer in the cellar, a riser climbing past each floor, a button plate in the vestibule, and audio stations of several different generations in the apartments, because each one was replaced when it died. The riser is where most of the quiet-floor faults live. A joint at the third floor that has corroded or been disturbed can silence the third and fourth floors while the first and second keep working perfectly, which is why two tenants in the same building can describe two different buildings. We test at the plate, at the bottom of the riser and at the lowest silent floor before deciding where the loss is.

The 1930s five-story: a longer line and a harder-working strike

The five-story walk-ups of the 1930s along Fort Hamilton Parkway and the cross streets near it carry more apartments on one entrance than the earlier four-story buildings. LucidRents lists about thirty units at 6623 Ft Hamilton Parkway, for example. More apartments means more presses of the release every day, a longer riser, and more stations sharing one transformer. The parts that fail first are the ones that carry the most load. The strike is cycled every time anyone in the building lets someone in. The transformer has to deliver enough current to pull the strike while every handset on the line is connected to it. When we find a strike that hums but will not release, we measure the supply's voltage while the strike is energized, not at rest, because a transformer that reads fine with nothing connected can sag badly the moment the strike pulls.

The attached two-family: a small system with two households on it

Borough Park's attached brick two-family houses usually have the simplest systems in the neighborhood: a transformer, two buttons at the front door, a bell or handset on each floor, and a release button in each apartment if the front door has a strike at all. The Brownstoner listing for a prewar two-family on 35th Street describes the classic layout, "a 6 room apartment over a 5 room apartment," with a full basement below. The common faults are just as simple to describe. The owner's bell works and the tenant's does not, which means the supply is alive and the upstairs branch or button has failed. Both bells work but neither apartment can open the door, which points to the strike or the release side. Or the bells ring weakly, which usually means a tired transformer. These jobs are often done well inside the three-hour minimum, and the written note tells the owner exactly what was replaced and what is aging.

The semi-attached house and the second entrance

Many Borough Park houses have a second way in. The Brownstoner listing for a 1910 semi-attached brick house on 11th Avenue mentions an English basement with high ceilings and a separate entrance, the kind of door that often sits under the stoop or along the side of the house. When a basement is rented or used by family, that door usually gets its own bell, and the wire for it runs from wherever the transformer is to a button that may be outside, below grade, where rain and melting snow collect. Those buttons fail more often than front-door buttons, and when they short out, they can take down the whole house's bells, not just their own. We treat a second entrance as its own circuit, test it separately, and where a button keeps getting wet, seal it properly or move it a short distance to a drier spot that a visitor can still find and reach.

The enlarged house: old wire at the bottom, new wire at the top

When a Borough Park house is built up from two families to four or more, the entry system rarely gets replaced. More often, someone extends what is there: new runs to the new apartments, a few extra buttons on the plate or a new plate beside it, and splices where the new wire meets the old. Some of those splices are made carefully, in a box, with proper connectors. Others are twisted and taped inside a wall during construction and then closed up behind new plaster. Those buried splices are among the most common reasons the newest apartments are the first to lose their bells, and the hardest to find, because nobody remembers where they are. We start from both ends, the plate and the silent apartment, and narrow the gap until we find the joint. We also check whether the original transformer, sized for two households, can still carry four or five, because an overloaded transformer runs hot, hums and fails early.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Borough Park Vestibule Before Opening Anything

We do not publish stories about particular Borough Park addresses that we cannot document, so this is not an anecdote. It is the routine our technicians follow at the entrance of an older Brooklyn walk-up with two doors, because it is the same every time and it decides most of these jobs.

We arrive and leave the system alone. We open the street door fully and let it go, twice, and watch whether the latch drops into the keeper or rides on its lip. We step into the vestibule and do the same with the inner door, then turn the cylinder with a resident's key to feel whether the mortise lock throws and retracts cleanly. We look at the strike's faceplate for fresh scrape marks, which tell us the latch is hitting it instead of sliding in, and at the hinge side for gaps that show the door has dropped. We ask a resident upstairs to press release while one of us rests a hand flat on whichever door carries the strike, feeling whether it lets go. Only then do we take out a meter and walk to the cellar. In a building this age, that order saves an hour, because a door that has drifted out of line will defeat any new part, and a pair of doors that latch cleanly lets us go straight to the transformer and the riser with confidence. Then we label what we find, so the next visit, ours or anyone else's, starts from a map instead of a guess.

Commercial buzzer work in Borough Park falls into two groups: the residents' door of a building with a shop downstairs, and a business's own door, such as a staff entrance, an upstairs office or the side door of an institution. The main shopping street, about a mile of it inside Borough Park between 39th and 55th Streets, lined with Judaica, clothing, electronics, wig, book and kosher food stores, nearly all closed on the Sabbath. Walk-ups over the stores reach their apartments through narrow doors between shopfronts.

16th Avenue

A second shopping corridor and home to the 66th Precinct at 5822 16th Avenue. Mixed buildings put residents' doors and shop doors side by side on the same frontage.

18th Avenue

The third of the neighborhood's shopping streets, toward the Bensonhurst side. Upstairs offices and apartments buzz visitors in from the sidewalk past ground-floor stores.

New Utrecht Avenue

The avenue under the elevated West End line, with stations at 55th Street, 50th Street and Fort Hamilton Parkway. Street doors beneath the structure take the noise, dust and vibration of the trains.

Fort Hamilton Parkway

The western edge, with 1930s apartment buildings like 6623 Ft Hamilton Parkway and the D and N station at Fort Hamilton Parkway. Larger walk-ups here carry more apartments on a single entrance.

McDonald Avenue

The eastern edge along the F line, including the stretch near Caton Avenue where Boro Park 24 described the 2020 package thefts. Buildings near the elevated tracks share the same exposed-door conditions as New Utrecht Avenue.

Where store doors and residents' doors meet

In a building with a store below and apartments above, the street door is shared in practice even when it is not shared on paper. Deliveries for the shop, deliveries for the apartments and residents' visitors may all come through the same narrow entrance, and if the shop props the door for a morning delivery, the residents upstairs see a lock that "does not work." The power for the residents' buzzer may also come from a transformer in the store's cellar or back room, which is fine until the store changes hands, renovates, or keeps its back room locked on the day we come. Before any repair on a 13th, 16th or 18th Avenue building, we ask the owner who controls each space the system passes through and arrange access with the shop in advance. Because nearly every store on 13th Avenue closes for the Sabbath and holidays, and because our visits run on weekdays anyway, we plan these jobs for the days the shop is open and someone is there with a key.

Institutions, offices and upstairs suites

Brooklynjewish.org counts more than 300 religious institutions in Borough Park, and many of them, along with schools, offices and medical suites, have a door that is buzzed open during working hours. Those doors take heavy, bunched traffic: everyone arrives at once, and the release may be pressed dozens of times in a few minutes. That wears strikes and release buttons quickly, and it tempts staff to prop the door or loosen the closer. Our repair on those doors focuses on the hardware that takes the abuse, a strike rated for the traffic, a closer set so the door comes back steadily and latches, and a release button at the desk that does not stick, and we leave a note of what we set so the next person does not undo it.

Compliance notes for Borough Park buildings and businesses

  • Self-closing doors. Residential multiple dwellings with three or more apartments must keep self-closing doors working. HPD treats a defective one as a class C, immediately hazardous violation, with 14 days to correct after notice, and reinspects within 20 days of the certified correction date. Local Law 71 of 2022 added proactive inspections of these doors.
  • The intercom law. According to the NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide, multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments also need a two-way voice intercom with a door release. Older buildings must provide self-locking doors and an intercom if a majority of the apartments request it, and the landlord may recover the cost.
  • Electrically locked doors. Some shop and office doors use a magnetic lock instead of a strike. People inside must always be able to get out, usually through a sensor or release that cuts the lock's power, so we test the way out before the way in.
  • Power failure behavior. Allegion explains that a fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power and a fail-safe strike unlocks, and that with a strike, people inside can still leave by the lever. On a residents' door, fail-secure is the usual choice.
  • Reach height. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach for a control at 48 inches or less, so a replacement call button or desk release goes where a visitor or employee in a wheelchair can use it.
  • Line voltage. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work at line voltage. Our work stays on the low-voltage side; if a transformer needs a new circuit, the quote names it as an electrician's job.
Who Keeps It Working

Owners, Agents, Boards and Tenants: Who Answers for a Borough Park Buzzer

The first question at a dead buzzer is rarely technical. It is who has to fix it and how fast. In Borough Park, with three renters for every owner and many buildings held by LLCs or owners who are hard to reach, that question can stall a repair for weeks. This section lays out what the sources say. It is general information, not legal advice.

Rental buildings. In a building with tenants, the owner is responsible for keeping the entrance door and any entry system working. The Housing Maintenance Code is the rule HPD enforces, and HPD's violation records, including the ones at 5510 13 Avenue, cite section 27-2005 among others. Where an intercom or buzzer exists, HPD writes failures in wording like "properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system from the building entrance door to panel," which tells you that the whole path counts: the plate at the door, the wiring, the station in the cited apartment and the release.

Co-ops and condos. Ownership by the residents does not make the intercom optional. In Habitat Magazine's February 2024 Q&A, Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group says courts treat a working intercom as an essential service, and that "as a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The same piece notes that if a neighbor's renovation broke it, responsibility may shift to that neighbor under the alteration agreement, according to Andrew Freedland of Herrick Feinstein. For a Borough Park board, that means a failed panel is a governance item, not something to defer to next year's budget.

Two-family houses. Where the owner lives downstairs and rents the upstairs apartment, the owner books and pays for the repair. These houses usually fall outside the multiple-dwelling rules that start at three apartments, but a tenant whose bell is dead still has a working-entrance problem worth raising with the owner in writing.

When the owner is hard to reach. If the lobby notice lists an LLC or a managing agent, start there, in writing, with dates. HPD keeps registration information for residential buildings it regulates, and 311's apartment maintenance category takes complaints about "doorbells, buzzers, or intercoms that are broken or missing." Habitat's Q&A adds that a tenant or shareholder can bring an HP proceeding in Housing Court, and that "you do not need a lawyer to start an HP case." We are not part of that process. What we can do, once an owner or agent books us, is find the fault and leave a dated written note of what failed and what we repaired, which gives everyone the same facts.

What a tenant can do today. Write down which apartments ring, whether voice works both ways, what each door does when release is pressed, and when it started. Take the three photos described near the top of this page. Send them to the owner or agent. If they book us, send them to us too, and we can often tell the owner on the phone what kind of visit fits before anyone comes out.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Borough Park: Questions Answered

The vestibule and the inner door

HPD cited our first-floor vestibule door for a defective latch, and around the same time the buzzer stopped letting visitors all the way in. Is that one repair or two?

Usually it is one entrance with two faults that feed each other, and we fix them on the same visit. If the strike is on the street door, the visitor gets into the vestibule and then meets an inner door whose latch no longer catches or no longer retracts, so to them the buzzer "half works." If the strike is on the vestibule door itself, a latch that stops short of the keeper or drags on it can keep the strike from releasing at all. HPD's 2026 records at 5510 13 Avenue use almost exactly this wording for that building's vestibule door. We set the latch, keeper and closer first so the door closes and latches on its own, then test the release from several apartments. On the quote and the note, the latch repair and the buzzer repair are listed separately, so the owner can match each one to its own notice.

The vestibule door in our walk-up has an old mortise lock with a deadbolt that residents throw at night. Could that explain a buzzer that "only fails after dark"?

Yes, and it is one of the first things we check when a fault follows the clock. A strike can only free the latch. If someone has thrown the deadbolt on the mortise lock, the door stays locked no matter how many times an apartment presses release, and the visitor at the street reports a dead buzzer. The fix is a decision for the owner rather than a part: either the vestibule door stays on its latch alone and the deadbolt goes unused, or the strike moves to the street door and the vestibule door is left to latch and lock with keys. We explain both and change nothing until the owner chooses.

Our street door opens with the release, but the inner door only opens with a key. Was the inner door ever wired, or did someone disconnect it?

Either is possible, and we can usually tell within a few minutes. We look at the inner door's frame for an old strike pocket, filled screw holes or a capped pair of wires, which tell us it was once electrified and later changed. If there is nothing, it was most likely never wired, and the building was set up for the street door to carry the strike while the inner door latches. Whether to electrify both doors is an owner's choice. It means a second strike, a run of wire to it and a supply that can pull two strikes at once, and we quote it as a change to the system, not as a repair.

Owners, agents and work by others

The upstairs neighbor renovated their apartment, and since then every apartment above them has lost its bell. Who should pay for the repair?

That depends on the lease, the bylaws or the alteration agreement, not on us. Habitat's 2024 Q&A says responsibility for an intercom broken during a renovation may fall on the neighbor who did the work, and that the neighbor may in turn have a claim against their contractor. What we can supply is the fact pattern. When a renovation cuts or disturbs a riser, the silence starts at that floor and runs upward, and the damaged joint is usually inside the renovated walls. Our dated note records where we found the break and what we did to repair it, which is the evidence whoever pays will need.

Our building is owned by an LLC, and the managing agent says the intercom is "too old to fix." How do we know whether that is true?

Ask for the specific part that cannot be found, because an old system is not the same as an unrepairable one. In Brick Underground's guide, Daniel Vasquez says older systems such as Lee Dan are often outdated but usually have sound core wiring. In our experience the parts that wear out in a walk-up are the strike, the transformer, a few buttons and some stations, and most of those can still be replaced with compatible parts. A test visit tells the agent which parts have failed, which are aging and which are fine, in writing. If replacement really is the better answer, the note says so and why.

Busy doors, deliveries and worn parts

Couriers press every button on our plate until somebody buzzes them in. Is that wearing out the system, and what can be done about it?

It does wear the system, mostly at the buttons and the release, and it wears the strike too, because every successful press cycles it. The r/AskNYC thread on apartment bells has replies saying Amazon and UPS drivers ring every unit anyway and that an unlabeled button is the one strangers press most. The practical fixes are modest: replace worn or cracked buttons, label every button clearly so drivers can find the right apartment, and make sure the strike and closer are sized for the traffic. Whether residents should buzz in people they did not invite is a building rule, not a repair, but a plate where every name is readable cuts down the random presses.

One button on our plate sticks in after it is pressed, and that apartment's bell keeps ringing until someone pries it out. Is the whole plate going?

Not usually. A button that sticks is most often worn, cracked, swollen by moisture or gummed with grit from the street, and on most plates buttons can be replaced one at a time. While it sticks, it holds that apartment's circuit closed, which can overheat a small transformer and in some systems silence the other apartments too. Brick Underground's guide suggests cleaning buttons with a rubbing-alcohol-soaked swab, which is a reasonable thing for an owner to try on the outside face. If the button stays stuck, we replace it and check the transformer it was loading.

Old systems, enlargements and the transformer

Since two apartments were added on top of our house, the transformer in the cellar hums loudly and feels hot. Is it too small for the building now?

It may be. A bell transformer sized for two households can be asked to ring and release for four or five after an enlargement, and when it is overloaded or when one of the new runs has a partial short, it hums, runs hot and delivers less voltage when the strike pulls. We measure its output at rest and with the strike energized, check each new run for a fault, and if the transformer is simply too small, replace it with a correctly rated unit. If the circuit feeding it needs work, that part goes to a licensed electrician, and the quote says so.

After our house was enlarged, the newest apartments are the ones whose bells fail first. Why would the newest wiring be the weakest?

Because the weak point is usually not the new wire itself but the place where it was joined to the old. During an enlargement, new runs are often spliced onto the existing bell wire inside a wall or ceiling that is then closed up, sometimes with twisted and taped joints that loosen or corrode over a few years. Those joints sit between the working floors and the silent ones. We trace from the plate and from the silent apartment until we find the splice, remake it with sealed connectors in an accessible box where possible, and label it so nobody has to search for it again.

Our buzzer worked for years on its old system, and a handyman replaced one apartment's handset with a new one from the hardware store. Now that apartment buzzes but cannot talk. What happened?

The new handset probably is not made for that system. Older building audio systems from different makers use different numbers of wires and different ways of carrying voice and the release, and a station that fits the wall plate may not speak the same language as the rest of the line. Sometimes the mismatch only kills voice; sometimes it upsets neighbors on the same riser. We identify the system from the plate, the remaining stations and the wire count, fit a compatible station, and test the apartments above and below it as well.

Timing and the mechanical side of the door

Many residents in our building never use the electric release and come and go with keys. If the strike is replaced, will their keys still work exactly as before?

They should, and we test it before we leave. In a strike-and-latch setup, the key cylinder and the lever or knob work the latch mechanically, and the strike only adds a second way to free it electrically. A fail-secure strike stays locked without power, while the inside lever still opens the door, as Allegion's explanation of fail-secure hardware describes. The 2015 dispute at LeFrak City in Queens, where tenants sued after lobby doors were changed to electronic fobs, shows why that matters to people who keep Shabbat. A buzzer repair in Borough Park should never take away the plain key.

If the part we need has to be ordered, what state is our street door left in until it arrives?

Closed and locked. If the missing part is a station, a panel module or a discontinued transformer, we leave the street door and the vestibule door latching and locking by themselves, with keys working, and tell the owner exactly what still does not work, for example that two apartments cannot release the door until the station arrives. If the missing part is the strike itself, we set the door so it locks on its latch and opens with keys until the new strike goes in. We never leave a door propped or a closer disconnected to get around a missing part.

Is the full three-hour minimum charged even if the fix turns out to be a bent keeper that takes twenty minutes?
+

Yes, the minimum is three hours at $195 an hour, $585, however quickly the fault is found. We use the remaining time to test every apartment, both doors and the supply, and to note anything else that is wearing, so the visit leaves the whole entrance checked rather than one part.

The stores on our block are open on Sunday. Can a technician come then, when the shop downstairs can let him into the back room?
+

No. Our technicians work weekdays, early in the day, in a two-hour arrival window, and we do not schedule weekend visits. We arrange with the owner and the shop for someone with the back-room key to be there on the weekday we book.

Our building has a front entrance and a separate basement entrance under the stoop. Is each door a separate visit?
+

No. Both entrances can be tested and repaired on the same visit at the same hourly rate. We treat each door as its own circuit, because a wet button at the basement door can take down the front bells, and the note lists what we found at each.

Can you tell on the phone whether our building needs a repair or a whole new system?
+

Often we can tell which is likely from the photos and the pattern of working and silent apartments. A firm answer comes after the test visit, when we know which parts have failed and whether compatible replacements still exist. The written note says which case you are in and why.

Do you work on Aiphone, Comelit and old Lee Dan systems, or only on equipment you sell?
+

We troubleshoot and repair Aiphone, Comelit, Siedle, TekTone, Lee Dan, Akuvox, DoorBird and ButterflyMX equipment, along with unbranded and older audio systems. Repairing a brand does not make us its certified dealer. Where a station is discontinued, we look for a compatible replacement before suggesting a new system.

Will the managing agent get something in writing to send to HPD after the repair?
+

Yes. The owner or agent receives a dated note of what failed, what we repaired or replaced, which apartments we tested and how both doors were left. Certifying a correction to HPD is the owner's step; the note gives them the facts to do it.

Does fixing the buzzer at our street door need a Department of Buildings permit?
+

Our work stays on the low-voltage side of the transformer and at the door hardware, where we work under NYS license #12000287431. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work at line voltage, so if the transformer needs a new circuit or outlet, that part is quoted as an electrician's job.

Where are your technicians dispatched from, and which rate applies to a Borough Park address?
+

Our Brooklyn office is at 1282 Troy Ave, and Borough Park is billed at the standard Brooklyn rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum.

Who needs to be home on the day of the repair?
+

Someone who can open the cellar or wherever the transformer is, and ideally a person in each affected apartment for the final test. Where tenants cannot be home, a neighbor or the super can answer the station, and the owner arranges access to the rest. We ask the owner to give tenants notice.

Can you leave a label inside the cellar showing which wire goes to which apartment?
+

Yes, and we do it as a matter of course. Every box, splice and supply we open is labeled with the apartments it serves, so the next repair, by us or anyone else, starts from a map instead of a search.
