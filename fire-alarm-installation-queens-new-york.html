The Borough

Why Queens Buildings Need Commercial Fire Alarm Installation Planned Around the Borough

Queens buildings need commercial fire alarm installation planned around the borough because almost no other part of the city mixes so many occupancies on so few blocks. A single walk down Jackson Avenue in Long Island City passes a new apartment tower, a converted factory full of offices, an auto shop and a film stage. Each of those buildings sits in a different part of the NYC Building Code, each one has a different trigger for a required fire alarm, and each one ends up in front of the same FDNY plan examiners and the same Fire Alarm Inspection Unit. A commercial fire alarm system is the detectors, manual pull stations, control panel, notification appliances and monitoring link that find a fire early, move people out and call the fire department. In Queens, designing that system well starts with knowing which kind of Queens building you are actually standing in.

Growth is the first reason the borough keeps fire alarm crews busy. The Department of City Planning's Housing Production Snapshot, reported by QNS in May 2018, found that Long Island City completed about 2,800 housing units in 2017, more than any other neighborhood in the five boroughs, with another 5,900 units in the Department of Buildings permit pipeline. Those towers rose next to the 778-foot Skyline Tower at 23-15 44th Drive, the tallest building in Queens, with 67 stories and 802 condominium apartments according to CityRealty. High rise residential buildings of that size carry voice evacuation, a fire command center, elevator recall and smoke control interfaces that all have to be filed, installed and tested together, and the retail and amenity floors at their base keep generating tenant fit-outs long after the ribbon cutting.

Industry is the second reason. NYCEDC lists seven Industrial Business Zones in Queens: Jamaica, JFK, Long Island City, Maspeth, Ridgewood, Steinway and Woodside. These are the borough's warehouses, food plants, printers, distribution centers, bus depots, scrapyards and auto shops, and they burn in ways an office does not. QNS reported a second-alarm scrapyard fire between Woodward and Onderdonk Avenues in Maspeth on July 28, 2025, with 106 firefighters and EMS personnel responding, and a third-alarm fire on March 11, 2025 at an auto body shop at 127-02 35th Avenue in Willets Point, where FDNY Deputy Chief Christopher Ritchie told reporters, "There was a lot of tires and debris in the rear." Large open fire areas, high racks and stored combustibles are exactly the conditions where warehouse smoke detection installation, beam detection and sprinkler monitoring earn their keep.

The third reason is the street-level economy. Queens has blocks of restaurants under apartments on Roosevelt Avenue, Main Street, 37th Avenue and Steinway Street, and a fast-growing trade in e-bikes and the batteries that power them. On July 4, 2025, an e-bike battery parked outside a restroom at a Queens pizzeria exploded, and a 76-year-old woman died in what the FDNY called the first lithium-ion battery fire death of 2025. At the briefing afterward, FDNY Commissioner Robert S. Tucker said, "These fires are treacherous. They move very quickly. They are very hot." ABC7 reported that King Electronic Hub, a shop on the ground floor of a mixed-use building at 119-07 Liberty Avenue in South Richmond Hill, caught fire on October 17, 2025, after the FDNY had already traced an earlier fire there, in January 2024, to a lithium-ion battery. A fire alarm cannot stop a battery from failing, but in a mixed-use building early detection on the commercial floor is what buys the apartments above their minutes.

The FDNY approval path for a Queens fire alarm, step by step

The approval path for a Queens fire alarm is the same one the FDNY uses in every borough, and it starts with design, not installation. Here it is in order, with what each step looks like in practice.

Step 1: a design professional confirms the requirement and seals the drawings

A New York State Professional Engineer or Registered Architect reads the NYC Building Code, mainly Section 907, against your occupancy, height, occupant load and floor area, and decides when a building needs a fire alarm system and what kind. That person designs and seals the fire alarm construction drawings: device locations, riser diagram, battery calculations, circuit classes and the input and output matrix. This is where the fire alarm system design requirements are settled, and it is the single most important hire on the job. Does my building need a fire alarm permit? If the work adds, removes or changes fire alarm devices, the answer is almost always yes; the fire alarm permit requirements in New York City run through this design professional and the FDNY filing that follows.

Step 2: bring in the fire alarm vendor

Next comes the installer: a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, whose principal holds an FDNY S-97 Certificate of Fitness. The FDNY's company certificate FAQ says the S-97 requires NICET Level II or higher in fire alarm systems or a NYC master or special electrician license. We coordinate this step and work under our NYS license #12000287431; we do not claim to hold the FDNY company certificate ourselves, and we tell you exactly who the certificate holder of record is on your filing so you can verify it.

Step 3: TM-1 plan examination on FDNY Business

The design professional files a TM-1 fire alarm plan examination application with the certified plans and the building's Certificate of Occupancy or equivalent documents through FDNY Business. The FDNY says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks, and that an objection should be answered within six months to avoid a new filing fee. Once approved, the job has its Project Authorization, the FDNY fire alarm system approval of the plans that works as the fire alarm installation permit for the fire alarm side of the job. Electrical work authorization through the Department of Buildings runs alongside it.

Step 4: install only to the approved plans

Fire alarm wiring installation, fire alarm conduit installation, device mounting and fire alarm cable installation follow the approved drawings exactly. Field changes go back to the design professional and, where needed, to the FDNY as an amendment.

Step 5: monitoring, pre-test and the FDNY acceptance test

Before the test, the central station monitoring contract must be in place and the connection active, the panel must be free of alarm, supervisory and trouble signals, and every interface must work. The owner requests the test, and the FDNY Fire Alarm Inspection Unit, which the FDNY lists at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn, sends an inspector to witness the fire alarm acceptance inspection in your Queens building. The fire alarm system inspection requirements for that visit are listed in the FDNY tip sheet: install 100 percent complete, permanent power, a clear panel and every document on site.

Step 6: the Letter of Approval

A passing test leads to the FDNY Letter of Approval. The FDNY puts the full path, from plan submission to approval, at about 20 to 24 weeks on average. Keep the letter, the as-builts and every test record in the building.

Compliance notes Queens owners ask about

  • Testing standard. The FDNY tests systems to NFPA 72 as modified by NYC Building Code Appendix Q, so NFPA 72 fire alarm installation, NFPA 72 fire alarm inspection and NFPA 72 fire alarm testing all read through the city's amendments.
  • Fans and duct detectors. The FDNY's tip sheet says fans over 2,000 cfm must shut down on any automatic alarm, which drives fire alarm duct detector integration on the rooftop units common in Queens retail and offices.
  • Carbon monoxide. CO detection at CO-producing equipment is a frequent FDNY drawing comment, and it matters in Flushing and Jamaica buildings with underground garages.
  • Elevators. Recall on waterflow or on elevator lobby, hoistway or machine room detectors must work at the test, which needs the elevator mechanic on site.
  • Accessibility. Fire alarm visual notification installation is designed into the drawings, not added after a complaint.
  • Landmarks. Jackson Heights, Hunters Point and other Queens historic districts can add Landmarks Preservation Commission review where work touches protected facades or interiors.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The Warehouse That Grew a Mezzanine

A situation our technicians meet often in the Queens industrial zones goes like this. A logistics tenant takes over a one-story steel warehouse with an approved fire alarm from its last occupant: a conventional panel, a few spot smoke detectors near the office, waterflow and tamper switches on the risers, and horn strobes along the walls. Six months later the tenant has added a steel mezzanine for packing, racking to within a few feet of the roof, and a battery charging area for forklifts. Nobody thought of it as fire alarm work.

The panel still shows normal, but the system no longer matches the building. The strobes on the walls cannot be seen from under the mezzanine, the racking blocks the smoke detectors' coverage, and the charging area has no detection at all. The fix is not a few extra devices hung from the steel. It goes back to the design professional, who redraws the system for the new layout, usually with beam detection across the bays and new notification under the mezzanine, and files it with the FDNY as a modification. We tell tenants the same thing every time: when the floor plan changes, the fire alarm drawings change first, and the cheapest day to call is before the racking goes up.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Queens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Queens owners, tenants and managers bring to our first call, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Each answer is short; call (347) 434-6392 for the version that fits your address.

Towers and offices in Long Island City

Our Court Square office lease says the landlord's fire alarm vendor must do all device work. Can we still bring our own installer?

Usually only for the parts the lease allows. Many Long Island City office leases name a base building vendor for anything that touches the panel's programming, while the tenant's contractor handles cable, back boxes and devices under the same filed drawings. Read that clause before you sign, and ask the landlord for the vendor's name so the design professional can include them from day one.

Why does a Hunters Point condo amenity floor need a separate fire alarm filing after the building already opened?

Because the amenity build-out came later than the base system approval. A gym, roof lounge or co-working room finished after the building's Letter of Approval adds devices and changes the matrix, so the design professional files it as a modification and the FDNY tests it again. Developers who leave amenity floors for later should expect a second fire alarm acceptance test.

The tower's fire command center sits in the lobby. Does our ground-floor restaurant connect to it?

Almost always. In a high rise, the restaurant's smoke and heat detection, hood suppression monitoring and notification normally report to the building's panel so the fire command center shows the restaurant as a distinct area. The restaurant's engineer and the building's vendor agree on the addresses and the voice messages before the work is filed.

Warehouses, cargo and industrial space

Our Springfield Gardens freight building has 36-foot ceilings. Are ordinary smoke detectors allowed up there?

Spot smoke detectors can technically be mounted at that height, but they are slow to see smoke that stratifies under a hot roof and hard to test and clean. New Construction and Tenant Fit-Outs in Queens

New construction fire alarm installation in Queens works best when the fire alarm is drawn with the rest of the building, not after the walls are framed. As a commercial construction fire alarm contractor, we join the general contractor's schedule at rough-in and stay through the acceptance test. Fire alarm rough in follows the approved drawings: back boxes, fire alarm conduit installation where required, and fire alarm cable pulling before drywall, followed by device termination, programming and testing once finishes are done.

Tenant improvement fire alarm installation is the everyday version of the same work. A new restaurant in a Tangram storefront, a medical office in Jamaica, a production office near Kaufman Astoria or a shop in Queens Center each changes the building's fire alarm. The tenant's design professional prepares fire alarm shop drawings and the filing for the space; the landlord's vendor programs the base panel; and the tenant's installer does the field work. Coordinating those three is most of our job on a fit-out.

A few habits save Queens projects weeks. Ask for the base building's fire alarm drawings and Letter of Approval before design starts. Get the landlord's vendor to confirm panel capacity, spare addresses and notification power. Keep the field team on the approved drawings and record every field change for the as-builts. Book the central station connection early, because the FDNY's own document guidance recommends long lead times for test requests and central station changes. And schedule the elevator mechanic and plumber for the test day the moment the FDNY gives a date.

FAQ

Queens Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Does a Queens fire alarm go through a different FDNY office than one in Manhattan?
+

No. Fire alarm plans for every borough are filed with the FDNY through FDNY Business on a TM-1, and the FDNY Fire Alarm Inspection Unit lists its office at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn. The inspection itself takes place at your Queens building.

Can a Queens store owner hire a fire alarm company directly, or does the landlord have to?
+

Either can, but the landlord usually controls the base building system and has to agree. In a Queens strip or mixed-use building the store's devices often tie into the landlord's panel, so the lease, the landlord's vendor and the engineer all need to be on the same page before drawings are sealed.

Our Long Island City office floor sits inside a building with its own fire alarm vendor. Who files for our fit-out?
+

The engineer or architect hired for your fit-out designs and files the fire alarm work for your floor, and the installer coordinates with the base building vendor who holds the panel programming. We help line those parties up and keep the drawings, the panel database and the FDNY filing consistent.

Is a JFK-area cargo warehouse inside the airport handled the same way as one on Rockaway Boulevard?
+

Not exactly. Buildings on Port Authority property at JFK and LaGuardia follow the Port Authority's own tenant construction review in addition to fire safety requirements, while warehouses on private lots nearby follow the normal FDNY and Department of Buildings path. Tell us which side of the airport line the building is on before the survey.

Do Queens co-op boards need to approve fire alarm work in the common areas?
+

In practice, yes. The board or its managing agent controls access to the basement, boiler room, roof and hallways, and most proprietary leases require board consent for work in common areas. Bring the managing agent in early so the survey, the drawings and the work schedule are approved together.

What should a Flushing restaurant owner have ready for the first fire alarm visit?
+

The lease or landlord contact, any existing FDNY Letter of Approval for the space, the hood suppression company's name and last service tag, photos of any panel or pull station already there, and the number of seats. Those details tell the engineer whether the restaurant needs its own system or ties into the building's.

Will an FDNY fire alarm acceptance test shut down our Queens hotel's elevators?
+

Briefly, yes. The test proves elevator recall, so each car is recalled to its designated floor and then released. We schedule with the elevator company and the hotel so the recall sequence runs at a low-occupancy hour and guests are warned in advance.

Can Abstract Enterprises Security Systems work in a Queens hospital that stays open around the clock?
+

We work in occupied buildings under the facility's own interim life safety rules, phased so that no area loses protection without the facility's approval and any required fire watch. The hospital's engineering department, its FDNY-certified fire alarm company and the design professional set those rules; we follow them.

Is there a fee to have a fire alarm Notice of Defect reviewed in New York City?
+

Yes. The FDNY's FA-27 instructions list a $105 fee for submitting documents in answer to a fire alarm Notice of Defect, which is sent to the FDNY plan intake office. Once the correction is approved the FDNY issues a Letter of Approval.

Do you service fire alarms in Queens buildings that were installed by another company?
+

Yes. Many Queens buildings change managing agents or owners and inherit a panel from a vendor nobody remembers. We start with the panel brand, the Letter of Approval and the as-built drawings, and tell you plainly whether the existing system can be serviced, needs parts or needs a replacement filing.

Is the FDNY S-97 certificate the same thing as a NYC electrician license?
+

No. The S-97 is an FDNY Certificate of Fitness held by a principal of an FDNY-certified fire alarm company. To qualify, the FDNY requires NICET Level II or higher in fire alarm systems or a NYC master or special electrician license, so an electrician's license can be the route to an S-97 but is not the S-97 itself.
