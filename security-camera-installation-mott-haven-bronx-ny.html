The Neighborhood

Why Mott Haven Properties Need Security Cameras That Hold Up When the Building Doesn't

Most camera pages talk about crime first. In Mott Haven we start somewhere else, because the calls we get here so often begin with a building system that failed and left people with no record of what happened while it was down. In February 2025 News 12 reported that tenants at 138 Bruckner Boulevard, a lottery building that opened in 2024 with a pool, basketball courts and a courtyard, had been without heat, power and a working elevator for more than 24 hours, and that their electronic key fobs would not let them into the building. Con Edison said a cable on one of four transformers had failed and that it was the building's only feeder. A building in that state has doors nobody can lock and lobbies nobody is watching, and if its cameras share the same dead power, it also has no video. That is the first thing we design against in Mott Haven: cameras and a recorder that keep running when the rest of the building stops.

The second thing is who is actually watching. Mott Haven has more organized, building-wide camera systems than most Bronx neighborhoods, and they vary enormously in how well they are used. The Diego Beekman Mutual Housing Association, which owns and runs 38 affordable buildings in Mott Haven after tenants took ownership in 2003, operates cameras across a 12-block zone that THE CITY reported had helped identify suspects in a shooting at 141st Street and Beekman Avenue. Its chief executive, Arline Parks, put it simply: "We monitor everything." A few blocks away, the Mott Haven Herald reported years earlier that cameras at Moore Houses on East 149th Street fed monitors that residents said no one watched, and that footage was routinely deleted. "There are no consequences because nobody's watching them," said Cheryl Cross of the Moore Houses tenant board. The hardware in both places is similar. The difference is the routine around it, and that routine is part of every system we hand over.

The third thing is permission. The NYU Furman Center's profile of Mott Haven and Melrose puts the homeownership rate at 6.1 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide, and public housing makes up 24.1 percent of rental units. Most people who want a camera here rent, and the wall they want it on belongs to someone else. In May 2024 News 12 reported that management at a Mott Haven apartment building gave residents a deadline to take cameras off their apartment doors; one tenant, Maria Bellechea, took down her Ring doorbell camera and said she felt more in danger without it. A tenant-rights advocate from Bronx Legal Services told residents to "review the lease." That is good advice, and it is where our renter conversations begin. On the rowhouse blocks the permission question goes further: the Landmarks Preservation Commission sets how many cameras a front can carry and how much conduit may show.

Then there are the reasons people finally call. The 40th Precinct, which covers Mott Haven, Port Morris and Melrose, reported 628 retail thefts so far in 2026, down from 712 a year earlier, but 74 in the latest four-week window against 46 in the same weeks of 2025. Robbery in that four-week window was up from 40 to 50, even though the year-to-date count is lower. In April 2024 a masked man robbed the Gourmet Deli on Willis Avenue at gunpoint at lunchtime, and the store's own video, which News 12 obtained, showed him point the gun and go over the counter. At The Hub, the Third Avenue BID is putting up new cameras at 10 locations chosen from 311 and 911 complaint data, along with 75 solar lights. Stores, buildings and families here are asking the same practical question: what will a camera actually record, and what will it take to keep it recording?

River Towers and Lottery Buildings

The New Towers on the Harlem River and Bruckner Boulevard: Cameras That Don't Depend on the Building's Other Systems

The southwest corner of Mott Haven has changed faster than any other part of the neighborhood. The city's Small Business Services assessment of the Mott Haven commercial district described "a dozen new mixed-income residential towers" being built, "mostly in the southwestern corner along the Harlem River and next to the bridges." Brookfield's Bankside plan alone straddles the Third Avenue Bridge with 1,350 apartments in towers of 25, 25 and 17 stories. Lottery buildings with pools, courtyards, gyms and roof decks have opened along Bruckner Boulevard. These buildings arrive with cameras, fob readers, smart intercoms, package rooms and a building network, often all from one integrator and all on one set of circuits. That is convenient on opening day and fragile on the day something fails.

One feeder, one network, one point of failure

The 138 Bruckner Boulevard outage is the clearest local example of what a single point of failure looks like. When the one feeder failed, the building lost power, heat and the elevator, and residents could not get in with their fobs. From a camera installer's point of view, the important question about a building like that is what kept recording. If the recorder, the camera switch and the fob controller all sit on the same unprotected circuit in the same closet, the cameras go dark at exactly the moment the doors stop locking. We size a battery unit for the recorder, the switch that powers the cameras and the internet equipment, so the cameras at the street doors and the lobby keep recording through an ordinary outage, and we tell management in writing how long that battery is expected to carry the load. For a longer outage, the building's own generator plan decides what stays up, and we wire the camera equipment to whatever protected circuit the building's electrician provides.

Keep the cameras apart from the fob system

Many new buildings run their cameras inside the same software as their fob readers and their intercom. That is fine until the platform has a problem, the subscription lapses or the integrator stops answering. A camera system that can record on its own recorder, with its own logins and its own switch, keeps working while the access system is being fixed. Where a building wants the camera picture to appear next to fob entries, we connect the two at the software level, so either side can fail without taking the other down. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, already sets rules for the data that smart access systems collect from tenants, including consent and deleting authentication data within 90 days; keeping cameras on their own recorder also keeps the camera footage policy separate and easier to explain.

Amenities, package rooms and the leasing office

Pools, courtyards, basketball courts and roof decks are shared spaces, and residents use them in ways they would not want recorded closely. We keep cameras on the entrances to amenity spaces and on equipment worth protecting, and away from changing areas, showers and anywhere people would reasonably expect privacy. Package rooms get a camera that shows the face of whoever opens a locker or lifts a box off a shelf, because a camera on the ceiling looking straight down shows only the top of a head. The leasing office and the mailroom usually need separate logins, so staff see the views their job needs and nothing else.

Wind, glass and height on the river

The waterfront towers present technical problems that older Mott Haven buildings don't. Lobbies are glass on two or three sides, so a door camera faces a bright street behind every face and needs a sensor that handles backlight. Corner cameras high on a tower take wind straight off the river, so mounts are chosen for weight and stiffness rather than looks, and long-range lenses are avoided where a small sway would blur the picture. Renters who want a camera on a balcony or terrace in a tower need management's written permission first, because the railing and the exterior wall are the building's, not the apartment's.

Mutual Housing and Public Housing

Diego Beekman, Moore Houses and St. Mary's Park: What Mott Haven Has Already Learned About Building Cameras

Mott Haven is one of the few places where the whole range of building camera programs can be seen within a short walk: a tenant-owned housing company running its own camera room, public housing complexes that received cameras through City Council money, residents who bought their own cameras because the building's were not enough, and now a police department connecting public housing cameras to its own software. Each one has lessons for a private owner, a co-op board or a tenant association deciding what to put in.

The Diego Beekman model: one network, staffed

The Diego Beekman Mutual Housing Association owns and operates 38 affordable buildings in Mott Haven, and its cameras cover a 12-block zone that includes 141st Street at Beekman Avenue, Cypress Avenue and Powers Avenue. A Powers Avenue resident told THE CITY, in Spanish, "It was the cameras. They've had a lot of impact." The same reporting quoted Albert Fox Cahn of the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project warning that "most of the time police don't even need a warrant because the landlords will hand over the footage voluntarily." Both points matter. A connected, staffed system across many buildings can change a block, and the organization running it needs a written policy on who sees footage, how long it is kept and when it is handed to anyone outside. We build multi-building systems so each building records locally, a central room can see what it needs, and every handover is logged.

The Moore Houses lesson: cameras need a routine

The Mott Haven Herald reported that five Mott Haven NYCHA complexes received cameras, electronic keys and door locks from $3.2 million allocated in 2011 by then-Councilwoman Maria del Carmen Arroyo, and that at Moore Houses the camera feeds went to monitors at 664 East 149th Street that residents said nobody watched. The precinct's housing commander explained that live monitoring at scale was out of reach: "The volume of people we would need to monitor it would be astronomical." Most private buildings are in the same position. Nobody will watch a wall of screens all night. What makes cameras worth having then is a routine: alerts aimed only at the doors that matter, a named person who checks those alerts, a weekly check that every camera is recording, and a clear way to save and export a clip the morning after something happens.

St. Mary's Park Houses: when residents buy their own

ABC7 reported in 2021 that residents at St. Mary's Park Houses had put up motion-detection cameras outside their own doors after people took food deliveries and slept in the stairwells, and that both building entrances did not lock properly. In 2025 News 12 reported a Mott Haven NYCHA resident who bought a Ring camera after several packages were stolen and described a broken mailbox, a flooded lobby and hallway lights out. Those residents were solving a real problem with what they could buy. In public housing, though, a resident does not control the hallway or the door frame, and NYCHA handles repairs to common areas through its Customer Contact Center and the MyNYCHA app. We are glad to help a NYCHA resident choose and set up a camera inside the apartment. We don't mount equipment on NYCHA common walls or doors for residents.

Police access to public housing cameras

New York Focus reported in October 2025 that connections made through Big Apple Connect, the city's free internet program for public housing residents, had linked 68 cameras at one NYCHA development to NYPD software, and that the NYPD planned 1,900 cameras across 19 more properties by the end of 2025 and 17,897 cameras across 119 developments in a later phase. The reporting did not name which developments. For a private owner across the street, the lesson is straightforward: a building's cameras go only where the owner decides, footage leaves the building only under a written policy, and the camera network is kept separate from residents' internet service.

Renters and Door Cameras

Renting in Mott Haven: Door Cameras, Leases and the Camera You Can Keep

Because so few Mott Haven households own their homes, a large share of the people who ask us about cameras are tenants, and the News 12 story from May 2024 is a fair picture of what can go wrong. Management gave residents a deadline to remove cameras from their apartment doors. One tenant said a gun had once been pulled on her inside the building and that she would have kept her camera up if not for the fear of a violation or a fine. The advice she was given, to read the lease for anything that says you can't have a camera on your door, is exactly where we begin.

Start with the lease and a written question

The Legal Aid Society notes that most leases prohibit tenants from making alterations without the landlord's prior permission. The apartment door, its frame and the hallway outside belong to the building. Before anything goes on them, a tenant should ask management in writing and keep the answer. If management says yes, the note should name the spot, the mount and whether drilling is allowed. If the answer is no, the camera goes inside.

The camera that never needs permission

An indoor camera on a shelf or a small bracket, aimed at the inside of your own front door, records anyone who comes through that door and touches no common area. It runs on your own internet, stores clips on a card or in the maker's cloud, and leaves with you when you move. In a new tower with thin interior walls and strong Wi-Fi, this is easy. In a pre-war walk-up with thick plaster and masonry, we test the signal at the door before recommending any camera, because Wi-Fi that works beside the router can fail three rooms away.

When neighbors want the same thing

When several households in one building are each asking to cover the same vestibule, stair or mailbox wall, the stronger answer is usually a building-owned system with a written policy. A tenant association can bring the owner a short list of the spots that worry residents, the incidents that prompted it and the hours that matter. We can price exactly those spots so the owner has a concrete number to approve or reject, and the owner decides.

Landmarked Blocks

Cameras on Alexander Avenue, Mott Haven East and the Bertine Block: Working Within the Landmarks Rules

Mott Haven has three city historic districts. The Mott Haven Historic District, designated on July 29, 1969, centers on Alexander Avenue, the street the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report notes was once called "the Irish Fifth Avenue." Mott Haven East and the Bertine Block were both designated on April 5, 1994; the Bertine Block alone holds 31 buildings, developed between 1877 and 1899, on both sides of East 136th Street between Willis Avenue and Brown Place. Owners on these blocks want cameras for the same reasons everyone else does. They also have to get a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission for anything on the front.

What the guidebook actually says

Chapter 11 of the LPC Permit Guidebook covers security cameras directly. "Security cameras must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance." For small residential or commercial buildings, "no more than two security cameras can be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage," and larger buildings may have three. "Installation of equipment cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists." Conduit has to be concealed, and if that isn't possible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit. Equipment is attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or concrete paving, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and the application includes the manufacturer's cut sheet for the camera.

How that shapes a rowhouse plan

On a Mott Haven Historic District rowhouse, the best camera positions are usually the ones Landmarks would prefer anyway: inside the vestibule looking out through the door glass, under the stoop or within the areaway, or on a side or rear wall. Where a front camera is the only way to see the door, we choose a small dome finished to match the masonry, set it in a mortar joint, and run the cable straight back through the wall so almost no conduit shows. The permit application is filed through LPC's Portico system, and staff can issue security camera permits when the installation meets the guidebook's criteria.

Rowhouses that are now apartments

Many of the district's houses are divided into apartments. That brings the renter questions above onto a landmarked block: the owner approves the cameras, the tenants are told what each camera covers, and nobody's apartment door is on someone else's screen. In a house with a garden apartment and two floors above, we often put the recorder in the cellar, cover the street door, the areaway and the yard, and give each household a login to the shared entrance only.

The fieldstone church and the piano factory

Mott Haven's individually notable buildings bring their own constraints. St. Ann's Church, built of fieldstone in 1841 by Gouverneur Morris Jr. as a memorial to his mother, sits in a churchyard. The former Estey Piano Company factory at Lincoln Avenue and Bruckner Boulevard, standing since 1886 and the oldest known former piano factory in the Bronx, is now loft housing. On buildings like these we favor cameras on gates, fences, rear walls and interior entrances, and keep the historic face of the building clear.

Riverfront and Bruckner towers: protect the head end first

In a new tower the camera locations are usually obvious: every street door, the lobby, the package room, the elevator landings at the lobby and the garage, the amenity doors, the stair doors at the ground floor and the roof, and the loading entrance. The design work goes into the head end. We want the recorder, the camera switch and the internet equipment in a locked room with a battery unit sized to carry them, a clock that sets itself from the network, and alerts that reach a named staff member. In buildings where the integrator's platform runs everything, we audit what records where and recommend a separate recorder for the entrances if a single platform failure would blind the whole building. Cable runs in the building's existing pathways from the head end to each floor's closet, so almost nothing new shows in finished halls.

Rehabilitated walk-ups: the stair is the job

Mott Haven's walk-ups, including the rehabilitated buildings run by mutual housing companies, share a pattern: a street door into a small vestibule, mailboxes on one wall, a stair that turns at every landing, a cellar door at the back and a roof door at the top. The core plan is a face-height camera inside the vestibule looking at the street door, one on the mailboxes, one at the top of each flight looking down so it sees faces coming up, one at the cellar door and one at the roof door. In a six-story building we often cover alternate landings first and add the rest later. The recorder goes in a locked cabinet in the cellar, off the floor and away from the boiler, on its own battery. Holes through floors are fire-stopped, and cable rises in a stair corner in painted raceway or through an existing chase.

Private buildings facing public housing: aim at your own doors

Many private buildings in Mott Haven face, or share a block with, NYCHA developments. Patterson Houses alone has 15 buildings of six and 13 stories and about 1,790 apartments. A private building's cameras should cover its own door, its own sidewalk frontage and its own yard, and stop there. We don't aim cameras into NYCHA grounds, walkways or windows, and we draw privacy masks over any part of a view that would show into someone's home. If a private building and a development share a problem, such as a path between them, the owner should speak to NYCHA management rather than extend a camera across the line.

Historic district rowhouses: inside out

On a landmarked rowhouse we work from the inside out. A camera inside the vestibule, looking through the door glass, sees the stoop and the face of anyone at the door without touching the facade. A small camera within the areaway or under the stoop covers the garden-level door. The rear yard gets a camera on the back wall. Only when a front camera is truly needed do we put one on the facade, and then in a mortar joint, small and color-matched, with the cable passing straight back through the wall. Every front camera goes through an LPC permit before it goes up, and we prepare the cut sheet and a marked photo for the owner's application.

Mixed-use corners: two owners, two systems

Where a store sits under apartments, the shopkeeper and the building owner each need cameras, and they should not share a recorder. The store watches its door, counter, aisles and back room; the building watches its residential door, mailboxes and stair. Each system has its own logins and its own cable path, the store's through the store ceiling and the building's through the stair or cellar. Field Notes

Field Notes: The Power Question We Ask First on a Mott Haven Walk-Through

On a Mott Haven visit the first room we ask to see is not the lobby. It is wherever the building's internet, fob controller, intercom power and any existing recorder live, usually a closet off the lobby in a new tower, a corner of the cellar in a walk-up, or a back office in a store. We look at what plugs into what: whether the recorder, the camera switch and the modem share one power strip, whether there is a battery unit and how old it is, whether the fob system's controller sits on the same circuit, and whether the clock on the recorder is right. A recorder that loses its clock in an outage keeps recording but stamps every clip with the wrong date, which is why the clock is the first thing we read on the screen.

Then we walk the doors in the order a visitor would use them, from the sidewalk to the vestibule to the lobby, the package room, the stairs, the cellar door and the roof door, or in a store from the front door to the counter to the aisles to the back. At each spot we note the light by day and ask about the light by night. Last, we ask who has to approve: management, the board, the housing company, the landlord or Landmarks. The written quote follows within a business day, with the battery and its expected runtime on its own line.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Mott Haven: Questions Answered

New towers and lottery buildings

When the key fobs at a new Bruckner Boulevard building stopped working during a power failure, the street door could not be locked. How can cameras keep a record of everyone who walks in while that system is down?

Give the cameras their own power and their own recorder. The cameras at the street doors and the lobby, the switch that powers them and the recorder should sit on a battery unit sized to carry them, separate from the fob controller, so they keep recording while the access system is dead. Put a face-height camera inside each street door, aimed at people coming in, rather than relying on the reader's own camera. Then, after any outage, check that the recorder's clock is still right, because a reset clock stamps every clip from the outage with the wrong time.

Our lottery building has a pool, a courtyard and a basketball court shared by every tenant. Which of those spaces can cameras cover, and which should they stay out of?

Cover the doors into each amenity space, the gates of the courtyard, the court itself from a distance and any equipment worth protecting. Keep cameras out of changing rooms, showers, restrooms and anywhere people reasonably expect privacy, and keep pool deck views wide rather than close. Management should post which amenity spaces are recorded and write down who may view that footage, because a pool camera that staff can browse at will is a complaint waiting to happen.

I rent on a high floor of one of the waterfront towers and want a camera on my balcony. Is that allowed, and will the wind off the river spoil the picture?

First get management's written permission, because the balcony railing and the exterior wall belong to the building, not the apartment. If they agree, use a small, light camera on a stiff mount close to the wall or ceiling rather than one hanging out on a long arm, and choose a wide or moderate lens; a long zoom magnifies every bit of sway. Run it on your own internet. Often a better answer is an indoor camera behind the balcony door, which sees anyone coming in from outside and needs nobody's approval.

Mutual housing, public housing and shared systems

The owners of several buildings on our block want one camera network covering all of them, the way Diego Beekman covers twelve blocks around 141st Street. Can separate owners share a system without seeing into each other's buildings?

Yes, if it is designed for it. Each building keeps its own recorder and its own cameras, so its footage stays in its own cellar, and a shared viewing station or account sees only the outward-facing views the owners agree on, such as street doors and sidewalks. Interior halls, stairs and mailrooms stay visible only to that building's own people. The owners sign a short written agreement on who watches, how long video is kept and who may hand a clip to anyone outside, and each owner pays for its own share on a separate line.

Moore Houses residents said their cameras fed monitors nobody watched. If no one in our building can watch the cameras live, how do we make sure the recordings actually get used?

Build a routine instead of a screen. Send alerts only from the few doors that matter, after hours, to one named person. Have that person check once a week that every camera is recording and that the clock is right. When something happens, the super saves the clip that morning, before it is overwritten, and writes down the date, time and camera. That routine costs nothing extra and is what separates a system that helps from one that only looks busy.

Our tenant association wants to ask the owner to put cameras in the lobby and on the stairs. What should the written request include so the owner can get a price?

List the exact spots, such as the street door, the mailboxes, each stair landing or the cellar door, with a sentence on what happened at each one and roughly when. Say which hours matter most, who residents think should be able to view the video, and that you want audio off and no camera aimed at apartment doors. Ask the owner for a written answer by a date. An owner can send that list to us and get a line-by-line quote for exactly those spots.

Port Morris yards, lofts and production floors

Can a camera go inside the still room of our Port Morris distillery, where the electrical rules are stricter?

Only equipment rated for that room's classification should go inside it. A process-safety firm that works with distilleries notes they usually have Class I areas because of the ethanol and Class II areas where milling grain makes combustible dust, and that problems often come from unrated outlets and extension cords brought in from outside. The simpler answer is usually to put an ordinary camera outside the classified area, looking in through a doorway or a window, and to put rated equipment inside only where your fire protection engineer or insurer says it is needed.

Customers' car keys hang in a key box in our auto shop's office. Where should the camera go so it shows who took a set of keys and when?

Put a camera on the wall facing the key box, at about head height and a few feet away, so it shows the face and hands of the person at the box and the hook they reach for. Add a second view of the office door, so the clip shows the person arriving and leaving. A lockable key cabinet makes the video far more useful, because the camera then shows who opened it, not just who walked past an open board.

Trucks and heavy traffic on Bruckner Boulevard shake our building. Will that make the outside camera picture blurry?

It can, and it is easy to prevent. Mount outside cameras directly on solid masonry, not on a sign bracket, a thin metal sheet or a long arm, and keep long zoom lenses off walls that vibrate, because magnification also magnifies shaking. Cameras with image stabilization help on the worst walls. We look at the picture live while a truck goes by during the install and move the mount if the image jumps.

Landmarks and the shopping streets

The Landmarks permit application asks for the camera's cut sheet. What is that, and who puts it together?

A cut sheet is the manufacturer's product page for the exact camera model, showing its size, shape, finish and mounting details, so Landmarks staff can judge how it will look on the facade. We supply the cut sheet for the camera we are proposing, along with a photo of the front marked with the camera's location and the cable path, and the owner or the owner's representative files them with the application. The camera is ordered only after the permit is issued.

The guidebook allows only about ten inches of visible conduit. How does camera cable reach the front of an Alexander Avenue rowhouse within that?

Mostly by going straight through the wall. The camera sits in a mortar joint as close as possible to a point where the cable can pass directly into the vestibule, the cellar or a front room, so the only exterior run is the inch or two between the camera base and the hole. Where that isn't possible, the cable can come up from the areaway or the cellar, inside the line of the stoop, and the short visible piece is painted to match. Running long conduit across a landmarked front is the one route we rule out.

The BID picked its ten new Hub camera spots from 311 and 911 complaint data. Can a shop owner use the same kind of data to decide where to put cameras?

Yes, and your own records are the best data you have. Write down where and when your incidents happened over the past year, which door, which aisle, which hours, and what you would have needed to see. Public 311 complaints near your address can add context about the block, such as doorway loitering or blocked entrances. That list decides the first cameras far better than a standard package does, and it tells us which views need faces and which only need a wide picture.

The Rules

New York Rules Behind a Mott Haven Camera Job

No law requires cameras in apartment buildings

As Brick Underground put it, "city buildings aren't required to have functioning security cameras at all." Owners put cameras in because of package theft, a broken door, a stranger in the stairs or an insurer's question, not because of a citation. That is a reason to buy for the building's real problems rather than a checklist.

Who may install

The NYS Department of State's rules treat a CCTV or video surveillance system used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement as a security system, so the business installing it needs a security and fire alarm installer license. An owner working on their own property with their own employees is exempt. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431.

Footage and who sees it

A landlord does not have to show tenants footage outside a legal process, but must give it up when police request it or a court orders it. FirstService Residential, which manages many New York co-ops and condos, says buildings generally keep footage 30 to 90 days, keep a log of requests and limit access; as its Christina Forbes put it, "Limiting access protects the privacy of residents and helps prevent potential misuse."

Smart access data

Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Law, requires owners of buildings with fob, app or biometric entry to get tenants' express consent, collect only minimal data and destroy authentication data within 90 days. HPD's page on the law does not mention cameras, so we keep camera recording on its own recorder and its own written policy.

Landmarks and public housing

In the three historic districts, front cameras need an LPC permit and follow the guidebook's frontage and conduit limits. In NYCHA developments, common areas are NYCHA's, and residents report problems through NYCHA's Customer Contact Center or the MyNYCHA app.

Mott Haven Security Camera FAQ

Our church runs a weekly food pantry with a line down the sidewalk. Can a camera cover the door without recording the faces of people waiting for food?
+

Yes. Aim the door camera at the threshold from inside, so it records who enters the building rather than the whole line outside, and draw a privacy mask over the sidewalk portion of any outdoor view. Many recorders let you mask part of the picture permanently, so it is never recorded at all. Tell volunteers what the camera covers, and keep the pantry's footage on a short retention period with access limited to one or two people.

Do we pay anything before the installation starts?
+

The estimate is free. Once you approve the written quote, a deposit is due before equipment is ordered, and the rest is due when the work is complete, as set out in the service agreement. Nothing is bought for your job until the quote is approved and any landlord, board, housing company or Landmarks approval is in hand, and special-order equipment is ordered only after the deposit.

The Third Avenue BID director said cameras and lighting alone don't create public safety. What should a store or building pair its cameras with?
+

Start with the door: a closer that shuts it, a latch that holds and a lock that works, because a camera can only record a door left open. Then light: a dim vestibule, stair or yard defeats any camera. Then habits: a named person who reviews alerts, staff who know how to save a clip, and a written rule on who gets footage. With those in place, a handful of well-aimed cameras does more than many cameras without them.

Our porter of many years just quit, and he had the camera app on his phone. How do we cut off his access the same day?
+

If he had his own login, delete that user on the recorder or in the app's account settings, and his phone loses access immediately. If everyone shared one login, change that password now and then give each remaining person a separate account, so the next departure takes one click. Also check the recorder's list of devices and shared users, because some apps let a user share access onward. We can do this on a setup-only visit if nobody has the admin password.

Should our shop's cameras share the same internet connection as the card machine and the register?
+

They can share the internet line, but they should not share the same open network. We put the recorder and cameras on their own network segment, or behind their own router, so a weak camera password can never become a path to the payment equipment, and so heavy video uploads don't slow down card payments. The recorder keeps recording locally even if the internet drops; only remote viewing pauses.

We rent our Third Avenue storefront on a short lease. Is a wired system still worth paying for?
+

Often yes, if it is planned to move. Cameras, the recorder, the switch and the battery unit are yours and come with you; what stays behind is cable inside the walls and ceiling. On a short lease we keep runs short and simple, put the head end in one cabinet, and write the quote so you can see what is reusable at the next store. Ask the landlord in writing what must be removed and patched at move-out.

A tenant in our small walk-up asked for live access to the hallway camera on her phone. Should residents get that?
+

Usually not for halls and stairs, because a live hallway view lets any resident follow neighbors' comings and goings. A common compromise is to give residents a view of the street door or vestibule only, so they can see who is at the entrance, while hallway, stair and cellar views stay with the owner and super. Put the rule in writing and apply it to everyone, so nobody feels singled out.

Our building's front door doesn't lock properly. Should we fix the door before we spend money on cameras?
+

Fix the door first, or at the same time. A camera at a door that stays open records a steady stream of people walking in, which helps afterward but does nothing to keep anyone out. ABC7's report from St. Mary's Park Houses described both entrances failing to lock while residents put up their own cameras. A working closer and latch, then a face-height camera inside the door, is the order that gets results.

If we put our shop's cameras on the Bronx DA's camera map, will anyone outside the DA's office see where they are?
+

As the Riverdale Press reported it, the map is confidential and not public. An entry records where the camera is, how to reach the owner, how footage can be shared and where it is stored, so investigators know whom to ask before video is overwritten. It does not hand anyone a login to your recorder. Joining is voluntary, and the decision to share any clip stays with you.

When you replace our old recorder, what happens to the drive and the video still on it?
+

You decide. If any incident on the old drive might matter, we export those clips first and hand them over. The old recorder can stay with you, unplugged and stored, until any dispute is settled, or its drive can be wiped or removed and given to you before the box is thrown away. We never take a customer's drive with footage on it off the property without written permission.

Taggers keep painting the side wall of our building on Bruckner Boulevard at night. Can a camera catch them, and where should it go?
+

A camera can record who is doing it and when. Mount it high on the corner or the wall facing the side wall, out of reach, aimed along the wall rather than straight at it, so it sees people approaching and their faces before they turn to paint. Low-light color cameras and a motion light help at night. Set alerts for that zone during late hours only, so the right person sees it while it is happening.

Our nonprofit is applying for a grant to pay for cameras. Can the quote be written so the funder can follow it line by line?
+

Yes. Our quotes already list each camera, mount, cable run, opening, recorder, drive and battery separately, with the location of each camera.
