The Neighborhood

Why City Line Properties Need Alarm Installation

City Line takes its name from a border that no longer exists on paper. Before the five boroughs were consolidated in 1898, the old city of Brooklyn ended here and Queens County began, and the neighborhood still sits in the far northeast corner of East New York, against the Queens line, with Atlantic Avenue to the north and Conduit Boulevard to the south. Definitions of the western edge vary, but most put it around Euclid Avenue. Liberty Avenue is the main shopping street, the A train stops at Euclid Avenue and Grant Avenue, and the housing is mostly brick two-family houses, three-family houses, small apartment buildings and mixed-use buildings with a shop on the ground floor.

That housing is the first reason an alarm here is planned differently from an alarm in a one-family suburb. Redfin’s City Line listings this year are mostly two-family, three-family and mixed-use buildings, including a Liberty Avenue building with a commercial first floor and two apartments above it. DataUSA puts the homeownership rate across Community District 5, which takes in City Line, at 24 percent, so most households in the district rent. In a neighborhood built of two- and three-family houses, many of those renters live on a floor of a house whose owner lives on another floor. One building, two or three households, one street door, often one basement and one back yard. An alarm that treats the building as a single house annoys everybody in it within a week.

The second reason is the value at stake. Redfin reported a median sale price of $898,000 in City Line in March 2026. For most of the families who own here, the house is the largest thing they own and also a source of rent, and its contents include a tenant’s belongings that the owner does not insure. When we survey a City Line house we are protecting the owner’s floor, the tenant’s floor and the shared parts between them, and we write the plan so that each household knows what the alarm does for them and what it does not.

The third reason is commercial. The restaurants, groceries and shops along Liberty Avenue, and the businesses on Atlantic Avenue and Conduit Boulevard at the neighborhood’s edges, sit empty for several hours every night. Three recent cases close to City Line show what happens in those hours. In May 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported that a man forced a door at a Guyanese Chinese restaurant near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street, stayed inside for about seven minutes and walked out carrying a safe that staff said held about $100,000 in personal valuables. Police tied the same man to a break-in on Conduit Boulevard four days later, where a window was broken and about $2,000 was taken from a register. In December 2025, News 12 described a pair of thieves who pulled a floor-bolted ATM out of an Arlington Avenue store in East New York and then, about two hours later, fled a Linden Boulevard gas station the moment its alarm went off. “When the alarm rang, they left right away,” the clerk said.

The fourth reason is easy to miss: a lot of the alarms already installed here no longer report to anyone. Systems put in during the 1990s and 2000s often called the monitoring company over a phone line that has since been cancelled. Systems fitted later often used a 3G or CDMA cellular radio, and AT&T and Verizon retired those networks during 2022. Google stopped supporting its Nest Secure alarm on April 8, 2024. The keypad on the wall may still light up and beep, and the sticker may still be in the window, while the signal goes nowhere. Checking an old system is part of every City Line survey, and we describe how we do it further down this page.

City Line facts that shape an alarm plan

  • Mostly two- and three-family buildings. Redfin’s 26 active City Line listings in October 2026 were mostly two-family, three-family and mixed-use properties, so the typical job protects more than one household behind one street door.
  • Most households in the district rent. DataUSA’s 2024 figures put homeownership in Community District 5 at 24 percent and the median household income at $55,739, which is why we quote the smallest system that genuinely covers a building before suggesting anything more.
  • Many languages at home. The same DataUSA figures list Spanish (29.9 percent) and Bengali (4.4 percent) as the most common languages other than English, and the Dominican Republic, Guyana and Jamaica as the most common birthplaces abroad. City Limits counted more than 2,000 Bangladeshi residents in City Line in 2013 and seven mosques.
  • Two subway stops and an el. The A stops at Euclid Avenue, where the C ends, and at Grant Avenue, the last Fulton Street Line station in Brooklyn; east of Grant Avenue the line climbs onto an elevated structure over Liberty Avenue and continues into Queens.
  • Public housing at the southern edge. NYCHA’s East New York City Line development lists 33 buildings on Hegeman Avenue, Fountain Avenue and Logan Street, and the Cypress Hills Houses, opened in 1955 with 1,442 apartments, stand between Linden Boulevard, Sutter Avenue, Euclid Avenue and Fountain Avenue.
  • One precinct, one community board. City Line is part of Brooklyn Community Board 5 and is patrolled by the NYPD’s 75th Precinct, which also covers the rest of East New York, Cypress Hills, Spring Creek and Starrett City.

One panel divided into areas: the owner’s floor, the rented floor and, where it makes sense, the basement. Each household arms and disarms only its own area with its own codes, and the shared street door and hall are set so that nobody sets off the other family’s siren by coming home.

Three-family and small apartment buildings

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for City Line’s Architecture

Almost every building in City Line is attached or semi-attached, made of brick, and two or three stories tall over a basement. What varies is how many families live inside, how the doors are shared, and whether the ground floor is a home or a business. Those three things decide the alarm far more than square footage does.

The brick two-family with a rental floor

Owner on one floor, tenant on the other, one street door, often a basement shared or used for storage. The core of the job is the partition: two areas on one panel, two keypads, and a shared-door rule both households can live with.

The three-family rental house

Three apartments, a shared stair, a cellar and sometimes a roof hatch. Under city housing law a building with three or more units is a multiple dwelling, which changes who is responsible for detectors and locks, so the alarm plan follows the building’s legal status.

The semi-attached house with a side passage

One side wall shared with the neighbor, the other side open to a passage or driveway that leads to the yard. The open side is where we spend first, because it is how a stranger reaches the back of the house without passing the street.

The Liberty Avenue mixed-use building

A store, restaurant or office at street level and one or two apartments above, with a separate residential door beside the shopfront. Two systems that share only the building, never one alarm with the business and the homes on the same siren.

The small walk-up apartment building

Pre-war and mid-century buildings with a handful of apartments on a shared stair. Tenants can have their own wireless system inside the apartment; owners can protect the cellar, roof door and street door as common areas.

NYCHA apartments at the southern edge

Apartments in the East New York City Line houses and the Cypress Hills Houses: wireless, battery-powered, inside the apartment only, put up with removable mounts and nothing that touches the hallway or the building’s systems.

The brick two-family: deciding who the alarm belongs to

The first question on a two-family survey is not about doors. It is about people. Who lives on each floor, who has keys to the street door, who uses the basement, whether the tenant wants to be on the alarm at all, and who should be called at night if something trips. The answers decide whether the house gets one area or two, how many keypads go on the walls, and which doors count as shared. A family that rents the upper floor to a relative usually wants one system with two areas. A family that rents to someone they found through a listing usually wants the same panel but a firm line between the two households, so the tenant can arm their apartment without the owner seeing every time they come and go.

Once the households are settled, the doors follow. The street door and the hall get a contact that belongs to both areas: it only sounds when both households are armed, and it starts the entry countdown for whichever household is armed when it opens. Each apartment door gets a contact in its own area. The basement door and the rear door to the yard usually belong to the owner, because the owner controls the basement and the yard, unless the lease says otherwise. Windows on the ground floor and at the back go on the list; upper front windows that nobody could reach without a ladder in full view of the street usually do not. A motion detector covers the owner’s main hall or the foot of the basement stair, never the shared stair the tenant climbs every night.

Then the people get codes. Every adult in each household has a personal code, so the panel’s history shows which household disarmed which area and when. Relatives who should hear about an alarm but never need to come in, such as a brother in Queens or a parent abroad, are set up in the app as people who receive alerts and cannot disarm. We write all of this down on the zone list, in plain words, and leave a copy with each household.

The three-family house: when the building becomes a multiple dwelling

HPD’s registration rules draw the line plainly: a building with three or more residential units is a multiple dwelling, and a one- or two-family house is a private dwelling. In City Line that line runs through the middle of the housing stock, because many houses that look identical from the street hold two apartments on one block and three on the next. For an alarm, the difference matters in two places. The first is detectors. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and sets out when carbon monoxide and natural-gas detectors are required, and those detectors have to meet the housing rules on their own. A monitored smoke detector on a burglar alarm adds a phone alert and a call from the monitoring center; it does not replace a detector the law requires, and we never tell an owner that it does.

The second is the common areas. In a three-family house the owner is responsible for the stair, the cellar and the street door in a way that is different from a two-family, and tenants have a reasonable expectation that their comings and goings are their own. So in a three-family we protect each apartment separately for any tenant who wants it, put the street door, the cellar and the roof hatch on the owner’s area, and do not put motion detectors in the shared stair. The owner sees when the cellar door opens at 2 a.m.; nobody sees when the tenant on the third floor comes home.

The semi-attached house: the open side comes first

Many City Line houses share one wall with the house next door and have a narrow passage or a driveway on the other side leading back to the yard. That passage is the most important line on the alarm plan. From the street, the front of the house is overlooked by neighbors, by people walking to the A train and by the family inside. The passage is not. A person who walks down it is out of sight within a few steps, and at the back of the house they find the rear door, the basement entrance, the kitchen window and, often, a gate into the yard.

So the rear door, the basement door and the ground-floor windows along the passage get contacts first. If the passage gate is solid and kept locked, a contact on the gate itself gives the earliest warning of all, though it has to be set as an alert rather than an alarm if the family uses the passage constantly. A floodlight camera at the back corner, on the same app as the alarm, shows who is there without anybody opening a door. The shared side wall needs nothing except attention to any window that faces a narrow gap between buildings.

The Liberty Avenue building: a business below, homes above

A mixed-use building has two kinds of occupant with opposite schedules. The store or restaurant is busy until late and empty overnight; the apartments upstairs are empty during the working day and occupied at night. One alarm cannot serve both well, and trying to share one creates exactly the false alarms and arguments that make people stop arming. We fit the business with its own panel and cellular communicator, wired to the shopfront, the gate, the rear door, the cellar hatch and the office, with an always-armed zone on the safe. The apartments get their own residential system, or several, through the separate street door.

The two systems can still help each other. The store’s camera over the rear yard can be shared with the owner if the owner lives upstairs, and the residential street door can report to the owner’s phone even when the store is closed. What they must not share is a siren, a code or a monitoring account, because a monitoring center that receives a burglary signal from “the building” cannot tell police whether to go to a restaurant kitchen or a family’s bedroom.

Old Systems That Went Quiet

Finding Out Whether the Alarm Already on Your Wall Still Reports to Anyone

A City Line house that has changed hands once or twice since the 1990s often has two or three layers of alarm history in it: a keypad from the first system, wiring in the basement ceiling from the second, a sticker in the window from a company that may no longer exist. Owners tell us the system “still works” because the keypad beeps when a door opens. The beep comes from the panel in the basement or the closet. It says nothing about whether a signal leaves the building.

Three changes in the last fifteen years stranded a great many systems. Households dropped their landlines for cable or fiber, and a panel that dialed out over copper had nothing left to dial. AT&T shut down its 3G network early in 2022, and Verizon’s CDMA network was closed after December 31, 2022; a panel with a 3G or CDMA radio, which was the standard cellular option for years, lost its only path at the same time. And on April 8, 2024, Google ended support for Nest Secure, after which, as 9to5Google reported, the system “won’t connect to the internet” and no longer works in the Nest app. In each case the house looked protected and was not.

Checking is not complicated, and we do it before we quote anything. We find the panel and read its make and model, find the communicator and read what network it uses, and look at the keypad’s trouble history. If anyone is still paying a monitoring company, we call that company with you, put the account on test, open a door, and ask whether the signal arrived. If nobody is paying, or the signal does not arrive, the system is a local siren at best. Then we tell you which parts are worth keeping. Hardwired door contacts and the cable to them often outlast two panels; a new panel with an LTE communicator, or a communicator added to a panel that is otherwise sound, can bring the old wiring back to life. On our Brooklyn list a panel replacement on existing wiring, with communicator, keypad and programming, runs $800 to $1,500.

There is a second reason to check, and it is about honesty with the people in the house. A tenant who sees a keypad by the door assumes the building is watched. A parent who stays alone in the house while the family is away assumes the same. If the alarm no longer reports, everyone in the building should know that until it is fixed.

Atlantic Avenue

The wide road along the northern edge. The May 2026 safe theft near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street is the case we use to explain why the office and the safe get their own always-armed zone.

Conduit Boulevard

The divided road along the southern edge, where cars move fast and a broken window can go unnoticed for minutes. Businesses here get glass-break coverage on every street-facing pane, because a window, not a door, was the way in during the May 2026 break-in police linked to the safe theft.

Near the Euclid and Grant Avenue stations

Businesses close to the two A train stations, where early commuters are on the street before most shops open. Opening reports tell an owner exactly when the first employee disarmed, which matters most when the street is busy and the shop is not yet staffed.

Mosques, churches and community rooms

Prayer halls and meeting rooms, including the mosques City Limits counted in City Line, that are busy at set hours and empty in between. Partitions keep the office and storage rooms armed while the hall is open, and a closing alert tells the committee if nobody armed the building after the last gathering.

What the recent break-ins near City Line have in common

The commercial burglaries reported close to City Line over the past year were short and targeted. The man in the May 2026 restaurant break-in near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street was inside for about seven minutes and left with one thing, the safe. Four days later, on Conduit Boulevard, police say the same man broke a window and emptied a register. The pair News 12 followed in December 2025 went for ATMs and left the rest of the store untouched; a worker at the Arlington Avenue store said, “There is nothing missing, they only come for the ATM,” and a clerk told News 12, “They break the lock in 20 or 30 seconds.” The Brooklyn District Attorney’s case against an earlier ATM ring, which ran from June 2022 to January 2023 and used apartments in East New York and Clinton Hill to plan its burglaries, described bolt cutters, ATMs rocked loose from their bolts, and one member listening to police scanners during the jobs.

Two lessons come out of those cases. The first is that an alarm has to report the first opening, not the last. A system that only has a motion detector in the middle of the shop gives a thief who goes straight to the office a head start; a contact on the door he forces and a sensor on the office door do not. The second is that the valuable thing in the store needs its own zone that stays armed around the clock. Staff disarm the front of the store every morning. The safe, the office and the back room should report whenever they are opened outside the hours somebody has a reason to open them.

Groceries and restaurants on Liberty Avenue

A grocery or restaurant here usually has the same shape: a glass front behind a roll-down gate, a long room with shelves or tables, a counter near the door, a kitchen or stockroom behind, a back door to a yard or alley, and a cellar reached by a hatch on the sidewalk or a stair inside. Walk-in coolers and kitchen equipment run all night. We put contacts on the gate, the front door, the back door and the cellar hatch; glass-break detectors on the front glass; motion detectors aimed down the length of the room and away from cooler fans, fryer hoods and heaters; and a separate zone on the office and the safe. The counter gets a hold-up button that sends a silent signal, for the hours when the store is open and somebody is alone behind it.

Many of these stores are family businesses where the person who closes at night is not the person who opens in the morning. The alarm’s opening and closing reports, sent to the owner’s phone, settle questions that used to cause arguments: whether the store was armed last night, who armed it, and at what time the first person came in. If the store is not armed by a set time after closing, the owner gets a message, which is often worth more than the siren.

Back doors, yards and roof access

The backs of the Liberty Avenue buildings face yards, alleys and the backs of houses on the side streets. A back door that opens onto an unlit yard is where we expect a forced entry in a store whose front is gated and lit. It gets a heavy-duty contact and, if the door is steel, a shock sensor on the frame as well. Where a building has a roof hatch or a skylight, it goes on the alarm too: a burglar who reached Brooklyn shops through their roofs and ceilings last autumn, as FOX 5 reported in November 2025 from Bedford-Stuyvesant, Flatbush and Kensington, showed how often that route is left unprotected.

Compliance notes for City Line businesses and buildings

  • Siren cutoff. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be able to shut itself off, and to do so automatically, within fifteen minutes of going off. Every siren we install is programmed to stop well inside that limit, which matters most in a building with apartments above the store.
  • Installer licensing. New York State licenses security and fire alarm installers through the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ask any company for its license number before it starts; ours is #12000287431.
  • False alarms are the user’s responsibility in law, not just in practice. General Business Law § 69-vvv lets a municipality fine an alarm company only where a false alarm was caused by its own error or defective equipment; otherwise any penalty falls on the owner or occupant whose conduct caused it. Careful programming and training at handover are how we keep that from happening.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm with a few monitored smoke detectors is not a code fire alarm system. If a building or a business needs a fire alarm under the building or fire code, that is a separate design and filing, and a combination panel does not replace it.
  • Detectors in rental homes. HPD requires smoke detectors in every unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide and natural-gas detectors where its rules apply. Those devices have to meet the housing rules on their own; anything we add on the alarm is extra.
  • No citywide alarm permit that we could find. Many towns in New York require a permit for a burglar alarm and charge for false alarms. We found no general New York City permit for a home or store burglar alarm and do not claim one exists.

Old alarms and systems left behind

Our City Line panel was installed around 2015 with a cellular radio instead of a phone line. After the 2022 network shutdowns, how do we find out whether it still reaches anyone?

Read the communicator’s model, then test it with the monitoring company on the line. Many panels fitted in the early and middle 2010s used 3G or CDMA radios, which lost their networks when AT&T closed 3G early in 2022 and Verizon closed CDMA after December 31, 2022. The keypad often shows a communication trouble when that happens, but not always, and some owners cleared the beeping years ago without knowing what it meant. If the radio is an LTE model and the monitoring company confirms a test signal, you are fine. If it is 3G or CDMA, an LTE communicator can often go into the same panel, which costs far less than a new system.

We put a Google Nest Secure in our two-family before Google ended it in April 2024. Is anything from that kit worth keeping?

The hub and its sensors are not: Nest Secure used its own devices that only talked to Google’s system, and since April 8, 2024 it no longer connects to the internet or works in the app. A Nest doorbell or Nest cameras are different products and may still work on their own, so keep them if they do what you need, alongside a new alarm. We take the Nest Secure parts down, fill the screw holes, and put the new contacts in the same places where the old ones made sense, so the walls are not marked twice.

Owners, tenants and family under one roof

Our upstairs tenant’s lease ends in the spring. If we install the alarm now, what happens to the upstairs area when a new tenant moves in?

Nothing has to be rewired. The upstairs area stays on the panel with its own keypad and contacts; on the day the tenant leaves we delete their codes and app login, which takes a minute and can be done from the owner’s app, and on the day the new tenant moves in they get fresh codes and, if they want it, the app for their area only. The contacts, keypad and zone names stay the same. If a new tenant prefers not to use the alarm at all, their area simply stays disarmed while the shared street door and the owner’s floors carry on as before.

Three generations live in our City Line house, and someone is nearly always home. Is a monitored alarm worth it in a house that is rarely empty?

Yes, but for different reasons than in a house that sits empty all day. In a busy house the alarm earns its keep at night, when it is armed in stay mode with the doors and ground-floor windows active and the motion detectors off, so a door forced at 3 a.m. wakes everyone before anyone reaches the stairs. It also covers the times the house does empty: a wedding, a funeral, a family trip abroad, a long day at the hospital. And monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on the same panel matter more, not less, when an older relative is home alone for part of the day.

Our tenant upstairs does not want to be on the alarm. If someone gets into her apartment through the shared street door, does our system catch it?

It catches the street door, if you set it that way, but not her apartment. With the tenant off the system, the street door belongs to your area alone, so it will sound when you are armed and anyone opens it, including the tenant coming home late. That is rarely workable. The better arrangement is a contact on the street door that sends you a phone alert at night rather than sounding the siren, contacts on your own doors and windows, and a written note to the tenant explaining that her apartment is not protected. If she changes her mind, her area can be added later without touching yours.

Stores, safes and the kind of break-ins reported nearby

In May 2026 a safe was carried out of a restaurant near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street in about seven minutes. Our safe is in the back office. What should our alarm do in those minutes?

Report before the thief reaches the office, and report again when he does. The forced door should be a contact that sends an alarm within the entry delay, which for a back door that staff never use to come in can be set to no delay at all. The office door gets its own contact on a zone that stays armed whenever the office is supposed to be closed, and the safe can carry a shock or contact sensor on a 24-hour zone. Seven minutes is enough time for a monitoring center to call the owner and dispatch police, but only if the first signal goes out in the first minute rather than the sixth.

The ATM ring the Brooklyn DA prosecuted had a member listening to police scanners. Does that make a silent alarm pointless for our store?

No. A scanner tells a lookout that police have been sent; it cannot tell him that an alarm reported to a monitoring center, that the owner’s phone is showing camera views, or that the next device on the route has just reported too. What the scanner detail does show is that a crew expects to be gone in minutes. That argues for a loud interior siren after the silent report, so the people inside know they have been noticed, and for the safe, ATM wall and office being on zones that report independently of the front door.

A window, not a door, was broken in the May 2026 break-in on Conduit Boulevard. Our shop window on Conduit has no gate over it. What protects glass with nothing in front of it?

An acoustic glass-break detector inside, mounted where it has a clear line to the whole window and within its rated distance, plus a motion detector that covers the floor directly behind the glass. The glass-break detector hears the particular sound of a pane breaking and reports before anyone has climbed through. On a very wide pane we use two detectors, not one, and we test each with a simulator that reproduces breaking glass. A roll-down gate or a security film on the glass is a physical improvement worth pricing too, but the alarm should not depend on it.

Prayer halls and community rooms

Prayer times move with the sun through the year, and our masjid opens before dawn. Can the arming schedule follow them, or does someone have to arm and disarm by hand?

For a house of worship we do not rely on a fixed schedule, because the first and last prayers move by more than an hour between summer and winter. Instead the caretakers who open and close carry keyfobs, the prayer hall is its own area so opening it does not disarm the office, the storage room or the donation box, and the panel sends the committee a message if the building is still disarmed a set time after the last prayer. We set those reminder times by season and change them in the app, which takes a minute, so nobody has to climb to a keypad at 5 a.m. to reprogram anything.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in City Line

The claims below turn up again and again in generic online advice and AI-written summaries about home alarms. Each is a fair starting point somewhere. Here is how each one holds up in a City Line building.

“A house needs one alarm system.”

Advice written for one-family houses assumes one household, one set of codes and one person to call. A City Line two-family has two households, and a three-family has three, under one roof and behind one street door. Put them on a single area and every arrival home by one family is a potential alarm for the other.

The right answer is still usually one panel, because one panel is cheaper than two and can watch the shared doors for everyone. But it is one panel with separate areas, each with its own keypad, codes and rules, and a street door that only sounds when both are armed.

Where the ground floor is a business, the answer changes again: two separate systems, never one. A monitoring center that receives a signal from a combined system cannot tell police whether the open door is the shop’s or a family’s.

“If the keypad lights up, the alarm is working.”

This is the most expensive mistake we see. The keypad is powered by the panel in the house; it beeps, lights and counts down whether or not a signal can leave the building.

Landlines dropped for fiber, the 3G and CDMA shutdowns of 2022, and the end of Nest Secure in April 2024 all left systems that look normal and report to nobody. Some show a trouble message; many owners silenced it long ago.

The only proof is a test signal received by whoever is supposed to receive it. We run that test before quoting, with the monitoring company on the phone if there is one, and tell you plainly what we found.

“Wi-Fi alarms are just as reliable as any other.”

An alarm that reports only over the home’s Wi-Fi stops reporting when the router is unplugged, the internet bill lapses, the tenant who paid for the internet moves out, or a burglar pulls the cable on the way in. In a two-family, the router is often on the tenant’s account.

As one Brooklyn installer told Brick Underground, “The point is, you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.” That is exactly the risk in a shared house.

The panels we fit report over their own LTE radio, with Wi-Fi as a second path, and run on battery when the power fails. The house’s internet can come and go without the alarm going deaf.

“An alarm stops a break-in.”

An alarm does not stop a determined person from forcing a door. The restaurant safe near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street went out in about seven minutes, and a clerk told News 12 that ATM thieves like the pair it followed “break the lock in 20 or 30 seconds.”

What an alarm does is shorten the time a thief feels safe and tell the right people at once. The Linden Boulevard clerk described the effect simply: when the alarm rang, the thieves left.

Physical measures still matter: a safe bolted down and kept out of sight, a solid rear door, a gate on the glass. We say so in the survey and price the alarm as one layer among several, not as a promise.

“Tenants cannot have an alarm because they do not own the building.”

A tenant on the top floor of a City Line house, in a walk-up or in a NYCHA apartment can protect their own apartment with a wireless system that uses removable mounts and touches nothing outside the apartment door.

What a tenant cannot do is put devices in the shared hall, on the street door or on the building’s wiring without the owner’s or management’s permission, and they should not try. The lease and the house rules come first.

In a two-family where the owner already has a panel, the simplest route for a tenant is often their own area on the owner’s panel, with their own codes, rather than a second system competing for the same front door.

“The bigger the package, the safer the house.”

Packages sold by the number of sensors push buyers toward devices they do not need, such as contacts on upper front windows that nobody could reach without being seen from the street.

A semi-attached City Line house is usually well protected by contacts on its street, rear and basement doors, the ground-floor and passage-side windows, and one motion detector. That is the $600 line on our list, or a little more.

Where money should go first is the open side passage, the back of the house and, in a store, the safe and the office. We would rather fit fewer devices in the right places than more devices in the wrong ones.

“Professional installation locks you into a long contract.”

Many national dealers recover the cost of equipment through multi-year monitoring agreements, and one Brooklyn owner on the Brownstoner forum describes waiting out a three-year commitment before switching.

We sell the equipment at our published Brooklyn list price, and it is yours. Monitoring through our partners is optional and month to month, from $30 a month, and you can start with phone alerts only.

If you later sell the house, rent out your floor or decide you do not want monitoring, the panel stays and keeps working as a local alarm with phone alerts, or it can be handed to the next owner.

FAQ

City Line Alarm Installation FAQ

Why would a monitored alarm still send the fire department after we told the operator it was a false alarm?
+

Because many monitoring centers treat a smoke or heat signal differently from a burglary signal and dispatch the fire department on it no matter what the person on the phone says, and Brooklyn owners on the Brownstoner forum describe exactly that, followed by summonses after repeated false alarms. Ask the monitoring company in writing what its fire cancellation policy is before you sign. On our side, we place smoke detectors away from kitchens and bathrooms, and we can set burglary and fire signals so a mistake at the keypad never looks like a fire.

Is putting the system in test mode a safe way to get through a week of renovation work in our City Line house?
+

For a few hours, yes; for a week, no. Test mode tells the monitoring center to ignore your signals, so a real break-in or fire during that time reaches nobody, which is the point one Brooklyn owner raised when asking what monitoring is for if the system lives in test mode. A better approach is to bypass only the zones where the work is happening, give the contractor a temporary code that only works on the days and hours they are there, and delete it when the job ends.

Before we sign up for monitoring, what should we ask about who actually answers when our alarm goes off?
+

Ask where the central station is, whether a trained person answers every signal or an automated message comes first, how quickly the operator calls the first person on your list, and how fast a change to your passcode or call list takes effect. An installer interviewed by Brick Underground suggested asking the same things, including whether account changes happen immediately or take up to 48 hours. We give you those answers for the partners we use, in writing, before monitoring starts.

Will a keypad or panel in our unheated front vestibule keep working through a January cold snap?
+

The panel should not go there at all. The 2GIG Edge panels we fit are rated to operate from 32 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit, and a brick vestibule with an outer street door can drop below freezing on a cold night. The panel goes inside the heated part of the house, usually in a closet or the owner’s hall, and the vestibule gets only a door contact. If you want a keypad near the street door, it goes on the inside wall of the heated hall, not between the two doors.

Our extended family is large. Is there a limit to how many people can have their own code on one panel?
+

Not one you are likely to reach. The 2GIG Edge data sheet lists 100 user codes, each a six-digit personal code, and the app can carry more people as alert-only users who receive messages without being able to disarm. We still recommend giving a code only to people who actually come in, so the history shows who opened which door, and adding everyone else who should hear about an alarm as an alert-only user.

We rent in the East New York City Line houses on Hegeman Avenue. Can we have an alarm in our apartment?
+

Yes, inside the apartment. We fit a wireless, battery-powered system with removable mounts on the apartment door and windows, a small panel on a shelf or the wall, and no wiring, drilling or devices in the hallway or on the building’s doors. Check your lease and the management office’s rules first and get any consent it requires in writing; we can describe the equipment for that request. When you move, the system comes down and goes with you.

If a relative sets the alarm off by mistake, how long do we have to stop the call to the monitoring center?
+

Panels programmed to the ANSI/SIA CP-01 false-alarm standard hold a burglary signal for a short abort window after the entry delay, typically about 30 seconds, and disarming in that window stops the report altogether. If the signal has already gone, disarming within five minutes sends a cancel message to the monitoring center. We set those windows at installation and show every household how they work, which is the single best way to keep a busy house from collecting false alarms.

Our old alarm sounded twice one night and then went silent on the third trip. Is it broken?
+

Probably not. That is swinger shutdown, a setting the CP-01 standard requires, which stops a zone from sounding again after a set number of trips in one arming period so one faulty door or a loose window cannot keep a whole block awake. The standard allows one to six trips and many panels default to two. It does mean the zone that kept tripping needs looking at, because a sensor that triggers repeatedly is either failing or mounted on a door that does not close properly.

Our rental floor needs a natural-gas detector under the HPD rules. Can it be part of the alarm?
+

Keep the required detector separate and add the alarm on top of it. HPD’s detector rules set out where carbon monoxide and natural-gas detectors are required in rental homes, and a detector that meets those rules has to do so on its own. A monitored carbon monoxide detector on the alarm adds a phone alert and a call from the monitoring center, which is useful in a house where the boiler and the gas meter are in a basement nobody visits, but it is an extra layer, not a substitute.

There is a metal siren box from an old alarm high on the brick front of our house. Should the new system use it?
+

Usually not. Old exterior sirens are often wired through cable that has been cut or painted over, and in attached houses a loud outdoor siren mostly wakes the neighbors. Interior sirens, a monitoring center and phone alerts do more. If the box is sound and you want it as a visible deterrent, it can stay unconnected, or we can test it and connect it to a siren output programmed to stop well within the fifteen minutes the city’s Administrative Code allows.

Does a City Line store need a different kind of panel from a house, or only a different setup?
+

Often the same family of panel with a commercial setup: its own monitoring account, opening and closing reports, a hold-up code or button that sends a silent signal, zones that stay armed around the clock on the office and safe, and stronger contacts on gates and hatches. What matters is that the store has its own panel, even when the owner lives upstairs, so the business and the home never share a code, a siren or a monitoring account.
