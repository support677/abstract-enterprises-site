**Rules that change how the work is done**
- Design and installation documents for fire alarm systems must be submitted to FDNY for review and approval before installation. — https://www.nyc.gov/assets/fdny/downloads/pdf/about/Chapter-09.pdf
- Replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair. Moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel or similar control units needs new plans filed with FDNY. — https://asmintegrators.com/fire-alarm-code/

### B. Neighborhood background

**History and development**
- Before 1900 the land was rural Town of Westchester, annexed to New York City in 1895. The New York, Westchester & Boston Railway came early in the 20th century. By the 1910s, speculators advertised "country homes in the Bronx" along Allerton Avenue. — https://cityneighborhoods.nyc/allerton
- The neighborhood is named for farmer Daniel Allerton (1818–1877). The city built Allerton Avenue across former estate land in 1914. — https://cornerfacts.com/new-york/bronx/allerton/
- A law-firm history page says the Lorillard family owned the land and sold parts of the estate in the early 1900s. I'd treat this as weaker than the other sources. — https://banvillelaw.com/the-history-of-the-bronx-neighborhood-allerton/
- The Allerton Avenue subway station opened March 3, 1917, built under the Dual Contracts. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allerton_Avenue_station
- After WWII, the area east of Boston Post Road was mostly Italian immigrants, and the area west of it was an older Jewish community. West Indian residents arrived in the 1980s, and Dominicans settled west of Bronxwood Avenue around the same time. — https://kids.kiddle.co/Allerton,_Bronx
- Today the area south of Allerton Avenue and east of Bronxwood Avenue has many Italian-Americans and Albanian-Americans, as well as Asian, Hispanic and West Indian residents. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allerton,_Bronx
- Boundaries are disputed. City Planning has labeled the southern part "Laconia" and the northern part "Bronxdale." — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allerton,_Bronx
- Sources disagree on which part is called Laconia or Bronxwood. One source gives Bronxwood to the south and Laconia to the north. — https://nycneighborhoods.app/map/neighborhoods/allerton-bronx/

**Main streets**
- Allerton Avenue is the main east–west artery. White Plains Road carries the elevated 2 and 5 trains. — https://cityneighborhoods.nyc/allerton
- Allerton Avenue runs from Bronx Park East to Williamsbridge Road. Waring Avenue and Pelham Parkway are the cross-town routes to the south (same source).

**Building stock**
- Half of the 1,463 residential lots hold buildings from 1945 or earlier, and 46% of those date from before 1940. The 2020 Census counted 34,623 residents. — https://cornerfacts.com/new-york/bronx/allerton/
- Housing is mostly brick-clad mid-rise apartment buildings, with over 80 percent of residents renting. Multifamily houses are more common east of U.S. 1. A few detached single-family colonial revivals exist. — https://www.homes.com/bronx-ny/allerton-neighborhood/
- South of Pelham Parkway there are brick two-family homes and attached rowhouses. That page is about the neighboring Pelham Parkway neighborhood. — https://cityneighborhoods.nyc/pelham-parkway
- Landmark districts: EMPTY.

**Parks and landmarks**
- Bronx Park lies to the west, home to the Bronx Zoo and the New York Botanical Garden. Local green space includes Waring Playground, Reiss Field, and the 109-acre Pelham Parkway. — https://www.homes.com/bronx-ny/allerton-neighborhood/
- The NYPL Allerton branch has served the area from Barnes Avenue since 1959. — https://cornerfacts.com/new-york/bronx/allerton/

**What it is known for**
- It is a working-class, mostly renter neighborhood with Italian and Albanian roots and West Indian and Dominican communities (sources above). The Allerton Avenue shopping strip is its commercial hub (Homes.com above).

**Recent local news on security and building safety**

### B. History and development
- Early history: Norwegian shipwright Hans Hansen Bergen bought farmland there in the 1600s, when it was in the town of Flatlands. The area was then known as Bergen Island. — https://brooklyneagle.com/303028/how-bergen-beach-got-its-name/
- Resort era: From 1896 to 1919, Percy G. Williams and Thomas Adams ran an amusement park there. The NAPHA article gives the dates as 1896–1918. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen_Beach,_Brooklyn and http://lostamusementparks.napha.org/Articles/NewYork/BergenBeach.html
- Williams was a theater builder, and Adams was of Chiclets chewing gum fame. — https://brooklyneagle.com/303028/how-bergen-beach-got-its-name/
- Landfill: It was connected to the rest of Brooklyn via landfill by 1918, though development did not come for many years. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen_Beach,_Brooklyn
- Residential plans: A residential community was planned in 1925 but never completed. The land stayed nearly untouched until the 1960s. By 1926 the last section had been sold for a development of one-family houses. — https://brooklynreporter.com/nabetags/bergen-beach/ and https://brooklyneagle.com/2829/on-this-day-in-history-march-30-the-bergens-settle-in-brooklyn/
- The marshy land held back building. Large-scale residential development waited for fuller landfilling in the 1960s. Until then there was a small cluster of houses along Avenue U. — https://forgotten-ny.com/2008/11/bergen-beach-georgetown-brooklyn/
- Georgetown: Georgetown was developed in the 1960s. Per Forgotten New York, development ran from the 1960s through the 1980s, and some lots stayed empty until the 2000s. — https://brooklyneagle.com/303028/how-bergen-beach-got-its-name/ and https://forgotten-ny.com/2008/11/bergen-beach-georgetown-brooklyn/
- Sources conflict on the Georgetown timeline. Wikipedia says a 1960s "Georgetowne" development was proposed but never built, and Bergen Beach wasn't fully developed until the 1990s. Treat the exact dates cautiously. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen_Beach,_Brooklyn
- Bergen Beach became a popular residential area in the late 1980s and early 1990s. — https://brooklyneagle.com/303032/bergen-beach-history/

**B. Neighborhood background**

- History: Bronx Borough Engineer Louis Risse proposed the boulevard in the 1890s. The Grand Concourse was begun in 1894 and completed in 1909. — https://cityneighborhoods.nyc/concourse
- Cost and design: built 1894–1909 during the City Beautiful Movement at a cost of $14 million. Risse designed it as a four-mile thoroughfare divided into three roadways by tree-lined dividers. It opened to traffic on November 25, 1909. — https://www.brickunderground.com/blog/2015/12/grand_concourse_bronx and https://hdc.org/hdc-across-nyc/bronx/hd-map/bronx-landmarks-grand-concourse/
- Southern extension: it initially ran from 161st Street to Mosholu Parkway, and was extended south to 138th Street in 1927 when Mott Avenue was widened and renamed. — https://www.welcome2thebronx.com/2020/05/the-grand-concourse-a-look-at-the-bronxs-most-famous-street/
- Subway-driven building boom: the IND Concourse Line opened in 1933, and by the mid-1930s almost 300 apartment buildings had been built along the Concourse. Developers built Renaissance Revival and Art Deco apartment buildings for a middle-class population, predominantly Jewish, Italian and Irish families. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Concourse_(Bronx) and https://cityneighborhoods.nyc/concourse
- Style timeline from LPC via Brick Underground: Tudor, Renaissance and Colonial Revival designs between 1922 and 1931, then Art Deco and Modern between 1935 and 1945. The emphasis on light and air in larger apartments was prompted by the Tenement House law of 1901. — https://www.brickunderground.com/blog/2015/12/grand_concourse_bronx
- Decline and non-conforming storefronts: during the 1970s–80s decline, non-conforming ground-floor storefronts appeared on many buildings; before then, stores weren't permitted on the Concourse. In the 1960s the grassy medians were replaced with green-painted cement. — https://www.welcome2thebronx.com/2020/05/the-grand-concourse-a-look-at-the-bronxs-most-famous-street/ and https://kids.kiddle.co/Grand_Concourse_(Bronx)

### B. Neighborhood background

**History**
- **Early land.** The Dutch West India Company "purchased" much of the East Bronx from the Siwanoy in 1640. Thomas Pell bought a good portion of the same land in 1654. Edenwald was long considered part of Eastchester. — https://theneighborhoods.substack.com/p/edenwald-the-bronx
- **The Seton estate.** William Seton built "Cragdon," a fifty-one-acre estate in the center of what would become Edenwald. — https://theneighborhoods.substack.com/p/edenwald-the-bronx
- **The name.** John H. Eden owned an estate called Edenwald from 1900 to 1913; in German it means "Eden's Forest." — https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/edenwald-playground/history
- **Hebrew Orphan Asylum.** After Eden sold the estate, it became a Hebrew Orphan Asylum for children with disabilities. The City acquired the land by condemnation in 1950 to build Edenwald Houses. — https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/edenwald-playground/history
- **Who built the Houses.** Rodgers & Butler designed it, and Paul Tishman Company started building in 1951 at a cost of roughly $12 million. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edenwald_Houses
- **Rail.** The Gun Hill Road station on the Dyre Avenue Line opened in 1912 for the New York, Westchester and Boston Railway. It closed in 1937 and reopened as part of the subway in 1941. This is the line for the 5 train. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_Hill_Road_station_(IRT_Dyre_Avenue_Line)
