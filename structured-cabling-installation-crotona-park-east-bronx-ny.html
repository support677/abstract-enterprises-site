The Neighborhood

Why Crotona Park East Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Most of Crotona Park East was built, or built again, in the last forty years, and that changes almost everything about how a network gets wired here. City-data's profile of the neighborhood counts 5,542 housing units built between 2010 and 2019, another 4,849 built between 2000 and 2009 and 4,111 built in the 1990s. Those three decades alone outnumber what survived from earlier eras. The reason is the history every Bronx resident knows. NYU's Urban Practices history of the South Bronx describes a neighborhood of tenements and densely built apartments in the early 1900s, generally better built and less narrow than those in Morrisania or the Lower East Side because of new tenement laws, with five- and six-story apartment houses lining Southern Boulevard, Charlotte Street, Freeman Street and Fox Street once the subway arrived. Wikipedia records what happened next: in the 1970s the neighborhood was ravaged by arson, and on October 5, 1977 President Jimmy Carter stood on Charlotte Street and declared the South Bronx the worst neighborhood in the country.

The rebuilding came in waves, and each wave left a different kind of wall for a cable to travel through. Community groups including SEBCO, Wikipedia notes, played a central role in building subsidized multi-family homes and apartment buildings across most of the neighborhood. On Charlotte Street itself, CNN Money reported in 2009 that the first three-bedroom, two-bath ranch homes were manufactured in Pennsylvania and trucked over the George Washington Bridge one night in 1983, and that within three years 92 homes stood on the street, renamed Charlotte Gardens. A Trulia record for 1435 Charlotte Street describes one of them: a three-bedroom, two-bath, 2,112-square-foot single-family house built in 1988. Later came attached two- and three-family houses, then the large energy-conscious complexes like Intervale Green, which WHEDco describes as the nation's largest Energy Star-certified affordable housing complex when it opened, and finally the mid-rises of the last fifteen years. A data drop in a 1988 ranch house, a 1990s attached two-family, a pre-1920 walk-up and a 2020s mixed-use building are four different jobs, and the neighborhood has all four within a few blocks of the park.

Then there is the question that decides more cabling jobs here than any wall: who controls the building. Wikipedia lists eight NYCHA properties in Crotona Park East, including Murphy Houses, two 20-story towers; the six-story building at Bryant Avenue and East 174th Street; the six-story building at Hoe Avenue and East 173rd Street; and seven three-story buildings at East 173rd Street and Vyse Avenue. Wikipedia's figures also show that more than nine in ten occupied homes are rented. In practice, that means most people who want a wired connection in this neighborhood do not own the wall it would go through. A tenant in a new lottery building needs the landlord. A NYCHA resident needs NYCHA. A store moving into a new ground-floor space needs the building owner's telecom room, riser and rules. We sort out who has to say yes before we price a drop, because a quote for work nobody has approved is not worth the paper.

The fiber question works the same way. BroadbandNow reports that fiber internet is available to 84.7 percent of the Bronx, with Verizon Fios and Optimum as the fiber providers. But a borough figure says little about one building. BuildingHealthX's Bronx provider guide calls Fios coverage much patchier, building by building, in NYCHA-heavy parts of the South Bronx, and explains that for Fios to come in, the landlord or building management has to give Verizon access to install conduit, risers and the in-unit boxes. The city's 2020 settlement with Verizon, as the Mayor's Office described it, committed the company to bring Fios to 500,000 more households, to target upgrades in low-income areas including parts of the Bronx, and to ensure connectivity for every NYCHA residential building. We could not confirm whether this community district was one of the settlement's priority areas, and we will not guess. What we can say is that the inside of a building, the riser, the cellar path and the closet at the top, is often what stands between a provider's fiber at the curb and a working connection upstairs. That inside path is cabling work.

The neighborhood is still building. The Bronx Times reported permits filed for a six-story mixed-use building with 15 homes on the vacant lot at 919 Freeman Street. New York YIMBY lists a nine-story mixed-use building at 1718 Crotona Park East whose housing lottery has launched, and permits for four-story residential buildings at 1410 Longfellow Avenue and 880 Home Street. In June 2026 the same outlet reported four projects near the north end of the park that will add more than 250 homes. In January 2026 it reported that 1723 Boston Road, an eight-story mixed-use building at the meeting of Boston Road and Southern Boulevard with 21,097 square feet of commercial space and a cellar with 50 enclosed parking spaces, was nearing topping out, with completion expected around the end of 2026 or early 2027. Every one of those buildings needs a telecom room, a riser, cable to each home or tenant space, and cabling for whatever cameras and entry systems the owner chooses. The cheapest moment to get that right is while the walls are open.

The older stock needs different help. Early-1900s walk-ups that survived the fires, and buildings gutted and rebuilt inside during the 1990s, often have a cellar full of cable from decades of different providers, telephone pairs nobody uses, coax spliced at the foundation wall and no record of what goes where. Ground-floor stores on Southern Boulevard, which WHEDco says has more than 45,000 people living within a half-mile of its commercial stretch between East 174th Street and Westchester Avenue, run card terminals, cameras and back-office computers on whatever wiring the last tenant left. We spend a large share of our time in buildings like these not adding cable but finding, testing, labeling and removing it.

When a Crotona Park East building gets a proper rack and a tested cable plant, the next request is usually to put the cameras, the entry panel and the door controllers on it. Those systems ride on the same pathways and the same closet, so planning them into the drop count now saves a second pull through the same walls later. Who Controls the Path

From the Curb to the Jack: Who Owns Each Stretch of Cable in a Crotona Park East Building

Every wired connection travels the same rough path: from the provider's line at the street, through a service entrance in the cellar or a wall, up a riser or along a ceiling, into a closet or rack, and out to the jack beside a desk, a TV, a camera or a card terminal. In a house the owner controls the whole path. In most Crotona Park East buildings, different people control different pieces of it, and a cabling job only goes smoothly when each of them has said yes. The table below sets out who usually decides at each point, for the building types the public record describes here. It is our working guide, not a statement about any particular building; leases and building rules vary, and we confirm on every job.

Houses: one decision-maker, sometimes two

A Charlotte Gardens ranch house or an attached two-family on one of the side streets is the simplest case: the owner controls everything from the foundation wall to the bedroom jack. The only complication comes when a two- or three-family has tenants. A tenant who wants a wired line in their own apartment needs the owner's agreement, and if the internet account sits with one unit, running a cable from that unit's router to another apartment raises questions of who pays and who can see what. We plan two-family jobs so each unit has its own path from the service entrance if they have separate accounts, and we ask both parties before we drill through a shared wall or floor.

Walk-ups: the landlord owns the cellar

In an early-1900s walk-up, the cellar is where every provider's cable has come in over the years, and it belongs to the landlord. A tenant can arrange a provider installation for their own account, but adding a structured cabling run, a second jack, or a cable from the cellar up to a store or office upstairs is an alteration to the building. We ask for the landlord's or managing agent's written agreement and for the super to give us access to the cellar and any locked closet or roof hatch on the day.

New buildings: the telecom room is not yours

The mid-rises of the last fifteen years usually have a dedicated telecom or communications room, often in the cellar, with risers to each floor. Lottery advertisements and listings for buildings like 1825 Boston Road and 1718 Crotona Park East mention amenities such as an on-site super and security cameras, but they say nothing about tenant data closets, and the room itself is managed by the owner. A store or office taking space in a new building needs to know, before signing, where its cable will come from, whether it may put its own equipment in the telecom room, and whether there is spare conduit to its space. Those are lease questions as much as cabling questions, and we can help you ask them.

NYCHA buildings: the owner decides

NYCHA owns and controls its buildings. Residents in many developments have been offered Big Apple Connect, which, as PoliticsNY and Optimum describe it, provides free in-home internet with a modem and router, basic cable TV with a box and remote, and Wi-Fi hotspots in common areas. We could not confirm which Crotona Park East developments take part; Optimum's published Bronx list did not name Murphy Houses when the research for this page was done. Whatever the service, a resident cannot authorize drilling, running cable through walls or adding equipment to building spaces. We do not take that kind of work at a resident's request alone. What a resident can usually do without touching the building, such as placing their own router well or using a flat cable along the baseboard inside their own rooms, is covered in the questions below.

Count the devices, then add a margin

The most common regret after a cabling job is one too few jacks. Walk each room and count what plugs in now: a computer, a printer, a TV, a game console, a card terminal, a camera, a wireless access point. Then think about the next few years. A store that takes cards today may add a second terminal, a self-order screen or a camera over the register. A two-family owner who wires one apartment may rent the other to someone who works from home. Pulling two cables to a location costs far less than coming back for the second one later, because most of the cost of a drop is the labor of making the path, not the cable inside it.

Find the route the building already offers

Every building has natural routes, and using them is what keeps a cabling job clean. In houses, it may be a basement ceiling, a utility closet stacked above another closet, or the space beside a plumbing chase. In walk-ups, it may be an old dumbwaiter shaft, an abandoned chimney flue that has been properly closed, or a path the telephone company used decades ago. In new buildings, it is usually the riser and a ceiling. You do not need to find these routes yourself, but if you know where the plumbing runs, where the old phone lines entered or where a previous contractor ran cable, tell us; it helps us look in the right place first.

Structured Cabling for Crotona Park East's Architecture

Crotona Park East packs several building generations into a small area, and each changes three things about a cabling job: what the cable has to pass through, which fire separations it crosses, and where the rack can sit. These are the types the public record and neighborhood histories describe here, and how we approach each.

Charlotte Gardens Ranch Houses

One-story factory-built houses from the 1980s, like the 1988 three-bedroom house on Charlotte Street. One floor keeps runs short; the question is whether cable travels through an attic space, a crawl space or a basement, which we find out on site.

Attached Two- and Three-Family Houses

The subsidized houses built from the 1990s onward. Separate units, sometimes separate internet accounts, and shared walls and floors that need both parties' agreement before we drill.

Early-1900s Walk-Ups

Five- and six-story apartment houses of the kind NYU's history places along Southern Boulevard, Freeman and Fox Streets. Masonry, cellars full of old cable, and few clean routes between floors.

Large Rebuilt Complexes

Developments like Intervale Green, built with energy performance in mind. Tighter construction and sealed envelopes mean every penetration needs planning and sealing.

New Mid-Rises and Mixed-Use

Buildings from the last fifteen years and those going up now, like 1723 Boston Road. Telecom rooms, risers, block walls, steel studs, and ground-floor commercial shells.

NYCHA Buildings

From three-story buildings at East 173rd Street and Vyse Avenue to the 20-story Murphy Houses towers. Owner-controlled; work only on NYCHA's own authorization.

Ranch houses: short runs, but find the space first

The Charlotte Gardens houses were built in a factory and trucked in, which usually means consistent framing from house to house and rooms on one level. That is good news for cabling: no stairs to cross and short distances from wherever the service enters to the farthest bedroom. The uncertainty is the space above or below the rooms. Some houses of this type have an attic reachable through a hatch, some have a crawl space, and some have been finished in ways that leave little room. We open the access points on the walk-through, look at insulation and framing, and choose between dropping cable down from above, bringing it up from below, or, where neither space exists, routing it along a closet or behind base trim. Owners who have added a driveway camera or a backyard shed often want a cable there too; outdoor runs get outdoor-rated cable and protection where they leave the house.

Walk-ups: work with the masonry, not against it

In an early-1900s walk-up, the walls between apartments and floors are often brick or plaster on masonry, and there is rarely a hollow cavity to fish cable through the way there is in a stud wall. The usual routes are the cellar ceiling, where cable can travel along joists to a point below each riser location; any existing shaft or chase that can be used safely; and, where nothing else exists, a neat surface raceway up a stair hall or inside a closet. Every hole through a floor or a wall that separates apartments or encloses a stair is a fire separation, and it gets sealed with a listed firestop product sized for the cable passing through it. We also look hard at what is already there. A walk-up that has had three or four providers since the 1990s may have several coaxial and telephone runs that go nowhere; removing abandoned cable where it is accessible leaves room for the new run and makes the next technician's job easier.

New buildings: the plan should exist before the cable

The newer mid-rises usually have block walls at stairs and shafts, drywall on steel studs for apartment partitions, and concrete floors. Cable travels through ceilings and up dedicated risers, and the telecom room is the hub. On a building still under construction, our first contribution is the plan: where the telecom room sits, how big it is, how many sleeves pass through each floor slab, how much spare capacity the risers carry, and how each tenant space and each apartment gets its own path. On a finished building, the first job is learning what the owner already installed and what spare capacity exists. Core drilling through block or concrete is sometimes needed to reach a network room; we confirm the location with the owner and their engineer where structure is involved, and we seal the opening when the cable is in.

A store network has to keep taking payments

The earlier rezoning record for Crotona Park East and West Farms describes Southern Boulevard as predominantly local commercial and community facility uses: groceries, pharmacies, clothing stores, phone shops, restaurants, services. For a business like that, the network is not about speed so much as staying up. A card terminal that loses its connection at the Saturday rush costs more than any cabling job. So we plan store networks around a few plain rules. Every payment device gets a wired drop rather than Wi-Fi where the processor allows it. The router, switch and recorder sit on a battery backup so a brief power dip does not reboot them mid-sale. Cables from the register and the back office are kept apart from the guest or customer Wi-Fi with network separation configured on the equipment. And the rack goes somewhere that is locked, ventilated and away from the mop sink, not on the floor of a storage room.

Ground floors under the elevated line

Many stores on Southern Boulevard sit beneath or beside the elevated 2 and 5 tracks. That brings two practical concerns. The first is access: work on a storefront facade or sign band facing a busy street needs to be done safely and, where it involves sidewalk space, within city rules for sidewalk use. The second is the cellar. Ground-floor stores in older buildings often share a cellar with neighbors, and cable that leaves a store through the cellar crosses spaces the store does not control. We confirm with the building owner who controls the cellar and get agreement before routing a store's cable through it.

Rules and practical limits for business cabling

  • Permits for low-voltage data work. NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which provides that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, effective December 21, 2025 with the 2025 Electrical Code. A hiring guide we read says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the code defines that term. One other contractor's guide says most commercial low-voltage cabling still needs permits, so the sources disagree; on a commercial job we confirm the filing requirement for your specific scope before work begins.
  • Fire alarm wiring is a separate trade. Fire alarm circuits fall outside the data exemption and carry their own licensing and filings. We do not mix fire alarm cable into a data job.
  • New outlets are electrical work. Our work is low-voltage. Where a rack, a camera recorder or an access point needs a new receptacle, that circuit is for a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Cable ratings match the space. Cable in an air-handling ceiling must carry a plenum rating; cable rising between floors needs at least a riser rating. We match the jacket to the space it passes through.
  • Penetrations get firestopped. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with a listed firestop product suited to the cable and the wall, and we leave the sealed penetration visible for inspection.
  • Abstract approval from the building. In a leased space, the lease usually controls alterations. We ask for the landlord's or managing agent's agreement and any insurance certificate they require before the visit.

Field Notes: The First Half Hour in a Crotona Park East Cellar

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow when the job starts in a cellar, which in this neighborhood's walk-ups and two-families it usually does.

We meet the super or the owner at the cellar door and ask who else uses the space: other tenants' storage, a store's stock, the boiler contractor. We find where each provider's cable enters through the foundation and follow each one with a light before touching anything. We count the coaxial splitters and telephone blocks and photograph them as they are, because the photos are how we explain later what was there. We put a tone on the cable from the apartment or store we are working for, and listen for it, so we know which line is yours and which belong to someone else. We look up: at the joists, the pipes and the cellar ceiling, and note any place where a floor above is a fire separation. We look for water, because a cellar that floods in a heavy rain decides where a rack cannot go. Only then do we talk about routes, and only after you have agreed to the route does anyone drill.

Who has to say yes: tenants, owners and NYCHA

I moved into one of the new lottery buildings near the park and want a wired jack at my desk. Who has to approve that?

Your landlord, through the managing agent or the super. In a new building the walls, the ceiling space and the telecom room all belong to the owner, and most leases forbid alterations without written consent. Ask the managing agent whether the apartment already has data outlets wired back to the telecom room; many new buildings include them, and the answer may be a matter of activating a jack rather than pulling cable. If new work is needed, we send the managing agent a short written scope listing the route, the wall openings and the sealing, which makes approval much easier to get. Without that approval we do not drill, and we will tell you what you can do inside your own rooms instead, such as better router placement or a flat cable along the baseboard.

Our NYCHA apartment has the free Big Apple Connect router. Can you run a cable from it to my kid's desk in the back bedroom?

Not through NYCHA's walls or ceilings, because NYCHA controls the building and a resident cannot authorize that work. What usually works without altering anything is simple: a patch cable of the right length laid along the baseboard and under a door threshold, secured with clips that do not damage paint, or moving the router to a more central spot if the provider's cable allows it. Both are things a resident can typically do in their own rooms. If the router is the provider's equipment, as Big Apple Connect's modem and router are, the provider should be your first call about relocating it. We are glad to advise on the phone, but we do not take in-wall work in NYCHA apartments at a resident's request alone.

I own a two-family on Vyse Avenue. The tenant upstairs wants Fios and I have Optimum downstairs. What needs to happen at the building?

Two providers can serve one house, but each needs a path from where it enters to the unit it serves. As the building owner, you grant the provider access, and the provider brings its line to an entry point and, for Fios, an in-unit box. What we usually do in a two-family is give each apartment its own clean route from the cellar or entry wall to a small panel or closet in that unit, so the two services never share a splitter or a cable. That keeps billing, privacy and troubleshooting separate. If you also want a building network for cameras or an entry panel, that gets its own run too, independent of either tenant's internet account.

Buildings under construction and new commercial space

We have permits for a new building on a vacant lot. At what point in construction should the low-voltage contractor first walk the site?

Before the floor slabs are poured, if possible, and certainly before framing closes. The decisions that are cheap on paper and expensive later are the telecom room's size and location, how many sleeves pass through each floor, whether risers carry spare conduit, and how each tenant space gets its own path. A walk at the plan stage lets us mark sleeve locations for the concrete crew. A second visit at rough-in, while studs are open, is when cable goes in. A third, after drywall and paint, is for terminations, testing and labeling. Builders who bring us in only after the walls are closed end up paying for core drilling and surface raceway they could have avoided.

While the walls of our new building are open, should we add spare conduit or pull strings even where we have no cable planned?

Usually, yes, and it is one of the best-value decisions in a new building. A length of empty conduit with a pull string, from the telecom room to each floor and to each commercial space, costs little during construction and saves a great deal when a tenant, a provider or a new system arrives years later. In Crotona Park East, where buildings like 1723 Boston Road combine homes, commercial space and a cellar garage, future needs are hard to predict: a store may want its own fiber, the garage may need cameras, a tenant may want a second provider. We mark each spare pathway on the drawings we hand over so the next contractor knows it exists.

Our business is signing a lease for a ground-floor space in one of the new mixed-use buildings. The shell has no data cabling. What should we ask the landlord before signing?

Ask four things in writing. Where will our cable come from: is there a conduit or path from the building's telecom room to our space? May we put our own equipment, or a provider's box for our account, in the telecom room, and who holds the key? Which providers already serve the building, and has the owner signed access agreements with them? And what rules govern alterations, insurance and work hours inside our space? The answers decide whether your fit-out is a simple job inside your walls or a longer one that involves the owner's risers. We can walk the space with you before you sign and tell you what the answers mean for your cabling.

Older walk-ups and houses

Our walk-up on Bryant Avenue has masonry walls between floors. How does cable get from the cellar up to the fifth floor without a mess in the hallway?

We look first for a vertical route the building already has: a closed chimney flue no longer in use, a dumbwaiter shaft, a plumbing chase with space beside it, or a path a telephone company used decades ago. Where one exists and can be used safely, cable rises in it, protected and sealed at each floor. Where none exists, the cleanest choice is usually a painted surface raceway inside a closet stacked floor above floor, or in a corner of the stair hall where it is least visible. Every floor crossing is a fire separation and gets a listed firestop seal. We show the owner the route before committing, because the hallway is shared and appearance matters to everyone in the building.

Our Charlotte Street ranch house is all on one floor. How do you get cable to the bedrooms at the far end?

One floor usually makes it easier, not harder. We first check the spaces above and below the rooms. If there is an attic reachable through a hatch, cable can run across it and drop down inside interior walls to each bedroom. If there is a crawl space or basement, it can come up from below. If the house has neither, or the space is too tight, we route through closets and behind base trim. Factory-built houses of this era tend to have consistent framing, which makes fishing walls predictable once we have confirmed the layout in one room. We bring the cable to a small panel where the service enters, so every room's jack connects back to one place.

The cable company left coaxial outlets in every room. Can the network use that coax instead of new Ethernet cable?

Often it can, as a sensible middle step. Adapters that carry a network over coaxial cable can turn an existing coax outlet into a wired connection without opening walls, which is useful in a rented apartment or a walk-up with masonry walls. The catch is that the coax has to be in decent condition, the splitters have to be the right type, and the provider's own signal on the same cable has to be considered. We test the coax runs, replace bad connectors and splitters, and tell you honestly whether the result will be steady enough. Where it will not, or where you need many connections, new Ethernet cable is the better long-term answer.

Fire safety, power and shared systems

After the August 2025 fire at 1700 Crotona Park East, our board asked how holes for cable through floors and walls get sealed. What do you do?

We do not know the cause of that fire and are not suggesting cabling played any part; firefighters responded to a fire through the roof of the building at around 3 a.m., according to the news report. But the board's question is the right one. A floor or a wall between apartments is built to slow fire and smoke, and every hole a contractor makes through it weakens that unless it is sealed properly. We seal each penetration with a listed firestop product chosen for the size of the hole and the type of cable, leave room in the seal for future cables where a sleeve is used, and photograph each sealed penetration for the owner's records. Open, unsealed holes from earlier work that we find on our route get pointed out to the owner too.

If the power goes out on the block, will the building's network and cameras keep running?

Only if the equipment is on a battery backup, and only for as long as the battery lasts. A battery backup unit in the rack keeps the switch, router and camera recorder running through short outages and lets them shut down cleanly in long ones, which protects both the equipment and the recordings. How long it lasts depends on how much the rack draws, including power sent to cameras and access points over the cable. We size the backup to the actual load when we build the rack and show you what it covers. Keep in mind that the provider's own equipment, outside your building, may lose power during a wider outage regardless.

Our building's cameras and entry panel will share cable with the tenants' and the office network. How are they kept apart?

They can share the pathways and the rack without sharing the network. Each system gets its own cables, labeled by system at both ends, and its own ports on the patch panel. On the switch, the camera and entry systems are placed on a separate network segment from office or tenant traffic, so a tenant's device cannot reach the camera recorder and a problem on one network does not take down the other. The building's systems belong to the owner and are documented in the owner's port map; tenant connections are documented separately. That separation also makes it easier to hand over to a future managing agent without exposing anyone's private network.

"The South Bronx is old tenements, so expect plaster-and-lath retrofits."

That picture fits some of the borough, but not most of this neighborhood. City-data counts more than 14,000 units in Crotona Park East built since 1990, and Wikipedia describes a neighborhood dominated by public housing and newly built subsidized townhouses and apartment buildings.

Older walk-ups do survive, especially along the avenues the NYU history names, and those jobs are true retrofits through masonry and cellars. But a large share of calls here involve drywall on steel studs, block walls and telecom rooms in buildings less than thirty years old.

That is why we ask the building's age and type before we quote. Assuming every job is a century-old retrofit overprices the newer buildings, and assuming every job is new construction underprices the walk-ups.

"Low-voltage data cabling in NYC always needs a DOB permit."

The law changed. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, effective December 21, 2025.

The exemption is not a free-for-all. Our practice is to confirm the filing requirement for each commercial scope before work starts, and to keep fire alarm wiring out of data jobs entirely. On a house or apartment network, the question usually comes down to who is doing the work and what voltage it carries.

"Fiber is available almost everywhere in the Bronx, so just call the provider."

BroadbandNow's figure, 84.7 percent of the Bronx with fiber available, is a borough average. It does not tell you whether a specific walk-up on Hoe Avenue or a new building on Boston Road is wired for it inside.

BuildingHealthX's Bronx guide notes that Fios availability is patchier building by building in NYCHA-heavy parts of the South Bronx, and that the landlord has to grant access for conduit, risers and in-unit boxes before service can come in.

So the honest answer is: call the provider to check the address, and ask the owner whether an access agreement exists. If the obstacle is the inside path, the riser and the cellar route, that is where a cabling contractor helps.

"A renter can hire any installer to wire their apartment."

With more than nine in ten homes here rented, according to Wikipedia's figures, this claim sends a lot of people in the wrong direction. Running cable through walls, ceilings or floors is an alteration to the building, and most leases require the landlord's written consent.

In NYCHA buildings, which make up eight properties in the neighborhood, the owner controls all building work, and a resident cannot authorize it.

We ask who owns the building at the first call, and we will not drill on a renter's word alone. What a renter can do inside their own rooms without altering anything is often enough, and we are glad to explain it.

"Mesh Wi-Fi makes in-wall cable unnecessary."

Mesh systems are useful, and in a one-story ranch house they may be all a family needs. But wireless signals weaken through block and masonry, and the walk-ups and newer buildings here have plenty of both.

A mesh system also has to send traffic between its units, and when that link is wireless through thick walls, every device on the far unit shares a weaker connection. A cable to each access point removes that weakness.

Card terminals, camera recorders and work computers are better on a wired drop where the equipment allows it. We usually recommend a few wired drops for the devices that matter most and cabled access points for everything else.

"Cable is cable; anyone can pull it."

Pulling cable is the easy part. What separates a working cable plant from a future headache is the jacket rating for the space, the bend and pull tension during installation, the termination at each end, sealing of every fire separation, and a test that proves each drop works.

In buildings where several contractors have come and gone, we routinely find cable lying on ceiling tiles, unsealed holes through floors, and drops that were never tested and do not pass. The cost of finding and fixing that is often higher than doing it right the first time.

Every drop we install is certified with a cable analyzer, labeled at both ends and listed on a port map. Every penetration we make is sealed with a listed product and photographed.

"A brand-new building already has everything a store or tenant needs."

New buildings come with a lot: the advertisement for 1825 Boston Road lists an on-site super and security cameras, and the lottery listing for 1718 Crotona Park East mentions security cameras among its amenities. Those are the owner's systems.

None of the public listings we read describes data cabling inside a commercial tenant's space, and a commercial shell, like the 21,097 square feet planned at 1723 Boston Road, is usually delivered without a tenant's network inside it.

A store moving into a new building should ask where its cable will come from and who controls the telecom room, before signing. The building being new does not mean your space is wired.

We need a cable from our ranch house out to the detached garage. How is that run protected?
+

A run between buildings uses cable rated for outdoor or underground use, protected in conduit where it is exposed or buried, and sealed where it enters each building so water and pests stay out. For longer distances or where lightning exposure is a concern, fiber between the two buildings avoids carrying electrical surges from one to the other. We choose the route on the walk-through.

+

Where it is accessible, confirmed dead and the owner agrees, yes. We trace each cable first so nothing still in use is cut, then remove the abandoned runs and splitters. Clearing them leaves room for new cable, reduces the fire load in the cellar and makes the next technician's job far easier. Removal is offered as its own line on the quote.
