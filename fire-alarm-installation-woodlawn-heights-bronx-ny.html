Six kinds of building cover almost every Woodlawn Heights fire alarm request. What separates them is less the brand of alarm than the materials in the walls, the number of households under one roof, and what sits underneath the bedrooms.

The detached frame house

One-family houses on the side streets and ridges, many from the subway years or earlier, with wood framing, plaster or replaced walls, a cellar with the heating plant and bedrooms on the upper floor.

The up-and-down two-family

Two apartments stacked in one house, often with the upper unit spread across two floors, like the 1918 two-family on East 236th Street with a three-bedroom duplex upstairs.

The converted house

Houses split into two or more apartments years after they were built, such as the 1965 one-family on East 237th Street listed as converted to a two-family, where the alarms rarely match the new layout.

The brick building over a store

Two- and three-story buildings on Katonah Avenue with a deli, bar or shop at street level, a business cellar below and apartments above, owned by one landlord and occupied by several parties.

The midcentury co-op

Brick elevator buildings such as the 1958 co-op on Van Cortlandt Park East and buildings on Herkimer Place and Webster Avenue, with a live-in super, a boiler room and a shared building system.

The detached frame house: fire travels up, so the plan has to look down

A wood-frame house built in the 1910s or 1920s is a vertical building in fire terms. Its open stair runs from the front hall to the bedrooms, its closets and pipe chases stack from the cellar to the attic, and in many houses of that era the spaces between the studs run unbroken for more than one floor. A fire that starts in the cellar or on the first floor has several routes upward, which is why the January 2016 fire on East 236th Street could move from the first floor to the roof so quickly. Our plan for a house like this works from the bottom up. The cellar gets detection suited to what is down there: a smoke alarm at the bottom of the cellar stair where the air is clean, and heat detection or a carefully placed smoke alarm near the boiler or oil burner rather than right over it. The first floor gets a smoke alarm in the main living area and one near, but not inside, the kitchen. Every bedroom gets its own, and the hallway outside the bedrooms gets another. Carbon monoxide alarms go outside the sleeping area and near the heating plant. Then everything is linked. In a house with plaster walls, sealed ten-year alarms with wireless interconnect from a single manufacturer usually do this without opening anything, and we test the link floor by floor, because old plaster and lath can weaken a wireless signal. Where an owner wants hardwired alarms and the walls are already open, we run interconnect cable and bring in an electrician for any new 120-volt circuit.

The up-and-down two-family: two households, one stair, one building

In a two-family, the owner usually lives in one apartment and rents the other, or a family occupies both. Either way the house has two sets of bedrooms and, very often, one way out to the street that both households share. The listing for the 1918 two-family at 70 East 236th Street describes a one-bedroom apartment on the first floor and a three-bedroom duplex upstairs, a layout common here. In that arrangement, the upstairs family's bedrooms can sit two floors above the cellar and one floor above the downstairs kitchen. If the downstairs alarm sounds and the upstairs family cannot hear it, they lose the minutes they most need. We usually recommend that each apartment has its own complete set of linked alarms, and that the shared spaces, meaning the cellar, the common stair and the entry, have detection that sounds in both apartments. How that is wired or linked depends on whether the two apartments are on separate electric meters and how the landlord wants to handle maintenance. The owner's record of each apartment's alarms, with dates, protects everyone if a dispute ever arises about what was installed.

The converted house: the alarms remember the old floor plan

Houses that were divided into apartments after they were built, sometimes decades ago and sometimes more than once, are where we find the most confused alarm arrangements. The 1965 house on East 237th Street that StreetEasy lists as a one-family converted to a two-family is an example of the type. In a converted house, a hallway alarm may sit in what is now one tenant's living room, a hardwired circuit may feed alarms in both apartments from one apartment's panel, and an interconnect wire may link a bedroom upstairs to a cellar alarm that nobody downstairs knows about. None of that is necessarily dangerous, but it is unclear, and unclear systems get disconnected. Our survey of a converted house maps every device to the apartment and room it serves, traces each hardwired circuit to its breaker, notes which alarms are linked to which, and then proposes a layout that matches the house as it is lived in now. Where the conversion itself is a question, meaning whether the apartments and exits were legally created, that belongs with an architect and the Department of Buildings, and we say so rather than guess.

The brick building over a store: a business underneath, families on top

This is the building type that both big Katonah Avenue fires happened in, and it is the one where planning matters most. A typical example has a deli, bar or shop at street level, a cellar used by that business for stock, coolers, kegs or storage, the building's boiler and meters somewhere in the same cellar or next to it, and two or three floors of apartments reached by a stair that often starts in a vestibule beside the store entrance. The landlord owns the building but may not have a key to the store or its cellar; the business owner controls the cellar but does not live there; the tenants upstairs are asleep above it. Our survey starts underground and works up. We note what is stored in the cellar and how it is lit and powered, what detection, if any, exists down there, and what it is connected to. We look at the cellar ceiling for openings, such as pipe holes, abandoned chutes and unsealed gaps around wiring, that would let smoke into the store or the stair. Then we ask the central question: if a fire started in that cellar at four in the morning, what would tell the families upstairs, and how soon? Depending on the building, the answer may be household alarms in each apartment plus linked detection in the cellar and stair, or it may be a building fire alarm system that the FDNY has to approve. We tell the owner which before pricing anything.

The midcentury co-op and the new senior building: systems with owners and rules

The co-ops here are small by Bronx standards. The Raveis listing for 4320 Van Cortlandt Park East describes a co-op built in 1958, seven levels, with a live-in super and oil heat, across the street from the park; Redfin lists elevator co-op apartments at 4200 Herkimer Place and 4315 Webster Avenue. In a building like that, the co-op corporation is responsible for the building system in the halls, stairs, cellar and boiler room, while the alarms inside each apartment depend on the city's rules for multiple dwellings and the co-op's own lease and house rules. We write those two halves up separately. For the building, we look at the panel, its event history and standby batteries, the boiler and oil tank rooms, the laundry and storage spaces, and the stair doors. For apartments, we offer the board a single standard so every unit is done the same way and recorded. Woodlawn Senior Living at 69 East 233rd Street is a different case entirely: HPD describes it as an 80-unit affordable Passive House development for low-income seniors, opened in December 2022. A new building like that was designed with its fire protection by a design team and accepted for its use, and any change belongs with its operator and design professional. Where we can help in a building of that kind is with routine service, in-unit accessories residents need, such as strobes for someone hard of hearing, and dated records, never with casual changes to the design.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Katonah Avenue Building From the Cellar Up

Go down before going up. On a survey of a building with a store at street level, the technician asks for the cellar first, with the business owner present if possible. The two fires in the local record both began below a business, so the cellar is where we spend the most time and take the most photos.

Look at the ceiling, not just the room. In the cellar we note what is stored and how close it is to the boiler, the meters and any light fixtures. Then we look up: open joists, holes left where old pipes were pulled, gaps around cable bundles, an old hatch to the store above. Each opening is a route for smoke into the floors above. We photograph them and tell the owner which ones a mason or an electrician should close, because sealing them is not fire alarm work but it changes how much time the detection buys.

Find the way upstairs. Next we follow the route a person, and smoke, would take from the cellar to the apartments: the cellar stair, the door at its top, the vestibule, the main stair. Does the cellar door close on its own? Is the stair the only way out of the apartments? Is there a fire escape at the back?

Knock on each apartment. Inside each unit we count the alarms, read their labels and dates, check whether they are sealed ten-year or hardwired, press the test buttons, and listen from each bedroom with the door closed while the cellar or stair device sounds.

End where the business does. Finally we look at the store itself: its kitchen or prep area, its back room, any small panel or monitoring box, and the hours it sits empty. Only then do we start a drawing or a price.

Compliance notes for Woodlawn Heights homes and businesses

  • Sealed or hardwired since 2019. Since April 1, 2019, every smoke alarm sold in New York State must have a sealed, non-removable battery that lasts at least ten years, or be hardwired. We install only those two kinds.
  • The city's replacement deadline. Brick Underground reports that a New York City local law required homeowners and landlords to replace old devices by 2021 with smoke detectors that use a sealed ten-year battery. Many alarms installed to meet that deadline are now more than halfway through their rated life.
  • Existing homes versus alterations. Published summaries of the city rule say an existing apartment can comply with approved sealed-battery detectors, while hardwired, interconnected alarms with battery backup are required for new construction and substantial alterations. That line matters in a neighborhood full of houses being renovated.
  • Building systems start with approved plans. The FDNY's fire code chapter on fire protection systems calls for fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted for review and approval before installation. We install, label and pre-test to approved plans; we are not the design professional.
  • Repair or redesign. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules treats replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change as a repair, and moving or replacing a control panel as work that needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Small residential buildings. UpCodes' copy of the 2022 Building Code says a manual fire alarm system is not required in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit. That exemption concerns building systems; the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms inside each home are a separate requirement.
  • Assembly spaces. Restaurants and bars are generally classed as assembly occupancies. Whether a particular pub needs a building fire alarm system depends on its occupant load and layout, which the design professional determines.
  • The Yonkers side. McLean Avenue is technically in Yonkers, which has its own fire department and code officials. A business on the north side of the line follows Yonkers' process, not the FDNY's, and we treat it as a Westchester County fire alarm job.
  • Landmarks. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district covering Woodlawn Heights. Woodlawn Cemetery is a National Historic Landmark, but it is not housing, and we found nothing that limits interior alarm work in the houses.
Two Fires, One Stretch of Avenue

Behan's in 2013, the Deli in 2021: What Two Basement Fires Teach Katonah Avenue Landlords

The cellar is where nobody is looking. IrishCentral reported that the 2013 fire started in the basement of Behan's around 10:30 on a Saturday night, when the bar upstairs was open. Norwood News reported that the 2021 fire was called in at 8:34 in the morning from inside the deli's basement. Either way, the room where each fire started was below the activity, and in a typical Katonah Avenue building the cellar is used for stock, coolers, kegs, cleaning supplies and the building's mechanical equipment. People go down to fetch something and come back up. A fire that starts while nobody is there can grow until it is big enough to notice from above, and by then it may already be finding its way through the ceiling. Cellar detection that sounds where people are, in the store, behind the bar and in the apartments, is the single change that addresses this most directly.

Fire goes up through the building's openings. News 12 was told the 2021 fire started in the basement but quickly spread to the two floors above, and Norwood News reported it extended to the first floor of the apartment building. Old mixed-use buildings carry plenty of vertical openings: pipe and cable holes through the cellar ceiling, old dumbwaiter or chute shafts, gaps around a cellar stair, and the hollow spaces of walls that were altered when the storefront was rebuilt. Detection does not close any of those, but knowing where they are tells us where smoke will show up first and where a detector, or a request to the owner to seal an opening, will do the most good. That is why our surveys of these buildings always include the cellar ceiling and the floor of the store above it.

Walls between buildings work when they are kept whole. In 2013, IrishCentral reported, the fire reached the adjoining building before 11 p.m., and a firewall in the Emerald Pharmacy stopped it from spreading further. Along an avenue of attached buildings, those dividing walls are a large part of everyone's protection. They are also what new cable, pipe and duct work tends to punch through without anyone thinking about it. When our work has to cross a fire-rated wall or floor, the hole is kept as small as possible and sealed with fire-rated material afterward, and we photograph the sealed penetration for the owner's file.

A big response follows a late discovery. The 2021 fire drew 138 firefighters from 33 units, with Yonkers firefighters seen helping, and four families living in four separate apartments above the damaged businesses lost their homes. ABC7 reported the 2013 fire took nearly two hours to bring under control. Large responses are what a fire that has already spread demands. The aim of detection is to make the first call earlier, while the fire is still in the room where it began, and to make sure the families upstairs are already on their way out when firefighters arrive.

Responsibility is shared, so the plan has to be written down. In a building like these, the landlord, the business tenant and the residential tenants each control part of the picture. The business decides what goes in the cellar and who has keys. The landlord decides what is installed and who maintains it. The residents decide whether their alarms stay on the ceiling. A written plan that names each device, where it is, who maintains it and who gets a call when it sounds is what keeps that arrangement from falling apart the first time a lease changes hands. We produce that plan as part of every mixed-use job, and we send a copy to whoever the owner names.

The Ten-Year Clock

The 2019 Sealed-Battery Rule, the 2021 Deadline, and the Alarms Coming Due Around Woodlawn Heights

Two rule changes, close together, put a large number of new smoke alarms on Woodlawn Heights ceilings in a short span of years. From April 1, 2019, every smoke alarm sold in New York State had to be hardwired or have a sealed, non-removable battery lasting at least ten years. And Brick Underground reports that a city local law required homeowners and landlords to replace older devices with sealed ten-year detectors by 2021. In a neighborhood of owner-occupied houses and small rental buildings, plenty of families and landlords did exactly that: they bought a box of sealed alarms around 2019, 2020 or 2021 and put them up in an afternoon.

What ten years means in practice. A sealed alarm is designed to run for about ten years from when it was made or activated, then signal that it has reached the end of its life. Alarms that went up in that 2019 to 2021 window will reach that point roughly between 2029 and 2031. The date printed on the back of each alarm is the guide, not the date someone remembers buying it. When we survey a house, we read every label and write down every date, because a house where all the alarms went up the same weekend is a house where all of them will begin chirping in the same season, usually in the middle of the night.

Why the first round may not be the right round. Many alarms installed to meet the deadline were placed where the old ones had been, often one per floor in a hallway. That met the battery rule but not always the placement that protects people best in an old frame house: an alarm in each bedroom, outside sleeping areas, on every level including the cellar, and carbon monoxide alarms near the bedrooms and the heating plant. They were also frequently not linked, so a cellar alarm could sound unheard by someone asleep on the third floor. When the first generation of sealed alarms comes due, the replacement is the moment to fix the layout and add interconnect, not just to buy the same model again.

What landlords in two-families should do now. If you own a two-family or a small building on Katonah Avenue, make a list today: each apartment, each room, each alarm, its type and the date on its label. If the alarms in the tenant's apartment and yours went up together, plan their replacement together. And if any apartment still has an alarm with a battery door, it predates the 2019 rule for new sales and should be on the list for replacement first. Our survey produces that list, and our per-unit pricing, $400 to $800 per unit on our list, covers replacing a whole apartment's alarms at once.

What a renovation changes. If the replacement coincides with a gut renovation, the rules change. Published summaries of the city requirement say hardwired, interconnected alarms with battery backup are required for new construction and substantial alterations, while existing apartments can comply with sealed-battery units. A family pulling a 1920s house down to the studs should plan hardwired alarms with the electrician, before the walls close.

These are situations Woodlawn Heights homeowners, landlords, business owners and co-op boards bring to us. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Fires that start under the shop

The 2021 deli fire on Katonah Avenue began in the basement and then reached the apartments above. How do you work out the route smoke would take from a store cellar up to the tenants in a building like ours?

We trace it physically, starting at the cellar floor and ending at each bedroom door, and we write down every opening along the way. In the cellar we look up at the ceiling first. Old buildings on the avenue often have holes left where plumbing or gas lines were rerouted, bundles of cable passing through unsealed openings, an abandoned hatch or chute, and the gap around the cellar stair. Each one is a route into the store or into the stair hall. Then we go to the store floor above and look down at the same places from the other side, along with any wall that was opened when the storefront was rebuilt. Next we follow the residents' route out: the cellar door, the vestibule, the stair and each landing, noting which doors close on their own and which are propped. Inside each apartment we note where the bedrooms are relative to that stair. The result is a simple sketch showing where smoke from the cellar would appear first, which apartments it would reach soonest, and where a detector would notice it earliest. That sketch decides the plan: detection in the cellar placed to catch smoke before it rises, a device at the bottom and top of the stair, and alarms in each apartment linked so the cellar is heard upstairs. Openings that should be sealed are listed for the owner and the right trade, because closing them buys time that no alarm can.

Our pub's cellar holds the walk-in cooler, the keg lines and the gas cylinders for the taps. Which detector can live in a cold, damp room with compressors cycling all night?

A bar cellar is a hard room for detection, and the wrong device there either fails early or cries wolf until someone disables it. Compressors and condensers for the walk-in and the beer lines throw off heat and run in cycles; the room may be cool and damp near the cooler and warm near the machinery; condensation forms on cold surfaces; and kegs and cylinders get moved around on deliveries. A standard smoke detector mounted over a condenser can be fooled by dust blown off the coils, and one mounted near a cold door can collect moisture. In most cases we recommend detection chosen for the room's actual conditions: smoke detection where the air is clean and stable, such as near the foot of the cellar stair and over dry stock, and heat detection near the compressors, the boiler or other equipment that runs hot. Cable in a cellar like that runs in protected routes, often conduit, so a keg delivery does not tear it down. Two things about the gas are worth saying plainly. The carbon dioxide in tap cylinders is not carbon monoxide, and a carbon monoxide alarm will not warn you about a CO2 leak; that is a separate safety question for your beverage supplier and equipment installer. And if the cellar also holds a gas-fired boiler or water heater, carbon monoxide detection belongs near it and in the apartments above. If the pub needs a building fire alarm system, the device choices for the cellar are made by the design professional, and we raise these conditions so they are designed for, not discovered later.

A firewall in the pharmacy next door stopped the 2013 Behan's fire from spreading further. If fire alarm cable has to pass through a wall like that, how do you keep the wall doing its job?

By crossing it as few times as possible, with the smallest hole that works, and sealing it properly afterward. A fire-rated wall between attached buildings, or a rated floor between a store and the apartments above it, only works if it stays continuous. Every hole drilled through it for cable, pipe or conduit is a weak spot unless it is filled with a listed firestop material made for that kind of penetration. On a Katonah Avenue job, we first try to plan routes that avoid crossing rated walls and floors at all, for example by keeping cellar detection cable on the cellar side and bringing it up through an existing sleeve rather than a new hole. When a crossing is unavoidable, we drill one hole, run every cable that needs to cross through it, and seal it with firestop material suited to the wall, whether that is masonry, block or a framed assembly. The sealed penetration is photographed and noted on the owner's records, so the next contractor and any inspector can see it was done. We also note any existing penetrations we find that are open, such as old pipe holes and gaps around cable bundles, because those matter as much as ours. Sealing them may be work for a mason or another trade, and we tell the owner who should do it. What we will never do is leave a hole in a dividing wall because it is out of sight, since the wall's job is exactly what saved the building next to Behan's in 2013.

Our staff lock up after a late night on Katonah Avenue. What should the last person out check on the fire alarm before leaving the building empty?

A short closing routine, done the same way every night, closes most of the gap between the last bartender leaving and the first delivery arriving. First, look at the panel or the monitoring keypad, if the premises has one. It should show normal, with no trouble light, no supervisory signal and no zone or device bypassed. If anything other than normal is showing, write down exactly what it says and call whoever services the system before leaving, because a trouble condition can mean part of the system is not watching. Second, walk the cellar and the back room. Make sure nothing is stacked against or hung on a detector, no cardboard is piled near the boiler or compressors, and the cellar door closes fully. Third, check that nothing has been left switched on that should not be: fryers, warming equipment, space heaters and chargers for scooters or tools. Fourth, confirm the monitoring arrangement is armed if your system needs to be set. Finally, make sure the staff know who gets the call if the alarm sounds overnight, and that the list of key holders the monitoring company holds is current. We set up this routine on paper as part of every pub and restaurant job, with a one-page card for the back office, and partner central station monitoring is available at $19.99 a month for premises that sit empty overnight. The routine matters most in the small hours, because that is when a cellar fire has the most time before anyone notices.

Frame houses on the hill streets

We are planning a gut renovation of our 1920s two-family. What turns the job from sealed battery alarms into hardwired, interconnected ones?

The size of the work. Published summaries of the city rule say an existing apartment can comply with approved sealed-battery detectors, while hardwired, interconnected alarms with battery backup are required for new construction and substantial alterations. A full gut renovation, where walls and ceilings come down and the house is substantially rebuilt inside, is the kind of job that usually falls on the hardwired side. Your architect or expediter, who files the renovation, is the right person to confirm how your specific scope is classified, and we coordinate with them rather than guess. In practice, if your walls are going to be open anyway, hardwired alarms are the sensible choice even if you could argue for less. The electrician runs a circuit, we run interconnect cable between every location, and when the walls close the house has alarms powered from the house, backed up by batteries, and linked so a fire anywhere sounds everywhere. In a two-family, we plan each apartment's alarms as a set and decide with you how the shared stair and cellar are covered and from which apartment's electrical panel the alarms are fed. The best time to call us is when the renovation drawings are being made, so alarm locations are on the plan. The second-best time is before the electrician's rough-in inspection, while cable can still be added. Our custom hardwired systems run $2,000 to $6,000 on our list depending on the number of floors and devices, and we will price yours from the drawings.

Our house still heats with an oil burner in the cellar. Where do carbon monoxide alarms go, and does the burner room need anything else?

An oil burner produces carbon monoxide when combustion goes wrong, just as gas equipment can, so a house heated by oil still needs carbon monoxide alarms. The main ones go outside each sleeping area, close enough to the bedrooms that a sleeping person hears them, and on each level with bedrooms. We also recommend one in the cellar near the burner and water heater, placed where a technician servicing the burner would see it but not directly beside the flue. Linking the cellar alarm to the ones upstairs means a problem in the boiler room wakes the family rather than sounding unheard below. The burner room itself has two other needs. A smoke alarm placed right over a burner tends to give false alarms when it fires up after a long idle, so we usually put smoke detection at the foot of the cellar stairs, where it sees smoke on its way up, and heat detection closer to the equipment if the owner wants the room itself watched. The oil tank, fill pipe and vent are not fire alarm devices, but we note their condition and anything stored against them, because rags, cardboard and paint cans stacked around a burner are a common finding in old cellars. If the house has a chimney shared by the burner and a fireplace, carbon monoxide alarms on the floors that chimney passes become more important. And if the alarm ever sounds, everyone leaves and the call goes to 911 and then the oil company, not the other way around.

Our two-family was converted from a one-family years ago, and nobody knows which alarms belong to which apartment or what powers them. How do you sort that out?

We map it, device by device, before touching anything. The survey starts with a walk through both apartments and the cellar, listing every smoke and carbon monoxide alarm with its location, type, label date and whether it is battery-only, sealed or hardwired. For each hardwired alarm we find the circuit that feeds it, which in a converted house can mean an alarm in the upstairs apartment fed from the downstairs panel, or a circuit shared with lighting from before the conversion. We then test linking: we sound one alarm at a time and note which others respond. In a converted house that often shows surprises, such as a cellar alarm linked to one bedroom but not the other apartment, or two separate sets of alarms that were each meant to be linked and are not. Where cable routes are unclear, we trace them with a tone generator rather than opening walls. The result is a drawing that shows which apartment each alarm serves, how each is powered, and what it is linked to. From there we propose a layout that matches the house as it is lived in now: a complete, linked set for each apartment, shared spaces covered so both households hear them, and hardwired circuits fed from a sensible place. Anything that raises a question about the conversion itself, such as whether a bedroom was ever approved, goes to the owner and their architect, not into our report as a guess.

Co-ops and assembly spaces

Our co-op's super lives in the building. Can alarm and trouble signals reach his apartment at night, and does that replace central station monitoring?

Signals can reach the super, and in a small co-op that is often worth doing, but it is not the same as monitoring, and the two should not be confused. A remote indicator or sounder in the super's apartment, or a notification to his phone where the system supports it, tells him that the panel has gone into alarm, trouble or supervisory condition, so he can respond to a trouble light at two in the morning instead of finding it at eight. That is useful for keeping a system in good repair, and it is especially useful in a building like the 1958 co-op on Van Cortlandt Park East, which its listing describes as having a live-in super. Where the building's fire alarm is a filed system, adding anything to it, including a remote annunciator, is a change the design professional and possibly the FDNY need to see, and we raise that before quoting. What a super's indicator cannot do is call the fire department reliably. The super may be asleep, out of the building, on vacation or simply not hear it. A fire alarm signal that matters should go to a central station that calls 911 and the building's contacts every time, which is what partner monitoring at $19.99 a month provides. Some buildings are required to be monitored and some are not; the design professional confirms which. For boards deciding, our suggestion is both: monitoring for the alarm signal, and the super informed of trouble and supervisory signals so problems get fixed quickly.

When does a Katonah Avenue pub or restaurant count as an assembly occupancy for fire alarm purposes, and what does that add to the job?

A pub or restaurant is generally treated as an assembly occupancy under the FDNY code because people gather there to eat and drink, and the pubs on Katonah Avenue run from small bars to large rooms; the Bronx Times described one venue on the avenue with a capacity of more than 500. Whether a particular premises needs a building fire alarm system, and of what kind, depends on its occupant load, its layout and whether it shares a building with homes, and that determination belongs to a registered design professional. We do not make it for you, and we would be wary of anyone who did on a phone call. What being an assembly space adds, when a system is required, is a defined process. Plans are drawn and submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. A Project Authorization is issued before work starts. The installation follows the approved plans, and an acceptance inspection confirms the system works as designed. In a building with apartments above, the design also has to account for how the residents are warned and how the two uses relate. Our role is the installation, labeling, pre-testing and support at the inspection, plus routine service afterward. Before any of that, we can survey the premises and tell the owner whether the work they are describing looks like household alarms, a like-for-like repair of an existing system, or a filed system that needs a designer first. That conversation is free and often saves a great deal of time.

These are claims that turn up again and again in generic online answers and national guides, paraphrased, each followed by what Woodlawn Heights buildings and the published rules actually show.

"Bronx fire alarm work is mostly about big apartment buildings"

Woodlawn Heights is the exception. The Department of City Planning found about 80 percent of residential lots here developed with detached housing, and the 2016 downzoning moved a large area from R7A to R4A to keep it that way.

Most jobs here are houses and two-families, where the work is choosing, placing and linking household alarms well. The building-system questions arise mainly on Katonah Avenue and in the co-ops.

"An alarm upstairs will catch a fire in the basement"

Both big Katonah Avenue fires in the local record began in a basement under a business. Smoke from a cellar has to find its way up through the building before an upstairs alarm can sense it, and by then the stair may already be filling.

The fix is detection in the cellar itself, linked to the alarms where people sleep, so the warning comes from where the fire starts, not from where it arrives.

"Old houses are grandfathered, so the old alarms can stay"

Since April 1, 2019, New York has required smoke alarms sold in the state to be hardwired or sealed ten-year units, and Brick Underground reports a city law requiring old devices to be replaced with sealed ten-year detectors by 2021. The age of the house does not change what alarm belongs on its ceiling.

An alarm with a battery door in a Woodlawn Heights bedroom is overdue for replacement, whatever year the house went up.

"Every home needs hardwired, interconnected alarms"

Published summaries of the city rule say existing apartments can comply with approved sealed-battery detectors, while hardwired, interconnected alarms with battery backup are required for new construction and substantial alterations.

For an existing house, sealed alarms with wireless interconnect usually give the same linked warning without the plaster repair. Hardwiring makes most sense when walls are already open for a renovation.

"Sealed ten-year alarms mean the job is done for good"

They are designed to last about ten years and then signal end of life. Alarms installed around the 2019 rule and the 2021 city deadline will reach that point roughly between 2029 and 2031, and a house where they all went up together will see them all come due together.

Read the dates on the back of each alarm, plan replacement as a set, and use that moment to improve placement and linking.

"National averages tell you what the job will cost"

An average cannot see a deli cellar under three apartments, a converted two-family with circuits shared between units, or a gut renovation that needs hardwired cable before the walls close.

"Everything on McLean Avenue follows New York City rules"

McLean Avenue is shared with Yonkers' McLean Heights and is technically in the city of Yonkers, which has its own fire department and code officials. Yonkers firefighters helped at the 2021 deli fire, a reminder of how close the line is.

FAQ

Woodlawn Heights Fire Alarm FAQ

Our carbon monoxide alarm is in the cellar near the boiler. Will it sound upstairs in the bedrooms too?
+

Only if it is linked to the alarms upstairs. A stand-alone cellar alarm sounds in the cellar, which may not wake anyone two floors up. Many sealed and hardwired models can be interconnected with smoke and carbon monoxide alarms from the same manufacturer, so a CO problem at the boiler sounds by the beds. We check whether your existing alarms can link and, if not, recommend a set that can.

We have one hardwired alarm in the hall and sealed battery alarms in the bedrooms. Is mixing the two acceptable in an existing house?
+

In an existing house, both hardwired alarms and sealed ten-year alarms are acceptable kinds. The real issue is whether they are linked. A hardwired hall alarm and separate sealed bedroom alarms will each sound alone. Some manufacturers make hardwired alarms with a wireless module that links to their sealed models, which lets a mixed house sound together without new cable.

The alarm is going off but we cannot see or smell any smoke. Should we still get out and call 911?
+

Yes. Treat every sounding smoke alarm as a fire until someone has confirmed otherwise. In an old house or a building over a store, a fire can start in a cellar, a wall or a closet well before smoke is visible in the room you are in. Get everyone out, close doors behind you, and call 911 from outside. Finding out why it sounded comes afterward.

How far from the stove should the smoke alarm near the kitchen be in a small apartment?
+

Manufacturers commonly advise keeping smoke alarms about ten feet from cooking appliances to cut false alarms, and a photoelectric model is less prone to sounding at normal cooking. In a small apartment over a Katonah Avenue store that distance is not always possible, so we place the alarm as far from the stove as the layout allows while still covering the route to the bedrooms.

Will the boiler or the heat have to be shut down while you work in the cellar?
+

Almost never. Mounting detectors and alarms near a boiler or oil burner does not require turning it off, and we keep a safe distance from flues and burners while we work. If a hardwired circuit has to be connected, only that circuit is switched off for a short time, and we tell you in advance which one and for how long.

Our cellar is unfinished storage and nobody sleeps down there. Does it still need a smoke alarm?
+

Yes. A smoke alarm on every level, including the cellar, is the standard advice, and in Woodlawn Heights the cellar is where the boiler, the electrical service and decades of stored belongings usually sit. The usual spot is at the bottom of the cellar stairs, linked to the alarms upstairs so a fire below wakes the people sleeping above it.

If every bedroom has its own smoke alarm, is one still needed in the hallway outside them?
+

Yes. The hallway alarm covers the route out of the bedrooms and catches smoke coming up the stair before it reaches any bedroom door. The bedroom alarms protect each sleeper in place, and linking them all means a fire anywhere sounds everywhere.
