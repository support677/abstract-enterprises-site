The Neighborhood

Why Staten Island Properties Need CCTV Installation

Staten Island does not look like the rest of New York City, and the camera plans we draw here don't either. Drive down Amboy Road through Eltingville or Annadale, or along the side streets off Victory Boulevard in Westerleigh, and you see what the borough is built from: houses that stand on their own lots, a curb cut and a driveway, a side gate, a deep backyard, often a detached garage or a shed at the back, and two or three cars in the family. A CCTV system on that kind of property is not about a lobby or a hallway. It is about the ground around the house — the length of the driveway, the dark side yard between two houses, the back fence that meets a wooded strip, the garage door nobody can see from the kitchen.

The Census Bureau's numbers show how different the building stock is. In the American Community Survey for 2019–2023, Richmond County had about 184,074 housing units. Of those, 34.5 percent were one-family detached houses and another 25.6 percent were one-family attached or semi-attached houses — the rows of townhouses that went up across Mid-Island and the South Shore in the 1970s and 1980s. Two-unit houses added 22.7 percent. Put together, more than eight in ten Staten Island homes are one- or two-family buildings, and 67.9 percent of occupied homes are owner-occupied. Buildings of 20 or more units make up only 9.9 percent of the borough's housing.

That shapes every design decision. On a Staten Island house, the cable runs are longer than in a Brooklyn row house, because the cameras sit at the corners of a wider building and sometimes at a garage 60 or 80 feet behind it. The camera you need at the end of a driveway is a different lens from the one under the porch roof. The recorder usually ends up in a basement or a utility room — and on the East and South Shores, that is exactly where the water came in during Hurricane Sandy.

The storm question nobody else asks

The city's own post-Sandy report, written by the Special Initiative for Rebuilding and Resiliency, records that storm surge reached 14 feet above ground at Tottenville. Of the 43 New Yorkers who died in the storm, 23 were on Staten Island, ten of them in Midland Beach alone. The report names the East and South Shores of Staten Island among the five coastal areas hit hardest, and it notes that 120,000 customers on Staten Island and in the Rockaways lost power because of substation damage. Since then, many East Shore houses have been raised, others were bought out, and the city's flood-resistant construction rules in Appendix G of the NYC Building Code now require utilities and their equipment in flood zones to sit at or above the design flood elevation.

A CCTV recorder is not a boiler, but the same logic protects it. A recorder and a PoE switch on a basement floor in Midland Beach, New Dorp Beach, Ocean Breeze or Great Kills lose every hour of footage the first time the basement takes water — and they lose it at the moment a homeowner most needs to show an insurer what happened. Placing the recorder high, on an upper floor or in a raised utility space, and putting it on a battery backup, is the single most Staten Island-specific thing we do. The power side matters just as much away from the water: when Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, Con Edison reported about 29,000 Staten Island customers out of service at one point, mostly from trees and branches falling on overhead lines.

What the police numbers say about Staten Island

Staten Island is the city's lowest-crime borough in most categories, and its burglary count has fallen sharply. But one property crime is moving the other way, and it is the one a driveway camera is built to record. The NYPD's CompStat report for Patrol Borough Staten Island for the week of November 2–8, 2026 shows these year-to-date figures:

  • Vehicle theft (grand larceny auto): 256 reports so far in 2026, up 24.9 percent from 205 at the same point in 2025.
  • Burglary: 222 reports, down 21.6 percent from 283 — and down from 3,641 burglaries in the whole of 1990.
  • Retail theft: 1,378 reports, down 30.2 percent from 1,973.
  • Petit larceny: 2,764 reports, down 19.8 percent from 3,447 — the category that covers most package thefts from porches and stoops.
  • Grand larceny: 1,271 reports, roughly flat against 1,285.
  • Housing stock: 184,074 units; 34.5% one-family detached, 25.6% one-family attached, 22.7% two-unit (ACS 2019–2023).

The vehicle-theft rise was big enough to draw a public response in the summer. In July, the Staten Island Advance reported 163 vehicle thefts through July 19 against 111 a year earlier, a 47 percent jump, and District Attorney Michael E. McMahon said: “We are closely monitoring the recent increase in auto thefts manifested in [NYPD’s] CompStat numbers which reflect a troubling trend that demands immediate attention.” In the same story, the NYPD was candid that theft crews wear masks and gloves and work fast, so video often confirms a theft without identifying the people. That is the honest case for good CCTV on a Staten Island driveway: framed and lit so it shows the vehicle that pulled up, the direction it came from and the time down to the second, footage that investigators can line up with plate readers and other evidence.

The figures are preliminary and change week to week; they come straight from the NYPD's published borough report. Read them as context, not as a sales pitch. A house on a quiet cul-de-sac in Annadale can go years without trouble and still be glad of the recording the one night a car is broken into or a package disappears.

Geography adds one more wrinkle. Staten Island is policed by four precincts — the 120th across the North Shore, the 121st on the West Shore and Mid-Island, the 122nd along the East Shore and the middle of the island, and the 123rd on the South Shore — and it is connected to the rest of the region by just four bridges and the ferry. A stolen car or a van full of tools leaves the island over the Verrazzano, the Goethals, the Bayonne Bridge or the Outerbridge Crossing within minutes. That is one reason we put weight on capturing direction of travel and an accurate time stamp: a detective who knows a vehicle turned toward Richmond Avenue at 2:14 a.m. can narrow the search to a short list of routes off the island.

Most Staten Island homeowners pair cameras with lighting and a doorbell view. A motion light over the driveway makes a camera's night picture far better than infrared alone, and a video doorbell at the front door covers the face-to-face moment. Building Specialists

CCTV Installation for Staten Island's Architecture

Staten Island's houses were built in waves, and each wave leaves the crew a different set of walls, crawlspaces and cable paths. The ACS figures show it: 15.5 percent of the borough's homes date from the 1980s, 16.1 percent from the 1970s and 10.5 percent from the 1990s — the building boom that followed the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge — while 17.8 percent were built before 1940, mostly on the North Shore. Here is how we approach each type.

A

Detached house on its own lot

Split-levels, high ranches, colonials and newer custom homes from Todt Hill to Tottenville. Four corners of the house, the driveway, the side yards and the back. Attic and basement give us clean cable paths; the challenge is distance to a detached garage or a back-fence camera.

B

Semi-attached townhouse rows

The 1970s and 1980s rows of Great Kills, Eltingville, Bulls Head and Heartland Village: a garage under the living space, a short shared driveway, a small yard behind. One camera often covers two garage doors, so we agree the view with the owner and keep it off the neighbor's windows.

C

Two-family house with a basement or garden unit

Common across Mid-Island and the North Shore. Separate entrances, a shared driveway and a tenant who may also want to see the front door. One recorder, an owner account with full access and a tenant account limited to the shared entrance.

D

Raised house in a former flood zone

East Shore homes lifted on piers or a new foundation after Sandy, with an open or enclosed space below the first floor. That space floods by design, so cameras mount on the raised structure and the recorder goes above the design flood elevation, never in the enclosure.

E

Townhouse complex or condo HOA

Shared parking courts, a pool or clubhouse gate, mailbox kiosks and a perimeter fence. The board owns the system; we put the recorder in a locked common room and give each manager or board member a login with the views they are entitled to see.

F

North Shore apartment building

Pre-war walk-ups near St. George, Stapleton and Tompkinsville, and the mid-rise and garden complexes beyond. Lobby, mailroom, elevator landing, laundry, trash room and rear door. See our apartment building security camera installation on Staten Island for building-wide systems.

Siding, stucco, brick and the salt air

Most Staten Island houses wear vinyl or aluminum siding over a wood frame, which is easy to drill and hard to seal. A camera screwed straight through vinyl lets water track behind the siding and pulls the mount loose as the panel expands in summer and contracts in winter. We use a siding mounting block cut to the profile of the lap, fix it into the sheathing or a stud, and run the cable through the block so the penetration sits behind a sealed plate. On stucco we drill with a masonry bit, set anchors in the substrate rather than the finish coat, and seal around the junction box. Brick fronts, common on newer two-family houses and older North Shore homes, get sleeve anchors in the brick face, never in the mortar alone.

Close to the water, in South Beach, Midland Beach, Great Kills Harbor and along the Kill Van Kull, salt air is hard on metal. We choose camera housings rated IP66 or IP67, use stainless or coated hardware, fit drip loops on every outdoor cable and seal each connector with weatherproof boots or gel-filled junction boxes. A cheaper camera with an exposed RJ45 plug on the back can corrode in a single winter at the shore.

Where the cable goes in a Staten Island house

CCTV installation with concealed wiring is the default on finished houses. In a split-level or high ranch, cable usually travels through the garage ceiling and into the basement or lower level; in a colonial, up through an interior wall into the attic and down to each eave camera. Semi-attached rows often have no attic access at all, so we route along the garage ceiling, through a soffit and behind trim. Where a run has to cross a driveway or a backyard to reach a garage or a fence camera, we use outdoor-rated direct-burial cable in conduit, or an aerial run along an existing structure when trenching isn't practical. Every hole through the building envelope is sealed, every cable is labeled at both ends, and we write the route on the camera map you keep.

Garages, sheds and the back of a deep lot

Garage CCTV installation is one of the most common requests we get on the island, because the detached garage is where the tools, the bikes, the second fridge and sometimes the boat or the classic car live. A camera on the house aimed at the garage door shows who walks up; a second camera inside the garage, wired from the house, shows what they touch. Where the garage is 60 to 100 feet from the house, a standard Cat6 run still works well within the 100-meter (328-foot) Ethernet limit. Beyond that, or across a lot with no practical cable route, we use a small PoE switch in the garage fed by one cable, or a point-to-point wireless bridge between two buildings — a fixed link, not a battery camera hoping to reach the house Wi-Fi.

A quick note on what we don't do in this section of the work: we don't open walls or patch drywall beyond the small holes the cable needs, and we don't do electrical work that needs a licensed electrician, such as a new outlet on a garage wall. If a camera location needs new line-voltage power, we tell you at the survey and plan the camera to run on PoE from the recorder instead, which avoids the outlet entirely.

New construction and rebuilt houses

Staten Island still sees a steady stream of new houses, teardowns and gut renovations, from infill lots in Rossville and Charleston to rebuilt homes on the East Shore. CCTV installation for new construction is the cheapest camera work there is, because the walls are open: we pull Cat6 to each future camera spot while the framing is bare, leave the cable coiled and labeled behind a blank plate or a sealed box at the eave, and run every line back to a planned recorder spot on an upper floor. When the siding and drywall are finished, the cameras go up in hours instead of a day, and nothing is surface-mounted. If the house is being built in a flood zone, we coordinate the recorder and UPS location with the builder so it sits with the other equipment above the design flood elevation. We work alongside the electrician and the general contractor rather than in place of them.

Commercial CCTV

Business CCTV Installation in Staten Island

Staten Island's businesses mostly sit in strip plazas with their own parking lots, along a handful of long commercial roads, rather than in storefront rows on a sidewalk. That puts the parking lot and the back of the building inside the camera plan, not just the counter. It also means the landlord, not the tenant, often controls the roof, the sign band and the lot poles. Our commercial security camera installation page for Staten Island covers multi-site and larger systems; here is how CCTV for business security plays out on the island's main corridors.

Hylan Boulevard, from Dongan Hills to Tottenville

Hylan Boulevard runs the length of the East and South Shores past plaza after plaza: supermarkets, pharmacies, pizzerias, nail salons, dental offices, bagel shops and bank branches. Retail CCTV installation here usually means the front door and register, the aisles, the stockroom and the rear delivery door, plus one or two cameras on the storefront looking across the tenant's share of the lot. Plaza owners sometimes add a separate lot system with cameras on the light poles. When both exist, we keep the tenant's recorder separate and the views coordinated so the plaza and the store don't end up watching the same spot twice.

Richmond Avenue, the Staten Island Mall area and New Springville

Richmond Avenue between Victory Boulevard and the Staten Island Mall carries big-box stores, car-wash bays, fast-food drive-throughs and medical offices. Restaurant CCTV installation along this stretch adds the drive-through window, the kitchen door and the cash office. Medical office CCTV installation focuses on the entrance, the waiting room and the records area, with no cameras in exam rooms. Further south, around the Bricktown Centre in Charleston, the same layout repeats at a bigger scale.

Forest Avenue, Victory Boulevard and Port Richmond Avenue

The North Shore's older retail roads have more traditional storefronts, often with an apartment above. Convenience store CCTV installation here looks a lot like a Brooklyn bodega job: domes over every aisle, a camera on the register from behind the customer, one on the door and one under the awning facing the sidewalk. Bars and late-night spots along Forest Avenue add the entrance line and low-light cameras over the bar.

New Dorp Lane, Bay Street and St. George

New Dorp Lane's restaurants and shops, and the Bay Street corridor from St. George through Stapleton, mix storefront retail with offices, bars and the waterfront crowds that come off the ferry. Empire Outlets in St. George and the busy ferry-terminal area mean more foot traffic than most of the island sees. Office CCTV installation in these buildings concentrates on reception, the suite entrance and the server or file room.

The West Shore: Bloomfield, Travis, Charleston and the logistics parks

The West Shore is Staten Island's industrial side, along the Arthur Kill and the West Shore Expressway. Matrix Global Logistics Park sits on a site of roughly 400 acres near the foot of the Goethals Bridge in Bloomfield, and in 2020 the Staten Island Advance reported Amazon leasing a 975,000-square-foot building there. Around those big sites sit smaller warehouses, contractor yards, bus depots, self-storage buildings, auto body shops and the Staten Island Teleport office park. Warehouse CCTV installation and logistics facility CCTV installation here are about distance and darkness: long fence lines, wide truck yards, dock doors and gates, often with nothing lit past the building wall. We plan long-range bullet cameras along the fence, cameras at every dock door aimed so the trailer number and the forklift are both in frame, and a gate camera framed for plates. Our warehouse security camera installation page for Staten Island goes deeper into dock and yard layouts.

Auto shops, body shops and car lots

Repair shops, tire shops, body shops and used-car lots are spread along Hylan Boulevard, Richmond Avenue, Forest Avenue and the roads of the West Shore. With vehicle theft rising on the island, owners ask for two things: proof of what was on the lot at closing, and a record of every vehicle that enters and leaves. Auto repair shop CCTV installation usually means a camera on each bay door, one on the customer counter, wide views of the lot from the building, and a gate or driveway camera framed tight enough to read a plate at the lot entrance, where cars slow down. Car dealership CCTV installation adds the showroom, the key box and the service drive. The recorder goes in the office with a battery backup so an overnight outage doesn't blind the lot.

Schools, daycares and houses of worship

Daycares, private schools, parish halls, churches, temples and mosques across the island ask for cameras at the entrances, the drop-off area, hallways and the parking lot, with nothing in restrooms or changing areas. School CCTV installation and daycare CCTV installation lean on clear face views at the door and a live view at the front desk, so staff can see who is at the entrance before buzzing them in. Footage access stays with the director or the board.

Rules a Staten Island business should know

  • No video in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording in those spaces. Cameras in a break room or office are allowed; the restroom corridor door is as close as we go.
  • No hidden recording of private spaces. NYS Penal Law §250.45 makes recording a person in a place where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, without consent, a crime.
  • Audio is a separate question. Many cameras have microphones. Recording conversations you aren't part of can fall under the state's eavesdropping law, so we install with audio off unless the owner decides otherwise after taking advice.
  • Biometric signage. If a store, restaurant or venue uses face recognition, NYC Administrative Code §22-1202 requires a clear sign at customer entrances. Most of our commercial systems don't use it.
  • Landlord approval. Plaza leases usually require the landlord's written OK for anything on the facade, sign band, roof or lot poles. We supply cut sheets and a camera map for that request.

These notes summarize the statutes in plain words; they are not legal advice, and a lawyer should review any particular case.

Your Questions Answered

CCTV Installation in Staten Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Staten Island callers actually ask us, grouped the way the survey runs: the ground around the house first, then cars, then storms, then businesses. Each answer comes from how we plan and install, not from a brochure.

Driveways, side yards and hills

Is a camera on a post at the end of the driveway better than one mounted on the house?

Usually the house is better, with one exception. A camera under the eave or on the garage header has power and cable right behind it, is out of reach, and looks down the driveway toward the street, so it sees a person's face as they walk toward the house and the side of any car that pulls in. A post at the curb is closer to the street, which helps on a very long driveway, but it is within reach of anyone passing, needs a buried cable back to the house and catches more headlight glare at night.

The exception is a driveway longer than about 80 feet, or one that bends so the house can't see the entrance. There we set a sturdy post or use an existing lamp post, put the camera at nine to ten feet, run the cable underground in conduit, and aim it across the driveway entrance rather than straight at oncoming lights. On most Staten Island lots, though, two house cameras — one wide over the parked cars, one narrower down the driveway — do the job without anything at the curb.

What camera covers the narrow side yard between two Staten Island houses?

The side yard between two detached houses, often four to eight feet wide with a gate at the front and the trash cans halfway down, is the path most break-ins on a detached house use, because neither neighbor can see into it. A wide-angle camera does a poor job here; it wastes most of its picture on two walls.

We mount a bullet or turret camera high on the back corner of the house aimed forward toward the gate, with a lens of about 2.8 to 4 millimeters, so the frame runs the length of the passage and anyone walking through it fills the picture. If the gate is the weak point, a second camera above it looks back the other way. Infrared works well in a side yard because the walls bounce light back, but we check the night image on the spot, since a white vinyl wall a foot from the lens can wash the picture out.

What changes when the driveway is steep, as on Todt Hill, Grymes Hill or Emerson Hill?

On the hills of the island's spine, driveways climb from the street to a house set well above it, sometimes with a retaining wall and a turn. A camera on the house looks down on cars from a steep angle, so it sees roofs instead of doors and windshields. A camera at the bottom looks up at headlights.

The fix is a mid-slope view: a camera on the garage or a wall partway down, angled across the driveway rather than along it, plus a camera at the front door for faces. Long cable runs downhill need outdoor-rated cable in conduit and drainage-aware routing, so a run doesn't sit in the path of rainwater coming down the slope. The recorder still goes inside the house, never in a detached garage at the bottom of the hill where it would be the first thing taken.

What camera works on a block with no streetlight in front of the house?

Plenty of South Shore and Mid-Island blocks are dark at the curb, especially between widely spaced streetlights or where trees cover them. Standard infrared cameras still see in that dark, but only in black and white and only as far as their own infrared reaches, usually 60 to 120 feet depending on the model. That is enough for a driveway and a front walk.

For color at night, we use cameras with larger sensors and a warm white light that comes on with motion, or we add a separate motion floodlight that lights the driveway for the camera. A light that switches on with motion does two jobs: it makes the picture usable, and it tells a person in the driveway they have been seen. We aim lights at your property, not your neighbor's bedroom window.

Can a camera keep an eye on a boat, trailer or RV stored in the driveway over winter?

Yes, and on Staten Island this is a common request in the off-season, when boats from Great Kills Harbor and the island's marinas come home on trailers. A boat on a trailer is tall and blocks the view behind it, so one camera rarely covers both sides. We usually place one camera on the house covering the hitch end and the trailer tongue, where a thief would connect a vehicle, and a second covering the far side from the garage or a post.

We set an alert zone around the trailer so that a person walking up to it at night sends a notification to your phone, while cars passing in the street don't. A wheel lock and a hitch lock still matter more than any camera; the camera tells you when someone is trying them.

Vehicle theft

Which footage actually helps police after a car is taken from a Staten Island driveway?

The NYPD was candid about this in the Staten Island Advance this summer: organized theft crews wear masks, gloves and hoods, and work in minutes, so video often confirms a theft without showing who did it. That doesn't make cameras useless; it changes what they need to capture. The useful clips are the ones that show the vehicle that dropped the thieves off, its color, shape and any visible damage, the direction it came from and left in, and the exact time.

So the camera plan for a driveway includes a view of the street in front of the house, framed wide enough to see a car passing in both directions, and the recorder's clock is synchronized automatically so its time stamp matches other cameras on the block and the plate readers investigators use. Storms, floods and outages

Can cameras be mounted on a raised East Shore house without going into the flood-vent enclosure below it?

Yes, and they should be. Many houses in Midland Beach, New Dorp Beach, Ocean Breeze and Oakwood were lifted after Sandy onto piers, columns or a raised foundation, leaving an enclosure underneath with flood vents that is designed to let water flow through. Anything installed in that space has to be able to get wet. Under the city's flood-resistant construction rules, utilities and their equipment in these buildings belong at or above the design flood elevation.

We mount the cameras on the raised structure itself, under the first-floor overhang or on the fascia, looking down at the stairs, the parking area beneath and the yard. Cable drops into the living space from above, not up through the enclosure. Where a camera has to cover the space under the house, it hangs from the underside of the floor framing with a sealed connection and drip loop, and its cable runs up into the house rather than along the enclosure wall.

What should we do with the CCTV system when a coastal storm or hurricane warning is issued?

Three things, all of them quick. First, export any footage you might need later, such as clips from a recent incident, to your phone or a cloud folder, because a recorder that loses power for days or takes water can lose recent footage. Second, check that the battery backup's indicator shows it charged and that the recorder and switch are plugged into its battery outlets, not its surge-only outlets. Third, if the recorder sits anywhere water could reach, move it up now; it is a ten-minute job for a homeowner with the cables labeled.

Leave the cameras running. During and after the storm they record what the wind and water did to the property, and that footage is often the most useful thing you have when filing a claim. Don't unplug outdoor cameras to protect them; they are sealed units built for rain.

How do we check the cameras and recorder after a storm, before the insurance adjuster arrives?

Open the app and look at every camera's live view and its recording for the storm hours. A camera that shows a black tile, a frozen image or a fogged lens is the one to inspect. On the recorder, the event log shows power loss and restore times, and the recording timeline shows whether anything was missed. Screenshots of both, with the date and time visible, are worth keeping for the claim.

If a camera is down, a call or text to us books a service visit, and the technician checks the cable, the connection and the camera before anything gets replaced. Storm damage to equipment is generally an insurance matter, not a product defect, so we document what we find in writing for your adjuster.

How much storage a Staten Island system needs

Storage depends on three things: how many cameras, how sharp the picture is, and how much movement each camera sees. What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV on Staten Island

Before they call, many Staten Island homeowners have already read an AI search summary, an Angi or HomeAdvisor cost page, or a Fixr guide. Those sources are written for an average American house, and Staten Island is close enough to that average that their advice sounds right. Here are seven claims we hear on surveys, and where they part ways with what we see on the island.

"Put the recorder in the basement, out of sight"

Generic installation guides recommend the basement because it is hidden, cool and close to the cable runs. On a large share of Staten Island's East and South Shore houses, the basement is the one place the recorder should never be. The city's Sandy rebuilding report lists those shores among the five areas hit hardest by the 2012 surge, and the water reached 14 feet above ground at Tottenville.

A recorder on a basement floor is also the first thing to fail in a heavy rainstorm that backs up a drain, which happens far from the beach too. The basement is fine for cable routes; the recorder itself belongs on an upper floor, in a closet, or on a raised shelf in a utility room, with a battery backup.

We still hide it. A locked closet on the first or second floor, a cabinet in a home office or a small wall enclosure keeps it away from a burglar's eyes without putting it where a storm can drown it.

"Skip the cable; Wi-Fi cameras do the same job"

This advice assumes a compact house with a strong router in the middle. A Staten Island detached house often has its router in a front room and cameras at the far end of a driveway, on a detached garage and on a back fence, with aluminum siding, a brick chimney and a garage door in the way. Wi-Fi cameras at those spots drop out, especially in wind and rain.

Battery cameras add a second problem: they need recharging, and cold weeks shorten battery life. A house left empty for the winter, or for weeks after a storm, is exactly when a dead battery camera stops recording.

Wired PoE cameras get power and network from one cable and keep recording through a power cut as long as the recorder's battery backup lasts. Where a cable truly can't reach, we use a fixed wireless bridge between buildings, not a camera's built-in Wi-Fi.

"Staten Island is safe, so cameras aren't worth it"

Part of that is true. Staten Island's burglary count is down 21.6 percent year to date in the NYPD's November report, and in 2025 the borough logged 328 burglaries against 3,641 in 1990. Retail theft is down 30.2 percent.

But vehicle theft is up 24.9 percent year to date, to 256 reports, and the summer spike was enough for the District Attorney to call it a troubling trend. Petit larceny, which covers most package thefts, still ran at 2,764 reports through early November. A homeowner in Elm Park told the Advance last year that a package was taken in daylight in full view of a big camera.

Cameras don't stop every theft. What they do on Staten Island is record the vehicle, the time and the direction of travel, show you what happened to the property during a storm, and give an insurer or a detective something to work with.

"Cameras will identify the thieves"

AI summaries often promise that good cameras catch criminals. The NYPD, speaking about Staten Island car theft crews in July, said the opposite about masked, gloved crews: video may confirm the commission of the crime but rarely identifies the individuals responsible.

That shifts the design goal. A face camera at the front door is still worth having, because most porch thieves and opportunists don't wear masks. For cars, the higher-value footage is the getaway vehicle and its direction, captured by a wider view of the street, with an accurate clock.

We set the recorder to synchronize its time automatically and show you how to export clips in the original format with the time stamp intact. That is what makes your footage fit alongside plate readers and other cameras on the block.

"Angi and HomeAdvisor averages tell you what it costs"

National averages from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr blend thousands of installs across the country, many of them a few cameras plugged into outlets. They don't account for a 90-foot cable run to a detached garage, a buried conduit across a driveway, or a recorder relocation to get it out of a flood zone.

Our Staten Island prices are published on the page: complete four-camera systems from $999 to $1,441 installed, typically $999 to $2,000 for a four-camera job on a single-family home, and $1,500 to $5,000 and up for commercial CCTV. Two weeks of recording and the phone app are included, with no monthly fee.

What moves the price is distance and surfaces. That is why extra cameras, buried runs and concealed wiring are priced per camera after we confirm the layout, not guessed from a national number.

"A 4K camera fixes poor night images"

More pixels don't add light. On a dark South Shore block between streetlights, a 4K camera with weak infrared records a sharp image of the first 30 feet and black beyond it. The camera's infrared reach, the sensor size and any added lighting matter more than resolution after dark.

We check night images on site, after sunset where the schedule allows, and adjust: a camera with longer infrared reach on the driveway, a color-night model where there is a porch light to work with, or a motion floodlight added to the camera plan.

4K is still worth it on wide views such as a lot or a long yard, where zooming into a recording afterward matters. On a front door, 4MP is usually enough.

"A UPS isn't needed; outages are rare in the city"

Generic advice treats a city address as a reliable power address. Staten Island's tree-lined streets tell a different story: Con Edison has announced single Staten Island outages from downed wires affecting 14,000 customers across Dongan Hills, New Dorp, Grant City and nearby neighborhoods. When Tropical Storm Isaias hit in August 2020, Con Edison reported about 29,000 Staten Island customers out of service at one point, and Sandy's substation damage cut power to 120,000 customers on Staten Island and in the Rockaways.

A recorder without a battery backup goes dark the second the power drops, and an outage is when burglars know alarms and lights may be off. A properly sized UPS keeps the recorder, the switch and the cameras running through short outages and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in a long one, which protects the hard drive.

We size the battery to the system's actual power draw and tell you roughly how long it should last, rather than adding a small unit that powers the recorder but not the cameras.

Is the $999 starting price realistic for a typical Staten Island detached house?
+

Will a new CCTV recorder work alongside the Ring or Nest doorbell I already use?
+

Yes, side by side. A Ring or Nest doorbell keeps working in its own app, and the CCTV recorder covers the driveway, yards and garage in ours. Most of those doorbells don't record into a third-party recorder, so the two systems stay separate, but we set the CCTV camera nearest the front door so it overlaps the doorbell's view and gives you a second, wider angle of the same moment.

How do you keep camera lenses clear of salt spray and spider webs near the water?
+

Placement does most of the work: cameras under an eave or a soffit stay out of the direct spray, and turret cameras avoid the night glare a salty or dusty dome cover causes, because their infrared lights sit beside the lens instead of behind a shared cover. Near South Beach, Midland Beach or Great Kills Harbor, a gentle rinse and wipe of each lens a few times a year keeps night images sharp. Our maintenance visits include that cleaning along with drive and firmware checks.

Will the cameras slow down our home internet when we watch them from work?
+

Not in normal use. The cameras record to the recorder inside your house over their own cables, which never touch your Wi-Fi. Internet bandwidth is used only while someone is watching live or playing back a clip on a phone, and the app sends a lighter sub-stream for small screens. Watching one or two cameras at a time from work uses about as much upload as a video call.

Will the crew need to get into the attic, the garage ceiling and the basement on install day?
