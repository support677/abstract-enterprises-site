New York School Camera Law, Read for Dutchess

Facial Recognition: Barred From Every Campus

The State Education Department’s September 2023 determination reaches every school in New York without distinction — district, charter, Catholic, boarding, independent, and residential program alike: facial-recognition technology may not be purchased or operated. A Register-listed boarding hall in Millbrook and a district building in Wappingers sit under the identical sentence. Ordinary video was never in scope — cameras, recording, and motion analytics remain fully lawful — and every system we deliver ships with face-matching disabled in firmware, the state logged in the commissioning file that retires the question for good.

Lawful Ground and the Felony Line

Point a recorder at a bathroom, a changing area, a dormitory living quarter, or any space where privacy is reasonably expected and Penal Law 250.45 calls it a felony — schools included, no carve-out written. Everything else is lawful coverage: entrances, corridors, stairs, dining halls, gyms, quads, drives, lots, fields, barns, mechanical rooms, perimeter. Classroom and seminar-room interiors route to your head, board, or counsel rather than into our defaults; the standing county drawing watches each classroom doorway from the corridor, entries and exits logged, no lens inside the room, with deliberate exceptions initialed on the plan. On boarding and residential campuses, coverage stops at living thresholds — drawn precisely, photographed at survey, documented for trustees, licensors, and the consultants who audit before recommending a placement.

When Footage Becomes a Student Record

Once identifiable video shapes a disciplinary outcome, FERPA can absorb it into that student’s protected record — parental viewing rights and disclosure limits attaching automatically — and for boarding schools, doing so across international families and their consultants. New York’s Ed Law 2-d separately binds any outside vendor holding student-identifiable data to enforceable contract terms. The architecture that keeps all of it manageable is our default: recorder on premises, drives on the school’s inventory, a written review-and-release procedure delivered at handover, and off-site duplication only under a 2-d-conforming agreement — so the eventual difficult conversation, in any language, opens a policy rather than a scramble.

Field Notes: Thirty Cameras, a Riverfront Campus, and the Downspout Crew That Picked the Wrong Chapel

Thirty cameras across a riverfront campus in the county’s midsection — a day school occupying buildings raised a century apart: a stone main hall from the 1910s with a slate roof and copper gutters, a chapel of the same vintage, and a 1990s classroom wing that behaved like an ordinary building. The head of school’s brief carried two non-negotiables. First: a village up the road had lost copper downspouts off two historic properties that spring, her insurance carrier had asked pointed questions at renewal, and the chapel’s untouched copper was, in her words, a standing invitation. Second: the buildings-and-grounds committee answered to a preservation consultant with documented veto power over visible fasteners, and the previous vendor’s proposal — eleven anchors into the stone facade — had died in that committee inside ten minutes. Cover everything; wound nothing.

We built the job around the second rule and let it carry the first. The main hall took no facade penetrations at all: routes ran through the attic and interior chases the 1910s builders had left generous, positions landed on soffits and eaves with reversible fasteners into non-character wood, and two window-adjacent interior mounts covered approaches through original glass. The chapel’s vulnerable elevations — where the copper lives — got their coverage from a single discreet pole set back in the treeline and one soffit head, both positions photographed, drawn, and approved by the consultant before a ladder opened. The 1990s wing wired conventionally. The drive got gatehouse framing at the road with plate capture. Exteriors went in winter-grade throughout, classification was tuned across one long dusk while the resident deer herd made its rounds, and the office staff drilled the two-minute export until the business manager could run it mid-phone-call. The consultant’s sign-off letter went into the insurance file beside the coverage map — the carrier’s renewal questions answered by attachment.

Twenty-six nights after handoff, 1:52 a.m., the drive camera read a plate on a pickup that rolled in with its lights off. The treeline pole watched two figures walk straight to the chapel’s north elevation — the copper side — and set a ladder against the downspout with the economy of men who’d done it elsewhere. The after-hours person alert hit the duty phone at 1:53; live view was open by 1:55; the Dutchess County Sheriff’s Office had the call, the plate, and a description while the first bracket was still being worked loose. The crew left with six feet of downspout and no idea they’d been in frame from three angles since the road. The export package — plate, faces, the ladder work in clean detail — connected them, deputies later confirmed, to a run of historic-property metal thefts across two counties, and most of the school’s six feet of copper came back in an evidence bag. The chapel has not been touched since; neither, per the standing rule, has its stone. Both facts were the assignment.
