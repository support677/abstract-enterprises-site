The Neighborhood

Why Tottenville Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Tottenville is the last stop on everything: the Staten Island Railway, Hylan Boulevard, Amboy Road and New York State itself. The neighborhood occupies the south-western tip of the island, with the Arthur Kill on its western and northern side, Raritan Bay and the open water of the New York Bight to the south, and Perth Amboy, New Jersey, close enough across the Kill to read the waterfront. Wikipedia's entry calls it the southernmost neighborhood in both New York City and New York State, and the westernmost in the city. The Staten Island Railway reached it on June 2, 1860, and the run from Tottenville station to St. George is still fourteen miles and about forty-two minutes. That distance from everything else is why the neighborhood looks and feels the way it does, and it is why the houses here split so cleanly into two groups.

The first group is the old village. Captain Christopher Billop called his land Bentley Manor after his ship; the name Tottenville arrived in 1869. Through the nineteenth century the place lived on oysters, shipbuilding and, later, the Atlantic Terra Cotta works, and the people who made money from those trades built houses that still stand along Amboy Road, Bentley Street, Hopping Avenue and Satterlee Street. Forgotten New York's walks through the neighborhood list a Greek Revival house at 76 Bentley from around 1850, a house at 88 Bentley built around 1890 for a shipbuilder, a Gothic Revival cottage at 92 Hopping from 1886, a Colonial Revival house at 65 Hopping from 1903, and an Italianate house on Satterlee built for the shipbuilder James Rutan. The Rutan-Journeay House at 7647 Amboy Road dates to 1848, and the Second Empire house at 7484 Amboy Road, built as the Bethel church parsonage, was landmarked in 2005 after a fight over its demolition. Behind the clapboard of houses like these sit plaster walls on wood lath, framing cut to sizes nobody has used since, and wall cavities that may hold anything from horsehair to old wiring.

The second group is much younger. Wikipedia notes that in the 1990s the area south-east of Hylan Boulevard, previously almost empty, saw intense levels of new home construction. The census numbers make the split vivid. Of Tottenville's 5,412 housing units, 20.4 percent were built in 1939 or earlier, then almost nothing for forty years — only about one unit in eight from 1940 through 1979 — and then 17.2 percent in the 1980s, 22.9 percent in the 1990s and 15.2 percent in the 2000s. The median year built is 1989. A 2006 profile in The Cooperator quoted a local broker describing old fourteen-foot-wide bungalows being replaced by “Florida Style” center-hall colonials, with stucco and brick the favourite exteriors, and a Tottenville real-estate guide describes the newer stock as side-hall and center-hall colonials that can be one- or two-family homes. Those houses have drywall on studs, builder-run coaxial cable, gas fireplaces in framed bump-outs and family rooms open to the kitchen — a completely different set of problems from the village.

Then there is the shore. Tottenville Beach was largely undeveloped until the 1990s and is now an all-residential strip bordered by Conference House Park. When Hurricane Sandy came ashore in October 2012, the Staten Island Advance reported from Yetman Avenue, where a gigantic wave tore through the end of the street near Billop Avenue and left cement foundations filled with muddy water where houses had stood. “This whole block has to be leveled,” one resident of twenty years told the paper. The Coral Bay Cafe at the beach was destroyed. Twelve years later, in September 2024, the state announced the completion of Living Breakwaters: eight partly submerged breakwaters, 2,400 linear feet in all, built for $111 million and designed to cut waves reaching the shore's buildings and roads to under three feet, with sand added to the beach from Manhattan Street to Loretto Street. The houses behind it are a mixture of survivors, repaired homes and new builds raised above the flood line, and each of them asks a soundbar installer the same question: what goes on the floor, and what never should.

Almost everyone here owns the wall they want to drill. The census estimate puts owner occupancy at 82.9 percent of occupied homes, and nearly a quarter of all housing units are in two-unit buildings, so the most common “permission” question in Tottenville is not a landlord's but a family's: who lives on which floor, whose cable account feeds which TV, and whether a subwoofer upstairs is fair to the people downstairs. Condo communities such as Surfside Village and the Captain's Quarters, which the 2006 Cooperator piece named as the neighborhood's two main condo developments, add an association's rules on exterior walls and common elements. We ask about all of that when you book, because it changes the plan more than the brand of soundbar does.

  • 20.4% built before 1940, 55.3% built 1980–2009: two building booms with a forty-year gap between them; median year built 1989 (ACS 2019–2023 estimates via Point2).

Put together, those numbers describe a neighborhood where the age of the house predicts the job better than its size. A 1993 colonial off Page Avenue and an 1886 cottage on Hopping Avenue might be a five-minute walk apart and have nothing in common behind the TV. That is why this page is organised by wall era rather than by room, and why the first thing we ask on the phone is roughly when the house was built.

Rules a Tottenville business soundbar should respect

  • Protruding objects: the 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2) limit wall-mounted objects whose leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to 4 inches of projection into a circulation path. A soundbar under a waiting-room TV usually sits inside that zone, so we place it where people don't walk or keep it shallow.
  • Music and TV in a business: federal copyright law (17 U.S.C. §110(5)(B)) lets many small shops and restaurants play broadcast radio and TV without a separate licence, within size, speaker-count and screen-size limits. Streaming playlists are a different matter. The question below sets out the limits.
  • Noise: the city noise code limits sound from commercial establishments into neighbouring homes. A bar under a TV rarely gets near those limits, but we set a maximum volume where a business shares a wall or floor with an apartment.
  • Paperwork: plaza managers and landlords often want a certificate of insurance naming them. We provide it free, usually the same day.
  • Landmarks: the George Cunningham Store at 173 Main Street is the subject of a Landmarks Preservation Commission designation report; exterior speakers or cable on any landmarked building need the Commission's view first. Interior bars don't.

The old village: houses from before 1940

Our 1890s house may still have knob-and-tube wiring inside the walls — can a soundbar cable share the cavity, and is the old outlet fit for the bar and subwoofer?

Possibly, but not before somebody qualified has looked at it. Knob-and-tube is the two-wire system of porcelain knobs and tubes that the home inspectors' association InterNACHI says was in common use from about 1880 to the 1940s, which is exactly the age of a good share of the houses north of Hylan Boulevard. It was designed to shed heat into open air. InterNACHI warns that insulation packed around it causes heat to build up and creates a fire hazard, that it has no ground wire, and that many insurers refuse to cover houses that still have it unless an electrician certifies the system.

That matters to a soundbar job in two ways. First, the cable route. If we open the stud bay behind the TV to fish an HDMI and power kit and find live knob-and-tube conductors running through it, we stop. Low-voltage cable should never be pulled against or tied to them, and the in-wall power kit we use is designed to connect to a modern receptacle, not to splice into century-old wiring. In that case the wiring goes in a paintable cord cover ($59) on the surface or down to a cabinet, and the cavity is left alone. Second, the outlet. A soundbar and an active subwoofer draw little power, but both expect a grounded three-prong outlet. Plugging them into a cheater adapter on an ungrounded two-prong outlet defeats the protection the plug was designed for.

So the honest order is: if you know or suspect the house still has knob-and-tube, have a licensed electrician evaluate it and, ideally, add a grounded outlet behind the TV first. We can work the same day the electrician finishes. If the wiring has already been replaced — many village houses were rewired decades ago — tell us when, and we will carry on as normal. Either way we look into the cavity with a light before anything goes into it.

The wall cavities and attic of our old house hold a loose, pebbly, grey-brown insulation — what happens if a soundbar cable has to pass through it?

Then the cable does not go through it. Loose, granular insulation that looks like small grey-brown or gold pebbles is very often vermiculite, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency says that homeowners should assume vermiculite insulation may be contaminated with asbestos. The EPA's advice is to leave it undisturbed, because disturbing it can release fibres into the air, and that any removal should be done by a trained, accredited asbestos abatement contractor.

In practice that rules out fishing a cable down from the attic, or up from the floor below, through a cavity that contains it, and it rules out opening a wall to install an in-wall kit if the vermiculite has settled into the bay. We check before we cut. If you have seen this material in the attic, tell us when you book and we will plan the job as a surface or cabinet route from the start: a paintable cord cover ($59) dressed into the corner of the chimney breast or the window casing, or a $99 cable run along the baseboard to a cabinet where the cable box can live.

None of this stops the soundbar going on the wall. The bracket's pilot holes go into studs behind plaster, not into the open bay, and we drill only where the bracket sits. If you later have the insulation professionally removed, the in-wall option becomes available and moving the cable inside is a short return visit.

Our plaster was skim-coated years ago over several layers of old wallpaper — will a bracket hold, and will drilling make the surface bubble?

The bracket will hold, because it never relies on the plaster. Whatever is layered on the surface, the screws pass through it and into a stud or into a backer board fixed to studs. The plaster and paper are only a finish that the bracket rests against. What does need care is the finish itself. Wallpaper under a skim coat is a weak layer: when a drill bit grabs it, it can tear the paper behind the skim and lift a coin-sized blister around the hole, sometimes larger.

We prevent that by scoring the skim coat with a sharp blade at each mark, drilling with a new, sharp bit at low speed with no hammer action, and backing out often to clear the dust. Tape over the mark helps hold the surface together while the bit breaks through. If the plaster under the paper turns out to be loose from the lath — you can sometimes hear it as a hollow sound when tapping — we move the bracket a stud over, or we use the TV-attached bracket option so the bar hangs from the TV mount and the plaster is left alone.

If a small blister does appear, it stays behind the bar and the bracket, which cover a larger area than the holes. We tell you before we start whether your wall looks like a candidate, and the plaster line on the price list (+$45) covers the extra time.

1980s and 1990s colonials south-east of Hylan

Our family-room TV goes above a direct-vent gas fireplace in a framed bump-out — could a drill hit the vent or the firebox?

It could, which is why we find both before choosing where the bracket goes. A direct-vent gas fireplace sits inside a framed box, usually with a vent pipe running up and out through the wall or roof, and the manufacturer's manual sets clearances between that pipe and anything combustible. One widely sold model's manual, for example, gives 2 to 3 inches of clearance around horizontal vent runs and states that a TV bracket must be fixed to framing, never to the fireplace itself. Builders in the 1990s framed these bump-outs in many different ways, and the vent may run straight up behind the spot where a TV would naturally go.

So we ask for the fireplace's make and model, or a photo of the label inside the lower grille, and we look for the vent termination outside the house to work out the pipe's route. On the day we scan the bump-out, check for a firestop at the top, and fix the TV mount and the soundbar bracket only into framing outside the manufacturer's clearances. If the framing in the safe zone isn't strong enough, a backer board spreads the load across it, or the soundbar hangs from the TV mount instead of from the wall.

Two related points. Heat rising from a working fireplace is hard on electronics above it; most bars tolerate it, but we keep the bar to the side of any hot air outlet and recommend checking the TV maker's guidance on mounting above a heat source. And a TV over the fireplace adds $130 to the TV mounting job, separate from the soundbar's own $129.

Our kitchen TV folds down from under the wall cabinets — is there any way to add a soundbar to it?

Usually yes, with a small bar and a careful look at clearances. Under-cabinet fold-down TVs were a popular kitchen option in houses of the 1990s and 2000s, and their built-in speakers are small and point down at the counter, which is why people struggle to hear them over a range hood or a running tap. The fix is a compact soundbar, often under 24 inches long, mounted either to the underside of the cabinet beside the hinge or on the wall just below the cabinet line, where the folded TV will clear it in both positions.

Before we drill, we fold the TV through its full arc with a tape measure in hand. Many fold-down mounts swing the screen forward and down by several inches, and a bar in the wrong place stops the screen closing or catches a corner. We also check the cabinet bottom: some are only a quarter-inch panel and can't carry a screw, so the bar's bracket goes to the face frame or to blocking above it. Power usually comes from the TV's own outlet in the cabinet, and audio goes over HDMI if the TV has ARC, or over optical if it is old enough not to.

Keep the bar away from the steam of the kettle and the heat over the range; grease is the enemy of speaker grilles. The job is priced like any wall mount: $129, plus the tile line (+$95) only if we have to drill through a tiled backsplash.

The house came with a home-theater-in-a-box and a subwoofer the last owners left behind — can that subwoofer work with our new soundbar?

Sometimes, but less often than people hope. Most modern soundbars pair only with their own wireless subwoofer, made for that bar, and have no input for a third-party one. A smaller number of bars, and almost every A/V receiver, have a wired subwoofer output, a single RCA jack marked SUB or LFE. If your bar has that output and the old subwoofer is a powered one with its own amplifier and line-level input, a single cable connects them and the bar's bass settings can be set to hand the low notes to it.

The catch is what the old system actually is. Many home-theater-in-a-box subwoofers are passive: they contain the speaker but not the amplifier, and they were driven by the system's main box through speaker wire. A passive subwoofer cannot be connected to a soundbar at all without an extra amplifier, which defeats the point. We identify which kind you have from the back panel — a power cord and a line input mean powered, two spring clips and no power cord mean passive — before you spend anything.

If the old sub won't work, the bar's matching sub is the cleaner answer, and we'll set it up and place it for the room. The abandoned speaker wire in the walls can be cut back and capped neatly behind a blank plate. Setting up a soundbar with an extra subwoofer is covered by the soundbar lines; a receiver-based system is the $129 receiver line.

Shore blocks and storm season

If a soundbar and subwoofer in our basement or ground-floor den are ruined in a flood, will our flood insurance pay for them?

Probably not, and it is worth knowing before you decide where they go. Under the National Flood Insurance Program, FEMA describes coverage in a basement as limited. CNBC's 2024 explainer of the rules lists televisions, computers and couches among the personal property a standard policy doesn't cover in a basement, while items such as furnaces, water heaters, electrical outlets and circuit-breaker boxes are covered as part of the building. FEMA defines a basement as any area with a floor below ground level on all sides, which can include sunken rooms and the lower level of some split houses.

Raised houses near the beach have a parallel rule. Coverage in the enclosed area below the lowest elevated floor — the ground-level garage or storage space of a rebuilt house on Yetman Avenue or Loretto Street — is also limited under the program. Check your own declarations page with your agent, because private policies differ, but the safe working assumption is that electronics stored or used below the living floor are not insured against flood.

That is one more reason we plan Tottenville shore jobs with everything that has a plug on the living floor. If the family room really is downstairs, we mount the bar on the wall rather than leaving it on a low console, put the subwoofer on a riser or choose a wall-mounted alternative, and put the power strip on a shelf so it can be unplugged quickly when a storm warning goes out. That costs nothing extra beyond the usual lines, and it may be the only protection those parts have.

After every storm warning we wonder about surges — is a plug-in surge strip enough for the soundbar and TV, or should we ask for whole-house protection?

Both have a place, and they protect against different things. A plug-in surge-protective strip behind the TV catches surges that arrive on the power wiring inside the house; a good one also protects the coaxial cable line from the cable company, which is a common route for a surge into a TV and soundbar. We put one on every installation, mounted where it can be reached and switched off, and we connect the soundbar, subwoofer, TV and cable box to it.

A whole-house surge protector is installed by an electrician at the electrical panel. Since its 2020 edition the National Electrical Code, in section 230.67, has required a Type 1 or Type 2 surge-protective device on all services supplying dwelling units, including when the service equipment is replaced. Many older Tottenville houses predate that rule, and so do panels replaced before it took effect locally. Whether your panel has one, and whether New York City's own electrical code requires it for your situation, is a question for a licensed electrician, not for us.

What we can say is that the two work as layers. The panel device takes the big hit from outside; the strip deals with what gets through and with surges generated inside the house by motors and compressors. Neither helps if the equipment is sitting in water, so in a flood-prone house we still keep the strip off the floor. And when a storm is forecast and you're leaving, unplugging the strip is the cheapest protection of all.

Shops and restaurants on Main Street, Amboy Road and Page Avenue

Do we need a music licence to play the TV through a soundbar in our Tottenville shop or restaurant?

Often not for ordinary broadcast TV and radio, provided the business stays within the limits in federal copyright law. Section 110(5)(B) of the Copyright Act exempts many establishments from needing a licence to play a licensed radio or TV broadcast, or a cable or satellite transmission, for customers. A shop or office qualifies if it has less than 2,000 gross square feet; a restaurant or bar if it has less than 3,750. Larger businesses qualify only within equipment limits: no more than six loudspeakers in all and no more than four in any one room or adjoining outdoor space, and for TVs no more than four screens, no more than one in any room, and no screen larger than 55 inches diagonally.

There are conditions in every case: no charge to see or hear the transmission, and no further transmission beyond the premises. And the exemption is about broadcasts. It doesn't cover streaming music services aimed at households, playlists from a personal account, or recorded music, all of which normally need a business music service or performing-rights licences.

This is information, not legal advice, and we don't decide whether your business qualifies. What we do is set the system up so it is easy to stay inside the lines you have chosen: one soundbar per screen counts as one loudspeaker source per room in most layouts, a bar under a 55-inch screen keeps you within the screen limit, and streaming apps can be removed from the bar so staff don't switch to a personal playlist by accident. For larger dining rooms that need many speakers, our commercial AV team on Staten Island designs to your licensing choice.

We run a small fitness studio off Page Avenue — is a soundbar loud enough for a class, or do we need real speakers?

For a class that follows a video on the screen, with a handful of people in a room up to the size of a two-car garage, a good soundbar with its subwoofer can do the job. Soundbars are designed to project speech and music forward to a seating area, and a fitness room is essentially a living room without the sofa. The bar sits under the TV at the front, the instructor's video carries the music and cues, and a subwoofer set back from the walls gives the beat some weight without booming.

Where a soundbar falls short is in larger rooms, loud spin or HIIT classes with a microphone, and spaces with hard floors and mirrored walls, which reflect sound and make speech harder to follow. A bar has no microphone input, so an instructor who talks over the music needs a small mixer and speakers that can handle both. In those rooms we hand the design to our AV team for wall or ceiling speakers.

If a soundbar is the right fit, mount it on the front wall below the screen, check for metal studs (+$30) if the space was fitted out recently, and ask us to set a volume ceiling so the class next door, or the apartment above, isn't part of the workout. Rubber flooring helps a little with bass travelling downward; isolating the subwoofer on a pad helps more.

Everyday listening in the house

The adverts and some channels still blast much louder than the show — can the soundbar even that out?

Partly, and it helps to know where the rule ends and the soundbar's job begins. Since December 13, 2012, the Federal Communications Commission's rules under the CALM Act have required TV stations, cable operators and satellite and other pay-TV providers to keep commercials at the same average volume as the programs around them. The FCC accepts complaints about loud commercials through its online consumer form. The rule speaks of average loudness, so an advert can still feel more insistent while meeting it, and the FCC's guidance doesn't say the rule reaches streaming apps.

Most soundbars have a feature that smooths the rest. It goes by different names — auto volume, volume leveller, night mode or dynamic range control — and it narrows the gap between quiet and loud passages. Some TVs have their own version too. Using both at once can make dialogue sound flat and pumping, so we choose one, usually the bar's, and set it so dialogue stays clear.

We also check that the TV is sending the bar the right signal. If the TV's audio output is set to a fixed level with its own processing on, or to a format the bar doesn't handle well, jumps between channels and apps get worse. Setting the TV to pass audio through to the bar and letting the bar do the levelling usually makes the biggest difference. We show you where the setting lives so you can turn it off for a film.

Almost every how-to says it, and on drywall it works. Zircon, which makes stud finders, publishes its own guidance on lath-and-plaster walls, explaining that the irregular surface and the lath strips themselves can produce false readings.

FAQ

Tottenville Soundbar Installation FAQ

Our TV is tilted down toward the sofa. Should the soundbar tilt to match?
+

Usually not. A soundbar is designed to fire straight out at ear height, and most brackets hold it upright. If the TV sits high enough to need a strong tilt, we mount the bar lower and level, or hang it from the TV on a TV-attached bracket so it follows the screen's angle. The bar's room calibration then corrects what the angle changes.

Can we reserve a window weeks ahead so the TV room is ready before a graduation party or a holiday?
+

Yes. Book as far ahead as you like and we hold the two-hour window. If the date can't move, book early so the window you want is still free. Moving the appointment with a day's notice costs nothing, and we confirm the day before.

Do you use the paper mounting template that comes in the soundbar box?
+

We use it to mark the hole spacing, but not to set the position. The template is drawn from the bar's own geometry, while where the bar sits depends on the bottom edge of your TV, the screen's centre and where the studs are. We measure from the picture, then lay the template over the right spot.

Can you link the soundbar's voice assistant to the Alexa or Google speakers we already have?
+

Yes, if the bar supports it. We sign the bar into the app you use, add it to the right room and test a voice command. If you'd rather the bar's microphone stayed off, we switch it off and show you the button. Your passwords stay on your phone; you type them in.

If we buy a bigger TV next year, does the soundbar have to come down?
+

Not always. A wall-mounted bar sits under the TV's bottom edge, so a bigger screen usually means raising the TV mount or the bar. If the bar is on a TV-attached bracket, check it fits the new set's mounting pattern. Moving the bar is priced like any other: $129 on the wall or $79 on a console.

Can the soundbar switch itself off when we fall asleep with the TV on?
+

Yes. Most bars switch off with the TV over HDMI, so we set the TV's sleep timer and check the bar follows it. Many bars also have their own auto-standby after a period of silence. We test both before we leave, so the bedroom goes quiet when the TV does.
