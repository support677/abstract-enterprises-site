The Neighborhood

Why Beverley Square Properties Need Alarm Installation

Beverley Square is a small piece of Flatbush, and on its main blocks it is almost entirely houses that stand apart from one another. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Beverley Square West Historic District on November 25, 2025, the City Planning Commission’s report described 118 free-standing houses along Marlborough, Rugby and Argyle Roads between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road, 114 of them built by 1906, on lots of similar size, about fifty feet wide. Most have full-width front porches, complex rooflines and angled bays, in the late Queen Anne, early Colonial Revival and Shingle styles. The blocks are zoned R3X, which allows single- or two-family detached houses. To an alarm installer, every one of those words matters. Detached means four walls facing out instead of two. Porches and bays mean more ground-floor glass than a city house usually has. And fifty-foot lots mean side yards and driveways where a person can stand next to a window without being seen from the street.

The commission’s press release dates the houses to between 1894 and 1910 and names three of the architects who built them, John J. Petit, John B. Slee and Benjamin Driesler. It also explains why the neighborhood exists at all: the Brooklyn, Flatbush and Coney Island Railroad opened in 1878 along what is now the district’s eastern edge, and the developers built a suburb beside it. That railroad is today the open cut of the B and Q line, with stations at Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. For an alarm, that history leaves a practical inheritance. These houses were designed for families who expected to leave the windows open in summer, sit on the porch in the evening and walk to the train. They were not designed to be locked up empty for a working day, which is what most of them are now.

The record of break-ins here fits that picture closely. In June 2012, Bklyner reported on a 70th Precinct Community Council meeting held after two daytime break-ins in one day: one on Westminster Road between Beverley and Cortelyou, where a girl at home was injured, and one on DeKoven Court near Rugby Road, where things were taken even though the family dog was in the house. Neighbors on that Westminster block counted four daytime burglaries, three of them in one month, and residents near DeKoven reported three attempts in two weeks, one of them on Argyle Road. A few blocks south, in four South Midwood burglaries the same summer, the burglar came in through an unlocked first-floor window every time. In July 2012 Bklyner reported another burglary on Rugby Road at Dorchester Road, where someone broke a window pane in a door, took money and jewelry, and set off the house alarm while doing it. These are old stories, and we use them for what they show about the houses rather than about today’s crime rate: the openings that were used were ground-floor windows and glass in doors, and the time of day was the middle of the working day.

Today’s numbers are much lower, and we will not pretend otherwise. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 70th Precinct, which covers Beverley Square, shows 80 burglaries reported in 2026 through November 8, against 117 in the same part of 2025, a fall of 31.6 percent, and a fall of 96.8 percent over thirty-three years. A precinct total says little about one house, though. What a Beverley Square owner needs to know is which of their own windows can be reached from the porch roof or the side yard, whether anybody would know within a minute if one of them were opened at eleven on a Tuesday morning, and who would be told. That is the question this page tries to answer, house type by house type.

There is a second reason people here call us, and it has nothing to do with crime. Many of these houses already have an alarm, put in by an earlier owner, sometimes decades ago. When the Brownstoner forum asked about monitoring in 2009, a Ditmas Park homeowner wrote that her alarm company had raised its rates by 12 to 15 percent and she wanted to switch; an installer who answered warned that most companies lock the panel’s programming with an installer code, and that some older systems are proprietary. Those old panels are still on walls all over Flatbush, often with a dialer that called the monitoring station over a landline the household cancelled years ago. Taking one over, or replacing it on the existing wiring, is a large part of the alarm work we do in neighborhoods like this one.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Beverley Square’s Architecture

Nearly everything we are asked to protect in and around Beverley Square falls into six kinds of property. They share a ZIP code and a precinct, but an alarm plan for one would be wrong for the next, mainly because of how many sides are exposed and how many households share the doors.

The porch-front Victorian kept as one home

A free-standing house of three floors and a cellar, a full-width porch, one or two angled bays and a yard behind, on a lot about fifty feet wide. The most openings of any property on this page, and the most ways to reach the second floor.

The Colonial Revival with a symmetrical front

A centered front door under a smaller porch or portico, evenly spaced windows on each floor, and a side or back entrance off the driveway. Fewer bays than a Queen Anne house, but the same four exposed walls.

The house divided into two or three homes

A detached house converted to two or three households, with one front door into a shared hall, sometimes a separate outside stair, and a cellar everyone uses. The alarm has to know whose floor is whose.

The house in the middle of a renovation

Plaster opened, windows being replaced, a porch rebuilt, the attic finished. The one moment when running hardwired cable through the walls is cheap, and the moment when the house is most at risk because it is full of tools and empty at night.

The apartment building at the district’s edge

Apartment buildings on the avenues around the house blocks: a lobby with a buzzer or a fob reader, a mail area, and a resident who wants only their own door and windows protected.

The shop with a flat or an office above

Storefronts on Cortelyou Road and Beverley Road: a gate and glass at the front, a back door to a yard or alley, a cellar below, and often an apartment upstairs with its own street door.

The porch-front Victorian: a house that can be climbed

Walk around one of the houses on Rugby or Argyle Road and count the glass you could reach without a ladder. On the ground floor there are the windows across the front behind the porch, the angled bay on one or both sides, the side windows along the driveway, the kitchen windows and back door at the rear, and often a pair of cellar windows at grade. Then look up. A full-width porch has a roof, and the second-floor windows above it open straight onto that roof. Anyone who can climb a porch post, a railing or the lattice at the end of the porch is standing beside a bedroom window that most owners never think of as reachable. A bay that rises only one story has a small roof of its own, sometimes within reach of a fence or a trash enclosure. That is why our list for a detached Victorian usually runs longer than the owner expects, and why it often lands in the $600 to $1,200 wireless range at the top end, or in complete home protection from $2,500 when the owner wants everything covered.

The same survey also takes things off the list. Windows on the third floor, in the attic gables and dormers, can rarely be reached by anyone, and we normally leave them out. Upper side windows that face a neighbor’s wall a few yards away, with nothing beneath them to stand on, usually stay off too. A Brownstoner commenter made the same point years ago when a townhouse owner was quoted $10,000: alarming windows on the upper floors is usually unnecessary. On these houses the exception is the second floor over the porch and over any one-story bay, and we explain each choice on the written price so you can decide.

Inside, the plan follows the way people move through a big house. Motion detectors go where anyone coming in from a forced window would have to pass: the main stair, the hall that joins the front rooms to the kitchen, and the back stair if there is one. On a three-floor house we often add a second keypad by the main bedroom, so the house can be armed for the night without walking down to the front hall, and the second-floor zones can stay off while the family is upstairs while the ground floor and the porch-roof windows stay armed. Where a dog has the run of the ground floor we use pet-tolerant detectors and aim them so a radiator under a bay window does not look like a person.

The Colonial Revival: fewer bays, the same four sides

The Colonial Revival houses in the district tend to be simpler to plan: a centered door, matching windows either side, a smaller porch or portico, and fewer projecting bays. That does not make them small jobs. A detached Colonial Revival still has two side walls with windows on them, a back door, a cellar entrance and, in many cases, a side door off the driveway that the family uses every day while the formal front door stays bolted. We put the main keypad by whichever door is used, not by the one an architect intended in 1905, because an alarm that has to be disarmed from the far end of the house is an alarm people stop setting.

Original windows in these houses are often large double-hung sashes, and many have been replaced at different times by different owners. Some replacement windows will take a small recessed contact in the frame; original wood sashes usually get a surface contact painted to match or set where the trim hides it. Where a front room has three or four windows on two walls, one acoustic glass-break detector in the room can cover all of them, which keeps the number of contacts and the price down.

The house divided into two or three homes: one panel, separate areas

R3X zoning allows two-family detached houses, and many Beverley Square houses have been lived in as two homes for decades, some as three with a finished attic or cellar. These houses usually have one porch and one front door into a shared hall, then separate apartment doors inside, and sometimes an outside stair or a separate side entrance for the upper household. The cellar, with the boiler, the meters and the laundry, belongs to everyone. An alarm that treats the house as one home either leaves the tenant unprotected or gives the owner a view of the tenant’s comings and goings that neither of them wants.

The answer is a partitioned panel. The owner’s floor is one area, the rental floor is another, and the cellar and the shared doors are a third that is armed when everyone has left, or around the clock with entry delays for everyone. Each household has its own codes and its own app access, and a tenant’s code can be deleted in a minute when a lease ends. Our per-unit setup for landlords is $400 to $800 per unit before sales tax, and a full partitioned house usually falls under complete home protection from $2,500. HPD’s detector rules matter here too: in a one- or two-family house that is not owner-occupied and has fuel-burning appliances or gas piping, carbon monoxide and natural gas detectors are required as well as smoke detectors, and they must be within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Monitored detectors on the alarm can be part of that plan, and we say so in writing.

The house in the middle of a renovation: wire it now or never

Most alarms we fit in these houses are wireless, because fishing cable through finished plaster and original woodwork means opening and patching walls we would rather leave alone. Renovation changes that. When walls and ceilings are open, a hardwired system becomes practical, and the contacts can be recessed into new window frames and doors where no one will ever see them. A Brownstoner contributor who installs alarms suggested exactly this division of labor during a renovation: let the electrician pull most of the cable and leave the terminations and programming to the alarm company. We are happy to work that way. Tell us the renovation schedule early, and we will mark where every contact, detector and keypad should go before the walls close. Custom hardwired systems run $2,000 to $6,000 on our Brooklyn list.

The renovation itself is also a security problem. A house with scaffolding, a porch half rebuilt, windows out and expensive tools in the cellar is an easy target at night and on weekends. We can put in a temporary wireless system that protects the cellar and the doors during the work, then fold those sensors into the permanent system when the house is finished.

The apartment building at the edge: your door, your windows, nothing in the lobby

The apartment buildings around the house blocks have their own entry systems, mail areas and building staff, and none of that belongs to a resident. In 2018, Bklyner reported that residents of one building near Cortelyou and Stratford Roads counted more than 100 packages missing over six months, and a camera went into the mail area only after residents pressed management. That kind of problem belongs to the building. What a resident can control is the inside of the apartment, and an apartment alarm here is small and removable: a panel with a cellular communicator in a closet, a contact on the apartment door, contacts on any window reachable from a fire escape, a courtyard or a lower roof, and one motion detector in the hall. Nothing is wired into the building and nothing goes in the corridor.

The shop with a flat above: two alarms that must never share a code

On Cortelyou Road and Beverley Road, the typical commercial building is two or three stories: a shop at street level, a cellar below reached from inside or through a sidewalk hatch, and one or two apartments above with their own door to the street. The shop and the apartments are different customers with different hours. The shop alarm covers the gate, the glass, the shop door, the back door, the cellar and the roof hatch if the shop has one, and reports opening and closing times to the owner. The apartment upstairs, if it gets an alarm at all, gets its own panel or its own partition and its own codes. A tenant upstairs should never be able to disarm the shop, and the shopkeeper should never be able to see when the tenant comes home.

Four Sides, a Porch and a Yard

Planning an Alarm for a House Nobody Can See All at Once

The single biggest difference between an alarm in Beverley Square and an alarm in most of Brooklyn is the shape of the lot. On a block of attached rowhouses, a burglar has two choices, the front, which faces the street, and the back, which faces a yard. Here a house sits on its lot with space all around it. One side usually has a driveway, often leading to a garage at the back; the other has a passage a few feet wide between the house and the fence. The neighbors’ houses are set back the same distance, so from the street the side walls are half hidden, and from inside the next house the view is mostly of a wall. The 2012 break-ins on Westminster, Rugby, Argyle and DeKoven all happened in daylight, and the precinct’s advice afterward was to lock windows even when stepping out briefly, light the outside of the house enough to see a silhouette, and trim shrubs so they do not hide the view into or out of a property. All three of those are about the sides of a house, where nobody is looking.

So our survey on these blocks goes around the house rather than front to back. We start on the sidewalk and look at the porch: how high the floor is above the ground, how the steps reach it, whether the railings and posts could be climbed to the porch roof, and which windows open onto that roof. Then we walk the driveway side, noting every window, the side door if there is one, the garage and anything a person could stand on, such as a fence, a bin enclosure or the air-conditioner bracket under a bedroom window. The precinct singled out air conditioners for a reason: an unsecured window unit can be pushed in or pulled out, leaving an opening no contact on the sash will report. Then the narrow side, which on many houses has the cellar windows and a bay. Then the back yard, the kitchen door, the cellar entrance and the back stair. Only then do we go in.

Inside, the question is where to put the motion detectors. In a narrow rowhouse one detector in the hall often sees every route. In a detached Victorian the routes split: someone who comes in through a side bay can reach the dining room without passing the front hall, and someone who comes over the porch roof is already upstairs. We usually end up with one detector on the main stair, one in the hall between the front rooms and the kitchen, and contacts or glass-break detectors on the second-floor windows over the porch, so that an intruder on the porch roof is caught at the window rather than once they are inside. The cellar gets its own zone, because cellars in these houses often have their own outside door and windows at grade.

Finally we think about sound. A siren inside a detached house is heard far less by the neighbors than it would be through a shared rowhouse wall, which is one reason monitoring or at least phone alerts make more sense here than on an attached block. We fit an interior siren loud enough to drive someone out, set its cutoff well inside the fifteen-minute limit in New York City Administrative Code § 24-238, and make sure the alarm’s phone alerts reach someone who will act, whether that is a monitoring station, a family member at work, or a neighbor who has agreed to look.

Business Alarm Installation in Beverley Square

The houses fill the middle of the neighborhood; the businesses sit on its edges and along the two roads that cross it. The commercial alarms we fit around Beverley Square cover small buildings that are busy all day and empty all night, often with somebody living upstairs.

The main shopping street on the north side, with restaurants, cafes, grocers and the Flatbush Food Cooperative near the Cortelyou Road station. Storefronts with gates and glass at the front, kitchens and back doors to small yards, and cellars below.

A shorter run of shops around the Beverley Road station on the south side of the district: delis, laundromats and service businesses in low buildings, most of them with apartments above and their own street doors.

The larger commercial street to the north, with longer opening hours, more cash and more glass. Stores here usually need gate contacts, glass-break detectors and a panic button at the register as standard.

Coney Island Avenue

The avenue along the west side, with car-related businesses, wholesalers and shops in deeper buildings. More roll-up doors, larger back areas and stockrooms that need their own zones.

Ocean Avenue

The apartment avenue to the east, with ground-floor offices, medical practices and small businesses in the bases of larger buildings, where the alarm must stay inside the tenant’s space.

Newkirk Avenue and the blocks south

Shops and offices near the Newkirk stations to the south, a similar mix of storefronts with flats above, and some professional offices in converted houses.

What a Cortelyou Road shop alarm has to cover

A typical shop here has more openings than it looks from the sidewalk. At the front there is usually a roll-down gate, a glass storefront and a glass door behind it; a gate contact tells the monitoring station the gate has been lifted, and a glass-break detector catches the storefront even if the gate stays down. At the back there is a door to a yard or a passage, often steel, often the one the staff use for deliveries and trash. Below there is a cellar, reached by an interior stair or by a sidewalk hatch, with the compressor, the stock or the boiler. Above there may be a roof hatch or a skylight. Each of those is one line on a commercial quote, and our standard small-business alarm, $1,500 to $3,000 before sales tax, covers a shop with that usual set.

Restaurants add two complications. Kitchens are hot and steamy, so motion detectors go in the dining room and the front, never facing the line; heat detectors, not smoke detectors, belong near a kitchen if the owner wants fire reporting on the panel. And restaurants keep late hours, so the arming schedule has to fit the last person out, which we set with user codes for each manager and an opening and closing report the owner can read the next morning.

Back doors, cellars and the hatch in the sidewalk

On these blocks the weak point of a shop is rarely the front. The front is lit, faces the street and has a gate. The back door faces a yard or a passage between buildings that nobody overlooks at night, and the cellar has a hatch that opens straight onto the sidewalk. We put heavy-duty contacts on steel back doors, contacts on both leaves of a cellar hatch, and a motion detector at the foot of the cellar stair. If the cellar is damp or has pumps or compressors running, we choose and aim the detector so it ignores them, and we walk-test it with the equipment on before we leave.

The shop and the apartment above

Where a shop has an apartment above, we keep the two completely separate. The shop alarm’s panel, keypad and siren stay inside the shop, the cellar is part of the shop’s system only if it belongs to the shop under the lease, and the upstairs tenant’s street door is not on the shop alarm at all. If the building owner wants the hallway and the roof door covered for the whole building, that becomes a third area with its own codes. This matters most when a shop changes hands: the new owner deserves new codes and a clean account, not the old owner’s settings.

Compliance notes for Beverley Square businesses and buildings

  • Siren cutoff. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be able to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes of going off. Every siren we install is set well inside that limit.
  • Licensed installers. Burglar and fire alarm installers in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, which requires 81 hours of qualifying education for the license. AESS installs under license #12000287431; ask any company you consider for its number.
  • Unnecessary fire alarms. The FDNY tells owners to take steps to prevent unnecessary or unwarranted fire alarms. If your panel reports smoke or heat, a monitoring station may send the fire department, so detector placement and a test mode during maintenance matter, as one Brownstoner poster found when repeated false alarms led to a summons.
  • Detectors in rentals. HPD requires smoke detectors in dwelling units and, in one- and two-family houses that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances, carbon monoxide and natural gas detectors. Monitored detectors on the alarm can be part of meeting that.
  • Keyless entry in apartment buildings. If a multiple dwelling uses smart access such as fobs, cards or phone apps, HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires consent, a written privacy policy and destruction of authentication data within 90 days, and owners may not require smart access to a tenant’s own apartment. An apartment alarm does not change any of that, and we keep it separate from building entry.
  • Landmarks. Inside the Beverley Square West Historic District, nothing about an interior alarm needs Landmarks review. The LPC’s guidebook does govern doorbells, intercom panels and light fixtures at a door opening, and anything else mounted on a facade may need a permit, so we keep sirens, keypads and communicators indoors and prepare photos and cut sheets for any outdoor device you choose.

This is general information about rules that touch alarm work, not legal advice. Your lease, your insurer or your board may have requirements of its own.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Beverley Square: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners and tenants around Beverley Square bring to us, grouped by the part of the house or the situation they concern. Some came from the Brownstoner forum threads on Brooklyn alarms, some from cost guides, and some from what we see on these blocks.

Porches, bays and the upper floors

Our second-floor windows open straight onto the porch roof. Do they really need sensors?

On most Beverley Square houses, yes. A full-width porch puts a flat or gently sloped roof right under the front bedroom windows, and its posts, railings and end lattice are easy to climb, so those windows are about as reachable as the ground floor. We put contacts on them, or a glass-break detector in the room if there are several, and leave the upper windows with nothing beneath them off the list.

We sleep on the second floor and the children are on the third. Can the ground floor and the porch-roof windows stay armed while we move around upstairs at night?

Yes. We set the porch-roof windows, the ground floor and the cellar as the zones that arm at night, and leave the stair and upper-floor motion detectors off in that mode so nobody trips them going to the bathroom. A second keypad or a key fob by the bedroom lets you arm and disarm the night setting without going down to the front hall.

We like to leave a couple of upstairs windows open on summer nights. Can the alarm still be armed?

It can, if you choose which windows and accept what that means. The panel lets you bypass named windows for one arming, so the rest of the house stays protected and the open ones are simply ignored until next time. We suggest keeping the porch-roof windows closed when the house is armed, because those are the open windows someone could actually reach, and we can fit a second contact higher on the frame of a back window so it can be locked open a few inches and still armed.

The contact on our bay window shows “open” every winter and works fine every summer. Is the sensor failing?

Usually not. Old wood sashes in angled bays swell and shrink with the seasons, and if the magnet was set at the edge of its range the gap opens up enough in one season to lose contact. We move the magnet and the sensor closer together, or switch to a contact with a wider gap tolerance, and check the window’s lock at the same visit, since a sash that moves that much may not latch properly either.

Doors and glass

Our front door has a large glass panel, like the door on Rugby Road where a burglar broke a pane in 2012. Does a door contact protect against someone reaching through to turn the lock?

Only once the door opens, which may be too late to matter. If a door has a big pane within reach of the lock, we add an acoustic glass-break detector in the hall so the alarm reacts to the glass itself, and suggest a lock that cannot be opened from inside without a key or a thumb-turn placed out of reach. In the 2012 Rugby Road burglary Bklyner reported that the house alarm did go off, but only after the pane was broken and the door opened.

Our cellar has its own outside door under the back steps. Should it be on the same zone as the kitchen door?

We give it a zone of its own. An outside cellar door is used far less than the kitchen door and is often out of sight, so the panel should report it by name and you may want it armed at times when the rest of the house is not. Separate zones also make the app useful: “cellar door opened” at two in the afternoon is information you want, and “back door” is not specific enough.

Old systems and other companies

The company that installed our old alarm says the panel is proprietary and won’t release it. What are our options?

A Brownstoner installer described exactly this: most companies lock the panel’s programming with an installer code, some older systems from national companies are proprietary, and sometimes the main board has to be replaced. In practice we test the existing wiring and contacts, and if they are sound we replace only the panel and keypad with one we can program, which is our panel replacement on existing wiring at $800 to $1,500. You own the new panel outright, and it is worth asking any installer, us included, to leave it programmable by others, so the next company never meets the same lock.

Our Nest Secure is no longer supported. Can any of it be used with a new system?

Google discontinued Nest Secure, with support ending around April 2024, as a Brownstoner poster noted. Its sensors were made to talk only to its own hub, so they are not something we can enroll on another panel. Existing Nest cameras or a Nest doorbell can keep working on their own app beside the new alarm, and if you want one app for everything, we can quote cameras and a doorbell that work on the same Alarm.com service as the panel.

Houses shared by two households

Our upstairs tenant works nights and comes through the shared cellar to the laundry at odd hours. How does the cellar stay armed without them tripping it?

We make the cellar and the shared hall a common area with its own entry delay, and give the tenant a code that disarms only that area and their own floor. When they come home at two in the morning they disarm the common area on the way in and re-arm it from their own keypad or the app once they are upstairs. You see that the common area was disarmed and re-armed, not anything about their apartment, unless you both agree otherwise.

Outside the house

The narrow passage along one side of our house is dark at night. Is an outdoor motion sensor there a good idea?

An outdoor motion sensor on the alarm itself usually causes more false alarms than it prevents, because cats, raccoons and leaves move through side passages all night. We would rather put a floodlight camera on that wall that lights up and records when something moves, and send that to your phone, while the alarm sensors stay on the windows and doors along the passage. The precinct’s own advice after the 2012 break-ins was to light the outside of a house enough to see a silhouette, with fixtures out of reach.

Since the houses here are spread apart, should we put an outdoor siren on the porch so the neighbors hear it?

We generally do not recommend one. An outdoor bell on a porch is easy to reach and silence, and on a house in the landmark district it is a visible addition to the facade. An interior siren drives an intruder out, and the real notification comes from the panel’s cellular connection to your phone or a monitoring station, which reaches you no matter how far apart the houses are. If you want a visible deterrent, the yard sign and window stickers do that job.

Renovations and cost

Our porch is being rebuilt and the front of the house will be under scaffolding for weeks. What should change while it is up?

Scaffolding turns every front window on every floor into a reachable opening, including the third floor. When the scaffolding comes down, the temporary sensors come off the upper windows, and any you want to keep can be moved to the back of the house.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Beverley Square

Ask an AI assistant or a cost site about alarm installation and you get answers written for an average American house. Several of the common ones are wrong, or at least misleading, for a detached Victorian in Flatbush. Here are the claims we hear most often, and what we find on these blocks.

“Alarm installation costs about $750.”

That figure is real, but it is national. HomeAdvisor’s cost guide gives a national average of $747 and a typical range of $300 to $1,238. Every one of those is a decision, and most of them end up on the list.

So we price by openings, not square feet or ZIP code. The written price after one visit names every sensor, so you can see what moves the number.

“Burglars come in through the front door, so start there.”

Many burglaries do start at a door, and on some blocks in Brooklyn the front door is the right place to begin. The local record here points elsewhere. In the daytime burglaries Bklyner covered in 2012, the four South Midwood break-ins all came through unlocked first-floor windows, and the Rugby Road burglary began with a broken pane in a door. The precinct’s advice afterward was about windows, air conditioners, shrubs and lighting, not about front locks.

On a detached house the front door is also the most watched place on the lot: it faces the street, it sits on an open porch, and on these blocks people walk past it to the train. The side windows along a driveway or a passage, and the back door to the yard, are not watched by anybody. That is where a burglar who wants a quiet minute will go.

So the front door gets a contact and often a video doorbell, but it is not where we spend most of the budget. The sides and the back are, and the first-floor windows along them are the first things on our list.

“Upstairs windows don’t need sensors.”

For a typical rowhouse, that is often fair advice; a Brownstoner commenter said much the same about a townhouse in 2008. It does not hold for the second floor of a house with a full-width porch. The porch roof puts a walkable surface right under the front bedroom windows, and porch posts and railings are an easy climb.

Angled bays create the same problem on a smaller scale. A one-story bay has a roof of its own beneath a second-floor window, and a fence, a trash enclosure or a parked car can make it reachable. Scaffolding during a renovation makes every upper window reachable for weeks.

The honest rule is not “ground floor only” but “anything you can reach without a ladder.” On most houses here that means the ground floor, the cellar, the windows over the porch roof and any window over a bay, and not the third floor or the attic gables.

“Any monitoring company can take over your existing alarm.”

Sometimes, not always. The 2009 Brownstoner thread from a Ditmas Park owner who wanted to switch after a rate increase is a good example. The installer who answered explained that most companies lock the panel’s programming with an installer code, that some older systems are proprietary, and that the main board may have to be replaced.

In practice, whether a takeover is simple depends on the panel brand, whether the installer code is known or can be reset, and whether the sensors are wired or wireless. Wired contacts in original window frames are usually reusable; old wireless sensors on a proprietary frequency may not be.

So we look before we promise. We read the panel and keypad, test the wiring and contacts, and tell you whether the cheapest path is reprogramming the existing panel, replacing only the panel on existing wiring at $800 to $1,500, or starting fresh.

“Crime is down, so you probably don’t need an alarm.”

Crime is down, and we say so. The 70th Precinct’s CompStat report shows 80 burglaries reported in 2026 through November 8, against 117 in the same period of 2025, and a 96.8 percent fall over thirty-three years.

But the trend moves, and averages hide houses. At the October 2025 Community Council meeting the commanding officer reported burglary down 75 percent; a month later the minutes list burglary among the categories that had risen. A precinct figure spread across tens of thousands of homes says nothing about whether your side window can be opened unseen at eleven in the morning.

An alarm also does things a crime rate never measures: it tells you a cellar is flooding, that smoke has been detected while you are at work, or that a child got home. We would rather you decide on those grounds than on fear, and we do not sell alarms with crime maps.

“A wireless alarm needs Wi-Fi, and a power cut switches it off.”

Some cheap systems do depend on the house Wi-Fi and a plug-in hub. The panels we fit do not. They have their own cellular communicator and a backup battery, so they report to your phone or a monitoring station without the internet and keep running when the power goes out.

It often does with national dealers, as forum posts describe: one Brownstoner poster was waiting out a three-year contract before switching, and another complained of being charged for a service call within a 90-day warranty.

Field Notes: Walking a Beverley Square House Clockwise, Porch to Porch

This describes how our technicians survey a detached house in Beverley Square, not a story about one job. The walk starts on the sidewalk, looking up rather than in: which second-floor windows open onto the porch roof, how easily the porch posts and railings could be climbed, and whether a bay on either side has a roof of its own under a window. Then the technician goes up the steps and tries the porch door and the windows behind the porch, and notes whether a storm door or a glass panel changes how the door should be protected.

From the porch the walk goes clockwise. Down the driveway side, counting windows, noting the side door and anything a person could stand on, such as a fence, a bin enclosure or an air-conditioner bracket, and checking whether the garage at the back has a door into the yard. Across the back, to the kitchen door, the back steps and the outside cellar door if there is one. Up the narrow side, where the cellar windows and a second bay often are. And back to the porch. Only then does the technician go inside, try each door and window, look at the cellar, find where the old panel is if there is one, and walk the routes from each side of the house to the stairs to decide where motion detectors belong. FAQ

Beverley Square Alarm Installation FAQ

Our dog has the run of the ground floor all day. Can the downstairs still be armed while we are at work?
+

Yes, with pet-tolerant motion detectors set for your dog’s size, mounted and aimed so the dog cannot reach their view by climbing onto a sofa or a window seat in a bay. For large dogs we often rely on contacts and glass-break detectors on the ground floor and keep motion detection to the stairs, which the dog may not use. We walk-test the settings with the dog at home before we leave.

A neighbor said repeated false alarms from his smoke detectors led to an FDNY summons. How do we avoid that?
+

The FDNY expects owners to take steps to prevent unnecessary alarms, and one Brownstoner poster described a summons after repeated ones from a monitored system. We keep smoke detectors away from kitchens, steamy bathrooms and the boiler flue, use heat detectors where smoke would cause trouble, and show you how to put the system in test mode before painters, floor sanders or a chimney sweep start work.

Will the old cast-iron radiators under our bay windows confuse a motion detector?
+

They can if the detector looks straight at them, because passive infrared sensors react to warm air moving across their view. In houses with steam or hot-water radiators under the windows we mount detectors so they look along the stair or the hall rather than at the windows, and cover the bay itself with a glass-break detector or contacts instead.

Do you also install on the blocks east of the B and Q tracks, outside the landmark district?
+

Yes. We work on both sides of the cut, from the houses on Marlborough, Rugby and Argyle Roads to the numbered East streets on the other side, and on the apartment buildings and shops around them. The prices are the same Brooklyn list on every block, and outside the historic district there is simply less to clear for anything mounted on the front of a house.

Do alarm companies still price a house by the number of doors and windows?
+

Largely, yes. In a 2008 Brownstoner thread one homeowner said basic packages ran about $70 per entry point plus extras such as motion detectors, and another said price depends on window and door counts, roof openings and how the alarm communicates. Our list works the same way in principle: a starter system covers four to six sensors, and the written price lists each extra opening and detector.

HomeAdvisor says the average Brooklyn alarm costs about $1,100. Is that a fair guide for a house here?
+

It is a reasonable middle, but a detached Victorian usually sits above an average that also includes apartments and small kits. HomeAdvisor’s Brooklyn page gives an average of $1,119 and a typical range of $450 to $2,198. On our list, most houses here fall in the $600 to $1,200 wireless range, and full coverage with life-safety sensors starts at $2,500.

Can our phones tell us when the outside cellar door opens during the day, even with the alarm switched off?
+

Yes. Each sensor can send its own notification in the app whether the system is armed or not, so you can choose to hear about the cellar door, the garage side door or a liquor cabinet at any hour, without being told every time the kitchen door opens. The panel records the event either way, so you can look back at the history later.

Our back door opens from an enclosed back porch into the kitchen. Which door should the alarm protect?
+

Usually both, on separate zones. A contact on the outer porch door tells you early that someone is inside the enclosure, and the inner kitchen door is the real line, so it carries the entry delay for the keypad. If the porch has windows of its own that a person could climb through, a motion detector or glass-break detector inside the enclosure may replace the outer door contact.

Can the main keypad go inside the front hall closet so it does not sit on the original woodwork?
+

It can, as long as the closet is within easy reach of the door you use and you are comfortable opening it every time you come in. Many owners in these houses prefer a small keypad on a plain wall section near the side door, with the panel itself hidden in a closet or the cellar. Arming from the app or a key fob means the keypad is touched less often anyway.

Our porch door has a storm door in front of it. Where does the contact go?
+

On the main wood door, not the storm door. The storm door is opened by delivery drivers and visitors all day and does not keep anyone out, while the main door is the one that matters. If you want to know when someone has stepped inside the storm door, a video doorbell on the porch does that better than a second contact.

The house stands empty for part of the winter. Can the alarm warn us before the cellar pipes freeze?
+

Yes. A low-temperature sensor in the cellar near the water pipes reports to the app and, if you have monitoring, to the station when the temperature falls below the level it is set for. In an empty detached house it is worth pairing with water sensors by the water heater and the washing machine, so a boiler failure or a burst pipe is caught early.

Can a video doorbell on our deep porch show up in the same app as the alarm?
+

Yes, if it is one that works on the same Alarm.com service as the panel, such as the Alarm.com doorbell we fit. The doorbell’s clips and the alarm’s events then appear together in one timeline, so a motion alert at the porch and a door opening a few seconds later are seen side by side. A doorbell from another brand will still work, but in its own app.
