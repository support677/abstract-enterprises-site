The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Bushwick's buildings were put up in bursts, torn down in bursts and rebuilt in bursts, and the camera work on any given block depends on which burst built it. The Dutch named the place Boswijck, which Forgotten New York translates as "town in the woods," and Bushwiki dates the town's founding to 1660. For two centuries it was farmland, much of it planted in tobacco, according to Forgotten New York. Then, the Brooklyn Eagle writes, Adrian Martense Suydam began dividing his family farm into lots, and by 1884 there were 125 houses on it while breweries opened around them. The elevated trains along Broadway and Myrtle Avenue arrived in the 1880s, and building picked up fast. By one count in the Eagle, a stretch called Brewer's Row held 11 breweries within 14 blocks. Builders worked at enormous scale: 6sqft notes that Paul Stier alone built more than 2,000 buildings in the Ridgewood and Bushwick area, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission credits the firm of Louis Berger & Company as architect of record on more than 5,000. Brooklyn Community Board 4's own history describes the result as two- to six-family houses from the 1880s on, mansions on Bushwick Avenue for brewers and doctors between 1880 and 1913, more grand houses around Irving Avenue from 1900 to 1913, and big apartment houses in the 1920s.

The decline was just as sharp. Rheingold, the last and largest of the breweries, closed in 1976. The NYC Municipal Archives blog counts some 4,000 fires in Bushwick by 1975, many in old wooden buildings, and in the July 1977 blackout stores along Broadway were looted and burned. Community Board 4 says a third of the neighborhood's stores closed and 43 percent stood empty a year later. City Limits reported in 2009 that the burned-out lots were concentrated in the southern part of the neighborhood, along Broadway and Bushwick and Central avenues, while the blocks in ZIP code 11237 came through largely intact. Rebuilding came in waves after that: public housing and Housing Partnership two-family houses in the 1980s, three-family rows in the 2000s, and since 2010 a run of larger rental and condo buildings, including The Denizen, the million-square-foot development on the old Rheingold site. City land-use records for Community District 4 still list 1,862 lots as six-family walk-ups, holding 11,032 apartments with a median height of three floors, alongside 307 lots built since 2010 with eight or more apartments each.

That mix matters for cameras because the people who need footage and the people who own the walls are rarely the same. The Department of City Planning's Bushwick plan puts renters at roughly 90 percent of households, and Census Reporter's figures for ZIP 11237 show 88 percent of homes rented and 98 percent of them in multi-unit buildings. The incidents in the sources follow the shape of those buildings. In a Lawton Street building on a July 19 evening, CBS New York reported, a man took packages from the lobby around 7 p.m., and police said the same man stood on a bucket outside a first-floor apartment to reach in through its window. On Eldert Street, shortly before 2 p.m. one September 10, a man looked over packages outside a building, walked away and returned with a black garbage bag. Near Central Avenue and George Street, two people went into a home just after 4 p.m. and left with a laptop, a MacBook, an iPad, a PlayStation and a camera. DNAinfo reported in 2017 that a couple were filmed prying open the door of a vacant apartment at 946 Bushwick Avenue and taking construction gear. In each case, the useful image came from a private camera aimed at a door, a lobby or a stoop.

The long-run trend is far better than it was. Bushwick Daily, reading police figures, reported robbery and burglary down 55.61 and 56.65 percent from their 2000 levels, and the neighborhood's Wikipedia entry records an 80.3 percent drop in crime from 1990 to 2018. But DNAinfo found Bushwick had 441 burglaries in 2015 to that December, the most of any neighborhood in the city at the time, and the commanding officer of the 83rd Precinct pointed to unlocked doors and windows and to row houses and small buildings linked by fire escapes and rooftops. Hoodline reported in July 2026 on a 2025 home invasion in a third-floor apartment near Pilling Street and Evergreen Avenue, where police said entry came through an unlocked kitchen window. None of this ranks one Bushwick block against another, and we have found no source that does. What it shows is where cameras earn their place: the stoop, the street door, the mailboxes, the ground-floor windows, the stairs and the rear of the building, recorded on equipment someone responsible can open the next morning.

Bushwick facts that shape a camera plan

  • Most people rent: about 90 percent of households, per the city's Bushwick plan, and 88 percent in ZIP 11237, per Census Reporter. That makes landlord permission and footage access the first questions on most jobs.
  • The walk-up is the signature building: 1,862 six-family walk-up lots in Community District 4 with 11,032 apartments, usually three stories, a street door, a narrow stair and mailboxes in a tight hall.
  • New construction is concentrated: 307 lots built since 2010 with eight or more apartments, the most of them along Bushwick Avenue, Stanhope Street, Troutman Street, Jefferson Street and Evergreen Avenue.
  • One historic district and several landmarks: the Linden Street Historic District, 32 brick and brownstone row houses from 1885 to 1901, designated in May 2023, plus individually landmarked buildings such as the Ridgewood Masonic Temple and the Ulmer Brewery.
  • The commercial streets run under the el: Broadway under the J and Z and Myrtle Avenue under the M, which the city's plan describes as dark spaces it wants to brighten.
  • Heavy housing enforcement: the city's plan says HPD issued over 50,000 violations in Bushwick, so many owners are already working through a list of repairs when they call about cameras.

What Bushwick owners often add next. Once the stoop, the street door and the stairs are covered, walk-up owners tend to ask about the buzzer panel and the lock at the street door, because a camera that shows who came in works best when the door itself closes and locks. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an intercom upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the entrance, so the plaster in the hall only has to be opened once.

Getting the Footage Out

In a Neighborhood Where Nine in Ten Households Rent, the Clip Is Only Useful If Someone Can Reach It

A tenant in a Bushwick walk-up usually learns how a building's camera system works on the worst possible day. A package disappears, or someone is seen on the stairs who does not live there, and the tenant asks the landlord for the video. Sometimes the landlord can pull it up on a phone in two minutes. Sometimes nobody remembers the password, the recorder sits in a locked cellar room, or the system was set up by a vendor who is the only one who can get clips out. Gothamist reported one New York City case, not one we could tie to Bushwick, in which a landlord could only refer a tenant to the camera vendor to retrieve footage for a fee. By the time anyone gets to it, many recorders have already written over the hour that mattered.

So the most important part of a Bushwick camera job is often not the camera at all. It is deciding, before anything goes on the wall, who owns the system, who can open it, who else gets a view and how a clip leaves the building. For owners: build a system someone else can open

When we set up a building system, the owner holds the main account and the password is written down and handed over in a sealed envelope as well as stored however the owner prefers. If the owner wants a super, a managing agent or a family member to handle clip requests, that person gets their own account, not the owner's, with only the rights they need: live view and playback, for example, but no permission to delete or change settings. Every camera is named in plain words, the recorder's clock is synced to an internet time server, and we walk the owner and anyone they choose through exporting a clip before we leave. That way a tenant's request on a Saturday does not have to wait for a vendor on Monday.

For tenants: what you can do inside your own door

If you rent, the outside of the building, the lobby, the halls and the stairs belong to the landlord, and putting equipment there needs the landlord's permission. Inside your own apartment, a camera aimed at your own rooms and the inside of your own door is generally your business. If package theft or strangers on the stairs are the problem, the most useful thing a tenant can usually do is ask the landlord, in writing, for working cameras at the stoop and the street door, and offer to share the photos and times of what has been happening. We will happily quote that job to the owner, and set up a camera inside your own unit for you in the meantime.

For buildings under PACT management

Wikipedia notes that NYCHA's Bushwick II, Hope Gardens and Palmetto Gardens developments moved to private management under the RAD/PACT program on July 18, 2019, with Pennrose and Pinnacle named in that conversion. Cameras on those buildings and grounds are decided and contracted by the managers there, not by individual residents. If you live in one of them, we can help with equipment inside your own apartment and tell you plainly where something you want is the manager's call rather than yours. Requests for cameras in halls, at entrances or on the grounds are best taken to the site's management office and your tenant association.

The Night Picture

Dark Blocks Under the El, Bright Headlights on Broadway: Getting a Usable Night Picture in Bushwick

Most camera complaints we hear after dark come down to light, not resolution. Bushwick has a particular version of the problem. The city's Bushwick plan talks about brightening the dark spaces under the elevated train along Broadway and Myrtle Avenue, which tells you what a storefront camera faces there: a sidewalk in shadow at midday, then at night a mix of deep shade between the steel columns, streetlights at odd angles, and car headlights sweeping straight into the lens. On the side streets the issue is different. A walk-up stoop may be lit by one bulb over the door, while the sidewalk beyond falls off into darkness. A camera that looks fine on a sunny afternoon can turn faces into silhouettes at either spot.

News 12 Brooklyn's 2026 footage of the shooting near Cooper Street and Broadway was recorded at about 3:30 a.m., and its exclusive video of a car racing along a sidewalk near Broadway and Myrtle Avenue with an NYPD car close behind came from private cameras on those streets. Clips like that show what a well-set camera on a Bushwick block can capture, and also how much depends on the hour. The goal for a home or a store is narrower than a news clip: one person, at your door or your gate, recognizable at 2 a.m.

How we set cameras for Bushwick light

For a storefront under the el, we usually mount the main camera inside the glass or under the awning looking along the sidewalk toward the door, rather than straight out at the street, so that headlights cross the frame instead of shining into it. Wide dynamic range has to be switched on and tuned on site, and we test it after dark, not just at the time of install. For a stoop on a side street, the camera goes beside or slightly above the door, aimed down the steps, so a person climbing them walks toward the lens while the bulb over the door lights their face. If that bulb is weak or burned out, the owner gets a note on the quote, because a camera can only record what light reaches it. Infrared night vision helps in a dark rear yard or alley, but it turns everything black and white, and it can wash out a face that is close to a pale wall, so we lower its strength where walls are close.

Six-family walk-ups: a narrow hall and a long climb

The walk-up is the building we work in most in Bushwick, and its layout decides the plan. The street door usually opens straight into a hall barely wider than the stair, with the mailboxes on one wall and the first apartment door a few steps in. A single camera mounted high on the wall at the back of the hall, looking toward the street door, sees every face coming in against the light and catches the mailboxes along the side of the frame. Each landing gets its own camera looking down the flight below it, set so that it shows the stair and the turn but not the inside of an apartment when a door opens; any door that does fall in frame is masked in the settings. Cable comes up from the cellar, where the runs travel along the joists from front to back, and rises through a stair chase or a closet if one lines up. Where nothing lines up, a slim painted raceway in the corner of the stair carries the cable up to each landing. The recorder never stays in an open cellar where every tenant and contractor has a key; it goes in a locked cabinet, or in the owner's own apartment if the owner lives in the building.

Frame houses: siding is a skin, not a mount

Many Bushwick frame houses have been re-sided more than once, so a camera bracket screwed into the outer layer has nothing solid behind it and cracks the siding within a season. We cut a clean opening, fix a mounting block through the siding into the sheathing and framing, seal it, and mount the camera to the block, with the cable passing through the middle so the hole is hidden and kept dry. The Archives blog's account of how easily the old wooden buildings burned in the 1970s is a reminder to keep every opening small and to firestop anything that passes between floors in a multi-family frame house. Side cameras on frame houses usually look along the alley rather than across it, because the next house is so close.

Linden Street and the landmark buildings: plan the back first

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's May 2023 release describes the Linden Street Historic District as 32 row houses on one block between Broadway and Bushwick Avenue, built between 1885 and 1901 for developer Samuel M. Meeker and designed by architects including Gaylor, Frank Keith Irving, Finksieper and Charles E. Palmer. On those houses, and on individually designated buildings such as the Ridgewood Masonic Temple at 1054 Bushwick Avenue and the Ulmer Brewery complex at Beaver and Belvidere streets, LPC rules apply to anything fixed to the exterior. The Commission's permit guidebook has a chapter on installing security cameras, and its 2018 rule amendments, as proposed, would bar many security fixtures from a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative. So we start with the rear wall, the inside of the vestibule and secondary surfaces, prepare photos and equipment details for an LPC staff application where a street-side camera is still needed, and do not mount anything on the historic front until a permit is in hand.

Mansions on Bushwick and Irving avenues: deep lots and back buildings

The big houses that Community Board 4 dates to 1880 through 1913 sit on deeper lots than the row houses around them, and some have a carriage house or garage at the back. Inside, many have been split into apartments, with entrances that were once secondary doors. We treat each entrance as its own camera job, keep the recorder in the owner's space, and reach any back building with an outdoor-rated cable in buried conduit or a short aerial run between the two structures, never draped across the yard. The brick on these houses is often softer and older than it looks, so mounts go into mortar joints.

Post-2010 buildings and converted lofts: rough-in, rated openings and long runs

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Bushwick Six-Family, From the Sidewalk to the Back of the Cellar

This describes how we work through these buildings, not one particular job. We start on the sidewalk, a few steps from the stoop, because that is where a package thief or a stranger first appears. From there we look at the light over the door, the angle of the steps and whether the ground-floor windows sit within reach of the sidewalk, since the Lawton Street report showed how a bucket can turn a first-floor window into an opening. Then we step inside and stand at the back of the hall, looking toward the street door, to see whether a camera there would catch faces and the mailboxes together. We climb the stairs landing by landing, noting which apartment doors would fall in frame when they open and where each landing camera can sit without being in reach. On the top floor we check where the stair ends and whether the roof door is in view. Back downstairs, we ask the owner who lives where, who already has keys, who handles repairs and who should be able to see which camera, because that decides the accounts. We go down to the cellar and look along the ceiling for the clearest run from front to back, trace any old coax or phone wire left from earlier systems, find where the internet line enters and judge whether the space is dry and secure enough for equipment. If it is not, we look for a closet upstairs. Last, we go out to the rear yard, stand at the back fence, and look back at the building to see where a rear camera covers the back door and cellar steps and where it has to stop short of the neighbors' yards. By the end of the walk we have the camera count, the cable lengths, the recorder location and the list of people who get a login, and the written quote is built from that.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Bushwick: Questions Answered

Stoops, halls and street windows

The man on the Eldert Street video looked over the boxes outside the building, walked off and came back with a garbage bag. Can a camera let us know during that first look, before the boxes are gone?

It can, if the alert is set up for that pattern rather than for every passer-by. Most recorders and many cameras can draw a small zone around the exact step or ledge where packages land and send an alert only when a person enters that zone and stays for more than a few seconds, which separates someone stopping at the boxes from someone walking past. We set the zone after watching how the block's foot traffic moves at the stoop, and we schedule the alert for the hours when nobody is home to take deliveries in. The camera keeps recording continuously regardless, so if the person does come back, the first visit and the second are both on the recording, which is what made the Eldert Street clip useful. An alert cannot stop someone, but it gives a tenant or owner a chance to bring the boxes inside.

Police said the Lawton Street suspect also stood on a bucket to reach through a first-floor window. How does a building's front camera watch the street-level windows without looking into the apartments?

By looking along the front of the building rather than at it. We mount a camera at one end of the facade, or beside the street door, aimed parallel to the wall so it sees the strip of sidewalk and the window sills, and anyone standing at them, from the side. From that angle the lens sees the outside of the glass, not the rooms behind it, and any part of a window that still shows the inside is blacked out with a privacy mask in the camera's settings before we leave. The view is set tight enough that a person at the sill is large in the picture. CBS New York reported that a man in the Lawton Street case took packages from the lobby and reached into a first-floor window; one camera along the facade and one in the hall would have covered both.

Our six-family's street door opens straight onto the stairs, with no vestibule and the mailboxes on the side wall. Where can one camera show a face and the mailboxes together?

Usually high on the wall at the far end of the hall, or on the underside of the first flight of stairs, looking back toward the street door. From there, anyone coming in walks toward the lens, so their face is in the center of the frame, and the mailboxes sit along one side of the same picture. Because there is no inner door, the camera also sees whoever follows a tenant in. We set the exposure for the bright doorway so faces do not turn into silhouettes in the afternoon, and we check it again at night with only the hall light on. If the hall is too short for that angle to work, a small dome on the ceiling just inside the door, looking down and in, is the fallback.

Houses and walls

Our frame house has vinyl siding over the old wood, and a camera the last owner screwed on has already cracked it. What should a camera be anchored to?

To the framing, through the siding, never to the siding alone. Siding is a thin outer skin that moves with heat and cold, so a bracket fixed only to it works loose and splits it. We remove the old mount, patch or replace the damaged piece, and fix a mounting block through the siding into the sheathing and the wood framing behind it, sealed around the edges. The camera then mounts to the block, and its cable passes through the center of the block into the wall, so the hole is hidden behind the camera and stays dry. On houses where the siding covers an older wood layer, we use fasteners long enough to reach solid framing through both.

Landmarks, entry systems and management

Linden Street became Bushwick's first historic district in 2023, and our camera went up a few years before that. Does it have to come down?

The sources we have do not say how LPC treats a camera installed before a district was designated, so we would not guess. What is clear is that from the designation in May 2023 on, new work on the exterior of the 32 Linden Street houses falls under the Commission's rules, including replacing or moving an existing camera. If yours works and you plan to leave it, there is nothing for us to do. If you want to upgrade it, move it or add more, we treat that as new work: we plan around the rear wall and the inside of the entry first and prepare an LPC staff application, with photos and equipment details, for anything that still needs to be on the street side. Asking LPC directly about the existing camera is the surest answer.

Management is putting in a new entry panel at our door that uses a phone or face to unlock and has its own camera. What does city law say they have to tell tenants about that data?

If it is a smart access system in a multiple dwelling, the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, sets out the rules. According to HPD, the owner must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, get express consent before collecting the data the system uses, collect only the minimum needed, and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is made anonymous. The owner may not sell that data, and may not require a tenant to use the smart system to get into their own apartment. That law covers the entry system itself; ordinary hallway cameras on a separate recorder are a different matter. We install and wire the hardware, but questions about whether a building's policy meets the law are for the owner's lawyer.

Our building is one of the developments that moved from NYCHA to private management under PACT in 2019. Who do we ask now about cameras in the halls?

The private manager that runs your building under the PACT conversion. Wikipedia records that Bushwick II, Hope Gardens and Palmetto Gardens converted on July 18, 2019, with Pennrose and Pinnacle named in that change, and from then on decisions about building equipment, cameras included, sit with the site's management rather than with NYCHA's central office. Put the request in writing to the management office, say which halls, doors or grounds you mean and why, and bring it to your tenant association as well. Inside your own apartment, a camera aimed at your own rooms and the inside of your own door is something we can help with directly.

Only the company that installed our building's recorder knows how to get clips off it, and they charge each time. Can the owner take full control of the system?

Usually, yes, as long as the owner can prove the equipment belongs to the building. Many recorders can have their administrator password reset through the manufacturer's own procedure, and some allow it from the unit itself, but a reset can erase settings and, on some models, recordings, so we copy anything important first. Once the owner holds the main account, we create separate accounts for the super or managing agent, rename every camera, sync the clock and teach the owner to export a clip. If the recorder is so locked down or so old that it cannot be taken over sensibly, we say so and quote a replacement recorder that works with the cameras already on the walls, where they are compatible.

Corridors, lofts and walls

The city's Bushwick plan talks about brightening the dark spaces under the el on Broadway and Myrtle. Should our store wait for better street lighting before putting in cameras?

We would not wait. The plan describes a goal, and we have no source on when or where any lighting work might happen. A store can be covered well now by choosing cameras and positions for the light that is actually there: entrance cameras inside the glass or under the awning, aimed along the storefront toward the door, with wide dynamic range tuned after dark, and a low-light color camera at the cellar doors or rear. If the street does get brighter later, those cameras only get better. If your own awning light or sign light is out, fixing it helps more than anything the city might do.

Our converted loft building shares one freight entrance between residents moving in and a ground-floor manufacturer's deliveries. Whose camera should watch the freight door?

The building owner's. The freight entrance is a shared part of the building, so the camera belongs on the owner's system, with views granted to the people who need them: the building manager, and perhaps the manufacturer for its delivery hours. We mount it inside, looking at the opening, so it shows the truck and the load when the door is up and the inside face of the door when it is down, and add a second view of the elevator or corridor beyond if goods move through it. If the manufacturer wants its own records of its deliveries, it can have its own camera inside its space aimed at its own receiving area, on its own recorder.

Our building's side wall carries a commissioned mural. Where can a camera go without cutting into the artwork, and can it help keep the mural from being tagged?

We look for a spot off the painted surface: under the cornice line, on the corner where the mural ends, on the building next door with that owner's written permission, or on the front wall aimed back along the side. From there a camera can cover the full face of the mural and the sidewalk in front of it. Night coverage matters most for tagging, so the view is set for low light, and an alert can be scheduled for late hours when nobody should be standing at the wall. The camera cannot stop anyone from painting, but it can record who did and when. NY1 reported that the Bushwick Collective, founded in 2012, works with building owners who offer their walls, so check with whoever arranged the mural before anything goes near it.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Landlord, Board, Landmarks Commission or Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Bushwick Camera Job

In Bushwick the rules that shape a camera job are mostly about ownership, privacy, landmarks on a handful of blocks, and who is allowed to do the installing. This is how they play out on the buildings here. It is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

Who may install. New York General Business Law §69-m says nobody may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems, or present themselves as able to, without a state license. The state Department of State's application form says installing purely "passive," non-detecting security devices does not need a license, which is why some people argue that a simple camera does not count; most professional camera systems now detect motion and people and send alerts, and we would not rely on that exception. A homeowner putting a camera on their own house is not in the business of installing. A contractor or handyman doing it for pay should hold a license and show you the number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431. Adding a new outlet or light fixture is electrical work for a licensed electrician.

Tenants and shared spaces. In a privately owned building, the facade, the lobby, the halls, the stairs, the cellar and the yard belong to the owner, and a tenant needs the landlord's permission before putting equipment there. Inside their own apartment, a tenant can usually place a camera aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door. In condo buildings, the board and the bylaws decide what goes on common elements. In the developments under PACT management, the site manager decides everything outside the apartment.

Neighbors. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. Bushwick's rear yards are small and sit side by side, often behind walk-ups whose back windows overlook several of them. Every rear camera we install is framed on the owner's own yard, back door and cellar steps, and anything else in the picture is masked out in the settings.

Landmarks. The Linden Street Historic District and Bushwick's individually designated buildings, including the Ridgewood Masonic Temple, the Ulmer Brewery and the old 83rd Precinct station house and stable at DeKalb and Wilson avenues, are under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Its permit guidebook covers security cameras specifically, saying their small, discreet nature, physical needs, legal constraints and widespread use underlie the Commission's rules, and LPC staff can approve installations that meet those criteria. We prepare that application before ordering anything for a landmark exterior.

Smart entry and tenant data. Where a camera is part of a smart access system at a building door, Local Law 63 of 2021 requires a written privacy policy, consent, minimal data collection and deletion of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and forbids making the smart system the only way into a tenant's home, according to HPD.

Public property. Cameras go on your building, your fence or a post inside your own lot, never on a lamppost, an el column, a street tree or a sign pole.

Openings. Every opening through brick, block or siding is sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a walk-up, a mixed-use building or a loft is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

FAQ

Bushwick Security Camera FAQ

Is a Bushwick camera job billed at a different rate from the rest of Brooklyn?
+

No. Bushwick is priced on the same Brooklyn rate we use across the borough, so a setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour then 30-minute blocks here just as it is in any other Brooklyn neighborhood. What changes from one Bushwick building to the next is the labor, because a hall camera above an open cellar takes far less time than a third-floor landing camera fed up the stairs in raceway or a camera on a landmark house that needs an LPC application first. Each of those differences shows up as its own line on the written quote, and estimates are free.

Our six-family is fully occupied. Do all the tenants have to stay home on the day the hall and landing cameras go in?
+

Usually not. Hall, stair, cellar and facade work happens in the building's shared spaces, so we rarely need to enter an apartment at all. If a cable route has to pass through a unit, or a tenant wants a camera inside their own home, we schedule that apartment for a set window agreed with the tenant and the owner in advance. We ask the owner to post a notice in the hall a few days ahead.
