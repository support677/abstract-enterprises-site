The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need First Alert Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley was settled house by house over four centuries, and almost none of those houses expected a camera on the gable. The Hudson River Valley Heritage Area describes Dutch colonists arriving after Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage, followed by enormous land patents granted first under the Dutch and later the English, and by 1750 a network of small local communities that still shapes the map today. The six counties we cover from our Bronx office, Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, carry every layer of that history at once. Stacker's analysis of Census Bureau data found that 28.0 percent of Westchester's homes, about 109,608 of them, were built in 1939 or earlier, with a median build year of 1958. In Dutchess the pre-1940 share was 18.0 percent, in Putnam 17.9 percent and in Rockland 11.5 percent. Those are old walls, old wiring and old routers tucked into old basements, and every one of them shapes where a First Alert camera can go and how its signal gets out.

The oldest walls are stone. Hudson Valley One reports that Ulster County has more Dutch-influenced stone houses than anywhere in the world except the Netherlands, and a Fordham University history paper notes that Hurley holds the oldest surviving group of colonial stone houses in the valley. Kingston's Stockade District sits on the site of the stockade Peter Stuyvesant ordered in 1658, and at Crown and John Streets early Dutch stone houses stand on all four corners. For a First Alert VX3 outdoor camera that matters in a very practical way. Resideo's install guide for the VX3 recommends a drill bit of 7/8 inch or larger for the cable hole, and pushing a hole that size through a stone wall two feet thick is slow, dusty work that must be sealed afterwards against water and frost. Where a hole cannot or should not go through, Resideo offers a back box for the VX3 that, according to Alarm Grid, accepts up to three half-inch conduits and carries an IP66 rating, so the cable can run on the surface in a neat, weatherproof way. Deciding between those two routes is the first conversation on almost every stone-house job.

Then come the brick cities. Newburgh's East End Historic District covers 445 acres and, according to Wikipedia, holds 2,217 buildings, mostly two- and three-story Italianate brick townhouses, along with Washington's Headquarters State Historic Site; counted as resources, its 2,239 entries are the most of any federally recognized district in New York. The city designated the district in 1977, enlarged it and saw it listed on the National Register in 1985, and adopted design guidelines in 2005. Those guidelines speak directly to cameras: security features must not damage historic building fabric, and security or motion-detection equipment should sit on side or rear facades. Kingston has five local historic districts, including the Stockade, Rondout-West Strand, Chestnut Street, Fair Street and Ponckhockie, and its Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work visible from the public right-of-way, including electrical and mechanical equipment. In a January 2025 approval, Kingston's staff recommended that the owner paint all externally mounted lines or line hides to match the facade. A First Alert doorbell or camera on a front facade in either city is exactly the kind of detail that gets noticed, so we plan for it rather than hope.

Out past the cities, the challenge changes from preservation to distance. The USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 620 farms in Dutchess County averaging 161 acres and 486 farms in Ulster averaging 150 acres. On properties that size the gate, the barn, the equipment shed and the house can sit hundreds of feet apart, and Wi-Fi rarely reaches all of them. That collides with how First Alert cameras work. Resideo's data sheet for the VX3 asks for a continuous upload of 2 Mbps or faster, and its install guide says to verify a signal of at least -65 dBm at the camera location before mounting it on Wi-Fi. Broadband itself is not a given everywhere: Ulster County announced a state ConnectALL award in September 2025 to bring fiber to nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities in remote parts of the county, and Hudson Valley One quoted a county official saying that in most of Ulster the available provider is Spectrum. A camera that is perfect on paper will drop clips all winter if it sits on the edge of the signal, which is why we measure first and choose between Wi-Fi and a PoE cable second.

Weather is the next test. In Earth Networks' 2020 lightning report, Orange County ranked first in New York with 275,059 lightning pulses, with Ulster at 182,760, Westchester at 168,634 and Dutchess at 166,641. The Times Union reported that thunderstorms on June 27, 2024 knocked out power to a combined 60,000 Orange and Rockland and Central Hudson customers, with winds of about 60 mph, and the Daily Freeman reported nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers without power after storms on July 5, 2026. First Alert cameras are cloud cameras: they save clips to the Total Connect service, and any cloud camera cannot save new footage once the internet connection drops, even if the power stays on. The VX3's data sheet lists an operating range down to -40°C, so January cold is not the weak point. The weak points are the router in the basement, the PoE switch with no battery backup, and the cable that leaves the house and invites a surge back in. A valley install has to think about all three.

The reasons people call are real and recent. In January 2025 the New York State Police issued an alert after a string of targeted residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties. Scarsdale police warned that burglary crews had been placing trail cameras and other hidden surveillance devices outside homes for days before breaking in. In July 2026, News 12 Westchester reported that several Port Chester neighbors shared Ring footage of car break-ins around 4 a.m. on and near Tower Hill Drive. In May 2026, News 12 Hudson Valley reported overnight smash-and-grab burglaries at businesses in Orange County, with Warwick police confirming three incidents and a store owner sharing surveillance video. Porch theft shows up in the record too: Hudson Valley Post published a reader's account of a thief working a house on Fitchett Avenue and then a porch on De Laval Place in Poughkeepsie an hour later, both caught by doorbell cameras; WPDH reported a doorbell camera recording a package theft on Carter Avenue in Newburgh; and Hudson Valley One covered Kingston police warning about porch thefts after a package was taken from a McEntee Street porch. News 12 reported in June 2026 that police data showed package thefts trending down across Westchester, but the same story featured a Yonkers resident who caught a theft on camera on Yonkers Avenue. None of those stories involved First Alert cameras as far as we know, and we do not claim any of those homeowners as customers. They show the job a camera is expected to do here.

The valley also has a strong opinion about who does the watching. The Times Union reported that Dutchess County is moving to shut down and remove all 65 of its county-operated Flock cameras after a packed public meeting in Pleasant Valley. Pine Plains had contracted with Flock Safety for 11 cameras and license plate readers before the plan was scuttled amid backlash. Hudson Valley One reported in September 2026 that Kingston police defended the city's camera network while residents raised privacy concerns, noting 71 conventional surveillance cameras in addition to plate readers. On October 23, 2026, Mid-Hudson News reported that Ulster County Executive Jen Metzger signed an executive order barring county departments from buying or deploying Flock cameras and similar AI-assisted surveillance tools. That tells us something about how private cameras should be set up here: aimed at your own property and driveway rather than the road and the neighbor's yard, with indoor cameras that can close their eyes. The First Alert VX5 indoor camera has an automatic privacy shutter, and every VX camera lets detection zones stop at the property line. We set both deliberately, not as an afterthought.

One more change has filled our phone lines with First Alert questions: the retirement of the older cameras. Alarm Grid reported that first-generation Total Connect 2.0 cameras would stop working after July 31, 2025, be removed from all accounts, and lose their recorded clips. Resideo's own end-of-life notice recommends the new First Alert VX Series cameras as replacements. Plenty of valley homes and businesses had Honeywell alarm systems installed years ago with a camera or two added along the way, and many of those owners discovered the shutdown only when the camera tile in the app went blank. Replacing those cameras is often the simplest First Alert job we do, because the Total Connect account, the panel and much of the wiring are already there.

We want to be clear about what our research did not turn up. We found no Reddit threads in r/hudsonvalley about First Alert cameras or installers, no Yelp reviews mentioning First Alert installs in the region, and no published count of how many valley homes use First Alert or Honeywell panels. Where this page describes walls, signal or weather, it relies on the public sources named above and on what we find in buildings of these types generally, not on a survey of valley homes. The only reliable way to know your property is to look at it, and that is where every job starts.

What valley customers often add once the cameras are up. A camera on a barn or a detached garage usually exposes a weak home network, and the next request is a proper cable to the outbuilding or a wired access point so phones, smart thermostats and the cameras stop fighting for one tired router. Yelp's own "people also searched for" list for camera installation in Poughkeepsie points the same way, naming home network installation. If your Wi-Fi is already a sore point, say so when booking, because running one cable for the camera and the network together costs less than two separate visits.

Westchester to Ulster: How Each County Changes a First Alert Camera Job

The Hudson Valley is one region on a map and six very different jobs on a ladder. A First Alert camera on a 1950s colonial in Rockland, a brick townhouse in Newburgh and a stone house outside Kingston uses the same Resideo hardware and the same Total Connect account, but almost everything around it changes: the wall, the route for the cable, the distance to the router, the weather exposure and the rules. The table below sets out what the public record says about each county and what that usually means for the install. Westchester: old suburbs, finished basements and doorbells

Westchester's housing is older than most people assume. With more than a quarter of its homes built before 1940 according to Stacker's census analysis, a typical call is a colonial or Tudor-style house with a finished basement, a router hidden in a closet or utility room, and a front door the owner wants to watch. In those houses the First Alert VX1 doorbell and a VX3 on the garage corner are the usual pair. The router's position decides a lot: a router two floors below the front door in a house with plaster walls can leave the doorbell at the edge of the -65 dBm signal Resideo asks for, so we test at the door and suggest moving or adding an access point before blaming the camera. Ida taught Westchester a hard lesson about basements as well. ABC7 reported in September 2021 that some Mamaroneck neighborhoods saw as much as 14 feet of water, with many residents reporting basements filling to the first floor. If your router and any PoE switch live on a basement floor that has flooded before, we will suggest putting the network equipment higher, because a camera with no network is just a lens on a wall.

Rockland: newer frame houses and roads that curve

Rockland's housing is younger, with a median build year of 1970 in Stacker's numbers, so we see more frame houses with vinyl or wood siding, standard drywall and accessible attics. Those are friendly walls for a VX3: a cable can often drop through the soffit or an attic space and come out behind the camera with no surface wiring at all. The harder part in Rockland is often the view. News 12 reported a doorbell camera in Stony Point capturing a crash on a stretch neighbors call very curvy, and another Stony Point doorbell recording a bike being walked off a porch just after midnight. Curving roads, cul-de-sacs and sloped front lawns mean a camera aimed carelessly will spend all night recording headlights. We aim at the walk, the porch and the driveway apron, and set detection zones so a car passing on the road does not wake your phone.

Orange: brick rowhouses, warehouses and lightning

Orange County holds two very different camera jobs. In Newburgh's East End, along streets the city's tourism page names as Grand, Montgomery and Liberty north of Broadway, the work is on brick townhouses where the design guidelines send security equipment to side and rear facades and forbid damage to historic fabric. Out along Route 17K in the Town of Montgomery, where the Hudson Gateway Association of Realtors reported Amazon and Medline distribution buildings of about one million square feet each beginning in 2020, the work is long PoE cable runs and high mounts. Orange also led New York for lightning pulses in Earth Networks' 2020 report. Any First Alert camera cable that runs from a house to a detached garage, or from a warehouse wall to a pole in the lot, is a path for a surge to come back to the switch. We protect those runs and keep outdoor cable away from the building's own lightning and electrical grounds as the codes require.

Putnam: lakes, hills and second homes

Putnam's homes sit on hills and around water. A listing site puts Lake Mahopac at about 587 acres, ringed by waterfront houses, some with boathouses below the main house. Those properties ask a lot of a single router: the dock, the boathouse and the driveway can all be out of reach. Putnam also appeared in the State Police's 2025 burglary alert along with Dutchess and Westchester, which brings a lot of owners to think about remote viewing. Many Putnam houses are second homes or seasonal places, and a First Alert camera that the owner checks from the city has to keep working through a week nobody is home. That means a battery backup for the router and PoE switch, cameras on wired connections where possible, and alerts tuned so the owner hears about a person on the porch and not every gust that moves a tree branch. Work near reservoirs and streams also falls under New York City's watershed regulations in parts of Putnam, so any trench for a buried cable is planned with that in mind.

Dutchess: farms, porch thefts and privacy

Dutchess combines city porches and very large farms. In the City of Poughkeepsie, Mid-Hudson News reported in December 2024 that detectives recovered numerous stolen items after searching a house on South Hamilton Street during a porch-theft investigation, and Hudson Valley Post's reader account placed doorbell cameras at the center of catching a thief on Fitchett Avenue and De Laval Place. Out in the towns, Patch reported State Police in Poughkeepsie asking residents with home cameras to check their footage after car break-ins on roads including Hoofprint Road in Union Vale and Fountain Place and Weatherford Lane in Millbrook. That is the Dutchess pattern in a sentence: a camera is often more useful for what it records on the driveway at 3 a.m. than for live viewing. On a farm averaging 161 acres, getting a clear picture of the gate and the barn door usually means CX4 PoE cameras on network cable rather than VX3s on stretched Wi-Fi. And because Dutchess has just pulled down 65 county Flock cameras after public pushback, we pay extra attention to aiming private cameras at the property, not the road.

Ulster: stone walls and thin broadband

Ulster is where First Alert installs take the most planning. The stone houses in Hurley, Kingston and the river towns have walls that resist a 7/8-inch bit, interiors without cavities to hide cable, and in Kingston a commission that reviews equipment visible from the street. The county's broadband is improving through the ConnectALL fiber program, but on the roads that are still waiting, upload speed rather than download speed is what decides whether a Wi-Fi camera can save its clips. Large buildings are part of the county too: Spectrum News reported in February 2026 that the former IBM Kingston campus, now iPark 87, sits on 200 acres with more than one million square feet of buildings along Enterprise Drive, with the East Campus hosting the Ulster BOCES Career and Technical Center and Archtop Fiber. In Ulster, we test the internet connection before we quote a wireless camera, and we would rather recommend a wired CX4 than leave you with a VX3 that drops clips.

Run the speed test where the camera will hang

A speed test at the router tells you what the internet company delivers. A speed test at the porch, on the barn's gable end or at the corner of the garage tells you what the camera will actually get. Stand where the camera will go, with your phone on Wi-Fi rather than cellular data, and run the test twice. Look at the upload number, not the download, because a camera sends video out. If the upload at that spot is under 2 Mbps, or the test stalls, we will plan a cable or a wired access point rather than a wireless camera. In older houses with plaster or stone walls, the drop from the router to the porch is often dramatic, and owners are usually surprised.

Find the account before we arrive

If your house already has a Honeywell, Resideo or First Alert alarm, you probably already have a Total Connect account through the company that installed the panel, and you may already pay for a video plan that covered your old cameras. Find the login and the name of that company before the visit. Resideo's guidance for the VX3 recommends finishing the Total Connect setup before mounting a camera in a hard-to-reach spot, so we want the account sorted on the ground, not discovered on a ladder against a barn wall. If you have no account at all, tell us, and we will explain at the quote how a new one is set up for your cameras.

Ask before you drill into a historic facade

If your house is in the Kingston Stockade or another Kingston district, or in Newburgh's East End, ask yourself one question before you book: can the camera spot be seen from the street? Kingston's HLPC page says exterior work visible from the public right-of-way is reviewed before a building permit, while changes not visible from the street are handled at staff level. Newburgh's guidelines prefer side and rear facades for security equipment. If the answer is yes, send us the address and a photo, and we can suggest positions that avoid review or help you describe the camera to the commission.

On stone walls. A stone house wall is not a uniform material. It is a mix of stones of different hardness held together with mortar that may be centuries old in places and modern repointing in others. A 7/8-inch hole of the size Resideo recommends for the VX3 cable, drilled straight through, can wander when the bit meets a hard stone, crack a soft one, or open a void inside the wall where water will later collect and freeze. We drill test holes first, prefer to pass the cable through a mortar joint that has been repointed and can be repaired, sleeve the hole so the cable is not chafed by stone, and seal both faces. Very often, though, the better answer on a stone house is not to go through the wall at all. The VX3 back box lets the camera sit on the surface with its cable in conduit running along a corner, under an eave or down to a basement window frame where entry is easy. Painted to match, as Kingston's staff asked of one owner in 2025, that conduit disappears into the shadows of the wall.

On barns and outbuildings. A camera on a barn is really a cable problem. The camera itself bolts easily to a gable board or a post. The work is getting power and network to it in a way that survives winter, rodents and lightning. Overhead runs between buildings sag and ice up; buried runs need outdoor or direct-burial cable, a trench below where the frost and the mower can reach, and slack so freeze and thaw do not pull the connectors apart. Inside the barn, cable stays in conduit or high on the framing, away from animals and machinery. At each building entry we add surge protection, because in a county with Orange's or Ulster's lightning counts a cable between two buildings is an invitation for a strike to reach the switch in the house. Doing this once, properly, is cheaper than replacing a scorched switch and three cameras after the next storm.

What a business camera has to get right

A home camera mostly has to see a door. A business camera has to identify a face at the register, read a plate at the lot entrance in the dark, and keep doing it through a winter of salt spray from the road. For a shop on a village Main Street that usually means a VX5 or CX4 dome inside covering the counter and the door, and a CX4 bullet or turret outside, mounted high enough that it cannot be knocked aside by a passer-by. For a warehouse off Route 17K or an older industrial building in Ulster it means cameras mounted high on long PoE runs, cable in conduit away from forklifts, and a plan for how many cameras the Total Connect video plans will carry, because each plan package covers up to four VX cameras. We plan business jobs camera by camera, with the owner on site, and the written quote lists each camera, each cable run and each plan before anything is ordered.

Rules every valley business camera has to follow

  • Licensed installers only. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems unless licensed, and a Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum explainer notes that closed-circuit cameras used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement fall under it. We work under NYS license #12000287431. State rules at 19 NYCRR 195.2 exempt an owner installing a system bought for their own property with their own employees.
  • Tell employees about monitoring. Under Civil Rights Law §52-c, as Outten & Golden explains it, employers that monitor electronically must give notice and post it in a conspicuous place visible to employees. We point it out when we hand over a business system.
  • No cameras in restrooms or locker rooms. Labor Law §203-c, as RecordingLaw summarizes it, prohibits video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and similar areas regardless of any posted policy. We will not install a camera there and will move one that is aimed through a door into one.
  • Audio is a separate decision. The Reporters Committee's guide notes that New York makes it a felony to record a conversation without the consent of at least one party. In a shop, a camera that hears customers talking at the counter is risky; we leave audio off unless the owner has a lawful reason and posted notice.
  • Historic storefronts. In Kingston's districts and Newburgh's East End, a camera visible on a storefront may need commission review. Side and rear facades and inside-the-glass placements avoid most of it.
  • Exits and door hardware. Cameras and their cable never block an exit sign, a path of travel or a door's closer and panic hardware. We check sight lines and door swing before fixing a bracket.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Farmhouse Before the First Camera Goes Up

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's property unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow on the kind of property the valley is full of: a farmhouse with a detached garage, a barn across the yard, a gravel driveway that bends out of sight, and an owner who wants to know who comes and goes.

We start indoors, at the router and the alarm panel, and ask for the Total Connect login. We note the panel model and its version. Then we walk the property with a phone running a signal meter and a speed test, stopping at every spot where a camera might go: the front porch, the kitchen door, the garage corner, the barn gable, the bend in the drive. We write the signal and the upload number down at each stop. We look at what each wall is made of and where the nearest outlet is, remembering the VX3's transformer cord stops at ten feet. We pace the distance from the house to the barn. We look up for overhead lines and down for where a trench could go. Then we sit with the owner and draw the plan: which cameras run on Wi-Fi because the numbers are good, which need a cable because they are not, and where the PoE switch and its battery backup will live. Only after the owner agrees to that drawing does anything get enrolled, mounted or drilled.

Your Questions Answered

First Alert Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Accounts, plans and the old cameras

Where do First Alert camera recordings go, and how long does the account hold them?

First Alert VX cameras save clips to the Total Connect service rather than to a recorder in your house, and Resideo requires a monthly storage plan and Total Connect service for them to work. According to Advanced Security's Total Connect video FAQs, the 7-day plan stores up to 350 clips and the 30-day plan stores up to 1,500 clips. For a valley driveway that sees a few cars a day, the 7-day plan can be enough; for a business lot or a farm gate that sees trucks all day, or a second home you only check on weekends, the longer plan makes more sense. We help you pick the plan based on how busy each camera view really is, and we tune detection so the clip count is spent on people and vehicles, not on trees moving in the wind.

Our Honeywell cameras from years ago disappeared from the Total Connect app. Do we have to start over?

Not usually. Alarm Grid reported that first-generation Total Connect 2.0 cameras stopped working after July 31, 2025, were removed from accounts, and lost their recorded clips, and Resideo's end-of-life notice points owners to the First Alert VX Series as the replacement. In most valley homes the account, the alarm panel and often the cable and mounting spots are still good. We remove the old cameras, test any existing cable, install VX models in the same or better positions, and enroll them on your existing account. The old clips cannot be recovered, so if you need footage of an old incident, there is nothing we can retrieve now.

We want six cameras around the property. Does one video plan cover all of them?

Alarm Grid's product page says each video plan allows up to four VX-Series cameras per package, so a property with six cameras needs more than one package. That is worth knowing before you decide how many cameras to buy, because the plan cost is ongoing and the camera cost is not. Often a valley property with a long list starts with four well-placed cameras covering the doors, the driveway and the garage, then adds a second group later for the barn and the gate. We map the positions first and show you which four cover the most ground, so the decision about a second plan is yours and not a surprise.

Signal, distance and power

Our farmhouse Wi-Fi barely reaches the front porch. Can a VX3 still work out there?

It depends on the numbers at that exact spot. Resideo's install guide asks for a Wi-Fi signal of at least -65 dBm where the camera is mounted, and the data sheet asks for a continuous upload of 2 Mbps or better. If your phone shows one bar at the porch and the speed test stalls, the VX3 will drop clips or go offline. The VX3 also supports a wired Ethernet connection and PoE power, which removes the Wi-Fi problem entirely. In thick-walled farmhouses we often run one network cable from the router to the porch camera, or add a wired access point that improves the signal for the whole front of the house. We test first and tell you which answer your porch needs.

The gate is at the end of a long gravel driveway. How far can a camera cable reach?

A standard Ethernet run, which carries both the video and the PoE power for a CX4 or wired VX3, is good for about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from the switch to the camera. Many valley driveways are longer than that. For a distant gate we usually put a small weatherproof enclosure with a switch partway along, powered from an outbuilding or a post with power, or bring the cable from the nearest building rather than the house. On farms the barn is often closer to the gate than the house is. We pace the route with you, plan the cable path and any trench, and include surge protection where the cable enters each building.

If the power stays on but the internet drops, do the cameras keep recording?

No. First Alert VX cameras are cloud cameras, and as a general rule a cloud camera cannot save new footage without an internet connection, even when it has power. That matters on valley roads where the internet line is a single cable on poles that come down in a storm. The practical steps are to put the router, the modem and any PoE switch on a battery backup so a brief power blip does not take the internet down with it, and to ask your provider about a backup connection if footage during outages matters to you. We will not tell you a cloud camera records through an internet outage, because it does not.

Stone, brick and historic districts

Our house is in the Kingston Stockade. What has to happen before a camera goes on the front?

Kingston's HLPC page says exterior work on historic properties visible from the public right-of-way is reviewed by the commission before a building permit is issued, and that review covers electrical and mechanical equipment visible from the street that does not comply with city code. Exterior changes not visible from the street are handled at the desk level. In practice that means a camera on a rear or side wall that cannot be seen from the street is usually the easier route, while a doorbell or camera on the front facade may need the commission's look first. We help you choose positions, describe the hardware for an application if one is needed, and paint any surface line hides to match the facade, as Kingston staff recommended in a 2025 approval.

Our Newburgh rowhouse is in the East End. Where can the cameras go without breaking the guidelines?

Newburgh's East End design guidelines say security features must not damage historic building fabric and that security or motion-detection equipment should be located on side or rear facades, with the Architectural Review Commission issuing certificates of appropriateness for exterior changes. For a rowhouse with no side wall, that usually means cameras at the rear, covering the yard, the back door and any alley, with the front door handled by a doorbell or an indoor VX5 watching through the glass. Fixings go into the brick face rather than the mortar joints, and holes are made where they can be repaired cleanly. If a front camera is truly needed, we suggest the smallest, best-hidden position and the paperwork to ask the commission.

How big a hole does a VX3 need, and what if our wall is two feet of stone?

Resideo's VX3 install guide recommends a drill bit of 7/8 inch or larger for the cable entry. In wood siding that is quick. In a thick stone or brick wall it is a slow masonry job, and a hole that size through old stone can crack a soft stone or open a void that later collects water. For those walls we often skip the hole entirely and use the VX3's optional back box, which Alarm Grid describes as IP66 rated and able to take up to three half-inch conduits, running the cable on the surface to a point where entry is easy, such as a basement window frame or a wooden eave. Where a through-hole is the right answer, we sleeve it and seal both faces.

Privacy, audio and the law

Can an indoor First Alert camera stop watching when we are home?

Yes. Resideo's VX Series page lists an automatic privacy shutter on the VX5 indoor camera, which physically covers the lens, and the VX5 can tell people from pets, so a dog walking past does not trigger the same alert as a stranger. We set the shutter to close when the alarm is disarmed or on a schedule you choose, and we place indoor cameras to watch entry doors, stairs and hallways rather than bedrooms or bathrooms. For a valley rental or a home with caregivers or guests, that combination keeps the camera useful when the house is empty and out of the way when it is not.

Is it legal for our doorbell camera to record what visitors say on the porch?

Video and audio follow different rules in New York. The Reporters Committee's recording guide explains that New York makes it a felony to record a conversation you are not part of without the consent of at least one party. A doorbell recording a visitor speaking to you is one thing; a camera that silently picks up two neighbors talking on the sidewalk is another, and nobody has clearly settled where passive audio pickup falls. That uncertainty is why we usually leave audio recording off on outdoor cameras aimed beyond your door, keep two-way talk on the doorbell, and explain the settings at handover so you decide with the facts. This is general information, not legal advice.

Does whoever installs our First Alert cameras need a state license?

Yes, if they do it as a business. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license, and the Department of State's licensing page sets education and exam requirements, with a two-year renewal cycle according to a licensing guide. A legal explainer from Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum notes that camera systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement fall under that rule, and First Alert cameras tied to a Honeywell or Resideo alarm panel are clearly security systems. We work under NYS license #12000287431. A property owner installing their own system with their own employees on their own property is exempt under state rules.

FAQ

Hudson Valley First Alert Camera Installation FAQ

Going by local reviews, what should we look for in a Hudson Valley camera installer?
+

The praise in Poughkeepsie-area Yelp listings comes back to the same thing: one reviewer wrote that the installation was clean, the wiring neatly concealed and the system worked flawlessly. The complaints point the other way, including a reviewer who said an installer failed to connect a sensor and simply tucked it into the wall. Ask any installer how they will hide the cable on your walls, how they will test each camera before leaving, and what they leave you in writing about what was installed and where.

Can you fix our home network at the same visit as the cameras?
+

Yes. In many valley houses the camera install shows that the Wi-Fi never reached the porch, the garage or the barn in the first place. We can run network cable to an outbuilding, add a wired access point or move equipment to a better spot during the same visit, which costs less than two separate trips. Tell us about any Wi-Fi trouble when you book so we bring the right cable and parts.

Why pay an installer for First Alert cameras rather than putting them up ourselves?
+

First Alert's VX line is sold as a professionally installed product. The cameras have to be enrolled on a Total Connect 2.0 account with a video plan, tested against signal and upload minimums at the mount, and fixed to walls that in the valley are often stone or brick. If they tie into an alarm panel, the work is security-system work that New York licenses. You can still do parts yourself; many owners just want the wall, the cable and the account done once and done right.

Is the First Alert decoy camera sold online the same as the ones you install?
+

No. The First Alert store sells a DC-1 decoy that looks like a camera but records nothing. The cameras we install are Resideo's working VX and CX4 models, which record clips to a Total Connect account. A decoy can sit alongside real cameras as a deterrent, but it will never show you who came up the driveway.

We already use the Onelink app for our First Alert smoke alarms. Will the cameras show up there?
+

No. First Alert support describes Onelink Home as the app for Onelink smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and the Environment Monitor. VX cameras run in Total Connect, so you will use two apps: Onelink for those alarms and Total Connect for the cameras and any Honeywell or Resideo alarm panel.

Should our cameras and router have battery backup for storm outages?
+

In most of the valley, yes. Storms in June 2024 and July 2026 each left tens of thousands of Central Hudson and Orange and Rockland customers without power. A battery backup on the modem, router and any PoE switch keeps cameras online through short outages and brief flickers. It will not carry them through a multi-day outage, which needs a generator, and the internet line itself has to stay up for clips to save.

Do you really come out to every Hudson Valley county from the Bronx?
+

Yes. We cover Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster from our office at 460 E Fordham Rd, heading up the I-87, Taconic and I-84 corridors. Each county has its own First Alert price list, and the quote uses the list for your address. Distant jobs are planned so the first visit can finish the work, which is why we ask for photos and measurements first.

Is a First Alert setup worth it on a weekend house or rental we rarely visit?
+

It is one of the better uses for it, because you see the property through the Total Connect app from wherever you are. The setup has to suit an empty house: wired cameras where the signal is weak, battery backup on the network, a longer clip plan if you check in only on weekends, and alerts tuned to people rather than wind. For rentals, indoor cameras should be limited and disclosed to guests.

Can First Alert cameras be installed in the middle of a valley winter?
+

Yes. The VX3's data sheet lists operation down to minus 40 Celsius, and most of the work is indoors or on a ladder for short spells. Winter does change two things: sealants need the right conditions to cure, and buried cable runs wait for ground that can be dug. We plan around both and tell you if a trench is better left until the thaw.

What should we have ready on the day the technician arrives?
+

Your Total Connect login or the name of your alarm company, your Wi-Fi network name and password, access to the router and any alarm panel, and clear paths to each camera spot, including garage and barn doors unlocked. If the property is in a Kingston or Newburgh historic district, have any approval letter handy. Pets kept away from ladders help everyone.

Will deer and wildlife set off camera alerts all night on our wooded lot?
+

They can if the cameras are set up carelessly. We aim outdoor cameras at the drive, the doors and the paths rather than the tree line, draw detection zones that stop short of the woods, and use the cameras' smart detection to favor people and vehicles. Indoors, the VX5 tells people from pets. You will still see the occasional deer, but not a phone full of them every night.

Can our old wired cameras' cable be reused for new First Alert cameras?
+

Often, if it is network cable in good condition. Existing Cat5e or Cat6 that tests cleanly can carry a PoE CX4 or a wired VX3 and saves drilling new holes through old walls. Old coaxial cable from analog systems is a different matter and is usually replaced. We test every existing run before deciding and list reused and new runs separately on the quote.

How quickly will someone get back to us after we send a request?
+

Requests are booked within 2 hours during business hours, and estimates can be same-day. The install date then depends on the cameras, the account and, in historic districts, any approval needed. We do not promise same-day installation, because rushing a stone wall or a barn cable run is how jobs go wrong.
