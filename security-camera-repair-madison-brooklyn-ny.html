Six kinds of property make up nearly all the camera repair calls we take from Madison. Each one fails in its own way, and each decides where the recorder ends up, how cables reach the cameras, and who has to agree before the work begins.

Detached brick and stucco houses

Free-standing houses with a driveway on one side, a narrow passage on the other, a yard behind and often a garage at the back of the lot. Four sides to watch, and long runs to the far corners and the garage.

Two-family brick houses

An owner on one floor, a tenant or relatives on the other, a shared front door or porch and a basement entrance. Camera power and internet often come from whichever apartment was easiest when the system went in.

Limestone row houses

Attached houses with a stoop or small porch, a front window bay and party walls on both sides. Only the front and the back can be watched, and cable routes are limited to the facade, the cellar and the window frames.

Two-story brick and modest single-family homes

Smaller houses on the inner blocks, often with one or two cameras added by the family over time, Wi-Fi cameras inside windows and a recorder on a bookshelf or in a bedroom.

Elevator buildings on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue

Larger prewar and postwar apartment houses and co-ops with lobbies, mailrooms, elevators, laundry rooms, basement corridors and sometimes a garage, and a recorder in the super's office.

Low apartment houses and mixed-use buildings

Walk-ups on the inner blocks and two- or three-story buildings on the avenues with a shop below and apartments above, where the store's cameras and the building's cameras may share a cellar.

Detached houses: four corners, a dark side alley and a garage at the back

The free-standing brick and stucco houses on Madison's inner blocks are where most of our residential camera calls come from, and the camera layout on them is nearly always the same: one camera over the front door or porch, one on the driveway side looking back toward the garage or the gate, one in the narrow passage on the other side, and one or two at the back covering the yard and the rear door. The recorder sits in the basement or a closet. The front and back cameras usually work well enough. It is the sides and the rear garage that fail.

The narrow side passage between two detached houses is the hardest place to put a camera on the whole property. The neighbor's wall is three or four feet away, so the camera's infrared lights bounce straight back off it at night and the picture turns white, or the camera dims its infrared to compensate and the far end of the passage goes black. In daylight the passage is in shade while the street at its end is bright, so a person walking in from the sidewalk is a dark silhouette. Most of this is fixed in settings: lowering infrared power, switching to a smart infrared mode that dims only the near part, adjusting exposure toward the shadow, or aiming the camera along the passage rather than across it. Where the passage has no light at all, a small motion light does more than any camera upgrade.

The garage at the back is the other weak point. When it has a camera, the cable has to cross the yard or the driveway, and installers did that in three ways: through the air from the house, along the fence, or in a trench under the driveway. Aerial runs get pulled down by branches and wind; fence runs get cut by landscapers and fence repairs; and buried runs get cut when the driveway is dug up and repaved, sometimes years later, by a crew who never knew the cable was there. A repair starts by testing the run end to end from the recorder, then fixing it on the most protected route available. Where no new route is practical, a wireless bridge between the house and the garage can carry a wired camera's signal across the yard.

Two-family houses: whose outlet, whose router, whose phone

Madison's two-family brick houses often have one camera system for the whole house, put in by the owner or the owner's family, but powered and connected wherever it was convenient. We regularly find the recorder plugged into an outlet on the downstairs tenant's electric meter, the cameras connected through the tenant's internet router, or a power supply in the shared basement on a circuit that belongs to one apartment. The system then works until that apartment's breaker trips, its router is replaced by the internet company, or the tenant moves out and the service is cancelled, and the whole house goes dark with it.

Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue elevator buildings: one recorder, many hands

The bigger apartment buildings on Madison's edges have camera systems that grew in layers. A first set of coax cameras went into the lobby, the elevator and the basement; later a network camera was added at the side door, a Wi-Fi camera in the laundry room, and in the last few years, as buildings turned a storage room or part of the lobby into a package room, a new room with no camera at all. The recorder sits in the super's office, and the person who knew which tile is which camera may have retired. Typical faults are drives full and overwriting within days, cameras that were never connected to anything, a PoE switch with a dead port, and a lobby camera that shares a power supply or a circuit with the intercom, so that both fail on the same night.

The repair starts with an inventory: every camera, whether it records, where it is powered, and how many days the drive really keeps. We then fix what is broken and move what is badly placed. Package rooms are the current gap in many buildings, and the r/AskNYC tenant whose super "pulled the video of someone breaking into the lobby and stealing packages" describes why boards care. Often an existing camera that watches a low-value spot can be moved to cover the package room door and shelves instead of buying a new one. Board and managing-agent decisions, such as how long to keep video and who may export it, should be written down; Brick Underground has noted that landlords generally are not required to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and that city buildings are not required to have working cameras at all.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Repairing a Parent's Camera System in a Madison House

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical Madison house where an older owner lives with the cameras and a family member manages them from elsewhere. It is our working order, not a story about one address.

Call the person who watches, from the house. Before touching anything, the technician phones whoever manages the system, often an adult son or daughter, so they can say which camera worried them and when, and so the person at home is not left to explain the app.

Save before diagnosing. If anyone needs a clip from a recent day, it is exported first, before any restart, and the recorder's clock is noted against the real time.

Find where the recorder actually lives, and why it gets switched off. In a parent's house the recorder is often in a bedroom or a den, where its fan, its blue light or its beeping bothers someone at night. If it keeps being turned off, the fix is to move it, quiet it or cover its lights, not to tell the owner to leave it alone.

Make the screen in the house foolproof. The monitor or television the parent watches is set to come back to the camera view by itself after a power blink, with nothing to press, and the cameras that matter most are given the largest tiles.

Price before parts, and leave two records. The cause and the written price come before any part is replaced. At the end, the person at home gets a one-page note of what to do if the screen changes, and the family member gets the logins, a list of cameras and the alerts set up on their own phone.

Fault by Fault

Remote Owners, Side Alleys, Cut Cables and Edge Lighting: Why Madison Camera Systems Fail

Four causes account for many of the camera failures we find on Madison houses, buildings and storefronts. Knowing which one you have is the difference between a short visit and a new system.

Remote owners: nobody in the house can tell what is wrong

When the person who understands the system lives somewhere else, small faults go unfixed for a long time. A recorder shows a setup wizard after a power cut, and the parent closes it and sees a blank screen. An app update logs everyone out, and only one sibling knows the password. A drive fails, the recorder keeps showing live pictures, and nobody notices that playback is empty. None of that is a broken camera. The repair is to make failures visible to the right person: alerts for video loss and drive errors sent to the family member's phone, a recorder set to return to live view on its own, and a short written note in the house.

Side alleys: infrared, a near wall and a bright street

The passage between two detached Madison houses is narrow, walled in and dark, with a bright sidewalk at one end. A standard infrared camera there sees its own light reflected off the neighbor's wall and washes out, or darkens the whole frame trying to cope. The fixes are settings first: smart infrared, lower infrared power, exposure weighted to the passage, wide dynamic range for the bright end, and an aim along the length of the alley. A small light switched by motion or a dusk sensor often solves what no camera setting can.

Cut cables: windows, roofs, fences and driveways

Madison houses are a century old, and most are on their second or third set of windows, roofs and driveways. Every one of those jobs is a chance to cut a camera cable without anyone noticing until the picture is gone: window installers through the old frame, roofers under the eave, fence crews along the property line, paving crews under the driveway to the garage. When a camera dies within weeks of a home improvement job, we start our search wherever that crew worked. The repair is a new run on a route that does not depend on the part that gets replaced, and labels at both ends so the next crew knows it is there.

Edge lighting: new streetlamps, bus stops and storefront glass

On Kings Highway, Avenue U, Ocean Avenue and Nostrand Avenue, cameras face changing light: brighter streetlamps like the fixtures the Kings Highway BID added near Sgt. Joyce Kilmer Square, headlights from buses pulling into stops, and the glass of a shop's own door and window. A camera that was well set for the street two years ago can be washed out, glared or reflecting the store's interior today. These are repaired with exposure and backlight settings, a hood, a small change of angle, or by moving the camera out from behind the glass.

What a shop on the edge of Madison needs from its sidewalk camera

The incidents that made the news on Madison's borders happened outside: on the sidewalk at East 21st Street and Avenue U, and near the corner of Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue on a weekday morning. When something like that happens near a store, the owner is often asked whether their outside camera saw it, and the answer depends less on the camera's resolution than on its aim. Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or service security systems, and its rules cover closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. An owner can work on their own system, or have an employee do it, without a license; a company working on someone else's system needs one. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch staff. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get acknowledgment. If a repair moves a camera onto a stock room, a register or a back office where employees work, the notice should be updated.

Footage requests. Detectives may ask for a copy of a clip; a USB copy with original timestamps usually satisfies the request without the recorder leaving the store. Ring ended its tool for police requests through its app in January 2024, and law enforcement can still obtain video with a warrant. Whether to give a clip to a neighbor, a business group or a patrol is the owner's decision.

Landmarks. We found no historic district covering Madison's blocks. The Wyckoff-Bennett Homestead at 1669 East 22nd Street is a National Historic Landmark and a private home, and the Kingsway Jewish Center is on the National Register; if you are not sure whether your building is designated by the city, ask before the visit and we will check.

A blue screen or a menu usually means the recorder or the television lost its video source after a power blink, or the recorder restarted into a setup screen. We set the recorder to boot straight to the live camera view without a login, turn off the setup wizard, and set the television or monitor to wake on the right input automatically. Where the screen is a television she also watches, we can give the cameras a dedicated small monitor instead, or use the television's input settings so it returns to the cameras when she presses a single labeled button. We also put the recorder and the screen on a small battery backup so short flickers never reach them. At the end she gets a one-page note with a picture of what the screen should look like, and you get alerts on your own phone if a camera or the drive stops, so you hear about a problem before she does.

Four of us check our parents' cameras, and we all use my brother's login. Should each of us have our own?

Yes. A shared login means that when anyone changes the password, everyone is locked out, that the recorder cannot tell you who exported or deleted a clip, and that if one phone is lost or sold, the whole system is exposed until someone notices. Most recorders and camera apps allow several user accounts, each with its own password and its own permissions. We set up one account for each of you, give the person who manages the system administrator rights, give the others viewing and playback, and keep the master password written down in one safe place. Alerts can go to one person or all of you; most families pick one or two people to receive them so four phones are not buzzing at once. If one of you has an old phone that should no longer see the cameras, we remove it.

Detached, two-family and row houses

At night the camera in the narrow alley between our detached house and the next one shows either a white glare or nothing at all. Is the camera bad?

Usually not. In a passage only a few feet wide, the camera's infrared lights hit the neighbor's wall and bounce straight back, so the picture whites out near the camera; when the camera tries to correct for that, the far end goes black. In daytime the opposite happens: the alley is shaded and the street at its end is bright, so anyone walking in looks like a dark shape. We fix most of it with settings, by switching on smart infrared, lowering infrared strength, weighting exposure toward the alley and turning on wide dynamic range, and by aiming the camera along the passage instead of across it. If the alley has no light at all, a small motion or dusk light mounted high often does more than any camera change. We test it after dark before we leave, because that is the only way to know it worked.

We had new windows put in last spring, and the camera over our row house's front door has been dead ever since. Is it the camera?

Almost certainly not. On many Madison row houses, the front camera's cable was brought inside through the old window frame, and window installers cut whatever crosses the frame when they take it out. The camera is usually fine; there is a short tail of cable outside and another inside with the new, sealed frame between them. We test the camera to confirm it is alive, then bring a new cable in on a route that does not depend on the window, usually through a mortar joint beside the frame, through the cellar ceiling under the stoop, or along the inside of the bay. On a limestone front we use mortar joints, not the stone itself. If the windows on the back of the house were done at the same time, we check the yard camera too, since it often has the same problem.

Our two-family's cameras are plugged into an outlet downstairs, and when our tenant's power was shut off for a day, every camera went dark. Can they run from our side of the house?

Usually, yes. Apartment buildings on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue

The lobby cameras in our Ocean Avenue co-op and the intercom panel at the front door both stopped working the same night. Is that a coincidence?

Often one can be moved. Most building systems have at least one camera on a spot that matters less than it used to, such as a second view of the same corridor or a storage area nobody uses. Shops on the edges of Madison

The outside camera at our Avenue U store covers a long stretch of sidewalk, but people walking past are only a few pixels tall. Should we narrow it?

In most cases, yes. A camera's resolution is spread across whatever it sees, so a view of half a block gives each face only a handful of pixels, enough to show that someone walked by but not who. Narrowing the view to your doorway and the curb in front of it, by re-aiming or zooming a varifocal camera, puts far more pixels on each person. The trade-off is that you see less of the street. Many shops on Avenue U and Kings Highway end up with one camera tight on the door for faces and one wide for what happened, and if you have only one outdoor camera, the tight view is usually the more useful one to keep. We set it, then walk past it ourselves and check the recording to confirm a face is readable at the distance that matters.

Since the new streetlights went up on our part of Kings Highway, the camera over our door shows a bright glare at night and dark figures underneath it. What changed?

The light, not the camera. Brighter fixtures, like the streetlamps the Kings Highway BID added around Sgt. Joyce Kilmer Square, change the exposure a camera set itself for, and a lamp now inside the camera's view makes it darken everything else to cope. We adjust backlight and wide dynamic range, set the camera's exposure for the doorway rather than the whole scene, and if the lamp itself sits in the frame, tilt the camera a few degrees or fit a hood so the lamp falls outside it. Sometimes the new light helps: a camera that used to switch to black and white infrared may now stay in color all night, which we set on purpose once we see the street's real lighting.

The recorder in our Nostrand Avenue store's cellar, down the sidewalk doors, has died twice in three years. Is it the location?

Very likely. A cellar under sidewalk doors gets damp air every time the doors open, water running down the stairs in heavy rain, and in summer, heat with no ventilation. Hard drives and power supplies wear out fastest in exactly those conditions. Before replacing the recorder a third time, we check whether the drive or the power supply failed, copy any footage you need, and then move the recorder up into the store or onto a high, ventilated shelf, with a battery backup. If the cables from the street-facing cameras pass the sidewalk doors, we check them for water as well, and seal or reroute them.

Power on a hot day

Every July, when the air conditioners are running, half our cameras go dark for a few hours and come back by evening. Are they overheating?

Sometimes, but more often it is the circuit. If the recorder, a camera power supply or the PoE switch shares a circuit with a window air conditioner, the breaker can trip, or the voltage can sag enough for a supply to shut down, whenever the air conditioner starts. Cameras on that supply go dark while the rest keep working, which is why only half drop. We measure the circuit, move the camera equipment off the air conditioner's circuit, and put it on a battery backup that smooths brief sags. If a recorder or switch is in a hot attic or a closet without air, we also check its temperature and move it. A camera in direct afternoon sun that shuts itself down from heat is rarer, and we can tell the two apart on site.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we find on Madison houses, apartment buildings and shops, and the faults each one tends to develop here.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, common on houses and buildings wired fifteen or more years ago. Typical faults: water in connectors at the eaves, failed plug-in adapters, worn infrared and cables cut during window or roof work.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras that get power and data through one cable from a PoE switch or the recorder. Typical faults: dead switch ports after a surge, cameras on the tenant's network, address conflicts after a new router.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the footage, in a basement, closet, bedroom, super's office or back room. Typical faults: worn drives, settings lost after an outage, boot to a setup screen, and boxes switched off because of their noise or light.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with adjustable zoom, common in lobbies, on driveways and over storefronts. Typical faults: views too wide to recognize anyone, focus that drifted after a knock, and covers clouded by sun.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Cameras inside windows, on shelves and in back yards. Typical faults: weak signal through brick and plaster, accounts tied to a relative's email, router changes, and batteries nobody charges.

Video door stations and doorbells

Door stations and video doorbells at the front or shared door, often on their own app. Typical faults: video reaching one household and not the other, night glare from a porch light, and a doorbell transformer too weak to run them.

PoE switches and wireless bridges

Switches that power network cameras, and bridges that carry a garage camera's signal across the yard. Typical faults: dead ports, overloaded power budgets, and bridges knocked out of alignment.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, supplies on a circuit shared with an air conditioner, and backup batteries that died years ago.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Madison

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is partly true somewhere, and each leads Madison owners, and the families who help them, toward the wrong repair or a lost clip.

"If the app shows a live picture, the system is fine"

Quick answers often treat a live picture as proof that everything works. Open the app, see the porch, and you are covered.

Live view and recording are separate jobs. A recorder can stream every camera live while its drive is failed, full, unmounted or set to record nothing, and a Wi-Fi camera can stream straight to an app without ever reaching the recorder. For an adult child checking a parent's house from another state, live view is the only thing they see, which is exactly why months can pass before anyone notices that nothing has been saved.

The test is playback. On every repair we play back each camera from the previous night, confirm how many days the drive really keeps, and set alerts for lost video and drive errors to go to the person who will act on them.

"More megapixels will fix a blurry face"

Answers about unreadable faces often recommend a higher-resolution camera as the first step.

On Madison's edges the problem is usually the view, not the sensor. A camera on Avenue U or Kings Highway set to cover half a block spreads even a 4K image so thin that a face at the door is a smudge, while a lower-resolution camera narrowed to the doorway shows it clearly. Slow shutter settings blur anyone walking, and glare from new streetlamps or bus headlights does the rest.

Narrow the view, fix the shutter and the exposure, and test it with a person walking at the real distance. Only if that is not enough is a new camera worth the money.

"Infrared night vision works the same anywhere"

Night-vision advice tends to assume a camera looking out over an open yard or street.

A camera in the narrow alley between two detached houses, or behind a storm door or shop window, is a different case. Infrared reflects off a wall a few feet away and whites out the picture, or bounces off glass and shows the camera's own lights. Those are among the most common night complaints we get from Madison houses, and neither is a failing camera.

Smart infrared, lower infrared power, a different aim, moving the camera out from behind glass, or a small light usually fix it. We test at night before we call the repair done.

"Forgot the recorder password? Factory reset it"

Password answers often go straight to the reset button or a reset code from the maker.

On many recorders a reset erases the camera settings, the recording schedule and the network setup, and on some it makes existing footage hard to reach. In a Madison house where the system was set up by a relative who is no longer around, or where four siblings shared one login, the footage on the drive is often the very reason for the call. Some makers also require proof of ownership before issuing a reset code.

Try the routes that keep the settings first, and copy any footage that matters before a reset. We check what your recorder's maker allows and tell you before anything is erased.

"A battery backup keeps your cameras running through any outage"

Outage advice often says to plug the recorder into a UPS and stop worrying.

A small computer UPS may carry a recorder for minutes, not hours, and it does nothing for cameras on a separate power supply or on adapters plugged into other outlets, which in a Madison two-family can be on another household's meter. A backup whose battery died years ago carries nothing at all. Con Edison's own outage pages say reported outages can take up to 30 minutes just to appear on its map.

Put the recorder, the switch and the internet box on one backup sized for all three, check its battery every year, and use it to ride through brief flickers and shut the recorder down cleanly in a long outage.

"A street camera or a police camera will have caught it"

Some answers suggest that public cameras on busy corners make a private camera less important.

When police released images the day after the July 2026 assault near Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue, and when footage of the robbery at East 21st Street and Avenue U circulated, the useful pictures came from cameras close to where it happened. A camera somewhere on a corner does not see your porch, your side door, your building's package room or your counter, and you cannot open it yourself.

The camera that shows a face at your door is yours, and it only helps if it was recording and aimed at the door.

"Camera repair is a handyman job"

Some answers suggest that anyone handy, or the relative who is good with technology, can fix a camera system, since the parts are all online.

An owner can work on their own system, and New York's licensing rules allow it. A company doing it for someone else needs a license from the Department of State, because the state treats CCTV used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement as a security system. More practically, the hard part of most Madison repairs is not swapping a camera: it is working out whether the fault is the camera, a cable cut by a window crew, a circuit on the wrong meter or a recorder in the wrong room, and not wiping the footage in the process.

Find the cause first. The $150 service call tells you what failed and what will work with your system, and the written price lets you compare our part against one you buy yourself. We also fit customer-supplied equipment at labor-only rates.

FAQ

Madison Security Camera Repair FAQ

Twice a year our recorder's clock ends up an hour off from the real time for weeks. Why does that keep happening?
+

That is daylight saving time. Many recorders either have the change switched off, are set to the wrong time zone, or have their own schedule that does not match the current rules, so the clock stays an hour off until someone fixes it by hand. We set the correct New York time zone, turn on automatic daylight saving, and where the recorder reaches the internet, set it to take its time from a time server so it stays right on its own. A clock that is an hour off can confuse anyone trying to match your footage to an event, so it is worth fixing even if nothing else is wrong.

When we try to copy a clip to a USB stick, the recorder says no device found or the copy fails halfway. Is the recorder broken?
+

Usually not. Many recorders only read USB sticks formatted a certain way, struggle with very large sticks, or cannot power some sticks from their front port, and a clip that is too long for the stick's format can stop partway. We test with a stick we know works, check the format and the port, and set the export to split long clips into pieces. If the port itself has failed, there is usually a second one at the back, and we show you which to use next time.

We gave a detective our clip, but he says it only opens in a strange player he cannot install at work. How should we export it?
+

Many recorders save clips in their own format by default, which needs the maker's player. Most can also export as a standard MP4 or AVI file that opens on any computer, sometimes as a separate option in the export menu. We set your recorder to export in a standard format, show you where the option is, and if a format with the original timestamps embedded is needed, export both. Keep the original copy as it came off the recorder and hand over a copy.

Our driveway was dug up and repaved, and the camera on the garage at the back of the lot has not worked since. Does the whole driveway have to come up again?
+

No. If the old cable ran in a trench under the driveway and the paving crew cut it, we test to confirm that is where it failed, then run a new cable on a different route, usually along the side fence or the wall of the house in protected cable. Where no route along the edge works, a pair of wireless bridges can carry the garage camera's signal across the yard while it stays wired at the garage end. Either way, the new driveway stays as it is.

Our front camera sits behind the glass of our storm door, and at night it shows only a bright reflection of its own lights. What can be done?
+

Infrared lights behind glass reflect straight back into the lens, so a camera behind a storm door or a window sees itself at night. The choices are to move the camera outside the glass, to press a camera with a rubber shroud flat against the glass so the light cannot bounce back, or to turn off its infrared and rely on a porch light. We check which works with your door and your camera and test it after dark before we leave.

A relative powered our outdoor cameras with an extension cord run out the bedroom window. Is that safe to leave?
+

It is not a good long-term setup. A cord pinched in a window frame wears through, the window cannot close properly, and an outdoor plug left in the weather is a common cause of tripped circuits and dead cameras. We replace it with proper low-voltage camera wiring from a power supply or PoE switch inside, brought through the wall on a sealed entry. If a new outdoor outlet is needed, that is electrician's work, and we tell you exactly what to ask for.

The recorder is in my father's bedroom, and he keeps switching it off at night because the fan and the blue light bother him. Can it stay where it is?
+

Sometimes, with changes. We can clean or replace a noisy fan, cover or turn down the front lights, and turn off the beeps for minor alerts. If it is still too much, the better fix is to move the recorder to a hall closet, the basement or another room, and run a cable to a small screen in his room if he likes to look at the cameras. A recorder that gets switched off every night records nothing at night, which is usually when it matters most.

Our video doorbell keeps rebooting when someone presses the button. Could the old doorbell transformer be the problem?
+

Often it is. Many old houses still have the original doorbell transformer, which was sized for a mechanical chime, not a video doorbell with a camera and Wi-Fi. When the button is pressed, the chime draws power and the doorbell drops out. We check the transformer's output, replace it with one rated for the doorbell, and confirm the chime and the doorbell work together.

After a reset, one camera's picture is upside down and the street is on the wrong side. Do we need a new camera?
+

No. A reset often returns a camera to its factory orientation, and a camera that was mounted upside down under an eave or flipped to look the right way will then show the picture flipped or mirrored. It is a setting in the camera or the recorder. We set the rotation and mirror, then check that the camera's privacy masks and motion zones still sit where they should, since those can move with it.

Can you repair the cameras at a synagogue or community building in Madison that is closed most weekdays?
+

Yes. We arrange the visit with whoever holds the keys, usually a custodian, an administrator or a volunteer, in a two-hour arrival window that suits the building's schedule. We need access to the recorder and to each camera's location. At the end, the login and the alert settings go to the office or the person the board names, not only to whoever happened to be there that day, so the building is not dependent on one volunteer's phone.
