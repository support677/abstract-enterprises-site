An alarm here is usually one of two jobs: protecting a single apartment inside a large co-op building without touching the co-op’s lobby, walls or wiring, or protecting a professional suite whose files, safe and after-hours routine matter more than its windows. The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need Alarm Installation

Cadman Plaza is first of all a park. The city condemned most of a ten-block stretch between the Brooklyn Bridge ramp and Borough Hall in 1935, cleared it, and named it Cadman Plaza in 1939, after the Reverend Samuel Parkes Cadman, the Congregational minister and radio preacher who led Central Congregational Church in Brooklyn for 36 years. The Cultural Landscape Foundation puts the park at 10.4 acres, built from 1940 to a Clarke and Rapuano plan of lawns and London plane trees, with the Brooklyn War Memorial at its northern end. According to Wikipedia, the park runs between Cadman Plaza West and Cadman Plaza East, from the bridge on the north to Tillary Street on the south, and sits on the line where Brooklyn Heights meets Downtown Brooklyn. Before the park there were rowhouses, industry, the Sands Street terminal and elevated train lines; NYC Parks records that the Fulton Street El came down in 1941 and the Sands Street terminal was razed in 1943.

What surrounds the park is what shapes an alarm. Its west side was rebuilt as housing. A Center for Brooklyn History finding aid describes the Cadman Plaza complex as apartment buildings and townhouses between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street, built in the late 1960s on the site of the old Brooklyn Bridge trolley terminal and financed under the state’s Mitchell-Lama middle-income program: Cadman Plaza North at 140 Cadman Plaza West, Whitman Close at 75 Henry Street, and Cadman Towers at 101 Clark Street and 10 Clinton Street. A New York urbanism history of Cadman Towers describes saw-toothed poured-concrete towers of 32 and 13 stories, about 420 co-op apartments, 50 duplex townhouses added to match the scale of the street, parking garages, and towers and townhouses set behind six-foot concrete walls. CityRealty lists 140 Cadman Plaza West as a 27-story co-op of 250 apartments built in 1967, and the 75 Henry Street tower as a doorman co-op of 32 stories completed in 1973.

That building stock is the reason this page exists separately from our Brooklyn Heights page. The Heights is a landmark district of brownstones and carriage houses, where most alarm questions are about old wood windows, stoops and plaster. The co-op towers around Cadman Plaza were left out of the 1965 historic district because their blocks were already in the urban renewal area. Their questions are different: a front door at the end of a long fire-rated corridor, windows twenty floors up that no one will ever climb to, walls of solid concrete that wireless signals and cellular radios have to get through, a co-op board whose house rules govern every screw, and a ground floor of townhouses whose windows sit at sidewalk height.

The co-ops are also changing hands in a way they did not for decades. In April 2024 THE CITY reported that Cadman Towers, with 421 apartments in its count, became the first Mitchell-Lama co-op to win City Council approval to convert into a city-sponsored HDFC co-op, after more than three-quarters of shareholders voted for it; resale prices are capped at levels affordable to households at 80% of area median income, sellers return half their profit to the building, and a new waitlist is drawn by lottery through Housing Connect. Brooklyn Paper reported in 2016 that the 75 Henry Street co-op had left the Mitchell-Lama program years earlier and operates at market rate. More sales mean more apartments changing hands, and every change of owner raises the same questions: what the last owner left on the wall, who still knows its codes, and whether it still reports anywhere.

On the east side of the park is the civic center. The Theodore Roosevelt United States Courthouse at 225 Cadman Plaza East, a 15-story César Pelli building completed in 2006, houses the federal district court for the Eastern District of New York. Across the street the Federal Building and Post Office at 271–301 Cadman Plaza East, a Romanesque building of 1885–91 with a 1930–33 addition, holds the Downtown Brooklyn post office station, the bankruptcy court, the United States Attorney’s offices and probation offices. South of Tillary Street the mall continues past the Kings County Supreme Court Building at 360 Adams Street to Brooklyn Borough Hall at 209 Joralemon Street, completed in 1848. Around those courts are law firms, accountants, court-related services and nonprofits in older office buildings, including the 35-story Montague–Court Building at 16 Court Street, which Wikipedia calls Brooklyn’s tallest office building and reports was in danger of foreclosure by 2025 because of low occupancy. Half-empty floors at night are a security fact, not just a real estate one.

We could not find a news report of a burglary in the Cadman Plaza towers themselves, and we do not invent one. What the record does show is the precinct’s pattern. The NYPD’s 84th Precinct, at 301 Gold Street, covers Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill and Vinegar Hill according to its own page, and DNAinfo lists Downtown Brooklyn and DUMBO in its area as well. In March 2017 DNAinfo reported 15 burglaries in the precinct in February 2017 against four a year earlier, and 32 for the year to date against 12; the case it described was an apartment entered while the tenant was out of town and work was being done in the unit. Two lessons carry straight into a tower apartment: an empty apartment is the target, and the people who come and go with keys and access, contractors, aides, cleaners, movers and previous owners, matter more than the view from the window.

Most Cadman Plaza apartment owners pair the alarm with a camera inside their own door, and co-op boards often ask about the lobby intercom at the same time. The lobby belongs to the corporation and the apartment to you; see our Cadman Plaza intercom installation and Cadman Plaza security camera pages.

Keypad Chirping on the 20th Floor, an Office That Will Not Arm, or a Panel Showing No Signal: Same-Day Alarm Repair at Cadman Plaza

In a tower, a misbehaving apartment alarm quickly becomes a building problem: the chirp carries into the corridor and a siren that will not stop brings the super to the door. A chirp every minute is nearly always a weak battery in a wireless sensor, a smoke detector tied to the panel, or the panel’s own backup battery. A panel that reports no signal, or an app that says the system is offline, is usually a communicator that has lost its cellular connection or its Wi-Fi after a router change; in a concrete tower it is sometimes a radio that never had a strong signal at the spot where it was mounted. An office alarm that refuses to arm at night usually has one door or one motion zone that still reads open.

Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the brand on the keypad, what the display or the app says, and whether the building needs a work order or a certificate of insurance before a technician goes upstairs. If the siren is sounding now and you cannot silence it, it should stop on its own: New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says a building’s audible burglar alarm must be capable of terminating, and must terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. A siren that runs longer than that needs attention the same day. If a panel is not worth repairing, we say so before any billable work starts.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services at Cadman Plaza

Each service below is quoted on its own line, but most jobs combine two or three. A typical co-op apartment ends up with an entry-door contact, one or two motion detectors, a keypad and an app; a typical law office adds a separately armed file room and a silent button at reception.

Alarms for single co-op and condo apartments

Wireless sensors and a small panel inside the apartment only, mounted with methods the co-op’s house rules accept, and nothing installed in the corridor, the lobby or the building’s riser. The system belongs to the shareholder and can leave with them.

Entry-door protection for high floors

A contact on the apartment door, a motion detector covering the hall inside it, and an entry delay set for the walk from the door to the keypad. On upper floors that is often the whole perimeter, and the budget goes into interior coverage instead of window sensors.

Townhouse and duplex alarms at street level

The townhouses at the base of the towers have ground-floor windows, patio doors and two floors of living space. They get the window and glass protection a tower apartment does not need, and a layout that lets the upstairs bedrooms stay armed at night.

Professional office suites near the courts

Door contacts on the suite entry and any secondary door, motion in the reception area and corridors, a separately armed file room or safe closet, and arming schedules that warn the office manager when nobody closed up.

Silent panic buttons

A button under the reception desk or in a private office that sends a hold-up signal to a monitoring station without sounding anything in the room. Useful anywhere visitors arrive upset, which near family and criminal courts is not unusual.

Signal surveys in concrete buildings

Before any panel is mounted we measure cellular strength and wireless reach at the planned spot. In a poured-concrete tower the right place for the communicator is often near a window, not inside the closet where a contractor would hide it.

Takeovers of systems left by previous owners

Many apartments that change hands come with a keypad nobody can use. We identify the panel, reset it where the maker allows, remove the old codes and monitoring account, and decide with you whether it is worth keeping.

Monitoring, app control and verification

App self-monitoring or a central station plan with no long-term contract, plus an optional indoor camera that records only when the alarm is armed, so a tripped zone can be checked before anyone is sent.

Leak, smoke and freeze add-ons

Water sensors under the kitchen sink, behind the washing machine and beside the radiator valve, and smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that report through the alarm. In a tower a leak travels down several floors before anyone notices, and the co-op will ask whose pipe it was.

Many office tenants near the courts pair the alarm with a card or keypad reader on the suite door, so staff unlock the office and disarm the alarm with the same credential. That work is on our Cadman Plaza access control installation page.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Cadman Plaza’s Architecture

Within a few hundred yards of the park there are at least six kinds of building, and each one changes how an alarm is designed. Facts come from the sources named in each card; the alarm notes are our practice.

Concrete co-op towers of the late 1960s and 1970s

The Cadman Towers buildings are poured concrete with large windowless walls, according to a New York urbanism history; 140 Cadman Plaza West and the 75 Henry Street tower are postwar high-rises of 27 and 32 stories. Apartments open off long interior corridors. Walls between apartments are not a place anyone drills.

Duplex townhouses at the base of the towers

Cadman Towers added 50 duplex townhouses to bring the complex down to street scale, and Whitman Close has about 16 two-story townhouses designed by William Conklin and built between 1967 and 1973, per a 2012 Brownstoner article. They have their own front doors, ground-floor windows and small outdoor spaces.

A new condominium tower over a library

The 36-story building at 280 Cadman Plaza West, developed by Hudson Companies and designed by Marvel Architects, holds about 134 condos above a new Brooklyn Heights library branch, which opened in June 2022 according to CBS New York. New construction usually means pre-wired low-voltage and a developer’s smart-home system.

Courthouses and federal buildings

The federal courthouse, the post office and bankruptcy court building, the Supreme Court and Borough Hall are run by government agencies with their own security staff and contractors. They are landmarks for directions on this page, never customers or projects of ours.

Pre-war office towers near Borough Hall

The Montague–Court Building at 16 Court Street, 35 stories and finished in 1927 to a design by H. Craig Severance, sits in the Borough Hall Skyscraper Historic District designated in February 2012. Suites in buildings like it have drop ceilings, old abandoned cable and a landlord who controls the lobby and the fire alarm.

Ground-floor retail on Pineapple Walk

Brooklyn Paper reported in 2016 that the 75 Henry Street co-op owns the strip of land between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street known as Pineapple Walk, with a restaurant and several stores; the Cadman Towers history also mentions a supermarket and retail at its base. Shops below apartments need alarms that never share codes with the homes above.

How we wire each type

Concrete co-op towers. Inside a poured-concrete apartment there is nowhere to hide a cable, so the system is wireless from the start. The panel goes where it can hear every sensor and where its cellular radio has a usable signal, which we measure with a meter before choosing the spot; a window-side wall in the living room often beats the hall closet by a wide margin. Sensors are fixed with removable mounting where the house rules ask for it, the keypad sits inside the door at a height the household agrees on, and the siren is an indoor unit. Nothing is mounted in the corridor, on the apartment door’s outside face, or in the building’s utility closets, because those belong to the corporation.

Duplex townhouses. These are the only homes in the complex where a burglar can reach a window from the ground. We treat them like small houses: contacts on every ground-floor window and on the patio or garden door, glass-break detection in rooms with large panes, a motion detector on the stair, and a night setting that arms the lower floor while the bedrooms upstairs are occupied. If the townhouse shares a wall or a garden fence line with the tower’s plaza, the outdoor side gets attention too.

Pre-war and postwar office suites. Drop ceilings let us run cable from the suite door to a panel in a supply closet in an hour. The harder part is the building’s rules, so the suite alarm is planned to stand alone inside the tenant’s space, with its own communicator.

An Alarm Inside a Concrete Co-op Tower: Why the Usual Plan Changes Above the Fifth Floor

Most alarm advice assumes a house: every door and every ground-floor window is an entry point, so every one gets a sensor. Twenty floors above Cadman Plaza West the geometry is different. An apartment in a tower has one door that anyone can reach, the one off the corridor, and windows that face the sky. Unless an apartment opens onto a terrace or sits low enough to be reached from a roof or a plaza, its windows are not how a burglar gets in. That changes where the money goes. Instead of eight window contacts, a typical high-floor design uses one heavy-duty contact on the entry door, a motion detector covering the hall inside it, a second detector in the main living space, and perhaps a third in a home office or a bedroom where valuables are kept.

The entry door is also where tower burglaries tend to be decided. The intruder who gets past a lobby, by tailgating behind a resident or by holding a door for a delivery, ends up in a quiet corridor with a row of identical doors. A locked apartment door buys minutes. A door contact that starts an entry delay, followed by an indoor siren and a notification to the owner, turns those minutes against the intruder. We set the entry delay to the real walk from the door to the keypad, usually fifteen to thirty seconds, because a delay that is too long helps the wrong person and one that is too short produces false alarms every time someone fumbles with groceries.

Concrete is the second difference. Poured-concrete walls and floors are excellent at stopping noise and fire and just as good at stopping radio signals. Wireless sensors on the far side of a concrete wall from the panel may report weakly or not at all, and the cellular communicator that sends alarms to the monitoring station needs a usable signal from a carrier tower outside. In a building like Cadman Towers, whose history describes large windowless concrete walls, the difference between a good spot and a bad one can be a few feet. We carry a signal meter, test the carrier at three or four candidate positions, and enroll every sensor at its final location before mounting it, so a weak link is found in the first hour rather than after the first missed alarm.

The third difference is governance. A co-op apartment is a share in a corporation and a proprietary lease, and the corporation’s house rules decide what a shareholder may attach, drill or change. Many boards ask for an alteration agreement or at least a notice to the managing agent before any contractor works in an apartment, and most want proof of insurance naming the corporation and the agent. A wireless alarm is usually the least intrusive alteration a board ever sees, but it is still an alteration in the eyes of some boards, and we would rather send the paperwork before the visit than have a technician turned away at the front desk.

The Townhouses Below the Towers: A Different Alarm on the Same Block

The duplex townhouses around the Cadman Plaza towers were a planning compromise. A New York urbanism history of Cadman Towers says the 50 duplexes were added to make the complex fit the scale of the neighborhood, and a 2012 Brownstoner piece describes the Whitman Close townhouses on Henry Street as two-story homes designed to echo the nearby brownstones. For an alarm they are a third building type: they share the co-op’s ownership and house rules with the tower apartments, but they have the exposure of a house, with front doors on the street or a walkway, windows a person can reach from the ground, and small outdoor spaces behind them.

That combination tends to produce two mistakes. The first is treating a townhouse like a tower apartment, with a single entry-door contact and a motion detector, which leaves every ground-floor window uncovered. The second is treating it like a brownstone and asking for exterior sirens, floodlights and drilled conduit that the co-op’s rules may not allow on a facade it controls. We design these homes inside both limits: perimeter contacts and glass-break detection on the lower floor, an indoor siren, wireless sensors that need no channels in the walls, and exterior devices only where the board has approved them in writing.

Townhouses also live on two floors, which is the main reason a single away/disarmed switch does not suit them. At night the household is upstairs and the danger is downstairs. A night setting arms every downstairs door and window and the stair motion detector while leaving the bedroom floor free, and an instant response on the patio door means a forced entry sounds the siren immediately instead of waiting out an entry delay meant for the front door.

Suites Beside the Courts: Files, Safes and Late Nights

The other half of the Cadman Plaza market is offices. The federal courthouse and the bankruptcy court face the park from Cadman Plaza East, and the state Supreme Court building at 360 Adams Street and Borough Hall are a short walk south. Around them, in the towers of Court Street and Montague Street and in smaller buildings on the side streets, are the businesses that serve the courts. None of the buildings named on this page is presented as a customer. What they have in common shapes the alarm: paper and electronic records that clients trust them to protect, a safe or a locked cabinet for escrow checks or evidence copies, staff who leave at very different hours in the weeks before a trial date, and a building whose lobby security the tenant does not control.

The first design decision in a law or professional suite is which rooms are armed when. Reception and the open office are armed when the last person leaves. A file room, server closet or partner’s office with a safe can be a separate area that stays armed during the day and opens only for the people who need it, with every opening logged. That is a small amount of extra wiring in a drop-ceiling office and it answers the question a managing partner actually asks after an incident: who went into that room, and when.

The second decision is how the office closes. In practice the alarm is armed by whoever leaves last, and on a busy week that may be a paralegal at midnight or a cleaner at six in the morning. We give each person a separate code, set the panel to send the office manager a message if the system is still disarmed at a chosen hour, and give the cleaning company a code that works only during its contracted hours and only disarms the areas it cleans. None of that requires a monitoring contract; it requires programming time and a clear list from the office.

The last decision is about people rather than doors. Courts bring stressful days, and some visitors arrive angry. A silent panic button under the reception desk sends a hold-up signal to a monitoring station without making a sound in the room, so staff can call for help without escalating anything. We install panic buttons only after the office has decided who will be called, what the monitoring station is told to do, and how to test the button without dispatching anyone.

Before the Visit: A Short List for Shareholders, Condo Owners and Office Managers

Most delays on Cadman Plaza jobs come from paperwork and building rules, not sensors. This list prevents them.

  • Read your house rules or lease. Look for sections on alterations, contractor hours, insurance and anything attached to walls or doors. If the building wants an alteration agreement for low-voltage work, ask the managing agent now.
  • Get the exact name for the insurance certificate. Co-ops usually want the corporation and the managing agent named; office landlords usually want the ownership entity and the property manager. Send us both names and the agent’s email.
  • Find any old equipment. Photograph a keypad, a grey metal box in a closet, sensors on doors or windows, or an old sticker from an alarm company. Note anything a previous owner told you about it.
  • List everyone who needs to come and go. Household members, an aide or a nanny, a cleaner, a dog walker, a contractor, and in an office every staff member and the cleaning company. Each gets a code; some get time limits.
  • Decide where alerts go. Your phone, a partner’s phone, a relative nearby, a central station, an office manager. For a co-op apartment, decide whether you want the front desk on the list and whether the building agrees.
  • Mark the rooms that matter most. The room with the safe, the jewelry, the files or the laptop is the one that deserves a motion detector even if nothing else in the apartment gets one.

What an office alarm near the courts needs

A heavy-duty contact on the suite door with a short entry delay, an instant zone on any door to a fire stair, motion detectors in reception and the main corridor rather than glass sensors on windows nobody can reach, a separately armed records room, arming reports with a late warning, and a tested silent button at reception. Each of these is explained in the section on suites beside the courts above.

Three legal points for apartments and suites alike

  • Sirens must stop on time. Under New York City Administrative Code § 24-238(a), the owner of a building may not have an audible burglar alarm in operation unless it is capable of terminating, and does terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. We program every siren inside that limit. In a tower with neighbors on three sides, they will hear every minute.
  • The installer must be licensed by the state. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers; its licensing page requires applicants to be 18, fingerprinted, and to complete at least 81 hours of qualifying education and an examination. Our license number is 12000287431. Ask any installer for theirs before they enter your apartment or suite.
  • Building fire alarms are not part of a tenant’s alarm. Towers and office buildings have their own fire alarm systems maintained for the owner. Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that report through an apartment or suite alarm add a warning to your phone; they do not replace or connect to the building’s system. Building fire alarm work is covered on our Cadman Plaza fire alarm installation page.

They are grouped by the kind of property. Shorter answers on scheduling, drilling and building rules are in the FAQ near the bottom of the page.

Apartments high in the towers

We live on a high floor of a tower off Cadman Plaza West, and nobody is climbing to our windows. What actually needs a sensor?

Usually the entry door and the inside of the apartment, not the windows. A heavy-duty contact on the corridor door starts the entry delay; a motion detector in the hall just inside catches anyone who gets the door open without tripping the contact, for example with a key; and a second detector covers the room where valuables are kept. That is the design for most apartments above the first few floors.

The exceptions are apartments with a terrace, apartments low enough to be reached from a roof setback, a plaza or a neighboring building, and windows next to a fire escape. Those openings get contacts exactly as they would on a house. We look at the floor plan and the view from each window before deciding, because a few towers in the area have setbacks and low roofs that change the answer floor by floor.

Our building is poured concrete and my phone drops calls in the hallway. Will the alarm still reach the monitoring station?

It can, but only if the communicator is put where the signal is good, and that is something to measure, not guess. We test cellular strength at several possible panel locations with a meter, usually finding a usable position near a window even when the hallway is dead. Where one carrier is weak, a communicator on a different carrier often works better.

Wireless sensors inside the apartment have a similar issue on a smaller scale: a concrete wall between a bedroom and the panel can weaken their signal. Every sensor is enrolled and tested at its final spot before it is mounted, and if one is marginal, a small wireless repeater or a different panel position solves it. You get a written note of the signal readings so the next technician starts from facts.

Should the siren inside our apartment be loud enough for the corridor to hear, or is a silent alarm better in a tower?

We recommend an audible indoor siren in almost every apartment, set to a sensible volume and a short duration. Its job is to make an intruder leave in seconds; a burglar who hears a siren in a quiet corridor rarely stays to search. A neighbor or a porter who hears it may also notice, which is a bonus, not the plan.

A silent alarm, where only your phone or the monitoring station is told, makes sense in a few cases: an apartment with an elderly resident who would be frightened by a siren, or a household that only wants records of who comes and goes. Whichever you choose, the siren time is set inside the City’s fifteen-minute limit, and the alarm also reaches your phone, because a siren alone summons nobody.

A home aide comes to my father’s apartment every weekday morning. Can her code work only during her hours?

Yes. Most current panels allow a user code with a schedule, for example weekdays from eight to one. Outside those hours the code does nothing, and every time it is used the app records it, so the family can see when the aide arrived and left without anyone having to ask.

If the agency sends a substitute, the family can create a temporary code from the app for that day and delete it afterwards, without calling us. We set this up during the visit and walk a family member through adding and removing codes, because staff change more often than alarm systems do.

Our second-floor apartment sits just above the shops along Pineapple Walk. Are low windows over a retail strip a real entry point?

They can be, and they are the reason we never apply the high-floor rule automatically. Where an apartment window sits above a shop canopy, a setback roof or a raised plaza, a person standing on that surface may be within reach of it. We check each low window from outside as well as inside before writing the plan.

Windows within reach get a contact and, in a room with several panes, a glass-break detector. Windows that are out of reach are left to the interior motion detector. The result is usually two or three more sensors than a tower apartment higher up, and none of them goes on the building’s facade.

Buyers will be touring our apartment while the co-op’s sales go on. Can parts of the alarm stay armed during a showing?

Yes. A panel can be divided so that one room, such as a home office or a bedroom with a safe, stays armed while the rest of the apartment is open, or a broker can be given a code that works only during the showing window. If the armed room is opened, you get an alert and the siren sounds as usual.

An indoor camera that records only while the alarm is armed can be added, as long as visitors are told about it; it is never aimed at the corridor.

Townhouses and the ground floor

We own one of the duplex townhouses at the foot of the towers. How should it be alarmed differently from the apartments above us?

Like a small house rather than an apartment. Every ground-floor window and the patio or garden door get contacts, rooms with large panes get glass-break detection, the stair gets a motion detector, and a night setting arms the lower floor while the family sleeps upstairs. The apartments above need none of that perimeter work, which is why a townhouse quote is usually closer to our complete home package than to the apartment one.

What stays the same is the co-op. The townhouse is subject to the same house rules as the tower apartments, so exterior sirens, lights or cameras need the board’s approval, and anything drilled into the facade is the corporation’s concern. We plan the inside first and propose exterior devices only if the household wants them and the building allows them.

Our townhouse sits behind the complex’s tall concrete wall. Does that wall mean the back windows are safe?

No. A wall keeps casual passers-by out of sight, and that cuts both ways: once someone is on the inside of it, neighbors and pedestrians cannot see them either. The history of Cadman Towers describes six-foot concrete walls around towers and townhouses, and we treat every window and door behind such a wall as fully exposed.

That means contacts on rear windows and the patio door, an instant zone on the patio door so a forced entry sounds the siren immediately, and if the family wants it, an outdoor detector aimed along the wall rather than toward a public walkway. The wall stays a landscape feature, not part of the security plan.

Offices near the courthouses

Our law office keeps closed case files in one back room. Can that room stay armed while the rest of the suite is open during the day?

Yes, and in law and accounting offices it is one of the most useful things an alarm can do. The records room becomes its own area with its own codes. During business hours the reception and open office are disarmed while the records room stays armed; only the people given access can open it, and every opening is logged with the user and the time.

At night the whole suite is armed together. If the firm later needs to show a client or an insurer who had access to a file room on a given date, the panel’s history answers it. We keep the records-room detector aimed at its door so that someone working inside with permission does not trigger it.

The building’s cleaning crew comes into our suite at night. How do they get in without the alarm going off or being given the master code?

Give the cleaning company its own code that works only during its contracted hours, disarms only the areas it cleans, and records each use. The records room or the safe room stays armed. When the cleaners finish they re-arm the suite with the same code, and if they forget, the office manager gets a message at a set time.

If the building’s cleaners change, the office deletes one code from the app. Nobody needs the master code except the people who manage the system, and nobody needs to leave a door unlocked for the night crew.

Our landlord controls the lobby, the fire alarm and the network. What part of an office alarm are we allowed to install in a leased suite?

Generally, anything inside your demised premises that does not connect to the building’s systems. That covers door contacts, motion detectors, a panel and keypad, a siren inside the suite and a cellular communicator. What landlords near Borough Hall usually forbid is any link to the building fire alarm, any device in the common corridor, and any use of the building’s network without permission.

We read the relevant lease clause or the building’s contractor rules with you before quoting, send the landlord a description of the work and our insurance certificate, and design the alarm to stand alone. When the lease ends, it comes out with you and the holes are patched.

We keep client trust checks and original wills in a fireproof safe. Can the alarm tell us if someone opens or moves the safe?

Yes. A small contact on the safe door reports every opening, and a shock or tilt sensor fixed inside or on the safe reports an attempt to drill, pry or move it, even when the room around it is disarmed for the working day. The safe can be its own always-armed zone that only named people can open without an alert.

Safes that are bolted down are harder to move but still worth a shock sensor, because an attack on the lock makes vibration long before it makes progress. We fix sensors without drilling the safe body, so its fire rating and warranty are not affected, and test them with the office present.

Can a silent panic button at our reception desk reach help without the visitor in front of us knowing?

Yes. A hold-up button under the desk sends a distinct signal to the monitoring station, separate from a burglary alarm, and makes no sound in the office. The station follows the instructions the office has agreed in advance, which normally means calling the police and then the office’s contact list.

Because a hold-up signal is treated as an emergency, we install these buttons only with a monitoring plan in place, train the staff who sit near them, and set up a test procedure that lets the office confirm the button works without anyone being dispatched.

Condos, sublets and systems already in place

Our new condo came with a developer’s smart-home screen in every unit. Can the alarm use it instead of adding another keypad?

Sometimes. Some developer systems can host door contacts and motion detectors directly and act as the alarm panel; others only control lights, climate and the intercom and cannot report an alarm anywhere. We identify the system during the first visit, check whether its maker supports intrusion sensors and monitoring, and tell you which it is before quoting.

If the existing screen can do the job, adding sensors to it avoids a second keypad by the door. If it cannot, a separate alarm panel is installed with its own communicator, and we keep the two independent so that a software update on the building’s platform never switches off your alarm.

The board approved a sublet of our apartment for two years. Who should control the alarm while the subtenant lives there?

Usually the shareholder keeps the administrator account and the subtenant gets an ordinary user code and their own app login. The subtenant can arm, disarm and receive alerts; only the owner can add or delete users, change the reporting path or see the full history. When the sublet ends, the owner deletes the subtenant’s access from the app in a minute.

Decide before the sublet starts who pays for monitoring, whose phone the central station calls first, and whether the owner should still receive alarm notices from abroad or across town. We set the notification list to match, and put the arrangement on the one-page guide left with the panel so a new subtenant is not guessing.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation at Cadman Plaza

Google’s AI Overview could not be loaded for Cadman Plaza alarm searches in our build environment, so nothing below quotes it. These are generalizations that recur in quick online answers, national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and marketing from national brands such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe, set against what an apartment, townhouse or office near the plaza actually needs. Each claim below is a fair starting point somewhere; the problem is that quick answers are written for a detached house in a suburb, and very little around Cadman Plaza is built like one. The towers are concrete, the apartments are governed by co-op boards, the townhouses share rules with the towers above them, and many of the offices sit in older buildings whose landlords control everything outside the suite door.

“Put a sensor on every door and window.”

It is the standard advice for a house with a yard, and on the ground floor of a Cadman Plaza townhouse it is correct.

In a tower apartment twenty floors up it sends money to windows nobody can reach and leaves the one real entry, the corridor door, with the same cheap contact as everything else.

We start from the floor plan and the elevation: the entry door and the inside of the apartment on high floors, full perimeter protection on low floors and townhouses, and each low window judged from outside.

“Wireless alarms work anywhere.”

Wireless is the right choice for most apartments here, because nobody wants channels cut in concrete. But the claim that it works anywhere skips over what concrete does to radio signals.

A sensor behind a poured-concrete wall can report weakly, and a cellular communicator in an interior closet can have almost no signal. The Cadman Towers buildings are described in their history as having large windowless concrete walls.

Every panel position is chosen with a signal meter, every sensor is tested where it will live, and repeaters or a different carrier are used where readings are marginal.

“Self-install kits are just as good as professional systems.”

A kit from Ring or SimpliSafe can protect an apartment well, and we install customer-bought kits for a flat labor price on our Brooklyn list.

What a kit cannot do on its own is navigate a co-op: insurance certificates naming the corporation, notice to the managing agent, house rules on what may be attached, and a front desk that will not let an unannounced contractor upstairs.

For an apartment owner the difference between a kit and a professional install here is mostly paperwork and placement, and we are happy to do either.

“Your building’s doorman is your security.”

A doorman controls a lobby, and some towers near the plaza have one; CityRealty lists a full-time doorman at the 75 Henry Street tower and none at 140 Cadman Plaza West.

A doorman cannot see inside an apartment, does not know when you are away, and is not on duty for your unit when an intruder is already on your floor. A 2017 DNAinfo report on the 84th Precinct described an apartment entered while the tenant was out of town and work was being done inside.

An apartment alarm covers what the lobby cannot: your door, your rooms and your phone. The two work together; neither replaces the other.

“Any alarm needs a long monitoring contract.”

National providers have long bundled equipment with multi-year monitoring agreements, and quick answers often treat the contract as part of having an alarm.

Around the plaza it rarely needs to be. Central station service through our partners starts at $19.99 a month on the Brooklyn list and can be cancelled, and plenty of apartment owners simply watch the alarm from their own phones.

Offices with panic buttons should have central station monitoring, because a hold-up signal needs someone to act on it; a high-floor apartment often does not.

“An office alarm is the landlord’s job.”

Office landlords secure lobbies, elevators and building systems. They rarely secure what is inside a tenant’s suite, and most leases make the tenant responsible for its own premises.

In older towers near Borough Hall, some with many vacant floors, the building may be quiet at night without being watched. The Montague–Court Building at 16 Court Street was reported by Wikipedia to be in danger of foreclosure by 2025 because of low occupancy.

A suite alarm that stands alone, with its own communicator, arming reports and a records-room area, is the tenant’s answer to that.

“Anyone handy can install an alarm.”

Owners may install systems on their own property, and New York’s licensing rules recognize that.

Anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining alarms for others in New York must hold a Department of State license, which requires fingerprinting, at least 81 hours of qualifying education and an examination, according to the Department’s licensing page.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Meter Before You Pick the Wall

This is how we work in concrete buildings, not a story about any particular apartment. The first thing a technician does in a tower unit is not open the box of sensors. He stands inside the front door with a signal meter and reads the cellular strength there, then in the hall closet where owners usually want the panel hidden, then on the living-room wall nearest the window, then in the bedroom. In a poured-concrete building those four readings can differ more than you would expect from rooms a few steps apart, and the closet is often the worst of them.

Then he takes one door contact, enrolls it at the panel’s planned position, carries it to the entry door and the far bedroom, and watches the signal report on the panel. Only after both tests does anything get mounted. It adds perhaps twenty minutes to the visit. It is the difference between an alarm that reports every time and one that loses its connection to the monitoring station the first week the weather changes, and it is the step most often skipped when a kit is installed in a hurry.

What hours can you work in a co-op or office building that limits contractor hours?
+

We schedule to the building’s rules. Most co-ops allow contractor work on weekday daytime hours, and most office landlords prefer early mornings, evenings or weekends for work inside occupied floors. Tell us the rule when you book and we plan the visit around it.

How many holes does an apartment alarm leave in a concrete tower when it is taken down?
+

Very few. Most sensors in a tower apartment are fixed with removable mounting, and the panel and keypad usually need a handful of small anchors in a wall that already takes picture hooks. When the system comes out, those anchor holes are filled and touched up; nothing is ever drilled into ceilings, floors or walls shared with neighbors.

Can someone jam a wireless alarm in a busy building?
+

Current wireless alarm sensors and panels use encrypted or supervised radio links, and many panels can report a jamming attempt or a sensor that stops checking in as a trouble condition. We enable those reports and send them to your phone, so interference is noticed rather than silent.

Can the alarm appear in the same app as my apartment’s door intercom?
+

Sometimes, if the intercom and the alarm are on platforms that work together, which is more common in newer buildings. Where the co-op’s intercom is a separate system, the two stay in separate apps; we never alter the building’s entry system to make an apartment alarm fit.

Is a keypad or an alarm sticker by my apartment door allowed under co-op hallway rules?
+

The keypad goes inside the apartment, so the corridor is untouched. Stickers and signs outside the door are a matter for your house rules; many buildings do not allow anything on the corridor side, and we leave that decision to you and the board.

Do you need access to the roof, the riser or the building’s utility closets for an apartment alarm?
+

No. An apartment alarm with its own cellular communicator lives entirely inside the unit and needs nothing from the building’s roof, risers or closets. Office suites sometimes need a short cable path to a supply closet inside the suite, which we arrange with the landlord.

Will a robot vacuum set off the motion detector while we are out?
+

It can if a detector is aimed at the floor along its route. We mount motion detectors high and angled across a room, choose pet-immune models where useful, and walk the vacuum’s path during testing. Scheduling the vacuum for times the alarm is in stay mode also helps.

Can you install a temporary alarm in an office suite while it is being renovated?
+

Yes. During a renovation a few wireless contacts and a motion detector on a battery-backed panel can protect tools, furniture and files, and the same equipment can be folded into the permanent system when the suite reopens. Contractors get their own time-limited codes.

How often should the alarm panel’s backup battery be replaced?
+

Panel batteries are typically replaced every three to five years, sooner if the app reports low battery after a power interruption. Sensor batteries usually last several years and the panel warns before they fail. We note the install date on the panel so replacement is easy to plan.

Some sources contradict each other, and we show both figures rather than pick one silently. Cadman Towers is counted at 420 apartments in one history and 421 in THE CITY’s reporting. The 75 Henry Street tower is described as 32 stories with 352 apartments in one CityRealty review and 33 stories with 370 in a 2012 article. The Brooklyn War Memorial is dated 1951 by NYC Parks and 1952 by The Cultural Landscape Foundation. The condo over the library is reported at 36 stories and 134 units by the Brooklyn Eagle and at 38 stories and 133 units in Brooklyn Paper’s library story. The 84th Precinct’s own page lists Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill and Vinegar Hill, while DNAinfo adds Downtown Brooklyn and DUMBO.

Some sources were out of reach. Our build environment had no access to Answer the Public, Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches, Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, or Yelp reviews. Reddit search returned only thread titles and short excerpts about the area, such as a question about living in the small brick buildings on Henry Street by Cadman Plaza, with no alarm discussion we could read. We found no news report of a burglary inside the Cadman Plaza towers, so the page gives none, and no source counting terraces or setback roofs in the towers, so the page describes how we check them rather than how many there are.
