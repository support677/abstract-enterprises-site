The County

Five Towns, Five Different CCTV Jobs

Rockland is small on the map, about 200 square miles and 338,329 people at the 2020 census, but it is split into five towns that build, police and wire very differently. Clarkstown is the suburban middle, with New City, Nanuet, West Nyack, Congers and Valley Cottage. Orangetown runs along the New Jersey line and the river, from Pearl River and Tappan to Orangeburg, Blauvelt and Sparkill, with the village of Nyack at its northeast corner. Ramapo, the most populous town at 148,919, holds Spring Valley, Suffern, Monsey, Airmont and most of the county's villages. Haverstraw holds the brick village on Haverstraw Bay, West Haverstraw and the hamlets around Garnerville and Thiells. Stony Point, the smallest at 14,655, is quiet streets, the battlefield and the river.

For a CCTV installer that split matters more than the county line. The town decides what kind of house you probably live in, whether there is an attic or a crawl space to run cable through, how many families share the driveway, who answers when you call the police, and even which police department keeps a list of private cameras. A system that is right for a detached colonial on a cul-de-sac in Stony Point is wrong for a three-unit house on a narrow lot in Spring Valley, and both are wrong for a store sharing a parking field on Route 59.

The housing numbers, town by town

The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 108,576 housing units in Rockland. A little over half are detached single-family houses, which is what most people picture, but a third of all units sit in buildings with two or more homes in them. That share swings hard by town. In Stony Point more than eight in ten homes are detached houses and almost nine in ten are owner-occupied. In Ramapo fewer than half are detached, 44.8% sit in buildings with two or more units, and 45.3% of households rent.

Planning notes for each town

Clarkstown. Mostly detached houses, 69% of the stock, on quarter- and half-acre lots in New City, Bardonia, Nanuet, Congers and Valley Cottage, plus the county's heaviest retail along Route 59 in Nanuet and West Nyack. Residential jobs here are usually the most straightforward in the county: split-levels and colonials with a basement or lower level that hides the recorder and the cable. Commercial jobs are the busiest, with shared parking fields, long store hours and the county's highest larceny count.

Orangetown. The oldest houses in Rockland, from the village blocks of Nyack and Piermont to the 1950s capes of Pearl River, Orangeburg and Blauvelt. Expect plaster, tight attics, small basements and porches that change where a door camera can sit. Tappan and Palisades carry historic-area rules, and the Route 303 office and industrial parks bring loading doors and fenced yards.

Ramapo. The largest and densest town, with Spring Valley, Monsey, Suffern, Airmont and a dozen villages. Two-family and small multi-unit houses are everywhere, so a single recorder often serves several households, and the right answer is separate logins rather than separate systems. Big families, many cars and busy driveways mean the driveway and side-entrance cameras earn their keep.

Haverstraw. A mix of older brick and frame buildings in the village, garden apartments and newer waterfront housing, and the hamlets of Garnerville and Thiells. Renters make up more than a third of households, so landlords are a big share of our calls, and shared entrances, laundry rooms and parking areas are the usual first cameras.

Stony Point. The most owner-occupied town in the county, with larger lots, detached garages and longer drives up toward Tomkins Cove and the Bear Mountain edge. These jobs look more like the rest of the Hudson Valley: a camera at the foot of the drive, one on the detached garage, and cable runs long enough to plan carefully.

What the police numbers say, department by department

Rockland does not have one police force. The state's crime tables list a separate department for each town, plus village departments in Spring Valley, Suffern and Piermont. The Village of Nyack has been covered by Orangetown police since January 1, 1991, when the village board voted to disband its own department, and South Nyack dissolved as a village on March 31, 2022, so both now sit inside Orangetown's numbers. Countywide, the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services counted 3,710 property crimes in 2024: 255 burglaries, 3,346 larcenies and 109 motor vehicle thefts, each slightly up on 2023.

A police camera list that only one town keeps

Clarkstown Police run a voluntary Video Surveillance Camera Registration Program. Homeowners and businesses list where their cameras are, and when something happens nearby, detectives know whose door to knock on and can ask for footage from a specific date and time. The department is clear that registration does not give officers a way into your recorder.

“It is important to note that the Police Department does NOT have unfettered, direct and remote access to the cameras that are registered. This is an enhanced Community Watch program for the 21st century.”
Clarkstown Supervisor George Hoehmann, Rockland County Times, May 3, 2018; program page: Clarkstown Police surveillance camera registration

That “specific date and time” detail is why we treat the recorder's clock as part of the installation, not an afterthought. A request for 9:40 p.m. last Tuesday is easy to answer when the recorder's time is right and the clip export works. It is a wasted evening when the clock drifted twenty minutes, the drive was set to overwrite after four days, or nobody knows the password. We cover all three on every Rockland job, registered or not.

The power line runs through your footage

Orange & Rockland Utilities supplies electricity to about 300,000 customers in New York and New Jersey and issues its storm updates from Pearl River. On the night of December 19, 2025, its 9:30 p.m. update counted 2,596 Rockland customers still without power, including 1,430 in Clarkstown, 394 in Spring Valley and 340 in Orangetown, with gusts of 40 to 50 mph still forecast. A PoE camera gets its power from the recorder or the switch, so when the house goes dark every camera on the system goes dark with it, unless the recorder and switch sit on a battery. Rockland's tree-lined streets and overhead lines make that a when, not an if.

Building Specialists

CCTV Installation for Rockland's Split-Levels, Capes and Two-Families

Rockland's housing was mostly built in three waves: river villages before the war, the 1950s and 1960s boom after the Palisades Parkway and the Tappan Zee Bridge opened the county to commuters, and the denser two-family and multi-unit building of the last few decades in Ramapo and Haverstraw. Each wave hides its cable routes in a different place, and each changes where a camera can sit.

🏠

Raised ranches and split-levels

The classic Clarkstown and Ramapo house. The front door sits between two floors, the garage tucks under the bedrooms, and the lower level often has a finished den with a drop ceiling, which is a gift for cable.

🏡

1950s capes and small colonials

Pearl River, Orangeburg, Blauvelt and Nanuet are full of them. Finished knee-wall attics, shallow soffits and plaster in the older ones make cable routing a puzzle that rewards patience.

🏘️

Two-family and multi-unit houses

Common in Spring Valley, Monsey and Haverstraw. Shared driveways, separate entrances, a basement unit and several households who all need to trust the same recorder.

🏛️

Village houses and mixed-use blocks

Nyack, Piermont, Haverstraw and Suffern have pre-war frame and brick buildings with shops below and apartments above, where a neat, small system beats a big one.

🏢

Garden apartments, condos and townhouses

Two- and three-story complexes around New City, Nanuet, Pearl River and Pomona, with carports, mail kiosks and a board that approves anything on the common elements.

🌳

Larger lots up north and west

Stony Point, Pomona, New Hempstead and the edges of Wesley Hills and Montebello have longer drives and detached garages, closer to the Hudson Valley country jobs.

How each building changes the install

Split-levels and raised ranches. The best recorder spot is usually the lower-level utility room next to the panel and the internet modem, which keeps the recorder cool, near power and away from the front door. Cable climbs inside the garage wall or through the joist bays above the lower-level ceiling, then out through the rim joist to the cameras on the eaves. The awkward camera is the one for the front door. Mounted on the soffit above the upper floor, it sees the tops of heads; mounted beside the door at about eight feet, it sees faces as people climb the half-flight of steps. We usually do the second and add a wider camera on the garage corner for the driveway.

Capes and older colonials. A cape's upper floor often has knee walls with a narrow crawl space behind them, which is where we run cable to the gable-end cameras. Where plaster and lath walls stop a fish tape, we go through a closet or follow a downspout line on the outside in painted raceway rather than tearing open plaster. These are also the houses where concealed CCTV wiring costs the most labor, so it is the line we explain most carefully in the quote.

Two-family houses. Each household gets its own login that shows only the cameras that concern it, the shared cameras on the driveway and side walk go to both, and the admin password stays with the owner. The recorder goes somewhere neither tenant passes every day, and the battery backup keeps it running when someone flips the wrong breaker in the basement.

Village buildings. On brick, we anchor into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so the holes can be patched invisibly later. On frame storefronts, we use the sign band or the cornice to hide the cable. On mixed-use blocks, the landlord, the store tenant and the apartment tenants often all want cameras; we separate their systems or their logins up front so nobody ends up watching someone else's hallway.

Condo and townhouse complexes. Common elements belong to the association, so the board approves where cameras go and who holds footage before we drill. Owners who want a building-wide plan for entries, mail kiosks and carports should read our apartment building camera systems for Rockland County page, which goes deeper into resident privacy, tenant access and board approvals.

Historic areas. Orangetown protects historic areas in the hamlets of Tappan and Palisades. Under the town's code, building permit applications for structures in those areas go to the Historical Areas Board of Review. If your camera work is part of a project that needs a permit there, plan for that review; either way, we keep cameras and conduit small, low on the facade and painted to match, which is also what makes a camera less of a target.

Car dealership CCTV installation: the walls, not the lot

The photos on this page come from our CCTV job at a car dealership in Rockland County, a 23-camera recorder system inside a building with a two-story showroom, glass offices, a guest services window, back hallways and work areas. The lesson we take from that building is about where the cameras are allowed to live. Tall painted walls, a mezzanine and drop ceilings give a camera height and a clean background, and height is what turns a view of a crowded showroom into a view of who was standing where. Inside a glass storefront, a turret dome on the ceiling looks out over the entry and the first rows of the lot, which is often a better first look at an arriving car than a camera fighting the glare outside.

Dealerships also run long hours with many people moving through, so the recorder and the network gear sit behind a door away from the sales floor, and the cable is carried above the ceiling grid to that room. The same thinking applies to an auto repair shop CCTV installation or a gas station on Route 59: keep the equipment out of reach, put cameras where people have to pass, and give the busiest views the most storage.

Rules that apply to business cameras in New York

This is general information, not legal advice; your attorney should confirm what applies to your business.

  • Staff bathrooms and locker areas. Under New York Labor Law §203-c an employer may not video record employees in a restroom, a locker room or a room used for changing, short of a court order, so those rooms get no cameras at all.
  • Audio needs consent. Under New York's eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.00 and §250.05), recording a conversation you are not part of without the consent of at least one person in it is a crime. We install with audio switched off at businesses and only enable it where the owner decides it is lawful and appropriate.
  • No secret recording where people undress. Penal Law §250.45, unlawful surveillance, makes it a crime to secretly record people in places where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as bathrooms and fitting rooms.
  • Signs help. A posted notice that the premises are recorded is good practice for stores and landlords, and insurers often ask about it.
Your Questions Answered

Rockland County CCTV Installation: Questions Answered

These come from calls and texts we get from Rockland owners, sorted by what people usually ask first: what kind of system, how it is wired, where the cameras go on Rockland houses, and what happens after the crew leaves.

Should my house get a DVR or an NVR?

A DVR, digital video recorder, records HD-analog cameras that connect over coax. An NVR, network video recorder, records IP cameras that connect over network cable, and most NVRs also power those cameras through the same cable. If your house already has coax running to the old camera spots from a system installed ten or twenty years ago, a DVR that speaks the modern HD-analog formats (HD-TVI, HD-CVI or AHD) can give you a big jump in picture quality without pulling new cable. If we are running new cable anyway, an NVR with IP cameras is the better long-term choice: one cable per camera, smarter detection, simpler expansion and higher resolutions.

There is also a middle path. Hybrid recorders take both kinds of camera, so a Pearl River cape can keep two coax cameras on the back of the house where the old cable is sound and add new IP cameras on the front where we have a clean route. On our price list, the A, F and C Series are DVR-based and the E and D Series are NVR-based, which is why the quote always starts with what cable you already have.

Does a PoE camera need an outlet next to it, or an electrician?

No outlet and no electrician for the cameras themselves. PoE, power over Ethernet, sends low-voltage power and video down the same Cat6 cable, so the camera on your eave gets everything it needs from the recorder or switch in the basement. That is why PoE CCTV installation is the default on most of our Rockland jobs: there is no plug hanging off the soffit, no transformer in the attic and no high-voltage work outside. A standard PoE port supplies up to 15.4 watts and a PoE+ port up to 30 watts, which covers fixed cameras with infrared; PTZ cameras and cameras with built-in heaters or deterrent lights need more, and we budget the switch for that.

What does need a normal outlet is the recorder, the switch and the battery backup, all in one place. In a split-level that is usually the lower-level utility room; in a store, the office or a back room. If there is no outlet where the equipment should go, a licensed electrician adds one before install day. We are licensed for low-voltage work, and we keep the two trades separate on purpose.

What cable goes outside on a Rockland house, and why not the cheapest roll?

Inside walls we use solid-copper Cat6 for IP cameras. Outside, on a run along the eaves, down a corner board or across to a detached garage, we use outdoor-rated, UV-resistant Cat6, and in the ground we use direct-burial cable or put the run in conduit. For HD-analog systems the cable is RG59 or RG6 coax with a power pair. The cheap rolls sold online are often copper-clad aluminum, which looks the same but carries power badly over distance and corrodes at the connectors, and indoor cable left in the sun cracks within a few seasons.

Every wall penetration is drilled with a slight downward slope toward the outside, sealed, and covered by the camera base or a junction box, so rain running down vinyl siding does not follow the cable into the wall. Every cable is labeled at both ends. That labeling is the difference between a ten-minute repair and a half-day hunt two winters from now.

Where should the camera for the front door go on a raised ranch or split-level?

Beside the door, not above the upper floor. On a raised ranch the front door sits at the landing between the two levels, so a camera up on the soffit looks almost straight down and records the tops of heads. A camera mounted on the wall next to the door at roughly eight feet, angled slightly down the steps, sees faces as people climb toward it, which is what you need when someone takes a package or tries the knob.

The garage on these houses usually sits under the bedrooms, so the second camera goes on the garage corner with a wider lens to cover the driveway and the street end of the walk. A third on the back deck or the slider and a fourth on the side gate finish a typical four-camera layout. Larger split-levels with a separate side entrance to the lower level add one more there.

How do you get cable to the cameras on a 1950s cape with a finished upstairs?

Through the knee-wall spaces. Most Rockland capes have a finished second floor with short walls under the roof slope, and behind those walls is a narrow triangle of attic that runs the length of the house. We get in through the small access doors many capes have, or through a closet, and pull cable along that space to the gable ends and down to the eave cameras. From the basement, a second route usually climbs inside the wall beside the chimney or a plumbing chase.

When a wall is plaster on wood lath, as in many prewar and early postwar houses in Pearl River, Nyack and Haverstraw, fishing cable through it is slow and risks cracks. In those spots we sometimes run a short length of painted raceway along an inside corner or follow a downspout outside instead. You decide which trade-off you prefer, and the quote shows the difference.

Does joining Clarkstown's police camera list let officers see into my recorder?

No. Joining tells the police where your cameras are; it does not give them a way into your system, and for Clarkstown homes and businesses it is worth considering for that reason. The Clarkstown Police Department's voluntary program records only where registered cameras are, so detectives can ask owners near an incident to check footage for a particular date and time. The department's own release says the program does not give remote access to registered cameras, and you decide what to share when they call.

Whether you register or not, the useful preparation is the same. Know how to find a time on the recorder, know how to export a clip to a USB stick or your phone, and keep enough days of recording that a request a week later still has something to find. We show every owner how to do all three at handover. Outside Clarkstown, call your own department's non-emergency line to ask whether it keeps a similar list.

Can a store's lot camera settle a fender-bender or a slip-and-fall claim?

It can help a great deal, if the footage still exists when the claim arrives. Parking-lot bumps, a cart rolling into a car, a customer who says they slipped at the door: on Route 59 and in the Nanuet and Suffern lots, these are the incidents owners most often go back to the recorder for. The trouble is timing. A letter from an insurer or a lawyer can show up weeks after the day in question, long after a two-week drive has overwritten it.

So two habits matter more than camera count. First, a lot camera and a door camera with views wide enough to show the whole event, not just the moment of contact. Second, a rule that whoever hears about an incident exports the clip that day and saves it somewhere safe, even if nobody has complained yet. We show managers how to do that at handover, and for businesses that expect claims we size the drive for a longer retention period. What the footage proves is for you, your insurer and your attorney to judge.

Will new cameras clash with the alarm system's motion sensors on the same wall?

They can share a wall without trouble if they are planned together. Many Rockland homes and businesses already have a burglar alarm with passive infrared motion sensors high in the corners, and those are often exactly where a camera wants to go. We keep cameras clear of the sensors' field of view so a camera does not block a sensor, we do not aim a camera's infrared illuminator straight into a sensor a few feet away, and we keep CCTV cable separate from the alarm wiring so a later alarm service call does not disturb the cameras.

On the dealership job in our photos, two of the cameras sit on the same tall walls as white motion sensors, mounted above them with clear space between. If you would like the two systems to talk, for example so an alarm event bookmarks the camera footage or sends you a clip, many current recorders can take an alarm input; we wire that only where your alarm company agrees.

Can I see each camera's actual view before it is screwed down?

Yes, and we prefer it. During the install we power each camera on a temporary connection, hold it at the planned spot and show you the live view on a phone or the recorder monitor before drilling the final holes. You see what the driveway camera actually covers, whether the neighbor's floodlight washes out the side gate view, and whether the front door camera catches faces on the steps. Moving a camera a foot at that stage costs nothing; moving it after the mount and the sealed cable entry are finished means patching siding.

We repeat the check after dark for cameras that depend on infrared, because night vision changes what a camera sees. A view that looks perfect at noon can glare off a white garage door at night, and a slight angle change fixes it.

What does the per-channel number table on my recorder screen tell me?

It shows how much data each camera is sending at that moment, usually in kilobits per second. On the dealership recorder in our hero photo, the live channels ranged from 86 Kb/s on the quietest views to 453 Kb/s on the busiest, about five times apart, and the unused channels D24 through D32 read zero. Busy scenes with people moving, waving trees or rain send more; empty hallways send less.

For an owner it is a quick health check. A camera reading zero when it should be live is offline. One camera reading far above all the others with nothing happening in its view may be set to a frame rate or quality it does not need, and it is quietly eating the hard drive. We check that table before we leave every multi-camera job, and you can glance at it any time something looks off.

What should cameras at a house of worship cover when the lot fills for services and holidays?

The entrances people actually use, the lot entrance from the road, the walk from the lot to the door, and any side door that stays unlocked during services. A congregation in Monsey, New City or Spring Valley may go from an empty lot to a full one in fifteen minutes, so the lot camera needs a wide view at height, and the entrance cameras need to see faces at the doors, where everyone has to pass close to the lens.

Two scheduling details matter for these buildings. Many are empty most of the week, so alerts after hours are worth more than live viewing during services, and some congregations do not want anyone using phones during holy days, so automatic recording and alerts to a designated person who is not attending matter more than an app everyone checks. We set schedules to match the calendar you give us.

Can each person in the family get only the alerts they care about?

Yes. Each person gets their own user on the recorder or app, and each user can have their own cameras, alert types and quiet hours. A parent might want person alerts from the driveway and the back door only at night; a teenager might only need the front door camera; a grandparent who watches the kids after school might only want the side gate between 3 and 6 p.m. Nobody needs to share one login, and you can remove a user without changing everyone's password.

For businesses the same setup gives managers alerts and playback, while staff can see live views at the counter without being able to delete or export footage. We write down who has which permissions in the handover notes so you can change it later.

How should a strip-center store cover a rear delivery door on a lane it shares with other tenants?

With a camera mounted on your own wall, looking along your own door and the stretch of lane directly behind it, high enough that a box truck does not block it. On Route 59 and Route 303 strip centers, the rear lane is often shared by every tenant and owned by the landlord, so the lease decides what you can mount outside. Cameras on your wall facing your door are usually acceptable; cameras that sweep the whole lane can need the landlord's consent.

Inside, a second camera faces the back door from the stockroom, so every delivery and every exit is seen from both sides. The pair answers the usual questions: who opened the door, what came in or went out, and which vehicle was at the door at that time. Ask your landlord before we mount anything on a shared surface; we can supply a short camera plan for that conversation.

How do you mount a camera on the foam-and-stucco front of a strip-center store?

Carefully, because that finish is thinner than it looks. Many Route 59 and Route 303 storefronts are clad in EIFS, a synthetic stucco laid over foam board, and a camera screwed straight into it pulls loose in the first windy winter and lets water into the wall behind. We find the framing or the sheathing behind the foam, use a mounting block or standoff sized for EIFS, and seal every fastener and the cable entry with a sealant made for that finish.

Where the facade belongs to the landlord, which is usual in a strip center, we mount under the canopy or on the sign band when the lease allows it, and keep cable runs inside the store wherever possible. That keeps the landlord's facade warranty intact, keeps the camera out of reach from the sidewalk and puts the cable where no one can cut it from outside.

Recording, storage and the network

Compression. The recorders we install use H.265, the newer video codec, whenever the camera on the other end supports it. Next to H.264 it typically halves the file size of the same scene, and that saving is what lets a 4-camera house-brand package hold its two weeks on a single drive.

Retention. Every system we sell includes two weeks of recording. If a business policy, an insurer or a landlord wants a month, we add drive space rather than lowering the quality, and we show you the real number of days after the first week of recording.

Recording modes. Continuous recording on the cameras that matter most, such as the register or the front door, and event recording on quiet views, such as a back hallway, stretches the drive further without losing what you need.

Network security. Every default password is changed, the recorder's firmware is updated on install day, and remote access goes through the manufacturer's app rather than an open port on your router. On business systems the cameras sit on their own network segment or VLAN, away from the point-of-sale and office computers.

Placement. We plan from the outside in: where a person or a car first reaches the property, every door that opens, and the places where something valuable sits. Faces at doors want a camera at about eight to ten feet; lots and drives want height and a narrower lens; and every view is checked in daylight and again in the dark.

AI Overview Reality Check

Where AI Answers Miss on Rockland County CCTV

Search summaries, chatbots and national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give camera advice for an average American house. Here is where that advice goes wrong in Rockland, and what we do instead.

“Rockland is a low-crime suburb, so a doorbell camera is all you need”

Rockland is quieter than the city, but the state's 2024 figures still count 3,346 larcenies and 255 burglaries in the county, with Clarkstown alone logging 1,427 larcenies. Package theft, car break-ins in driveways and shoplifting on Route 59 do not care about the county's reputation.

A doorbell sees one door from one angle and usually records to a cloud account in short clips. It does not see the driveway, the side gate, the back slider or the detached garage, and it misses the seconds before motion wakes it.

A small wired system with four cameras covers the doors and the drive, records continuously to a recorder in your basement, and still works with the doorbell you already like.

“PoE cameras keep running in a blackout because the cable carries power”

The cable carries power from the recorder or the switch, and those plug into the wall. When O&R loses a line, as it did for 2,596 Rockland customers on the night of December 19, 2025, the switch stops, and every PoE camera stops with it.

The fix is a UPS battery under the recorder, the switch and the modem. It keeps recording through short outages and lets the recorder shut down cleanly before the battery runs out, which protects the drive.

If the house has a standby generator, we put the equipment on a circuit the generator covers, so the battery only has to bridge the seconds before the generator starts.

“If you register cameras with the police, officers can watch them live”

Not in Clarkstown. The town's voluntary registration program lists where cameras are so detectives can ask owners for footage from a date and time. The department states plainly that it does not get remote access to registered cameras.

Nothing about registration changes how your system is installed or who holds the passwords. You stay the administrator, and you decide what to share when someone asks.

What registration does reward is preparation: an accurate clock, enough days of storage and an owner who knows how to export a clip. Those are part of every handover we do.

“National average prices tell you what CCTV should cost in Rockland”

National cost guides average together cheap DIY kits, single cameras and big commercial jobs from every state, then call the middle a typical price. They do not know whether your camera wires go through an open basement in New City or through plaster in a Nyack village house.

Our Rockland prices are written down: $1,099 for a house-brand 4-camera A Series system with recording, up to $1,541 for the 4K C and D Series, $1,228 to $2,500 for a 4- to 8-camera home system on an NVR, and $1,500 to $5,000+ for commercial systems.

Anything that changes the price, such as concealed wiring, conduit or a longer run, is listed separately in the quote after we confirm the layout, so you can compare it line by line with anyone else's.

“Any recorder keeps accurate time on its own”

Many recorders ship with a clock that drifts, a time zone set to wherever they were built, and daylight saving switched off. After a power cut, some revert to a default date until someone corrects them.

That matters in Rockland because police and insurers ask for footage by date and time. A clip stamped forty minutes off is harder to find and easier to question.

We sync the recorder to an internet time server, set the Eastern time zone with daylight saving, and compare it to a phone before we leave. On properties without internet, we set it by hand and show you where to check it.

“Put the recorder in the attic so burglars cannot find it”

An attic hides the recorder, but a Rockland attic in July is one of the hottest places in the house, and heat is what kills hard drives and power supplies early. In winter, an unheated space swings the other way.

We would rather put the recorder in a conditioned closet, a lower-level utility room or a locked office, where it stays cool, sits near the modem and the panel, and can be reached when you need to export a clip in a hurry.

If hiding the recorder is the priority, we can lock it in a small cabinet, keep it out of sight lines from windows, and add an off-site copy of key events so a stolen recorder does not take everything with it.

“More cameras always means better coverage”

Ten cameras pointed at the same front yard give you ten views of the same lawn. On a quarter-acre lot in Nanuet or a narrow two-family lot in Spring Valley, the useful views are few: each door, the driveway, the side passage and the back of the house.

Extra cameras also add storage, bandwidth and more alerts to sort through, which on a busy street turns into notification fatigue.

We plan each camera around one job, such as identifying a face at the door or seeing a car enter the drive, and leave spare channels on the recorder so you can add views later if a real gap appears.

Rockland County CCTV Installation FAQ

Can some cameras record all the time while others record only when something moves?
+

Yes. Each camera on the recorder has its own schedule. Most Rockland owners keep continuous recording on the front door, the driveway or a store register and set quieter views, such as a back hallway or a side yard, to record on person or vehicle events. That keeps the important views complete and makes the hard drive last longer.

What happens to the footage when the recorder's hard drive is full?
+

The recorder overwrites the oldest footage first and keeps going, so it never stops recording because the drive is full. That is why the number of days the drive holds matters. Every system we install keeps at least two weeks, and if you need a clip kept longer, you export it to a USB stick or your phone before it rolls off.

I bought a Rockland house that already has cameras. How do I get the previous owner off the system?
+

The safest route is a reset done in person: we identify the recorder, reset or recover the admin account, remove the old owner's app access and any cloud link, change every camera password and set the system up under your name. If the old recorder is too dated to secure properly, we tell you which cameras and cable can be reused with a new one.

Will I see a camera plan before I pay the deposit?
+

Yes. The written quote lists each camera, where it goes and what it is meant to show, along with the cable route, the recorder spot and every priced item. For larger houses and businesses we add a simple marked-up sketch or photo set. You approve the plan first; the 50% deposit only books the date.

Is there any CCTV work you turn down?
+

Yes. We do not hide cameras in bathrooms, bedrooms, changing areas or anywhere people expect privacy, we do not aim cameras into a neighbor's windows or yard, and we do not switch on audio recording at a business without the owner confirming it is lawful there. We will also talk you out of Wi-Fi cameras for spots they cannot reliably reach.

What if I forget the password to my CCTV recorder?
+

Call us. Password recovery usually needs someone at the recorder with the right tools and proof that you own the system, and some recorders need the manufacturer's reset code. On a service visit we recover or reset the admin account, set a new password you choose, and update the app on your phones so nothing else breaks.

Do outdoor cameras keep working through a hard January cold snap in Rockland?
+

Weather-rated outdoor cameras are built for winter; the cameras in our E and D Series are rated IP67 and the C Series IP66. The weak points in deep cold are cheap power supplies, cracked cable jackets and snow building up on a lens, so we use outdoor-rated cable, mount cameras under eaves where we can, and avoid long, unprotected outdoor runs.

Should a home EV charger or a business's charging spaces have a camera?
+

If the charger is outside, it is worth covering. Charging cables and connectors are tempting targets, and a camera on the charger also shows who parked and plugged in. In a driveway the driveway camera can usually be aimed to include the charger; at a business, the charging spaces are often covered by the lot camera with a slightly tighter view.

What does H.265 on a recorder spec sheet mean for me?
+

H.265 is the video compression standard most current cameras and recorders use. It stores about the same picture as the older H.264 in roughly half the space in typical scenes, which means more days of recording on the same drive and lighter streams when you watch from your phone. We switch it on wherever both the camera and the recorder support it.

Does a CCTV system add much to the electric bill?
+

Not much. A fixed camera typically draws a few watts, well under the 15.4 watts a standard PoE port can supply, and the recorder, switch and battery backup add a modest amount on top. A typical home system runs around the clock on roughly the power of a few household light bulbs, though cameras with heaters, PTZ motors or deterrent lights draw more.
