The Neighborhood

Why Bayside Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Bayside looks suburban from the street, but its living rooms were built in three quite different eras, and each one treats a soundbar bracket differently. Wikipedia's account of the neighbourhood places it in Queens Community District 11, between Little Neck Bay and the Cross Island Parkway on one side and Francis Lewis Boulevard and Auburndale on the other, with Oakland Gardens and Hollis Hills usually counted in. It grew as a resort and farm district, gained film-star houses in the 1920s and 1930s, and then filled up quickly after the Second World War, largely because the Bayside station on the Long Island Rail Road's Port Washington Branch put Penn Station one train away. The result is a neighbourhood of detached and attached houses, two-family homes, low-rise garden-apartment co-ops and a handful of towers by the bay — and very few of the glass-and-concrete rooms that most soundbar advice now assumes.

The oldest layer is the inter-war house. Bayside Hills, the subdivision between 48th Avenue, the Long Island Expressway, 211th Street and Springfield Boulevard, was born in 1936 on what had been a farm and then a golf course, according to the Queens Chronicle's history of the enclave, and today counts more than 1,200 households behind its gatehouses and planted street malls. Houses from that period across Bayside were generally finished with plaster rather than drywall, and plaster is where most of our planning goes: it is hard, it can crack if it is drilled carelessly, and depending on what it was applied over — wood strips, gypsum lath or metal mesh — it can leave an ordinary stud finder either silent or beeping the whole way across the wall. We find the framing by other means before the bracket goes anywhere.

The biggest layer is the 1950s. American Community Survey figures compiled by Point2Homes put roughly 31 percent of Bayside's homes in that single decade, more than in any other. That is the era of the Bay Terrace garden apartments — nine co-op sections, Habitat magazine reports, built on land owned by Cord Meyer Development — and of the garden co-ops of Oakland Gardens, low brick buildings set around lawns and courtyards. It is also the era of many of the Capes, ranches and two-family houses on the side streets. A garden-apartment living room may share a wall with the next unit, a floor with the apartment below and a ceiling with the one above, and it sits in a co-op whose board and managing agent have their own alteration rules. Most of the questions we hear from Bay Terrace and Oakland Gardens are about those two things: the neighbours and the paperwork.

Then there is ownership. Point2Homes' summary of the same survey counts about two-thirds of Bayside's occupied homes as owner-occupied, and more than a fifth of its housing units sit in two-unit buildings. In practice that means many visits are to a family that owns the house and rents out a floor, or to a tenant whose landlord lives upstairs. The owner decides what goes into the walls; the tenant needs the owner's yes before a bracket is fixed; and the subwoofer is placed with the other household in mind, not just the one paying for the bar.

Finally, Bayside works as well as sleeps. Bell Boulevard is the neighbourhood's main street, lined with bars and restaurants for eight blocks north of Northern Boulevard, and Northern Boulevard itself carries a long run of restaurants, including Korean restaurants and all-you-can-eat barbecue rooms in and around the 11361 ZIP code. Those rooms want a TV people can follow without anyone shouting, in spaces with hard floors, open kitchens and, at a barbecue table, grill smoke in the air. We quote them as carefully as a living room, because the conditions are just as particular.

What the walls are made of, house by house

Plaster in the inter-war houses. A 1930s Bayside living room was almost always plastered, and the plaster was applied over something: thin wood strips, panels of gypsum lath, or expanded metal mesh. Each behaves differently. Wood-lath plaster gives a stud finder false readings because the strips run across the whole wall; gypsum lath reads more like drywall; metal mesh can make a scanner light up everywhere. So we do not trust a single tool. We scan the full width of the wall, look for nail heads with a strong magnet, check where the outlet box is fixed, and confirm with one small pilot hole where the bracket will hide it. Plaster is a $47.25 line on our Queens table because it takes longer and needs more care.

Textured finishes. Some houses, particularly those finished in a Spanish or Mediterranean manner, have a sand, swirl or trowelled texture on their walls. Texture does not stop a bracket, but it changes how it sits and what sticks to it. We shim the bracket where the surface rises and falls, so the bar stays level and the screws are not levered against a bump, and we fix cord covers with screws rather than relying on adhesive tape alone.

Brick around the fireplace. Many Bayside living rooms are arranged around a brick chimney breast, and it is a popular place for the TV. Brick takes a sleeve or screw anchor well when the hole is drilled into the face of the brick rather than the mortar joint, and the bar can sit just below the screen on the same breast. There is no cavity to hide leads in, so cables come down in a painted channel or travel sideways to the cavity wall beside the breast. On the Queens wall table, brick or stone is $68.25.

Block and concrete in the garden co-ops and towers. Some garden-apartment partitions, basement walls and every tower's structure are block or poured concrete. A hammer drill sets anchors into the solid part of each block, and the bracket goes on just as firmly as it would on timber. Hidden wiring is rarely possible in solid masonry, so a painted channel is the usual finish. Concrete or cinder block is $78.75 on our table.

Newer drywall. Renovated rooms, extensions and the houses built since the 2005 rezoning are usually drywall on wood studs, which is the easiest wall in Bayside to work with and carries no surcharge. Before drilling, we ask whether the builder or a previous owner ran anything in that wall we should know about, and we probe the bay before promising hidden leads.

Rules that apply to a business soundbar in Bayside

  • A game's commentary counts as commercial music. Under the Department of Environmental Protection's summary of the Noise Code, sound from a business may not exceed 42 decibels when measured inside a neighbouring home; overnight, from 10 PM to 7 AM, it also may not rise more than 7 decibels above the street's background level measured 15 feet or more away, and there is a separate test for bass that pervades a building. A Bell Boulevard bar with apartments over or behind it is therefore best served by a speech-tuned soundbar with little or no subwoofer.
  • Mind the aisle. The 2010 ADA Standards, section 307.2, allow a wall-mounted object between 27 and 80 inches off the floor to stick out no more than 4 inches into a route people walk along. A screen plus a soundbar exceeds that, so in a restaurant passage it is mounted above 80 inches or placed behind the service counter.
  • Power outlets are outside our licence. We are licensed for low-voltage work — HDMI, the bar's connections and its wireless links — which does not itself need a Department of Buildings permit. If the restaurant wants a socket behind the screen, a licensed electrician installs it before our visit; otherwise the screen goes near an existing outlet.
  • Paperwork for the landlord. A shopping-centre or storefront lease will often require any contractor to carry insurance naming the property owner and its agent. Forward the exact names required and we issue that certificate at no charge, normally within the day.
  • Keep gear out of the kitchen's air. Bars and their power strips stay out of the plume of a range hood or a table grill and away from the floor that gets mopped every night; the right spot is usually on a dining-room wall well back from the cooking.

Bayside Hills and the inter-war houses

The stud finder beeps all the way across our 1930s Bayside Hills living-room wall — what is behind the plaster, and where does the bracket go?

Almost certainly plaster over metal mesh, or plaster thick enough that a basic scanner cannot see past it. Houses of that period were plastered over one of three backings: thin wood strips, panels of gypsum lath, or expanded metal lath. Metal lath is the one that makes an electronic finder light up from corner to corner, because the scanner reads the mesh rather than the studs behind it. A mounting company's guide to plaster walls puts it plainly: electronic stud finders misread plaster, and metal mesh, nails and reinforcement create false readings.

So we switch tools. A strong magnet run slowly across the wall finds the lath nails or screws, which line up vertically over each stud; the side of the nearest outlet box shows where one stud is; and a deep-scanning sensor narrows the rest. Then we drill one small pilot hole where the bracket will cover it and read what comes out — wood shavings, gypsum or metal — before choosing the fixing. Through mesh we drill slowly with a sharp masonry bit and no hammer action, so the plaster key is not shaken loose around the hole.

Plaster on metal lath is usually strong, and a bracket screwed through it into a stud holds very well. Where the bar has to sit between studs, a toggle sized for the bracket spreads the load behind the plaster. If an old patch turns out to be soft or hollow, a plywood backer board ($89.25) fixed into sound framing carries the bracket instead. On the Queens table, plaster adds $47.25 to the $135.45 mount.

Our Mediterranean-style house has heavily textured plaster walls — will the bracket sit flat, and will a cord cover stay stuck to that surface?

The bracket will, with a little work; the cord cover needs screws. A trowelled, swirled or sand-finish texture rises and falls by a few millimetres across a hand's width. A bracket pulled hard against a ridge can rock or twist, and the bar then sits a fraction off level with the screen. We find the high points under the bracket's feet and pack the low ones with thin shims, so the screws pull the bracket square rather than levering it against a bump.

Adhesive-backed channels are made for smooth paint. On a deep texture the tape only touches the peaks and lets go, often after the first warm spell. We fix the channel with small screws and anchors at intervals along its length, cut it neatly where it meets the baseboard, and leave it ready for your painter to match the wall. If you would rather keep a textured wall completely untouched, a bar on the console or on a TV-attached bracket needs only the holes the TV mount already uses.

One caution applies to any textured finish applied before the late 1970s. Washington State's health department, summarising the federal position, notes that asbestos may be present in textured paint and in patching compounds used on wall and ceiling joints, and that their use was banned in 1977. A handful of small bracket holes drilled slowly with dust collected at the bit is a very different matter from sanding a wall, but if you know or suspect an old textured coat, have it tested before any larger job such as chasing a cable into the wall, and tell us before we arrive.

Post-war houses and two-families

We want TVs back to back on the wall between our living room and the den — can both share one cable route without one room's bass coming through to the other?

They can share the wall; they should not share a stud bay. When two TVs, two brackets and two sets of in-wall plates sit directly back to back in the same cavity, the cavity behaves like a little drum: the bar and sub in one room push sound straight into the other through two thin layers of board or plaster and an open hole. Offsetting the two mounting positions by at least one stud bay, so each room's plates open into a different cavity, keeps most of that sound where it belongs and costs nothing extra.

The cables can still meet in one place. A cabinet or closet on one side of the wall, or the basement below a ranch or split-level, can hold the cable box, the router and the streaming boxes for both screens, with a run of up to 25 feet ($103.95) bringing each TV's HDMI to it. The subwoofer, if there is one, goes on an outside wall of its own room, not against the shared partition.

On the Queens menu, a living-room bar on our bracket with the leads hidden in the wall is the $402.14 Premium combination, and a den bar on its own bracket with a painted channel is $224.69 + $61.95 = $286.64. Together that is $688.78 on drywall or plaster-on-gypsum walls over wood studs, before any surcharge for older plaster or a cable run to a shared cabinet. If one room is a tenant's, the owner decides about the shared wall and the tenant's bar is planned with them.

The garden co-ops

Our garden co-op's basement is shared space under several apartments — can the soundbar cable go down there and come back up behind the TV?

Only with the co-op's written approval, and many co-ops will say no. In a garden-apartment building the basement, its ceiling and the floor structure between you and it are usually the corporation's property rather than part of your apartment. Drilling down through your floor, running a cable across a common basement and coming back up behind the TV is an alteration to building elements, and it creates a new opening between apartments and common space that the board, its managing agent and its engineer will want to know about first. Some sections allow it with conditions such as fire-stopping the holes; some do not allow it at all.

If you want to try, ask the managing agent for the alteration application before we book. We can supply a written description of the route, the cable type and how each opening would be sealed, plus a free certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the agent. We do not drill a floor or a common-area wall in a co-op without that approval in hand.

Most Bay Terrace and Oakland Gardens owners end up not needing it. A cable run of up to 25 feet ($103.95) along the baseboard, round the room and into a cabinet usually reaches where the boxes live, a painted channel ($61.95) tidies the drop from the bar, and wireless rears and subwoofers only need a socket nearby. Everything stays inside your own apartment, which is the part of the building you control.

Bell Boulevard and Northern Boulevard

Our Northern Boulevard barbecue restaurant has a grill at every table — will smoke and grease in the air ruin a soundbar?

Not if it is placed well, but placement is everything. A dining room with table grills carries fine grease and smoke even with good extraction, and the film it leaves builds up on whatever sits in the moving air — ceilings, light fittings, and the fabric grille of a soundbar. A bar mounted in the path between the grills and the extraction, or right above a row of tables, will end up coated and its controls sticky; a bar on a side wall away from the cooking, at the height of the screen, stays much cleaner.

We plan the screen and bar position with the airflow in mind: away from the exhaust path and the range hood, out of the direct heat over the grills, and off the floor that is mopped every night, with the power strip fixed to the wall rather than left by the baseboard. A bar with a metal grille is easier to wipe down than one wrapped in cloth, and a short monthly wipe with the power off keeps it presentable. We would also set the bar for speech at a fixed level, because sizzling grills and conversation compete with the commentary, not the bass.

Two cautions. Never mount anything inside a kitchen hood's capture area or near a fire-suppression nozzle; those spaces belong to the hood installer and the fire inspector. And if the screens are on a wall shared with a neighbouring business or a flat upstairs, the city's commercial music limits still apply to the TV's sound.

Our Bell Boulevard bar runs the games with captions and the sound off — when does adding a soundbar actually help?

When people at one end of the room want to hear the commentary and everyone else is happy talking. Captions do a fine job on a busy Friday when the room is loud and the action on screen speaks for itself; a soundbar earns its place at the moments when the sound matters — a fight night, a playoff overtime, the quieter afternoon hours when regulars come in for a particular match. A bar aimed down at the seats nearest one screen, set to its speech or dialogue mode, can make that screen audible to the tables in front of it without turning the whole room into a contest of volumes.

The set-up matters more than the model. We mount the bar under the screen where the listeners are, not the screen nearest the door; set a fixed maximum level and lock the buttons where the bar allows, so the volume does not creep up through the night; leave the captions on for everyone else; and route the HDMI so the bar follows whichever source the screen is showing. The sub, if there is one, stays low or switched off, because bass is what carries through the floor and ceiling.

Where there are flats above or behind the bar, the Department of Environmental Protection's commercial music rules apply to TV sound as much as to a playlist, measured inside the nearby home. A bar tuned for speech at a sensible level is usually the most a busy room can use anyway.

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Bayside

A word on method first. We could not load Google's AI summaries while researching this page, so we do not pretend to quote them. Headings in quotation marks are things a cost guide or a published source actually says; headings without them paraphrase advice that circulates widely and that search summaries and chatbots tend to repeat. Under each is what it means in a Bayside home or business.

“Professional TV mounting costs between $153 to $353 on average”, with a soundbar mount added for $30 to $100

Angi's cost guide gives those national figures, and a summary built on them will tell you a soundbar adds a modest sum to any TV job. As national ranges they are not unreasonable, and they are often the first numbers a Bayside homeowner sees.

What a national add-on cannot know is your wall. On our Queens menu the soundbar wall mount is $135.45 including connection and set-up; plaster adds $47.25, brick $68.25 and block $78.75; our Sanus bracket is $89.24 if the box came without one; and hiding the leads is $177.45, or a painted channel $61.95. A Bayside Hills living room and a newer drywalled den can therefore cost quite different amounts for what sounds like the same job.

The fair test is a written quote that names your wall and your finish. Ask every installer for one; ours breaks the job into the same published lines shown on this page, so nothing is hidden inside a single round number.

Use the anchors that come in the box

Many quick guides treat the plastic plugs packed with a soundbar as the standard fixing, and for drywall over studs they often are adequate for a light bar. Summaries repeat that as if it applied to every wall.

Bayside's walls are rarely that simple. In inter-war plaster a small plastic plug can spin in a crumbly hole or crack the key around it; in brick it needs the right bit and depth; in block it may land in a hollow core. The weight of a bar is modest, but a bracket also takes knocks from cleaning, children and the occasional elbow.

We choose the fixing after the scan and the pilot hole: a screw into a stud where one lines up, a toggle sized to the load behind plaster or board, sleeve anchors in brick and block, and a plywood backer board ($89.25) where the surface is too weak to hold anything directly. The in-box hardware goes back in the bag for you to keep.

“Permits: Required only for electrical work or structural changes”

Angi's guide says that about TV and soundbar work, and as a statement about city permits it is broadly right: a bracket and a bar need no Buildings permit, and our low-voltage licence covers the signal wiring.

The trap is reading “no permit” as “no permission.” In a Bay Terrace or Oakland Gardens garden co-op, or a tower like the Bay Club, the proprietary lease and the house rules decide what counts as an alteration, and many boards want an application, a certificate of insurance and agreed working hours even for a bracket. In a two-family house the owner decides for the whole building.

So the first question in a co-op is not “does the city need a permit?” but “what does our managing agent need?” We supply the description and the certificate; the approval itself comes from your board.

Wireless surround speakers mean no more wires

Wireless rear speakers and subwoofers are sold on the promise of no cables, and summaries often stop there, as if a full surround system could be set up anywhere in the room with nothing to plug in.

The audio travels wirelessly; the power does not. Each rear speaker and each sub still needs a mains socket. In many older Bayside living rooms the sofa floats in the middle of the room or backs onto a window, and the nearest outlets are on the walls. That is where cords end up crossing the floor if nobody plans them.

We plan the rears' power before the speakers come out of the box: a socket behind the sofa if there is one, leads routed along the baseboard and under a rug edge if not, and stands placed where nobody will trip. A new outlet, if you want one, is an electrician's job and is booked before our visit.

“Why You Should Use the Same SSID for 2.4GHz and 5GHz Wi-Fi”

Network vendors publish that advice, and for everyday use it is sound: one network name lets phones and laptops move between bands without fuss. Summaries carry it over to every device in the house.

Some streaming soundbars and their apps are fussier during first-time setup, especially when the router steers devices between bands on its own. When a bar will not join, a router maker's own instructions for band steering describe the fix: turn steering off and give the two bands different names so you can choose.

We do that only if your bar needs it, only with your say-so, and we can put the names back afterwards. A bar that plays the TV's sound over HDMI needs no Wi-Fi at all; the network matters only for streaming music, app control and updates.

Turn on night mode and the neighbours will not hear a thing

Night mode, or dynamic-range compression, is a genuinely useful setting: it keeps quiet speech audible while taming sudden explosions. It is widely described as the answer to late-night viewing in shared buildings.

In a two-storey garden-apartment building or a two-family house, though, what travels is mostly low bass through the structure, and night mode does not always reduce the subwoofer's output on its own. A sub sitting on a floor that is the ceiling of the bedroom below, or pushed against a party wall, can still be felt next door with night mode on.

So we combine settings with placement: the sub on an outside wall and on a solid part of the floor, its level set for the room rather than the showroom, and night mode or the bar's own bass control shown to you as one button for the evening. In the most sensitive layouts, a bar without a separate sub is the honest recommendation.

You cannot hide wires in an old house

A common assumption, repeated in summaries, is that older houses rule out concealed wiring and that surface channels are the only tidy option before 1950.

Many Bayside houses of the 1930s and the post-war years have plaster or early gypsum walls on wood studs, which means hollow bays that cables can travel through. What gets in the way is not age but specifics: horizontal blocking between studs, an old pipe or vent, a chimney breast, a wall that turns out to be brick, or plaster that is too fragile to cut cleanly.

We probe the bay before promising anything, then quote the code-compliant in-wall kit ($177.45) where it will work and a painted channel ($61.95) where it will not. Either way you know which it is before the drill comes out.

Garden Co-ops

A Soundbar Checklist for Bay Terrace and Oakland Gardens Garden Apartments

Garden apartments were built to feel like houses, with lawns, courtyards and low brick buildings, but they are shared buildings with shared rules. A soundbar in one is a small job that goes smoothly when a few things are settled first. This is the list we work through with co-op owners before booking.

  • Ask for the alteration rules. Each section or co-op has its own. Some treat a bracket as routine maintenance; others want an application, a description of the work and the board's sign-off. The managing agent's office will know.
  • Get the certificate wording right. If a certificate of insurance is required, ask exactly which names it must show — usually the corporation and the managing agent — and send it to us. It is free and usually goes out the same day.
  • Check the working hours. Many co-ops limit contractor work to weekdays and set hours for noisy work. Drilling a bracket takes minutes, but it still counts.
  • Think about all four directions. A garden apartment may have neighbours through the side walls, upstairs and downstairs. The TV wall is better on a wall you do not share where the layout allows it, and the sub belongs on an outside wall and a solid part of the floor.
  • Keep everything inside your apartment. Basements, attics, hallways and outside walls usually belong to the corporation. Cable runs along your own baseboards, painted channels and wireless rears avoid them completely.
  • Expect plaster or block. Garden apartments of the 1950s were commonly finished in plaster, and some walls are masonry. We bring fixings for both and confirm with a pilot hole.
  • Keep the paperwork. Hold on to the approval letter, the certificate and our itemised invoice. Boards sometimes ask about past alterations when an apartment is sold.

None of this needs to delay the sound. Until approval comes through, a bar on the console with the TV remote running it gives you nearly everything; the bracket can follow later without redoing the connections.

Two Households

Two Households, One House: Soundbars in Bayside Two-Families

More than a fifth of Bayside's homes sit in two-unit buildings, and a soundbar in one of them affects both households. Many of these houses are owned by a family living on one floor and renting the other; some are shared by relatives across generations. Either way, three questions decide the job.

  • Who decides about the walls? The owner, always. A tenant can have a console setup at any time, but a bracket, a hole or a hidden lead needs the owner's go-ahead, ideally by text or email so we can see it on the day.
  • Whose ceiling is under the subwoofer? If the living room sits over the other household's bedroom, the sub goes on a solid part of the floor near an outside wall, or the bar is chosen without a separate sub. If you live downstairs, the sub's bass rises less, but a bar on a ceiling-height bracket is still heard upstairs through the joists.
  • Which walls carry the other household's services? Water pipes, gas lines and the upstairs unit's wiring can run through walls on either floor. We scan and probe before drilling, and we do not hide leads in a wall whose contents nobody can confirm.

The same thinking applies to attached houses that share a party wall with the next home. The bar can go on that wall; the subwoofer should not. And if both households want soundbars, booking them at one visit means one set of decisions about the shared floor and one trip on the menu.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Finding a Stud Behind 1930s Plaster

What follows is the routine our installers use on old plaster in general, written down; it does not describe one household's job.

Start by not trusting the scanner. On old plaster it will either find nothing or find everything, and both answers are wrong. Run a strong magnet slowly across the wall at bracket height and mark every point where it grabs; the lath nails line up in vertical rows over the studs, usually at regular spacing, and a row that breaks the pattern is often a window or door header rather than a stud.

Check the marks against the outlet box, which is normally fixed to the side of a stud, and against the trim around the nearest door. Then drill one small pilot hole where the bracket will hide it, slowly and without hammer action, and look at what comes out: pale wood means a stud, grey grit and then air means plaster on lath over a cavity, and a bite of metal means mesh. Only then choose the fixing, and if the plaster crumbles around the pilot, stop and talk about a backer board.

Before leaving, hold the level on the bar one more time with the TV on. Old houses settle, floors slope and ceilings are rarely true, so the bar is levelled to the bottom edge of the screen, not to the ceiling line.

Bayside Soundbar Installation FAQ

How much is a soundbar on the brick chimney breast of a Bayside house, with the cable box moved into a nearby closet?
+

On the Queens menu: $135.45 to wall-mount the bar, $89.24 for our Sanus bracket if the bar came without one, $68.25 for drilling brick, and $103.95 for a cable run of up to 25 feet to the closet. That is $396.89 in all. If the leads come down the face of the brick rather than travelling sideways, add a painted channel at $61.95.

A relative brought our soundbar back from Korea — can it be plugged in and mounted here?
+

Usually, but check the power rating first. South Korea uses 220-volt, 60-hertz power with round-pin Type C and F plugs, while US outlets supply 120 volts. If the label on the bar or its power adapter reads 100–240V, a plug adapter or a US power cord is all it needs; if it reads 220V only, it needs a step-up converter rated for its wattage, and we will not plug it straight into a US outlet. The menus, app and warranty may also be region-specific, so check those before the visit.

Our TV's menu has nothing called HDMI-CEC — what is the setting actually called?
+

Each maker uses its own name. Samsung calls it Anynet+, LG SimpLink, Sony BRAVIA Sync, Philips EasyLink, Sharp Aquos Link, Toshiba CE-Link or Regza Link, and Panasonic VIERA Link or HDAVI Control, while Vizio and TCL simply say CEC. Switch it on together with ARC or eARC and set the TV's sound output to the HDMI or receiver option, and the TV remote will run the bar's power and volume.

Our soundbar already works over an optical cable — is it worth switching it to HDMI?
+

Usually, if the TV has an ARC or eARC socket. Over HDMI the TV remote controls the bar's volume and power, the bar switches on with the TV, and it can receive formats an optical link cannot carry, such as Dolby Atmos inside Dolby Digital Plus over ARC or lossless audio over eARC. If the TV has no ARC socket, optical remains the right link. On its own, the change is a setup-only visit at the Queens rate; during an installation it is part of the job.

The HDMI sockets on our new TV face sideways and the cable sticks out past the edge once it's on the wall — can that be hidden?
+

Yes. A right-angle HDMI adapter, or a cable with an angled head, turns the plug flat against the back of the set so nothing shows past the edge, and the lead then runs down behind the screen to the bar. For the eARC socket we use a cable or adapter rated for the job, because cheap adapters are a common cause of audio dropouts. We check the clearance between the TV and the wall before choosing which one.

Can we switch back to the TV's own speakers for the early-morning news without unplugging the soundbar?
+

Yes. Nearly every TV lets you choose its sound output in the audio menu, between its internal speakers and the HDMI or receiver option the bar uses, and many put that choice in a quick-settings panel a couple of presses away. The bar simply goes to standby until you switch back. We show whoever uses the TV in the morning exactly where that setting is before we leave.
