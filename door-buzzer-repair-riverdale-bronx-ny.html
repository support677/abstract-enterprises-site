TERMINOLOGY

The Riverdale Repair Lexicon

Agreed names make honest minutes: entry buzzer, apartment entry system, building entrance system, visitor entry system, door release system, building access system — on the ridge those labels stretch from a two-family’s front vestibule buzzer to a tower’s lobby entrance buzzer, and beneath each sits the same chain: a door release button, the electric strike it fires, the buzzer transformer and buzzer power supply behind the circuit, the entry system wiring climbing the riser, and on current platforms a remote door release riding a phone.

Console. The lobby’s multi-line brain, often original to the conversion. Fails at its components — supplies, switches, amplifier stages — far more often than as a whole.

Desk station. The doorman’s end of the system: handset, release controls, camera feeds. Part of the intercom, serviced as such.

Garage circuit. The second entrance’s own wiring — intercom, release, call box. Independent of the lobby and fails independently.

Gatehouse run. The buried span between a Fieldston gate and its house panel. Long, old, weather-exposed — and traceable.

COI. Certificate of insurance, issued to building and agent before work. In this neighborhood, the visit starts here.

Board-ready finding. Photos, cause, both paths priced, reusable wiring named — the document a vote actually needs.

Weather seam. The gasket or conduit joint wind-driven rain exploits on west-facing panels. Resealed as the cure, not just dried as the fix.

Riser span. The section of vertical trunk between floors. One line dead in a healthy tower usually means one span or one station — nothing more.

Strike. The electric latch in the frame, lobby brass or garage steel. The most-blamed innocent on the ridge and the cheapest line on the sheet.

Station. The in-unit wall unit, any vintage. Fails alone, replaced alone — the shareholders never know.

Package pressure. What daily delivery volume does to buttons, releases and doors held open. Hardware rated for it lasts; hardware not rated for it becomes a repeat customer.

Platform vs. part. The entire repair-or-replace question in four words: parts fail cheap and often, platforms rare and dear. Diagnosis keeps them separate.

Our 1960s lobby console is original — can it actually still be repaired?

Usually, yes, and that is the honest answer most buildings do not hear. A sixty-year service life is not an argument for replacement; it is evidence the thing was built to be repaired.

The garage intercom is down but the lobby works — is that one repair or two systems?

Two systems, one visit. Garage entrances run their own intercoms, releases and wiring, independent of the lobby, and they fail on their own schedule — usually faster, because the garage door works harder and lives closer to the weather. We diagnose it as the separate system it is, repair at the failed component, and residents get their second front door back without anyone touching the lobby console.

Do you handle the board and managing-agent paperwork, or is that on us?

On us. Wind-driven rain off the Hudson keeps killing our west-facing panel — is that fixable for good?

One apartment's intercom is dead in our tower — does the whole system need work?

Who is responsible for the buzzer in a Riverdale rental building?

Our doorman's console works but residents say video is frozen — what failed?

A component on the video path, almost certainly — a camera, a feed stage or the monitor side — while the audio pairs stay healthy. Split-path failures are good news dressed as bad: the system is alive, one element is not, and the repair targets that element at component prices.
