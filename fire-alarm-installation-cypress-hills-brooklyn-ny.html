The Neighborhood

Why Cypress Hills Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Cypress Hills is a neighborhood of houses that hold more than one household. Brownstoner's architecture writers describe it as a late-19th and early-20th-century suburban neighborhood of row, detached and semi-detached houses, and the listings that come up here are full of two- and three-family homes, frame houses with porches and side driveways, and brick rows built by a single developer. Most of those buildings have no lobby, no super and no fire alarm panel. If a fire starts on the first floor at one in the morning, the only things that can warn the family on the third floor are the alarms inside the house and the people who happen to be awake.

That second warning is not something to rely on. When News 12 covered the fire at 503 Ridgewood Avenue in May 2026, a three-story building where flames started on the first floor just after 1:20 a.m., a neighbor named Jose Mena described what he saw: "One of the guys who lives there, he was trying to wake everybody up." He added, "People were sleeping." Nobody was hurt that night, but a vacate order went up, the Red Cross took in two households with three children, and burned bikes and scooters were left across the property while Hazmat crews dealt with the lithium-ion batteries. One resident running through a building to wake the others is exactly the job a set of linked alarms is supposed to do faster and without anyone having to be awake first.

The rest of the local record points the same way. Two months after the May fire, a two-alarm fire at a home at 485 Ridgewood Avenue, just after 11:30 p.m., injured two civilians and a firefighter and took about 141 fire and EMS personnel nearly an hour and a half to control. In April 2023, a fire amNY reported at a home on what it spelled as Nicholas Avenue spread to three other homes nearby. In 2016 a fire in a vacant house at 420 Lincoln Avenue spread to the two buildings beside it and killed a person a police source believed was a squatter. On Fulton Street, under the J and Z, three-story apartment buildings burned in 2016 and 2019. Each case is covered in more detail further down, with what it means for the alarms in a similar building.

Then there is the calendar. HPD's detector rules add natural gas detectors on January 1, 2027, about seven weeks after this page was reviewed, for multiple dwellings and for one- and two-family homes the owner does not live in, wherever there is gas piping. Gas ranges, gas boilers and gas water heaters are ordinary in Cypress Hills houses, so a large share of the rented homes here are affected. For an owner who has to get into every apartment anyway, it is the obvious moment to fix the smoke and carbon monoxide side at the same visit, and to link alarms across floors where they stand alone today.

Five Cypress Hills facts that change a fire alarm plan

  • Several households, one stair. Two- to four-family houses and small walk-ups are a large part of the housing, each floor a separate household sharing one front door and one stair. In a building like that, an alarm that only sounds inside the apartment where the fire started leaves everyone else depending on a neighbor's knock.
  • Fires here have started at the back and at night. At 503 Ridgewood Avenue, flames were seen shooting from the rear of the property after 1:20 a.m. At 3400 Fulton Street in February 2019, Patch reported a three-alarm fire that erupted on the first floor of a three-story building at about 4:30 a.m. and displaced six children and 18 adults. A plan has to catch a fire that starts on a lower floor while everyone upstairs is asleep.
  • Fires here have jumped between houses. The 2016 Lincoln Avenue fire spread to two adjacent buildings, and the 2023 fire amNY reported spread to three other homes. Alarms cannot stop a neighbor's fire, but they decide how early your own household hears it once smoke or heat reaches your side.
  • Batteries are part of the picture. The FDNY counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires citywide in 2023 and 277 in 2024, while deaths from them fell from 18 to 6. The Ridgewood Avenue fire left burned e-bikes and scooters across the property. Where those batteries are kept and charged now belongs on every survey we do here.
  • New buildings bring building systems. Logan Fountain at 265 Logan Street, finished in 2025, has 13 stories and 343 units with retail on the ground floor, and Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place has 275 apartments over a community center with a commercial kitchen. Buildings like these have engineered fire alarm systems run by the landlord, which changes who does what inside them.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Cypress Hills' Architecture

Almost every fire alarm job we see in Cypress Hills is in one of six kinds of building. They differ in how many households share them, what their walls and floors are made of, where cable can travel, and which city rules apply. Those differences shape the plan long before any brand or model comes up.

Wood-frame houses with porches

Detached and semi-detached frame houses from around 1900, many with deep front porches, bay windows, side driveways and rear garages, and in many cases the original shingles hidden under later siding.

Developer-built brick rows

Rows such as the Richards Real Homes on Sunnyside Avenue, built between 1900 and 1920 with tile roofs, porches and bays, where every house in the row shares the same plan.

Two- to four-family houses

Houses with a household on each floor, one shared stair to the street and a cellar holding the boiler, meters, water heaters and storage, sometimes with a room or unit below grade.

Three-story apartment buildings

Small walk-ups on Fulton Street, Crescent Street and the side streets, often with a store on the ground floor and two or more apartments above it.

New affordable and supportive buildings

Large buildings such as Logan Fountain and Chestnut Commons, with hundreds of apartments, engineered fire alarm systems and ground-floor retail or community space.

Public housing

Cypress Hills Houses, the NYCHA development between Sutter Avenue and Linden Boulevard completed in May 1955, where the building fire alarm belongs to the housing authority.

Wood-frame houses with porches: fast-burning materials, one stair and a long way to the top floor

Brownstoner's building profile of 218 Arlington Avenue gives a sense of what these houses are: a Queen Anne built around 1900 on a block that was empty in 1887 and filled in by 1904, with a 1941 tax photo showing fish-scale shingles that have since disappeared under vinyl siding. Another Brownstoner listing describes 339 Jamaica Avenue as a standalone Italianate frame house with a porch, a bay window, a driveway and a garage at the back. Houses like these are wood from the cellar sill to the roof rafters, and many have been altered several times. A fire inside one has wood framing, lath, old finishes and an open stair to travel through, and the stair is usually the only way down from the bedrooms.

For detection, that means the alarms have to win time at the bottom of the house. We put a smoke alarm at the foot of the main stair and at the top of the cellar stair, where smoke from a lower floor reaches first, and we link every alarm in the house so the one at the bottom sounds in every bedroom. Bedrooms get their own alarms inside and a second in the hall outside. In frame houses with plaster walls, hardwired alarms can often be fished up through interior partitions and closets without opening much, and where that is not practical, wireless interconnected alarms with sealed ten-year batteries do the same job. Before anything is mounted, we sound an alarm in the cellar while someone listens in the farthest bedroom with the door closed.

Developer-built brick rows: masonry party walls, shared plans and a roof over everyone

Brownstoner's profile of 149–181 Sunnyside Avenue describes ninety houses built by Richards Real Homes between 1900 and 1920, in red and buff brick with tile roofs, deep porches and bays, set on the steep ground the glacier left behind, with the north-side houses standing above the street on long stairs. Rows like this put masonry between neighbors, which is a real advantage when a fire breaks out next door. Inside each house, though, the floors, stairs and roof framing are still wood, and the identical plans mean the weak points repeat house after house.

The good news about a shared plan is that the alarm plan can be shared too. Once we have worked out where the alarms belong in one house on a row, the same placements usually suit its neighbors, and an owner who wants the work done for a few houses on the same block can get a plan that has already been tested. We still survey each house, because over a century one owner has finished the attic and another has moved the kitchen, and the alarm plan follows the house as it is today. Brick and plaster can weaken the radio signal between wireless alarms, so we test linking from the cellar to the top floor before deciding between wireless and hardwired interconnection.

Two- to four-family houses: whose alarms, whose duty, and one stair for all

These are the core of the neighborhood's rental housing. Each floor is a separate household, the owner may live on one of them or nowhere in the building, and everyone shares the front door, the stair and the cellar. HPD's detector page treats the building differently depending on its size and on who lives there. With three or more apartments it is a multiple dwelling, and every apartment needs at least one approved smoke detector within fifteen feet of the main entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, plus carbon monoxide detection if the building has a fuel-burning device. With one or two apartments and an owner who lives elsewhere, HPD's carbon monoxide and, from January 1, 2027, natural gas rules apply.

Our plan for these houses has two layers. Inside each apartment are the detectors the rules ask for, placed for that apartment's bedrooms, kitchen and appliances. Then there are the shared spaces: the stair, the entry hall and the cellar, where a fire can grow while every household is asleep behind its own door. For those, linked alarms that sound on every floor are usually right at this scale, and we write down which devices the owner maintains and which each tenant is expected to test each month, as HPD asks tenants to do.

Three-story apartment buildings: a store below, families above, and fires that have reached the roof

The small apartment buildings along Fulton Street are some of the most exposed in the neighborhood. CBS New York reported that 3301 Fulton Street, between Euclid Avenue and Crescent Street, covers two three-story apartment buildings, and that flames were seen coming from the roof during the March 2016 fire there while the J and Z kept running past at reduced speed. Patch reported the February 2019 fire at 3400 Fulton Street near Autumn Avenue as a three-alarm fire in a three-story building that started on the first floor. Buildings like these have three or more apartments, so they are multiple dwellings with self-closing door and posted-notice duties, and many have a store on the ground floor that is closed and empty all night.

The plan for one of these buildings covers four places: each apartment, the stair, the cellar and the store. The apartments get the HPD detectors. The stair and the cellar get detection that sounds where the households can hear it. The store gets detection suited to what it sells and how it is used, arranged so a fire there at night reaches the families above rather than burning unnoticed behind a rolled-down gate. Whether the building needs a full fire alarm system with a panel, or linked household devices are enough, depends on its height, construction and use, and we tell the owner which case applies, in writing, before any price is given.

New affordable and supportive buildings: an engineered system, a landlord, and the space on the ground floor

New York YIMBY reported that Logan Fountain at 265 Logan Street, on a former gas station site bounded by Atlantic Avenue, Fountain Avenue and Logan Street, rose 13 stories with 343 units, among them 105 supportive apartments and 169 transitional units for families operated by HELP USA, over ground-floor retail. Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place, built by MHANY Management with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation and Urban Builders Collaborative, has 275 apartments, three retail suites and a community center with a gym, a computer lab and a commercial kitchen meant to incubate culinary businesses. None of the reports describe their fire alarm equipment, and we make no claims about it.

What we can describe is how work in buildings like these goes. The building system was designed by an engineer, filed with the FDNY and accepted, and it is the landlord's. Inside an apartment, residents report detector problems to management. Inside a retail suite or a program space on the ground floor, a tenant's fit-out ties its devices into the landlord's system to approved drawings, coordinated with the building's fire alarm company of record. That is the kind of work we do in a new building: survey the tenant space, install and label the devices on the approved drawings, pre-test them, and hand the results to the people who keep the building's records.

Public housing: report to management, not to a contractor

Cypress Hills Houses, between Sutter Avenue and Linden Boulevard and Fountain and Euclid Avenues, was completed in May 1955, and public sources disagree on how many buildings it has, so we give no count. As in every NYCHA development, the building fire alarm and the detectors inside the apartments are the housing authority's responsibility. A private installer does not work on those systems unless the authority hires them, and residents with a missing or broken detector should report it through their development's management or 311. We mention it here because we are asked, and because a resident deserves a straight answer rather than a sales call.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Begin at the Back Door, Count the Batteries, Then Climb

The rear of the house first. On a Cypress Hills survey the technician starts outside, at the back door, the porch and the yard, because that is where the local fire record keeps pointing. Is there a porch or shed where bikes and scooters are parked? A tarp in the side yard? Extension cords running out of a window to a charger? Each of those goes on the survey sheet, along with how far it is from the nearest door into the house and the nearest stair.

Count the batteries, not only the bikes. Delivery workers often keep spare packs, and they rarely live where the bike is parked. The technician asks where every battery is charged and where spare ones are stored, including the cellar, a closet under the stair and a hallway by the front door. A battery charged next to the only exit is the one finding we flag in red on the device list, whatever else the house has.

Then the cellar and the gas. Downstairs, the technician follows the gas from the meter to every appliance it feeds and notes each by room, because from January 1, 2027 rented homes need natural gas detection where gas appliances are, and because carbon monoxide planning starts at the same boiler and water heater. Anyone who sleeps below the first floor, even occasionally, changes the plan, and we ask directly.

Last, the climb with the doors shut. Only then do we go up, floor by floor, to every bedroom. With the bedroom doors closed and any air conditioner running, a second person sounds an alarm at the bottom of the house. If the sleeper in the farthest room would not wake to it, the plan changes, whatever the code minimum says.

The Local Fire Record

Ridgewood Avenue, Fulton Street, Lincoln Avenue, Euclid and Etna: What Ten Years of Cypress Hills Fires Say About Detection

The fires below were reported in Cypress Hills by News 12 Brooklyn, amNY, ABC7, CBS New York, Patch and QNS. We describe each one only as those outlets did. None of the reports says whether smoke alarms were present or worked, and we do not guess. We include them because each one maps onto a decision in a fire alarm plan for a similar building, and because a plan built on what has actually happened on these blocks is more useful than one built on a national average.

503 Ridgewood Avenue, May 13, 2026: the rear of the building, after 1:20 a.m. News 12 reported a fire that began on the first floor of a three-story building near Lincoln and Autumn Avenues, with flames shooting from the rear, and that 21 units and 79 fire and EMS personnel responded. Hazmat crews came because of lithium-ion batteries, burned bikes and scooters were left across the property, and a large metal drum of the kind used for damaged batteries was hauled away. There were no injuries, the cause was under investigation, and a full vacate order was posted. The lesson for a similar house is about the back: a fire that starts at the rear, at night, has to be heard by people asleep at the front and on the floors above, which means an alarm near the rear door, one at the foot of the stair, and every alarm linked.

485 Ridgewood Avenue, July 2026: two alarms just before midnight. News 12 reported a two-alarm fire at a home just after 11:30 p.m. on a Thursday, with about 141 fire and EMS personnel, nearly an hour and a half to bring under control, and three people hurt, two civilians and a firefighter. The report gives no cause and no description of the house. A fire that big at that hour is the case for making sure the earliest warning is inside the house, where the people are, not on the street.

Highland Place and McKinley Avenue, November 2025: fires in the middle of the morning. News 12 reported a top-floor fire at a home on Highland Place between Etna Street and Ridgewood Avenue, with the FDNY responding around 9:45 a.m., and a fire four days later that erupted on the second floor of a home at 174 McKinley Avenue, with firefighters called just after 9:30 a.m. Nobody was hurt in either. Both are reminders that fires do not wait for night, and that a house empty during the working day has nobody inside to hear its alarms. That is where monitoring, or at least a neighbor who knows the sound, comes in.

3301 and 3400 Fulton Street, 2016 and 2019: three-story buildings under the el. CBS New York reported a multiple-alarm fire in March 2016 at 3301 Fulton Street, an address covering two three-story apartment buildings, with flames coming from the roof. In February 2019, Patch reported a three-alarm fire that erupted on the first floor of a three-story building at 3400 Fulton Street near Autumn Avenue at about 4:30 a.m., displacing four families, and that firefighters asked Buildings Department inspectors to check the building's stability after reporting vibrations. Fires that start low and finish at the roof are the ones that a stair alarm, linked to every apartment, is meant to get ahead of.

420 Lincoln Avenue, November 2016: a vacant house and two neighbors. amNY reported a three-alarm fire that began in a residence described as vacant, spread to two adjacent buildings and caused a partial roof collapse, and that one person died, believed by a police source to be a possible squatter. In January 2025 ABC7 reported another overnight fire in a Cypress Hills house that neighbors said had been vacant for a long time and frequently occupied by squatters, with two people hurt. The owners next door to a house like that cannot put alarms in it, but they can make sure the rooms on their side of the shared wall, and the attic above, are covered and linked to their bedrooms.

Euclid Avenue and Etna Street, January 2018: smoke, not flames, sent thirty people to hospitals. amNY reported a fire in an apartment building there that the FDNY described as an accidental electrical fire, with thirty people taken to hospitals, mostly for smoke inhalation or minor injuries. In an apartment building, the smoke in the stair and halls is what injures people who never see the fire. That is why hall and stair detection, working self-closing doors and the fire safety notice inside each apartment door belong in the same conversation as the alarms.

Crescent Street near Fulton Street, October 2012: a fire upstairs while the store was open. QNS reported a one-alarm fire around 11:20 on a Saturday morning in a second-floor apartment above a storefront, handled with a single hose line. The direction matters. Most store-and-apartment planning worries about a fire in a closed store reaching the families upstairs. This one went the other way, during business hours, and the store downstairs needed to know too.

Batteries and E-Bikes

Bikes, Scooters and Spare Batteries: Planning Detection Around the Charging Spot in a Cypress Hills House

Lithium-ion batteries are not the only fire risk in Cypress Hills, and the cause of the May 2026 Ridgewood Avenue fire was still under investigation when it was reported. But batteries were plainly there, and they change how we plan a house. The FDNY's own figures show why the subject is not going away. The department counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires across the city in 2023 and 277 in 2024. Deaths from those fires fell sharply over the same two years, from 18 to 6, which the department credits to prevention, education and outreach. Fewer deaths is real progress. The number of fires did not drop.

Where the bikes are, the alarm cannot always go. Household smoke alarms are made for indoor rooms. They do not belong outdoors, in an unheated porch that freezes in January, or right beside a charger where they would be soaked by the first gust of rain. So in a house where bikes are kept on the back porch, in the side yard or in a detached garage, the plan works from the inside out. The room just inside the rear door gets its own smoke alarm, linked to every other alarm in the house, so that smoke coming in from a fire outside reaches a detector before it reaches the stair. Where a garage or shed is enclosed, a heat alarm suited to that space can be linked to the house where the product allows it, and we test that link before relying on it.

Where the batteries charge matters more than where the bikes sleep. A battery charging overnight in a hallway by the front door, or under the only stair, sits between the bedrooms and the way out. A battery fire there can block the exit before anyone is awake. We put detection near any indoor charging spot, but we also say plainly, on the written survey, when the spot itself is the problem. Moving the charging to a room with a door that closes, away from the stair and the front door, protects a household more than any device we can sell.

Tenants and charging. In a rented two- or three-family, the person charging the battery is often a tenant or a tenant's relative who works deliveries. An owner can set house rules about where charging happens, and we can place detection that matches those rules, but the alarm plan should never depend on everyone following them. That is one more reason to link the alarms between floors: a battery fire in the tenant's apartment then sounds in the owner's bedroom, and the reverse.

January 1, 2027

Gas Detectors in Cypress Hills Rentals by January 1, 2027: Which Houses Are Affected and How to Fit the Work Into One Visit

HPD's page on detectors says natural gas detectors become required on January 1, 2027 in Class A and Class B multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping, under the city's Building Code. In multiple dwellings, a sleeping room needs one only if it contains a fuel-gas burning appliance; otherwise the detector goes where the gas appliance is. In a typical Cypress Hills two- or three-family, that points to the kitchen of each apartment and the cellar where the boiler and water heaters stand.

The owner-occupied question. HPD's wording ties the one- and two-family requirement to homes the owner does not occupy. Many Cypress Hills two-families have the owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, and that case is answered below in the Q&A. A three-family is a multiple dwelling whether or not the owner lives in it, so the requirement reaches it either way.

One device or three. HPD allows owners to use one device that combines smoke, carbon monoxide and natural gas detection, as long as the installation meets each requirement. It also sets what an owner may charge Class A tenants for a new installation: $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device, with a year to pay, while Class B tenants owe nothing and tenants of one- and two-family homes owe $25 per CO or gas detector. JustFix adds that unpaid detector charges cannot lead to an eviction.

Paperwork that goes with it. HPD asks owners to give a notice to at least one adult in each unit when devices are installed, to post a notice in the entrance area of a multiple dwelling, and to keep three dates for each type of device: when the notice was posted, when each device was installed, and when its manufacturer's useful life runs out. The device list we leave has the last two for every detector, by apartment and room, so the record exists the day the work is done.

Why the visits should be combined. Getting into every apartment is the slow part of detector work in a rented house. If the gas detectors are going in anyway, it costs far less to check and replace tired smoke alarms, add missing CO alarms and link the house at the same visit than to come back. We price smoke, CO and gas work together, apartment by apartment, using our per-unit landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit.

Rules that shape fire alarm work for Cypress Hills businesses and buildings

  • A building system is designed before it is installed. The FDNY Fire Code requires fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. A New York State registered engineer or architect decides the system type and prepares those plans. A store that needs a filed system should start the design months before its planned opening.
  • Like-for-like is a repair; anything else is a change. Replacing a failed detector, pull station or control panel with an identical part and no change in design is treated as a repair in the code summaries we rely on. Moving a panel, swapping it for a different one or adding devices changes the system, and changes go back to the FDNY.
  • Small residential buildings can fall under an exception. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published by UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. Whether a particular Cypress Hills house qualifies depends on how it is really built, which we check rather than assume.
  • Carbon monoxide in businesses with a fire alarm. FDNY guidance requires CO detection in certain occupancies that have a fire alarm system and contain fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed parking garage or a loading dock, including restaurants, places of worship, clinics and stores, with the detectors connected to the fire alarm rather than standing alone.
  • Who may work on it. Fire alarm systems in New York City must be serviced by an FDNY-recognized company, required detectors are installed by DOB-licensed electrical contractors, and programming is done by an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness holder. We are a New York State licensed installer, license #12000287431. On filed work, we install, label and pre-test to the approved drawings with the design professional and the building's fire alarm company of record.
  • Monitoring before inspection. The FDNY does not schedule the inspection of a new monitored system until the central station connection is active and the panel is free of alarm, supervisory and trouble conditions. The monitoring contract belongs early on the schedule.
  • Records and nuisance alarms. Owners keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years, report new connections and changes to the FDNY, and face enforcement if a monitored system sends two or more unwarranted alarms within three months. A store under the el that keeps calling the fire department for nothing has a detector in the wrong place or a fault to find.
  • Residential notices. HPD requires multiple dwellings to post a Fire Safety Notice inside every apartment door, with different versions for fireproof and non-fireproof buildings, and to keep self-closing apartment doors working. In a three-story building on Fulton Street, those duties sit alongside the detectors.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Cypress Hills: Questions Answered

These are the situations Cypress Hills homeowners, landlords, store owners and program managers bring to us. Each one is answered in full here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down the page.

Bikes, batteries and the back of the house

After the May fire at 503 Ridgewood Avenue, burned e-bikes and scooters were left all over the property. Our tenants park their delivery bikes in the side yard under a tarp. Can anything detect a fire out there?

Not with an ordinary smoke alarm. Household smoke and CO alarms are listed for indoor use, and a device in an open side yard would be exposed to rain, freezing nights and dust, and would either fail or false-alarm. What works is planning from the inside out. The rooms that face the side yard, and especially the room just inside the nearest door, get a smoke alarm linked to every other alarm in the house, so smoke from a fire outside sets off the bedrooms upstairs as soon as it gets in. If the bikes sit under an enclosed porch or in a shed with walls and a roof, a heat alarm made for unheated spaces can sometimes be linked to the house, and we test the link before relying on it. The most useful change is not a device at all. A tarp over a pile of bikes against the house wall, next to a window, is the arrangement to move. Bikes parked away from the house, and batteries charged indoors in a room with a closing door away from the stair, change the risk more than anything we can mount.

One of our tenants does deliveries and keeps two spare batteries on a shelf in the cellar next to the boiler, to keep them warm in winter. Is that a problem, and what detection belongs down there?

It is a problem worth fixing this week. A cellar holds the boiler, the water heater and usually the gas meters, and in most Cypress Hills houses its stair opens straight into the first-floor hall that everyone uses to get out. A battery that fails down there can fill that stair with smoke before anyone upstairs knows. Heat from a boiler is not what a lithium-ion pack wants, either. Ask the tenant to store the batteries somewhere cool, dry and away from the boiler, the stair and anything that burns, and to charge them in their own apartment in a room with a door, never in the cellar or the hall. For detection, the cellar should have a smoke alarm at the top of the cellar stair and coverage in the cellar itself suited to the space, plus carbon monoxide detection where the boiler and water heater are. If dust or temperature in the cellar would make a smoke alarm unreliable, a heat alarm covers the room and the smoke alarm at the top of the stair catches what rises. All of it should be linked to the apartments above.

Stores with apartments above them

The 2012 fire on Crescent Street started in an apartment above a store while the store was open. If a fire starts upstairs during business hours, how would our staff and customers downstairs know?

Only if the building is set up for it. In many Cypress Hills storefront buildings, the apartments have their own alarms and the store has its own, and the two have never been connected. A fire upstairs then sounds only upstairs, and the store finds out from the smell or from the street. There are a few ways to close that gap, depending on the building. If the building has a fire alarm panel, detection in the stair and the apartments' common areas can sound in the store as well. If it does not, and the building qualifies for household devices, a linked alarm can be placed in the shared stair or hall so that it sounds in the store's back room, with the store's own alarms linked to it where the products allow. What we will not do is tie a store's monitored commercial system to apartment alarms that a tenant can take down, because a gap in one then becomes a gap in both. We look at how the stair, the doors and the walls actually connect before choosing, and the store owner and the building owner both sign off on the plan.

Vacant houses on the block

The boarded-up house next to ours has people sleeping in it some nights, like the vacant houses that burned on Lincoln Avenue in 2016 and on another Cypress Hills block in January 2025. Can we, or the city, put alarms inside a house we do not own?

You cannot, and we cannot do it for you. Entering or working on a property you do not own is not something an installer can do without the owner's permission, however good the reason. What you can do is report it. Vacant buildings that are open, being used for shelter or not being maintained can be reported to 311 for the Department of Buildings and HPD to look at, and anyone who sees an active fire or smoke should call 911 straight away. On your own side, the plan changes in three ways. The rooms along the wall you share or face get smoke alarms linked to your bedrooms, so heat or smoke coming through or around that wall is heard upstairs. If the two houses share an attic space or a roof, the top of your stair and any finished attic room get alarms too. And because both fires in the record were at night, the sleeping rooms closest to the vacant house are the ones we test first with the doors closed. Your insurer will also want to know about the vacant house, and a dated device list showing your own protection helps that conversation.

We are buying a long-vacant frame house to renovate, and neighbors say people have slept in it. What fire protection should be in place while it sits empty during construction?

Plan for two stages. While the house is empty and open, the main jobs are keeping people out and keeping a fire from growing unnoticed. Secure the doors and windows, clear out debris and anything left by people who slept there, and keep construction waste, rags and fuel out of the house overnight. Battery smoke alarms in an empty, unheated house help only if someone hears them, so a vacant building under renovation is a place where a simple monitored device or a neighbor who agrees to call 911 is worth more than a ceiling full of alarms nobody will hear. Once the walls are open, that is the cheapest moment the house will ever offer for hardwired, interconnected alarms: cable runs from the cellar to the attic in an afternoon, and the alarm plan can be drawn for the layout you are building rather than the one you bought. If the renovation changes the number of apartments, the code that applies changes with it, and the design should be settled with your architect before the electrician roughs in. We work from the drawings and make sure the boxes go where the alarms belong.

Apartment buildings and their paperwork

We own a three-story walk-up near Euclid Avenue. HPD says a fire safety notice has to hang inside every apartment door. What goes on it, and does it have anything to do with the alarms?

It does. HPD requires owners of multiple dwellings, buildings with three or more apartments, to post a Fire Safety Notice inside the door of every apartment, under section FC401.6 of the city Fire Code and the FDNY rule at 3 RCNY 401-06, with fire safety signage in the public areas as well. The notice comes in two versions, one for fireproof buildings and one for non-fireproof buildings, and the difference is the instruction it gives about whether to stay inside or leave during a fire. Most small Cypress Hills walk-ups are non-fireproof, with wood floors and stairs, which changes what tenants should be told to do when they hear an alarm. That is why we look at the notice during a survey. The alarms, the self-closing doors and the notice are meant to work together: the alarm warns, the closed door keeps smoke out of the stair, and the notice tells people what to do next. After the 2018 fire at Euclid Avenue and Etna Street sent thirty people to hospitals, mostly for smoke inhalation, it is worth making sure all three are right in your building.

Our second-floor tenant uses a home oxygen concentrator. Should that change the alarms in her apartment?

Yes, and it should change a few habits too. Oxygen does not burn, but it makes everything around it burn faster and hotter, so a small fire near someone using oxygen grows very quickly, and a person on oxygen may also need longer to get out. For detection, we make sure her bedroom has its own smoke alarm inside the room and another just outside it, that her apartment's alarms are linked to the rest of the house so the owner's household hears a fire in her apartment and can help, and that carbon monoxide detection is in place near her sleeping area. If she is hard of hearing, an alarm with a strobe or a bed shaker can be added. Beyond the alarms, the advice from her equipment supplier matters most: no smoking or open flames anywhere near the oxygen, the concentrator kept clear of curtains and bedding, and tubing kept away from the stove. Tell us about her when you book, so the technician knows to plan for a slower exit and to show her how each alarm sounds.

Basements, slopes and the city's pilot

We signed up for the city's basement conversion pilot with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation, but the program closed before our plans were filed. Does that change what alarms the basement needs?

The program closing does not change the fire code, and it does not change what keeps a person in that basement safe. HPD's page says the Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program covered East New York and Cypress Hills, offered loans of up to $120,000 to owner-occupants of one- to three-unit homes, had the Cypress Hills LDC manage the Certificate of Occupancy process with the Buildings Department, and is no longer accepting applications. Without that Certificate of Occupancy, the basement is not a legal apartment. Two things follow. If nobody sleeps down there, the basement still needs detection as part of the house, with a smoke alarm at the top of the stair and carbon monoxide detection near the boiler, linked to the floors above. If someone does sleep there, even a relative, we will put smoke and CO alarms in that room and link them to the house, and we will tell you in writing that an alarm does not make an illegal room safe. A room below grade without a proper second way out is dangerous to sleep in, and the fix is a legal conversion or not using it as a bedroom.

Our house stands above the street on a long stair, like the north-side houses on Sunnyside Avenue, and the basement is underground in front but opens onto the back yard. Where does the bottom smoke alarm go?

Treat that basement as a full story, because for a fire it is one. A basement that walks out to the yard usually has a door, windows and often a finished room or a workshop, so it holds more to burn than a buried cellar does, and its stair rises straight into the hall of the main floor. We put a smoke alarm in the basement itself, near the bottom of its stair, and another at the top of that stair on the main floor, both linked to the bedrooms. If the walk-out door is where bikes, tools or a grill are kept, the room just inside it gets an alarm as well. Carbon monoxide detection goes on the basement level where the boiler or water heater is, and near the sleeping rooms upstairs. Hillside houses also have a long walk from the bedrooms to the street, down a high front stair, so the alarm plan should leave time for it. In practice that means linking every alarm, so the first one to sense smoke sounds everywhere, rather than relying on the one closest to the fire being heard through the house.

New buildings and shared spaces

Several small food businesses share one incubator kitchen in a community center, each on its own shift. When one of them sets off the building's fire alarm, who is responsible, and how do we stop it happening again?

The building's fire alarm belongs to the landlord, and in a new building like Chestnut Commons it was designed, filed and accepted as a system. The businesses using the kitchen do not own any of it, and none of them should move, cover or adjust a detector. Responsibility for the alarm itself sits with the building's management and its fire alarm company. Responsibility for the cause sits with whoever was cooking, which is why a shared kitchen needs written rules: hood on before any burner, doors to the corridor kept closed, no smoking, steaming or open-flame techniques that the kitchen was not designed for, and a log of who was on shift. If the same detector keeps going off, the right fix is a review by the building's fire alarm company of whether the device type and location suit how the kitchen is really used, and any change goes through the proper filing. We can survey the space, read the panel history with management's permission and recommend changes, but on a filed system in a new building we work through the landlord, never around it.

The 2027 gas rule in owner-occupied two-families

We live on the first floor of our two-family and rent the second floor. Does the January 2027 natural gas detector rule reach our apartment, the tenant's apartment, or both?

Read HPD's wording carefully. Its page applies the natural gas detector requirement to multiple dwellings and to one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, where there is gas piping. An owner living in a two-family is occupying it, so on that wording your house is outside the 2027 requirement, and the same wording governs HPD's carbon monoxide rule for one- and two-family homes. Before you settle on that answer, check how your building is classified and whether anything in your lease, mortgage or insurance asks for more. Then think about the tenant. A gas range in her kitchen, a gas boiler and water heater in the cellar you share, and a gas leak that would reach your floor as quickly as hers are reasons to install gas and CO detection anyway. It costs little when we are already in the house, and it protects both households. If you ever move out and rent both floors, the house becomes a non-owner-occupied two-family, and the CO and gas duties, notices and records become yours.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Cypress Hills

AI tools and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr answer fire alarm questions for an average American house with one family in it. These are the shortcuts that mislead Cypress Hills owners most, and what the local reality looks like.

"Smoke detector installation costs about $115"

Angi's cost guide does say that smoke detector installation costs $70 to $150 for most homeowners, with an average of $115. That figure describes swapping or mounting a few battery alarms in a single-family house, and the same guide notes that hardwiring can easily triple the total and that local code can quadruple it depending on the size of the home.

A Cypress Hills two- or three-family is not that job. It has a separate household on each floor, a cellar with gas appliances, and often a stair that every household depends on. Placing alarms for each apartment, adding CO and gas detection, linking them across floors and recording every device is a different scope.

That is why our Brooklyn list starts from $600 for one apartment or a small house and runs to $2,500 for linked alarms through a whole house. The useful comparison is not the price of a device. It is whether the alarm at the bottom of the house wakes the people at the top.

"One smoke alarm per floor is enough"

Angi's rule of thumb is roughly one alarm per floor, bedroom and kitchen, or one per 500 square feet. Neither matches how New York writes the rules. HPD requires a smoke detector within fifteen feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room in a multiple dwelling, and a Brownstoner forum poster quoted NYC Building Code 907.2.11.1 as requiring alarms both outside and inside each room used for sleeping.

In the frame houses and two-families here, bedrooms are often at the front and back of a long floor, with the stair in the middle. One alarm on the landing can be fifteen feet from one bedroom door and thirty from the other, and closed doors make the gap worse.

We place alarms by sleeping room, not by floor, and then test what a sleeper hears with the door shut. The code minimum is the floor, not the target.

"Battery alarms are fine; just change the batteries twice a year"

Since April 1, 2019, New York State has required new and replacement residential smoke alarms offered for sale to have a sealed, non-removable battery lasting at least ten years, or to be hardwired. The Department of State said at the time that existing code-compliant alarms with replaceable batteries do not have to be removed.

So both kinds still hang on Cypress Hills ceilings. HPD's tenant duties still mention replacing batteries at least twice a year and right away when the low-battery chirp starts, which applies to the older type. A sealed alarm is replaced as a whole when it reaches the end of its life.

The bigger problem is the alarm with its battery pulled after a cooking incident. Then-Secretary of State Rossana Rosado said the sealed detectors "reduce the possibility of human error." Replacing the old type with sealed or hardwired alarms takes that failure off the table.

"E-bike battery fires are getting worse every year"

The FDNY's figures tell a more mixed story. The department counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires in 2023 and 277 in 2024, so the number of fires rose slightly, while deaths from them fell from 18 to 6, a drop of two thirds.

That is real progress, and it is not a reason to relax. The May 2026 fire at 503 Ridgewood Avenue left burned e-bikes and scooters across the property and brought out Hazmat crews, whatever its cause turns out to be.

The practical lesson for a Cypress Hills house is where batteries are charged and stored. A charging spot by the only exit is the arrangement to change, and detection near it should be linked to every bedroom.

"A vacant house is not a fire risk because nobody lives in it"

The local record says otherwise. amNY reported that the 2016 fire at 420 Lincoln Avenue began in a residence described as vacant, spread to two adjacent buildings and killed a person a police source believed was a squatter.

In January 2025 ABC7 reported an overnight fire in another long-vacant Cypress Hills house that neighbors said squatters often used, with two people hurt. A vacant house can have people in it without power, heat or light, and nobody inside it is protected by alarms.

For the families next door, the vacant house is part of their own fire plan. Their rooms along the shared side and their attic need alarms linked to their bedrooms, and an open vacant building should be reported to 311.

"Every New York City home falls under HPD's smoke detector rule"

HPD's own page frames its smoke detector requirement around Class A and Class B multiple dwellings and says smoke detectors are not required in one- and two-family homes under those rules. Its rules for one- and two-family homes are about carbon monoxide and, from 2027, natural gas, and only where the owner does not live there.

That does not mean a one- or two-family house can do without smoke alarms. Building code requirements apply when houses are built or altered, insurers and lenders ask about them, and a smoke alarm is still the device most likely to wake a family in time.

Angi, citing the NFPA, notes that homes without working smoke alarms have nearly twice the death rate per thousand reported fires as homes with them. Whatever the rule says, every Cypress Hills house should have them on every level.

"In a brand-new building, the building's system protects every apartment"

Large new buildings such as Logan Fountain and Chestnut Commons have engineered fire alarm systems that were designed and filed, and the landlord maintains them. That system watches the building's common spaces and does what its design calls for.

It does not remove HPD's in-unit duties. Every apartment in a multiple dwelling still needs its own smoke detection within fifteen feet of each sleeping room and carbon monoxide detection where the rules require it, with the notices and records that go with them.

For residents, the practical point is who to call. A detector problem inside an apartment in a new building goes to management, which works with the building's fire alarm company, rather than to an outside installer.

FAQ

Cypress Hills Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Online cost guides say smoke detector installation averages about $115. Why does a Cypress Hills job on your list start at $600?
+

The $115 average in Angi's guide describes mounting a few battery alarms in a single-family house. Our $600 starting point covers a plan for each bedroom, kitchen and gas appliance, smoke, CO and gas devices where they are needed, installation, testing with the doors shut and a dated device list. Angi itself notes that hardwiring can triple the total and local code can quadruple it, which is closer to what a Cypress Hills two-family needs.

Roughly how many smoke alarms does a typical Cypress Hills two-family end up with?
+

It depends on the bedrooms, but more than most owners expect. A common two-family with three bedrooms on each floor usually needs an alarm inside and outside each sleeping room, one at the top and bottom of each stair, one in the cellar and one in each kitchen area, often twelve to sixteen in all, plus CO and gas detection. We count them from your floor plan or photos before quoting, so the number is in writing.

We already have carbon monoxide alarms on each floor. Do we still need separate smoke alarms?
+

Yes. A carbon monoxide alarm senses a gas produced by burning fuel, and a smoke alarm senses the particles a fire gives off. They answer different dangers, and one does not stand in for the other, as Angi's guide also points out. Combination smoke and CO alarms do both in one housing, which means fewer devices on the ceiling, but each function has to be listed on the label.

Can I still walk into a store in New York and buy a smoke alarm with a battery door?
+

Not a new one. Since April 1, 2019, New York State has required new or replacement residential smoke alarms offered for sale to have a sealed, non-removable battery that lasts at least ten years, or to be hardwired. Retailers were allowed to sell off stock they already had. Existing alarms with replaceable batteries that met code when installed do not have to come down, but anything bought today should be the sealed or hardwired type.

Is there a limit to how many hardwired smoke alarms can be linked together?
+

Yes, there are practical limits set by each manufacturer, and posters in a Brownstoner forum thread on a large house discussed a limit of twelve interconnected wired units. In a big two- or three-family the answer is usually to use the maker's relay or a second interconnect arrangement the product allows, rather than overloading one chain. We check the instructions for the exact model before planning the wiring, so every alarm in the house still sets off every other.

If we are renovating, will the electrician automatically put in the smoke alarms the code requires?
+

Do not assume so. The alarms belong on the architect's drawings, and the electrician should rough in boxes and interconnect wiring where those drawings show them. A poster in a Brownstoner forum thread asked exactly this, and others pointed out that the code now calls for alarms both inside and outside each sleeping room. Ask to see the alarm layout before the walls close. We can review it or install to it.

Our grandparents speak only Spanish. Are there alarms that announce a fire in Spanish?
+

Yes. Voice-announcing smoke and CO alarms that speak in English and Spanish are made by major manufacturers, and they say whether the danger is fire or carbon monoxide instead of only beeping. We check that the model is listed for each function it claims and that it links with the rest of the house, and our technician can go over the sounds with your family when the work is finished.

Are lithium-ion battery fires in New York City going up or down?
+

Both, depending on what you count. The FDNY counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires in 2023 and 277 in 2024, a small rise, while deaths from those fires fell from 18 to 6. The fires have not gone away, as the burned e-bikes and scooters at the May 2026 Ridgewood Avenue fire showed, so where batteries are charged and stored still matters in every Cypress Hills house.

Is the ten-year life of a sealed alarm counted from the day it goes up or from the day it was made?
+

Manufacturers count the useful life from the date of manufacture printed on the alarm, not from installation, so an alarm that sat on a shelf for a year has less life left. HPD asks owners of rentals to record each device's expiration date. When we install, the device list shows both the install date and the expiration date for every alarm, so you know when to plan replacements.

Does anyone from the city actually check the smoke alarms in a rented Cypress Hills house?
+

Usually when a tenant reports a problem. JustFix explains that tenants can report missing or broken detectors to HPD through 311, which creates a record, and HPD can contact the owner or make repairs through its Emergency Repairs Program and bill the owner. Tenants can also complain to the FDNY or bring an HP action in housing court. Owners are better off keeping dated records so any inspection finds the work already done.

You say requests are booked within 2 hours. Does that mean a technician arrives within 2 hours?
+

No. It means that within 2 hours of your request we have called you back, gone over your photos and booked a visit. The visit itself is scheduled in a two-hour arrival window on a day that works for you, and the technician arrives inside that window. A carbon monoxide alarm that is sounding is a 911 call first, before any call to us.

How often do fires in Cypress Hills spread from one house to the houses beside it?
+

Often enough to plan for. amNY reported that the 2016 fire at 420 Lincoln Avenue spread to two adjacent buildings, and that a 2023 fire at a home on what it spelled as Nicholas Avenue spread to three other homes nearby. Your alarms cannot stop a neighbor's fire, but alarms on the shared side and in the attic, linked to the bedrooms, give your household the earliest possible warning.
