Mount Hope is a neighborhood of apartment houses on a hill. Between Jerome Avenue and Webster Avenue, and from the Cross Bronx Expressway up to East Burnside Avenue, most homes sit in five- and six-story buildings put up before 1940 along streets like Walton, Creston, Morris and Valentine Avenues and Mount Hope Place, with a protected row of two-family houses on Morris Avenue and a growing number of tall new buildings on Walton and Jerome. Ninety-four percent of residents rent, which means most fire alarm work here is ordered by an owner, a managing agent or a housing nonprofit, apartment by apartment, with tenants at home. Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Mount Hope's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every fire alarm call we take from Mount Hope. They differ less in style than in how many households share one stair, how the roof and cellar connect the floors, and whether the building's protection lives only inside the apartments or also in the halls.

The five-story walk-up

Prewar apartment houses on Walton, Creston, Valentine and the streets between, with one or two stairs, a cellar boiler room and a flat roof over a cockloft, usually protected only by alarms inside each apartment.

The six-story elevator apartment house

Larger buildings on the Grand Concourse, Mount Hope Place and Walton Avenue, like the roughly 75-apartment building at 1920 Walton Avenue, with long halls, an elevator machine room and sometimes a lobby panel.

The rehabilitated building

Walk-ups and apartment houses rebuilt after the 1970s arson years, many now owned or managed by housing nonprofits, with rehab-era wiring, partitions and often a thirty-year-old hall system.

The Morris Avenue row house

Three-story two-family houses with curved bow fronts, stoops and paired wood-and-glass doors, designed by John Hauser between 1906 and 1910 and protected inside the historic district.

The building with stores below

Apartment houses on East Burnside Avenue, East Tremont Avenue and Jerome Avenue with bodegas, bakeries, restaurants and salons at street level and families on every floor above.

The new building and the old house

Tall new buildings on Walton, Morris and Jerome with filed systems of their own, and at the other end of the scale, the few wood-frame and late-1890s houses tucked between apartment houses.

The five-story walk-up: one stair, one roof, and alarms that live only inside the apartments

In a typical Mount Hope walk-up, every household leaves by the same stair, and the only fire warning in the building is whatever hangs on the ceilings inside each apartment. That arrangement is legal in most older buildings. After a fire in Wakefield, FDNY Chief Hodgens explained that smoke detectors are required inside apartments but common smoke detectors throughout the building are not, and officials noted that in older buildings tenants will not hear an alarm unless their own apartment is affected. Our plan for a walk-up therefore starts inside the apartments and makes every one of them right. Each apartment gets smoke alarms where its bedrooms and sleeping areas need them, a carbon monoxide alarm where the household sleeps and near any gas range or heater, and alarms that meet the city's sealed-battery or hardwired rule. We work up one vertical line at a time, so the tenants in 1A through 5A know the day and the hour, and we record each device against its apartment and room as it goes up. Then we look at the parts of the building nobody rents: the stair, the halls, the top-floor ceiling under the cockloft and the cellar. Adding detection in those spaces that warns the whole building is usually filed work designed by a registered design professional, and we say so before an owner commits to anything. What we can always do is tell the owner what is there now, what condition it is in, and what the apartments themselves need to be beyond question.

The six-story elevator apartment house: long halls, a sixth floor under the roof, and residents who need time

The six-story elevator buildings on the Grand Concourse, Walton Avenue and Mount Hope Place are where the most people share one roof, and where a fire on the top floor can do the most damage before most of the building knows about it. The 2017 Walton Avenue fire is the example every owner of one of these buildings should study. It began on the sixth floor just after 6:30 a.m., smoke filled the halls while many residents were asleep, and the FDNY found heavy fire in the cockloft above the top-floor apartments. DOB records described the building as home to many elderly and disabled people. We do not know what alarms that building had or whether they sounded, and the reports do not say. What the case shows is the shape of the risk. In a building this size we survey the sixth-floor hall and its ceiling, any access to the roof space, the elevator machine room, the lobby and the cellar, and we read whatever panel the building has. We ask the managing agent which apartments house residents who use wheelchairs, walkers or oxygen, or who cannot hear well, not to put that on a list for us but so the plan inside those apartments accounts for them, with alarm types and placement chosen for how those residents actually sleep and move. Where the building has a hall system, we test it end to end. Where it does not, we explain what a designed system would involve and price the apartment work separately so the owner can do the urgent part now.

The rehabilitated building: reading two histories before adding a third

A Mount Hope building that was gutted and rebuilt in the 1980s or 1990s often looks newer inside than its facade suggests, and its fire protection was usually designed for the apartments as they were laid out during the rehab. Since then, apartments may have been combined, kitchens moved, rooms divided, and alarms replaced one at a time by different contractors. Before adding anything, we map what is there: every apartment alarm with its type and date, every hall or cellar device, the panel and its zone descriptions, the standby batteries and their dates, and any monitoring account. We compare the zone descriptions with the apartment numbers on the doors, because a panel that says a signal came from a zone that no longer exists is not much help at four in the morning. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules sets the boundary for what follows: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel with something different needs new plans filed with the FDNY. Many rehab-era systems can be brought back with repair work. Some are too old for parts, and for those we say so early, with the apartment work priced on its own so it does not wait on a filing.

The Morris Avenue row house: two households behind a curved front

The Historic Districts Council describes the Morris Avenue houses as two-family row houses and tenements designed by John Hauser and built in five campaigns between 1906 and 1910, most with three-story curved bow facades, and the LPC report on the district notes their stoops, wrought-iron railings and paired wood-and-glass doors with transoms. Inside, a row house like this usually holds an owner on one floor or two and a tenant on another, sharing a stair and a cellar. The fire alarm job is closer to a house than an apartment building, but it carries its own care. The front rooms behind the bow can be bedrooms or parlors with tall windows, the ceilings are often original plaster, and running new cable between floors can mean opening walls nobody wants opened. We usually recommend sealed ten-year smoke and CO alarms with wireless interconnect for houses like these, so a fire in the cellar or on the top floor sounds in both households without cutting a channel through a hundred-year-old wall. Because the interior work stays inside, it does not touch the facade the district protects. We found no LPC rule written specifically for fire alarms, so if a job ever needs anything on the outside, a strobe, a conduit, a box on the roof, the owner should raise it with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before the work is scheduled. The two-alarm fire that tore through the roof of the three-story building at 1998 Morris Avenue, a building named in the district's report, is a reminder that these houses burn like any others, and that a fire reaching the roof leaves several families looking for a place to stay. PIX11 reported that 18 people from three families needed emergency help.

The building with stores below: a shop's hours and a family's hours

East Burnside Avenue, East Tremont Avenue and Jerome Avenue are lined with apartment buildings that have shops at street level: bodegas, Dominican bakeries, Caribbean restaurants, salons and small groceries, according to CityNeighborhoods.nyc. Each of these businesses has its own heat sources, stock and hours, and each sits directly under bedrooms. A bakery may be working before dawn while the floors above sleep. A grocery may close at night and leave a cellar of stock and compressors alone for hours. A salon runs hot tools and aerosols all day. Our plan for a building like this treats the store and the apartments as two halves of one problem. Inside the store, detection is chosen for the room: heat detection where ovens, fryers or compressors would fool a smoke detector, smoke detection in clean areas and stockrooms. Then we decide, with the owner and the tenant, how a fire in the store reaches the families above, whether through monitoring that summons a response, through alarms the apartments can hear, or both. We write down who owns each device, who is called after hours and who has keys, because a fire in a locked store under thirty apartments is the case this plan is built for.

The new building and the old house: two ends of the same block

The tall buildings now going up on Walton Avenue, Morris Avenue, Mount Hope Place and Jerome Avenue are designed and filed with full building fire alarm systems, and buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor according to SPARC's summary, must include one-way voice communication. Inside those buildings, our work is usually narrow: an apartment owner or tenant who wants a carbon monoxide alarm moved, a commercial tenant fitting out a ground-floor space that has to tie into the building's system under the landlord's rules, or a management company that needs extra hands on test day. At the other end of the block, a wood-frame house or a survivor like the 1899 house on East 176th Street has nothing but what its owner put up. There we plan as we would for any older house: smoke alarms on every level and near every sleeping room, CO alarms by the fuel-burning equipment and where people sleep, linked so one warning becomes every warning. The two ends of the block need different things, and we price them differently. What they share is that a fire next door does not care which kind of building it started in.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Mount Hope Apartment House Before Drawing Anything

Start at the top, because the recent fires did. The technician goes to the sixth or fifth floor first, looks at the hall ceiling under the roof, finds the roof access and notes whether the space above the top-floor ceilings can be seen or reached. The Walton Avenue and West Tremont Avenue fires both started on top floors.

Knock on a sample of doors, line by line. With the managing agent's notice to tenants in hand, we look inside one apartment in each vertical line: where the bedrooms are, what alarm is there, its date, whether it is sealed, hardwired or the old kind with a battery door, and whether a CO alarm exists at all.

Find what the rehab left. In the lobby and cellar, we photograph any panel's screen, check its battery dates, read its zone list against the apartment numbers, and note devices in the halls that no longer connect to anything.

Look at what tenants keep. Clutter, a space heater by a bed, a scooter charging by the door: we note them without judgment, because the East 176th Street fire showed how clutter changes a fire, and the plan has to fit the apartment as it is lived in.

Walk out the way the tenants would. Finally the technician goes down the stair from the top floor to the street, the way every household in the building would leave, and notes what they would see and hear on the way. Only then do we start the apartment list.

Compliance notes for Mount Hope buildings and businesses

  • Apartment alarms are required whether or not the building has a system. FDNY Chief Hodgens summarized it after the Wakefield fire: smoke detectors are required inside apartments, while common smoke detectors throughout an older building are not.
  • Sealed or hardwired. Brick Underground reports that by 2021 all residential smoke detectors in New York City had to be powered by a ten-year sealed, non-removable battery or hardwired. Sources disagree about whether some multi-family buildings need hardwired devices, so owners should confirm against the Housing Maintenance Code or with the FDNY before relying on either reading.
  • Bedrooms in subsidized apartments. HPD's inspections page says the NSPIRE standard, effective December 29, 2024, requires smoke detectors to be hardwired or sealed ten-year and at least one working smoke alarm inside each bedroom.
  • Owners file a certificate. The Habitat Group's summary says an owner must file a Certificate of Satisfactory Installation with HPD within ten days after all detectors are installed, when devices are changed on schedule, or whenever broken or missing devices are replaced.
  • Plans first on a filed system. Design and installation documents go to the FDNY for review and approval before installation, plans are approved by a New York State registered engineer or architect, the fire alarm plan involves a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder, and the FDNY issues a Project Authorization before work begins. We install, label and pre-test; we are not the design professional.
  • Repair versus redesign. Identical replacement with no design change is a repair; moving or replacing a control panel with different equipment needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Complete before inspection. A filed system must be 100 percent complete and pre-tested before the FDNY's Fire Alarm Inspection Unit is asked to inspect it, and owners keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years.
  • Voice communication in tall buildings. Buildings over 125 feet need one-way voice communication. Mount Hope's walk-ups are nowhere near that height; the new towers of 14 and 15 stories are the buildings where the question arises, and their designers decide it.
  • One historic district. Exterior work on the 35 buildings in the Morris Avenue Historic District may need Landmarks Preservation Commission review. We found no LPC rule specific to fire alarms, so we keep work inside wherever we can and flag anything exterior before it is scheduled.
Sixth Floor, 6:30 a.m.

What the 2017 Walton Avenue Fire Teaches About Protecting a Mount Hope Apartment House

On September 27, 2017, a fire broke out on the sixth floor of the apartment building at 1920 Walton Avenue, at East 177th Street, just after 6:30 in the morning. ABC7 reported that many residents were still sleeping as smoke began to fill the halls, and that the fire reached five alarms. FDNY Commissioner Daniel Nigro said crews found heavy fire in the area between the top floor and the roof, the cockloft, and called it an area in this type of building where fire spreads rather quickly. DNAinfo added that the building has about 75 apartments and, according to Department of Buildings records, is home to many elderly and disabled people, and that in 2003 a caller had reported sparking wires coming through the walls of a second-floor apartment, which DOB inspectors could not get into. We do not know what caused the fire, what alarms were in the building, or whether they sounded, and nothing in those reports says. We make no claims about any of it. What the reporting does describe is a set of conditions that exist in dozens of Mount Hope buildings today, and that is why we use it to explain how we plan.

A top-floor fire threatens the floors below through the halls and the floors above through the roof. In a six-story apartment house, the top floor has no floor above it, only the cockloft and the roof. A fire there can move into that space and run along the building above the ceilings of other top-floor apartments, while smoke moves down and out through the public hall and the stair. An alarm inside the apartment where the fire started is the first warning. Whether anyone else in the building hears anything depends on what is in the halls, and in most older buildings that is nothing. Our surveys always include the top-floor hall and the roof access for this reason, and when the owner wants a designed system, the top floor is where the design conversation starts.

Early morning is when warnings matter most and are least likely to be noticed. At 6:30 a.m. most households are asleep with bedroom doors closed. A smoke alarm in the hall of an apartment may not wake a person behind a shut bedroom door, especially an older resident or someone who sleeps deeply. This is the reason HPD's NSPIRE standard now asks for a working smoke alarm inside each bedroom in apartments it inspects, and the reason we recommend linking the alarms within an apartment so that a fire in the living room sounds in every bedroom.

Residents who need more time need more warning. A building that is home to many elderly and disabled people needs alarms that those residents can hear, and a plan for how they get out that does not depend on them reacting as quickly as everyone else. The fire alarm can only do the first part. We ask managing agents about residents with hearing loss, mobility limits or medical equipment, choose devices and placement for those apartments with that in mind, and suggest that the building's own emergency planning take account of them. We do not keep those details after the job; we only use them to put the right device in the right room.

Reports that never get inspected still matter. The 2003 complaint about sparking wires is a reminder that buildings carry warning signs long before a fire, and that some of them are never resolved because nobody could get into an apartment. A fire alarm survey is not an electrical inspection, and we do not present it as one. First Floor and Fifth Floor

East 176th Street, Weeks Avenue and the Other Ways Fires Start Here

Not every Mount Hope fire begins under the roof. In February 2024, ABC7 reported a fatal fire in a four-story building on East 176th Street that officials said started on the first floor and spread to the other floors, with firefighters facing heavy clutter inside. On Weeks Avenue, between 175th Street and the Cross Bronx Expressway, News 12 Bronx reported a fire that broke out on the fifth floor at around 5:48 a.m., took nearly 60 firefighters to put out and left one person in serious condition. And in May 2026, just west of the neighborhood's usual boundary on West Tremont Avenue between Phelan Place and Sedgwick Avenue, a fire just after 1 a.m. started on a top floor, spread to an adjoining apartment and killed one person; the Red Cross said it was helping seven families, thirteen adults and four children. As with Walton Avenue, we do not know what alarms were in these buildings or how the fires were noticed, and we make no claim about it. Taken together, these fires describe how a plan has to work in buildings like these.

A fire on the first floor rises past everyone. In a four-story building, a fire on the first floor is beneath every household and beside the only stair. Smoke takes the stair before the people above can. The alarms inside those upper apartments respond only when smoke reaches them, which may be after the stair is already full. That is the strongest argument for warning that reaches the whole building, and also the strongest argument for keeping the first-floor apartment's own alarms in perfect order, because they are the building's earliest chance.

Clutter changes everything about a fire. Firefighters at East 176th Street faced heavy clutter. Belongings stacked high give a fire more fuel, block the path smoke takes to the ceiling, block the way out and slow firefighters coming in. An alarm on the ceiling still works in a cluttered apartment, but only if smoke can reach it and only if someone can then get out. We place alarms where stacks do not bury them and tell owners honestly when an apartment's condition is a fire hazard of its own.

Fires between midnight and dawn depend on alarms that wake people. Weeks Avenue at 5:48 a.m., West Tremont Avenue just after 1 a.m., Walton Avenue at 6:30 a.m.: in every case most residents would have been asleep. An apartment alarm that is present, dated, powered and placed where a sleeping household will hear it is the one piece of protection every Mount Hope apartment can have regardless of what the building has. Our work starts there, and we test it the way it will be used, with someone listening from the farthest bedroom with the door closed.

Adjoining apartments share the fire. The West Tremont Avenue fire spread to the apartment next door. In buildings where apartments share walls, ceilings and the space above them, one household's fire becomes the neighbors' within minutes. That is part of why an owner should know the state of every apartment's alarms, not just the ones that tenants complain about.

Apartment houses, old systems and new neighbors

Could the smoke alarms in every apartment of our building be wired together so all of them sound when any one does?

Linking alarms within one apartment is good practice; linking every apartment in a building to every other apartment is not how apartment alarms are meant to work, and it usually makes a building less safe rather than more. Smoke alarms inside a dwelling are designed to warn that household, and inside an apartment we link them so a fire in the kitchen sounds in every bedroom. If every apartment in a sixty-unit building were tied together, every piece of burnt toast in any kitchen would sound in sixty homes, several times a week. Within a month most households would be pulling batteries, covering alarms or ignoring the sound, which is the worst possible outcome. Warning that reaches the whole building is the job of a building fire alarm system, with detection placed in the public halls, stairs and other common spaces and reporting to a panel that sounds notification across the floors. In most cases that is filed work, designed by a registered design professional and approved by the FDNY. If what you want is for a fire in one apartment to reach the rest of the building, that is the conversation to have, and we can price the apartment alarms separately so they are not held up while the building system is designed.

A tenant's apartment is packed with stored belongings, some stacked close to the ceiling. Can an alarm still protect that apartment, and what can the owner do?

An alarm still works there, but clutter limits what it can do, and the owner's options are practical rather than technical. Smoke rises to the ceiling and spreads across it; stacks near the ceiling can trap it in pockets and delay the moment it reaches the alarm. Clutter also adds fuel, blocks the way out and slows firefighters, which officials pointed to at the fatal East 176th Street fire. On our side, we place each alarm where stacks are least likely to bury it, in the center of a clear stretch of ceiling and away from the tallest piles, add an alarm inside the bedroom where the tenant sleeps, link the alarms so a fire anywhere in the apartment sounds by the bed, and check that the carbon monoxide alarm is reachable and audible. We note the condition in our report factually and without comment on the tenant. Neither we nor the owner can simply remove a tenant's belongings. The owner can raise it with the tenant, and where the tenant is elderly or struggling, with family or a caseworker, and can make sure the apartment's alarms are tested more often than the rest of the building's. We will come back to retest an apartment like that whenever the owner asks.

Our walk-up was rebuilt in a 1980s rehab, and the hall devices and panel date from then. Before we spend money on repairs, how do we tell which parts of that system are worth keeping?

We separate the system into the parts that age by the calendar, the parts that age by condition, and the parts that age by availability, and each gets a different verdict. Detectors age by the calendar: manufacturers specify replacement intervals, and devices from a 1980s rehab are long past them no matter how clean they look. Standby batteries in a panel age the same way, and they should carry a date. Cable and boxes age by condition: if the wiring tests sound, is supported properly and has not been cut or spliced by later renovations, it can often stay. The panel itself ages by availability: some rehab-era panels are still supported, and some have no parts left anywhere. The rule that matters for the budget is the one in ASM Integrators' summary: replacing detectors, pull stations or a panel with identical parts and no design change is a repair, while replacing a control panel with different equipment, or moving it, needs new plans filed with the FDNY. So a system whose panel can still be serviced can often be restored with new detectors and batteries as repair work, while a system whose panel cannot be serviced becomes a design and filing job. Our survey tells you which of those you have, in writing, before you commit.

Over the years, apartments in our building were combined and renumbered, and now the panel's zone descriptions name apartments that no longer exist. How is that sorted out?

We start by finding out what each device really is and where it really sits, and only then change any label. A zone description that names an apartment that has since been merged into its neighbor means a signal sends the super, or a firefighter, to the wrong door. We walk the building with the panel's zone list, trigger or trace each device and circuit, identify where it terminates and which rooms it covers today, and build a new device list matched to the current apartment numbers. Where cable runs are unmarked, we trace them with a tone generator and probe so nothing is guessed. If the fix is purely documentation, new zone descriptions, a new device list and labels on the devices, that is straightforward. If the investigation shows that the zones themselves no longer make sense, for example one zone now covers parts of two apartments that should be separate, rearranging them may be a design change, and the owner should ask a design professional whether the building's filed documents need updating. Either way, the owner ends up with a list that matches the doors.

A tall new building has been filed for the lot beside our 1901 walk-up, with a demolition permit on the old structure. What should we watch in our own fire protection while that work goes on?

Watch for dust, power interruptions and disturbed walls, and test your alarms more often until the work next door is finished. Marketproof's record for 29 Mount Hope Place, a 1901 four-family walk-up, shows a filing next door for a 15-story, 99-unit building and a demolition permit, and similar filings in 2026 on Morris, Walton and Jerome Avenues mean many Mount Hope owners will live beside a construction site for a year or more. Demolition and excavation raise fine dust that drifts through old windows and gaps, and smoke alarms near lot-line windows can foul or begin nuisance alarming. Construction often brings brief power and utility interruptions, which can leave a hardwired alarm on battery or put an old building panel into trouble. Vibration can loosen devices mounted on old plaster. And if the work exposes or disturbs a shared or adjoining wall, openings that once separated your building from the old one may need attention by the right trade. Before the work starts, photograph your apartments' alarms, the panel screen if you have one, and the lot-line walls inside and out. During the work, ask the super to check the panel and the apartments nearest the site after any outage. When it ends, have every alarm on that side tested.

Inside the apartments: certificates, bedrooms and batteries

We are replacing alarms a few apartments at a time over several weeks as tenants let us in. Does HPD expect one Certificate of Satisfactory Installation when the whole building is done, or a filing after each round?

Plan as if each round starts its own ten-day clock, and confirm the details with HPD or your attorney. The Habitat Group's summary of the rule says an owner must file a Certificate of Satisfactory Installation with HPD within ten days after all detectors have been installed in a building, when devices are changed according to the required time frames, or whenever broken or missing devices are replaced. Read plainly, that covers a building-wide installation and also individual replacements, so a staggered job that replaces devices in apartments over several weeks touches the obligation more than once. The safest course is to file within ten days of each completed round rather than waiting for the last apartment, especially if any of the work corrects a reported missing or broken device. We make that easy by delivering the records for each round the day it is finished: the apartments covered, each device's type and location, the installation date and the test result. If the building also has a violation on record, the certification for that violation has its own requirements, which your managing agent should check separately. We are not lawyers and do not file with HPD on your behalf, but we can make sure the facts you file are complete and match what is on the ceilings.

HPD's NSPIRE inspections ask for a working smoke alarm inside each bedroom, and the apartments in our 1920s building only have one alarm in the hall outside the bedrooms. What has to change?

Each bedroom in an apartment inspected under that standard needs its own working smoke alarm inside the room, and the hall alarm stays where it is. HPD's inspections page says the NSPIRE standard, effective December 29, 2024, requires smoke detectors to be hardwired or sealed ten-year and at least one working smoke alarm inside each bedroom. For an older Mount Hope apartment with a single hall alarm, that means adding alarms in every room used as a bedroom, mounted on the ceiling where the manufacturer's spacing allows, clear of the corner where the wall meets the ceiling and away from the window and the radiator. We recommend linking the new bedroom alarms with the hall alarm, which sealed wireless models can do without opening walls, so that a fire in one bedroom sounds in the others and in the hall. Where the apartment has a hardwired hall alarm, we confirm what it can link to before choosing the bedroom devices. Before the job, ask the managing agent which apartments are subject to these inspections and whether any room used for sleeping is not shown as a bedroom on the lease; the safest course is to protect every room where someone actually sleeps. Each new alarm is recorded with its room and date for the inspection file.

Some apartments in our walk-up still have older battery smoke alarms with a door you can open to change the battery. Are those still acceptable, and what replaces them?

Those alarms belong to the generation the city moved away from, and replacing them should be part of the next round of work in the building. Brick Underground reports that by 2021 all residential smoke detectors in New York City had to be powered by a ten-year, sealed, non-removable battery or hardwired to the apartment's electrical system. A battery-only alarm whose battery can be pulled out does not meet that description, and an alarm whose battery has been pulled out protects nobody; NBC News reported that at the 2017 Prospect Avenue fire none of the four battery-operated smoke detectors in the apartment were working. The replacement depends on what each apartment has. Where there is no hardwiring, a sealed ten-year alarm goes in the same place or a better one. Where an apartment already has hardwired alarms with a replaceable backup battery, those are in the hardwired category, and we check that they are working, in date and correctly placed rather than tearing them out. A source we found argues that some multi-family buildings need hardwired devices, while others say sealed ten-year alarms are enough, so we recommend confirming against the Housing Maintenance Code or with the FDNY for your building. We record what we replaced, with what, and when.

A tenant took down the smoke alarm we installed and put up a Wi-Fi smoke alarm she bought online. Does her alarm count, and should we leave it?

Her alarm may be a perfectly good device, but the owner cannot rely on it for the building's records, and the simplest answer is usually to put the owner's alarm back and let her keep hers as an extra. The owner is the one who files the Certificate of Satisfactory Installation and answers to an inspector, so the owner needs to know that each apartment's alarm is listed, sealed or hardwired as the city requires, in date, placed correctly and documented. A consumer Wi-Fi alarm may or may not meet those requirements, its battery may be replaceable, and its app belongs to the tenant, who can disconnect or remove it when she moves. We can look at the device and tell the owner whether it meets the sealed or hardwired rule and whether it is placed sensibly. Most of the time we recommend reinstalling the owner's alarm in its proper location and leaving the tenant's alarm up if she wants the phone alerts it gives her, as long as it does not replace the owner's device or sit where it confuses which sound is which. We note both in the apartment's record. If the tenant removed the owner's alarm because it kept going off, that is worth solving at the same time, usually by moving it away from the kitchen or bathroom door.

Row houses and storefronts

Our Morris Avenue row house has the curved bow front, and the front room on the top floor is a bedroom with tall windows around the curve. Where does the smoke alarm go in a rounded room like that?

On the flat part of the ceiling, back from the curve and away from the windows, at a point that still covers the bed. The bow front gives these rooms beauty and a problem: tall windows around a curve mean drafts in winter and heat from the glass in summer, and a smoke alarm mounted out in the bay, close to the windows, sits in moving air that can push smoke away from it and in temperature swings that shorten its life. We look for the spot on the ceiling where the room's main volume is, typically just inside the line where the curve begins, keeping clear of the corner where the wall meets the ceiling as the manufacturer directs, and not directly over a radiator. If the ceiling has original plaster ornament, we avoid drilling through it and choose a plain area nearby. In a two-family row house, this bedroom alarm should be linked to the alarms in the rest of the owner's floors and, ideally, to the tenant's, which sealed wireless interconnect does without opening walls. Because all of this happens inside, it does not touch the facade the historic district protects. We test the finished alarm from the bed with the door closed, and if the curve swallows the sound, we add a second linked alarm rather than move the first one into the bay.

A prospective tenant wants to open a beauty-supply store in the empty storefront under our apartments, selling aerosols, hair dyes and styling products. Does that kind of stock change the detection we need?

It changes what the store needs and what the families above it need from the store, and it is worth settling before the lease is signed. A beauty-supply store holds a concentrated stock of aerosol cans and products that burn readily, often in a back room stacked to the ceiling, and a fire in that stock can grow quickly and send pressurized cans bursting. Inside the store, we would expect heat detection in the stockroom, where dense shelving and the stock itself make early smoke detection less reliable and where nuisance alarms from dust are likely, and smoke detection in the clean sales area, chosen so routine aerosol testing by customers does not set it off. The bigger question is the apartments above. The store will close at night, and a fire there after hours is beneath sleeping households, so detection in the store should report to someone who acts, through central station monitoring at $19.99 a month through our partners, and should be tied to a warning the residents can hear. How much flammable stock a store may keep, and how it must be stored, is a fire code matter for the tenant and, where a filed design is involved, the design professional, not something we decide. Put the detection, the monitoring and who pays for each into the lease negotiation, and we will give both sides a written scope to work from.

"Apartment buildings in New York City have a fire alarm that warns the whole building"

Generic answers often assume that any apartment building has a building-wide alarm, the kind found in offices and new towers.

FDNY Chief Hodgens said after a Wakefield fire that smoke detectors are required inside apartments but common smoke detectors throughout the building are not, and officials noted that in older buildings tenants will not hear an alarm unless their own apartment is affected.

Most of Mount Hope's prewar walk-ups fall into that category. Their protection lives inside each apartment, which is why every apartment's alarms have to be right, and why building-wide warning, where an owner wants it, is designed and filed work rather than an add-on.

"Any smoke alarm works if you test it once a month"

Many answers treat all smoke alarms as equal and put the whole burden on monthly testing.

New York City moved to alarms powered by a ten-year sealed battery or hardwiring, according to Brick Underground, precisely because removable batteries get taken out. At the 2017 Prospect Avenue fire in the Bronx, NBC News reported that none of the four battery-operated smoke detectors were working.

In a building of renters, monthly testing by each tenant cannot be assumed. Sealed or hardwired devices, a dated record of every one, and periodic checks by the owner are what keep a Mount Hope building protected between inspections.

"If the alarm sounds right away, the building is safe"

Some summaries suggest that a working alarm system is the end of the fire safety story.

At the 2022 Twin Parks fire in the Bronx, the building's fire alarm system was triggered immediately, yet smoke spread through the building because two self-closing doors malfunctioned, and people died on floors far from the fire.

Detection buys time. Doors that close on their own, stairs that stay clear of smoke and households that know what to do are how that time is used. We note doors that do not close when we are in a building, and we say plainly that the alarm is one part of the plan.

"One smoke alarm in the hallway outside the bedrooms is enough"

A common answer for apartments is a single alarm in the hall between the bedrooms.

HPD's inspections page says the NSPIRE standard that took effect December 29, 2024 requires at least one working smoke alarm inside each bedroom, along with hardwired or sealed ten-year power.
