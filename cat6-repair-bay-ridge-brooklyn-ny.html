The Neighborhood

Why Bay Ridge Properties Need Cat6 Repair: Buildings Here Are Renovated, Not Replaced

Most of what stands in Bay Ridge today will still be standing in thirty years, and that is by design. In 1978, after residents objected to the twin 30-storey Bay Ridge Towers and the Shore Hill Apartments, the city wrapped the neighbourhood in the Special Bay Ridge District, whose stated purpose is “to preserve, protect and maintain the existing scale and character of the residential and commercial community.” In 2005 the City Council went further and contextually rezoned 249 blocks between 65th Street, Seventh Avenue and Shore Road, more than tripling the blocks limited to one- and two-family homes, from 33 to 101. The result is a place where a house is far more likely to get a new kitchen than a new building, and where the network inside it grows the same way: piecemeal.

That shapes almost every Cat6 call we take here. A limestone row house might have its oldest runs from an attic office set up years ago, a second batch from the cellar renovation, a camera line drilled through the front during a security scare and two newer jacks added for working from home. Each was put in by a different person with different parts, on a different route, often without a drawing. When one link fails, the question is rarely “is this cable broken” on its own. It is which of those layers the run belongs to, what that installer used, and which way the building lets us reach it today. Community Board 10 describes the district plainly: one- and two-family homes make up most of the housing, ageing multiple dwellings line Shore Road, Ridge Boulevard and Fourth Avenue, and small clusters of four- to six-storey buildings sit elsewhere. Every one of those types keeps its cable in a different place.

Ownership makes the layering deeper. The NYU Furman Center puts the homeownership rate for Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights at 41.0% in its latest figures, against roughly a third in 2000, and it counts only 452 new housing units added between 2010 and 2025. People stay, and they keep improving the place they already own. That is good for the neighbourhood and hard on cable: a run that was fine when it went in has since had a bathroom tiled over it, a boiler replaced beside it, a radiator moved in front of it and a picture rail nailed through it. The failures we see are usually injuries from later work on the building, not cable that wore out on its own.

There is a second Bay Ridge pattern behind many calls: buildings carrying more households than they were built for. In 2014 Community Board 10’s zoning chair reported, from 311 data, more than 1,000 illegal-conversion complaints since 2010 in the council district covering Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton, and the council member’s office warned of “faulty wiring that can cause fires.” We do not judge anyone’s building, but we do see the network side of subdivision: a single internet service split across floors with consumer switches on shelves, extension runs stapled along stair stringers, and jacks fed from another household’s closet. Those are the links that fail first, and they need a careful hand because someone else’s home is usually on the same wire. Most Bay Ridge owners who call us about a dead jack also end up asking about camera coverage for the house on the same visit, because the camera line is often the newest and most exposed layer of all.

Bay Ridge facts that shape a Cat6 repair

  • Zoned to stay low: the Special Bay Ridge District dates from 1978, and the 2005 rezoning covered 249 blocks and raised one- and two-family-only blocks from 33 to 101 (CityLand, April 2005). Old buildings keep getting renovated, so their networks keep getting patched.
  • Owners who stay: a homeownership rate of 41.0% for Bay Ridge/Dyker Heights, and just 452 new units built from 2010 to 2025 (NYU Furman Center). Most Cat6 here lives in buildings far older than the cable.
  • A protected limestone block: the Bay Ridge Parkway–Doctors’ Row Historic District, the neighbourhood’s first, covers 54 two-storey-and-basement limestone-fronted row houses built between 1906 and 1913, still home to many medical offices (Landmarks Preservation Commission, 2019).
  • Busy avenues: the Bay Ridge 5th Avenue BID counts 376 ground-floor retail businesses on 40 block faces, and the 86th Street BID 145 on 16 block faces (NYC Small Business Services directory). Nearly all of them run registers, card terminals or cameras over copper.
  • Post-war co-ops on the water: 9201 Shore Road, built in 1960, holds 254 apartments, and its neighbour at 9323 Shore Road, from 1961, has 72; a local broker counts nine co-op and condo buildings along Shore Road.
  • Storms still find the low spots: after Hurricane Ida in September 2021, Brooklyn Paper photographed Parks staff shovelling mud in Owl’s Head Park and a large puddle blocking part of the Shore Road promenade; most of the borough took five to six inches of rain that night.

Six kinds of Bay Ridge building, each with its own habits. The neighbourhood’s housing is unusually varied for a few square miles, and the cable inside follows the building, not the street.

Limestone and Brick Row Houses

Houses of two or more storeys over a basement or cellar, built in long matching rows; Doctors’ Row on Bay Ridge Parkway is the protected example. Masonry party walls on both sides, plaster on lath or block inside, and a cellar that usually holds the network.

Detached Houses Near Shore Road

Larger free-standing houses on the streets near the water, some of them landmarks, like the 1917 Arts and Crafts house at 8200 Narrows Avenue. Long internal distances, several floors and often a second switch upstairs.

Semi-Detached and Two-Family Houses

Brick pairs and two-family houses across the blocks rezoned in 2005. Two households, sometimes one internet service, and runs that cross from one unit’s space into the other’s.

Post-War Co-ops on Shore Road

Elevator buildings from around 1960, with board rules and a superintendent. Where partitions are plaster on masonry there is little hollow space to fish through, so owners often ran cable along floors, baseboards and door frames.

Pre-War and Mid-Rise Apartment Buildings

The ageing multiple dwellings along Ridge Boulevard and Fourth Avenue and the four- to six-storey clusters elsewhere. Landlord-installed camera and intercom cable shares hallways with tenants’ own runs.

Mixed-Use Avenue Buildings

Shop at street level, apartments above, cellar below, along Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street. The shop’s network usually lives in the cellar or under the counter, and the cable climbs through the shop floor.

How the repair changes with each building

Row houses. The cellar is the starting point: most runs leave a cabinet or shelf down there and climb through floor openings, stair walls or old pipe routes. Because both side walls are shared masonry, cable rarely runs inside them; it hugs interior partitions, which is where later bathroom and kitchen work catches it. We test from the cellar, measure the distance to each fault, and convert that distance into a room and a wall before anything is opened. Plaster on lath is cut small and neatly so it can be patched without a full skim coat.

Detached houses. The problem is reach, not masonry. Runs can be long enough that a marginal termination at each end adds up to a failure, and a house with switches on two floors depends on whichever single cable joins them. We test every segment separately and check the length of the longest runs against the 90-metre permanent-link limit before blaming connectors.

Semi-detached and two-family houses. We map whose cable is whose before working, because a repair in one unit can interrupt the other. Where one household’s run passes through the other’s ceiling or closet, we agree access first and look for a route that keeps each household’s cable on its own side.

Post-war co-ops. With little hollow wall to fish through, the route options are surface raceway, closets, door casings and the gap behind baseboards. A repair is normally planned so it fits the building’s alteration or decoration rules; we provide our licence and insurance details for the managing agent if the board asks for them.

Pre-war and mid-rise rentals. Hallway raceway, shared closets and a superintendent’s locked cellar are normal. We identify which runs serve the owner’s cameras or intercom and which serve tenants, and repair only what we were asked to touch.

Mixed-use buildings. The cable that matters most is short: cellar to counter, counter to card terminal, counter to the camera recorder. It is also the cable most exposed to deliveries, mops, heat and shutters, so the repair usually includes moving it out of harm’s way.

Shops, restaurants and practices: what fails and why

Avenue businesses in Bay Ridge are mostly small, which means their network is small too: a modem, one switch, a handful of runs. That makes each link more important, not less. When the only cable to the register fails on a Saturday, the shop is cash-only until it is fixed. The repairs we do most often on the avenues are re-terminations at the counter, replacement of cords crushed by drawers and stools, and short new runs from the cellar to a counter that was moved during a refit. Cameras over the door and in the cellar come next, usually after a power or picture loss traced to a corroded plug or a cable pinched by a roll-down gate housing or awning bracket.

Practices are different. A ground-floor dental or medical office in a row house can have more network points than the rest of the building put together: front desk, each operatory or exam room, a server cupboard and often imaging equipment. Downtime there is measured in cancelled appointments, so we plan around the schedule, test every link once at the start of the visit, and repair in the order the practice needs them back.

Compliance callouts for Cat6 repair in Bay Ridge buildings

  • Landmarks. Doctors’ Row and individual landmarks such as the 8200 Narrows Avenue house are regulated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission. LPC’s own guidance says no permit is needed for “ordinary repairs or maintenance chores,” while interior work needs one when it requires a Buildings Department permit or affects the exterior. Replacing cable through an existing opening is the first kind of job; a new hole through a designated facade is the kind of thing to raise with LPC before anyone drills.
  • Electrical code. The current New York City Electrical Code, based on the 2020 National Electrical Code with city amendments, took full effect on December 21, 2025. Communications cable must keep its separation from power wiring and be properly supported, and accessible abandoned cable is meant to come out rather than be left in place (NEC 800.25).
  • Older paint. Federal lead-safe rules apply to paid work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes built before 1978, with the main requirements triggered above six square feet of interior surface per room, and New York City’s Local Law 1 of 2004 has owners of buildings built before 1960 presume their paint is lead-based. Our access openings are normally far smaller; if a repair would need more, you hear it before work starts.
  • Co-op and condo rules. Shore Road and Ridge Boulevard boards set their own alteration rules. A small, like-for-like repair is often handled under a short decoration agreement through the managing agent, while bigger re-routes may need board review; we supply licence and insurance details on request.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Bay Ridge: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bay Ridge owners, tenants and shopkeepers describe when they call, grouped by the kind of building they happen in. Each answer comes first; the detail follows.

Row houses, shared walls and stone fronts

The house next door is being gutted. Can work on the far side of our shared party wall damage the Cat6 on our side?

Yes, and it usually happens in one of three ways, all of which we can test for and repair from your side. The first is drilling: anchors for new cabinets, shelving or steel brackets on the neighbour’s side can travel further into old masonry than the installer expects, especially where the wall has voids, and a bit that breaks through can catch a cable fished up a chase or a furred-out section on your side. The second is chasing: a neighbour’s electrician cutting a channel for new wiring can crack plaster on your side and pull on anything fastened to it. The third is plain vibration and dust from demolition shaking loose a termination that was already marginal, typically a jack screwed to a box that was never firmly fixed.

The fastest way to know is to test before the heavy work starts if you can, and again afterwards. If the work has already happened, a certification test from your cellar or cabinet tells us which links changed and at what distance the fault sits, so we can say whether it lines up with the party wall or with something inside your own house. A fault at the right distance, with fresh plaster damage or a new anchor visible behind a small opening, is about as clear as these things get, and we photograph it for you.

The repair itself stays on your side: a short new length spliced in properly is not an option for Cat6, so the damaged section is replaced with a new run or the link is re-terminated nearer the damage if there is enough slack. Where runs sit close to a shared wall that is going to be worked on again, we suggest moving them onto an interior partition so the next renovation next door cannot reach them.

Camera cable through a limestone, brick or stone front

An existing hole in the facade can usually carry the new cable, as long as it is lined, sealed and the cable never touches raw stone. Many front-of-house camera runs in Bay Ridge were put in quickly, with a bare hole through the stone or a mortar joint and the cable pushed straight through. Over a few winters the jacket rubs on the cut edge, water follows the cable inward, and the plug at the camera end corrodes. By the time the picture goes, the cable is often damaged right where it passes through the wall.

We pull the old cable out, check the hole with a light and a mirror, and fit a sleeve so the new run never touches raw stone or brick. Outdoor-rated Cat6 goes in with a drip loop outside, the opening is sealed, and the plug or jack at the camera is replaced rather than reused. If the old hole is too damaged to sleeve, a new entry may be needed; in a historic district or on an individual landmark that is the point to check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before drilling, because a new opening through a designated facade is not the same as an ordinary repair. Exterior entries are priced from $185 each, sealed and bonded.

One more check is worth doing while we are at it. A camera that lost its picture may also have lost power, and power-over-Ethernet faults on a long or damaged run can look like a dead camera. We read the power and the link at the camera end before deciding what to replace, so a sound camera is not swapped for nothing.

Flat roofs, cocklofts and the top-floor run

Many Bay Ridge row houses and small apartment buildings have a flat roof with a shallow space under it, and owners sometimes ask whether a failed run to the top floor can be replaced across that space instead of up through finished rooms. Sometimes it can: if there is an access hatch and room to work, a new run can travel along the top and drop into a closet or a wall cavity. The catches are practical. These spaces can be cramped, hot in summer and hard to reach, they are no place for cable lying loose on insulation, and anything run there has to be supported and kept clear of roof drains and vents. If a roofer is about to open the roof anyway, a few minutes of coordination can make a top-floor repair much simpler.

Detached houses and two-floor networks

Detached houses where one cable carries the whole upper floor

When every upstairs room drops at once while downstairs carries on, the usual suspect is the single run joining an upstairs switch to the one below. Larger houses near Shore Road are often wired this way because it saves pulling every bedroom’s cable to the cellar. It works well until the one trunk link develops a fault: a termination loosened by a door slamming on the closet where the switch sits, a cable crushed where it turns through a floor, or a plug that has been unplugged and replugged so many times that it no longer seats firmly.

The symptom pattern is the giveaway. If every upstairs room drops together and comes back together, while downstairs carries on, we test that one trunk link first and look at the switch’s own logs if it keeps any. A link that passes on a basic tester can still fail a full certification under load, so we test it properly rather than just checking for continuity. We also check that the upstairs switch is not overheating in a closed cupboard, because that produces exactly the same symptom without the cable being at fault.

The repair is re-termination or replacement of that one run, and then a choice for you: leave it as a single point of failure, or have a second run pulled alongside while the route is open so the two switches can share the load and keep running if one cable is ever damaged again. In a detached house with a long internal route, the second cable is usually cheap compared with the cost of the access to install it.

Shore Road co-ops and apartment buildings

Runs under carpets and across door saddles in Shore Road co-ops

A cable laid under a rug or over a threshold strip is stepped on thousands of times a year, and that alone is enough to ruin it. Cat6 depends on the precise twist and spacing of its four pairs. Repeated pressure flattens the cable, changes that geometry and eventually breaks a conductor; long before that, the link starts failing the tests that matter for speed. A run that has been walked on for years cannot be repaired back into specification, so it is replaced.

The question is where the replacement goes. In post-war co-ops with plaster on masonry, the honest options are usually a slim surface raceway along the baseboard and around the door casing painted to match, a route through closets that back onto each other, or occasionally behind a baseboard that can be removed and refitted. Under the floor is rarely possible in a concrete building. We show you the options on the day with the measured run length, and the one you choose is installed so it never crosses a walking surface.

Because this is a co-op, the building’s rules apply. A small like-for-like repair inside your apartment is often treated as decoration rather than alteration, but boards differ, and new raceway or any opening in a wall may need the managing agent’s agreement first. We can send our licence and insurance details and a short description of the work in advance so the paperwork does not hold up the visit.

Hallway cameras and tenants’ own runs in Bay Ridge walk-ups

In the four- to six-storey buildings and the older multiple dwellings along Fourth Avenue and Ridge Boulevard, the landlord’s camera and intercom cable often shares a hallway raceway or a cellar ceiling with runs that tenants paid for themselves. When an owner calls us about a dead hallway camera, we identify the owner’s runs at both ends before touching anything, test those, and leave tenants’ cable exactly as we found it. If a tenant’s run turns out to be the problem, or to have been damaged by the same event, we tell the owner and leave the decision with them.

Avenue shops and ground-floor practices

The cable to the card terminal at our take-out window keeps failing where it passes through the window frame. What is going on?

The window is almost certainly pinching or bending the cable every time it opens and closes, and a stationary network cable is not built for that. Take-out and walk-up windows on Third Avenue and the side streets are often retrofits, and the terminal’s cable was simply run through the gap at the frame. Each slide of the sash squeezes it, the jacket splits, and the pairs inside are slowly flattened. Cold and rain at the opening make it worse, and grease from a kitchen nearby speeds up the damage to the jacket.

The repair is to stop the cable moving. We end the permanent run at a proper jack inside, beside the window, and connect the terminal with a short, flexible patch cord that can be replaced in seconds if it is ever damaged. Where the terminal sits outside the opening, the cable passes through a fixed, sleeved point in the frame or the wall beside it, never through the moving gap. The run back to the counter or the cellar is tested end to end, because years of flexing at the window can sometimes damage it further back as well.

If the shop is busy, we can usually put a temporary cord in place first so card payments keep working, then make the permanent repair once the window is quiet. The whole job is typically a couple of hours.

The imaging workstation in our ground-floor dental office keeps losing its connection in the middle of a patient visit. Is the cable likely to be the cause?

It can be, and it is quick to rule in or out: we test the link from the operatory to the cupboard where your switch and server sit, then the patch cords at both ends. Row-house practices on and around Bay Ridge Parkway are often networked room by room as the practice grows, so the operatory run may be newer or older than the rest, and it often passes through the busiest wall in the building. Chair movement, cart traffic and cleaning at the floor level are hard on the cord and the jack, while the run in the wall is more likely to have been damaged by later building work.

If the link passes full certification and the cords are sound, the cause is more likely the workstation, its network settings, the switch port or the imaging software, and we will say so plainly so your IT provider or equipment vendor can take it from there. If the link fails, we repair it with the same parts family as the rest of the practice and give you the test result, which is often exactly what an equipment vendor asks to see before they will look at their own hardware.

Because a practice cannot be offline during appointments, we test all the practice’s links early in the day or between sessions, and do the repairs in the order you need the rooms back.

Cellar cabinets that grew one switch at a time

The most common sight in a Bay Ridge cellar is not a rack. It is a shelf or a small cabinet holding the internet provider’s box, a router, one or two small switches, a camera recorder and a tangle of cords, each added by whoever was there at the time. Faults there are usually simple — a cord pulled half out, a power adapter failing, a switch that has run hot for years — but the tangle hides them. Tidying it is part of most repairs we do in a house: every run identified, labelled at both ends, cords replaced with ones of the right length, and a short list left showing which jack is which.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Bay Ridge

We could not open Google’s AI Overview for these searches from our research environment, so the claims below are ones commonly repeated in cost guides, forums and automated answers about cabling. Each is set against what a Bay Ridge building actually does to network cable.

“Network cable is permanent: once installed, it can be forgotten”

Cable in a quiet wall can last a very long time; that part is true. The trouble is that very few walls in Bay Ridge stay quiet. In a neighbourhood where zoning keeps the buildings and owners keep renovating them, the cable inside is exposed to every later project: new floors, moved radiators, tiled bathrooms, re-hung kitchen cabinets and work in the house next door.

The faults we find are overwhelmingly injuries rather than old age. A nail through a run behind a new baseboard, a cable kinked when a radiator was swung out and back, a jack loosened by a demolition next door. None of that shows up as gradual ageing; it shows up as a link that worked on Friday and not on Monday.

So the useful question after a renovation is not whether Cat6 lasts but whether the work touched it. A short certification of the links nearest to where the work happened answers that in an hour, while the walls are still easy to open.

“A fast enough Wi-Fi system makes wall jacks unnecessary”

Modern Wi-Fi is good, and plenty of apartments do without wired jacks. But masonry party walls, plaster on metal lath and the long, narrow footprint of a row house all weaken wireless signals between floors, and the access points of a mesh system work best when they are themselves joined by cable.

In practice, the Bay Ridge houses with the most reliable wireless are the ones with a sound Cat6 run to an access point on each floor. When that run fails, the mesh quietly falls back to a wireless link between units and the whole house slows down without any obvious fault.

Shops are clearer still: a card terminal, a register or a camera recorder that drops off a busy wireless network on a Saturday afternoon costs real money. For fixed equipment, a working cable is still the dependable choice, which is why repairing the one that failed is usually worth it.

“If the computer shows it is connected, the cabling has passed”

A link light only means the two ends agreed to talk at some speed. Gigabit Ethernet needs all four pairs; a fault on one pair can drop a connection back to 100 Mbps while the light stays on, and a damaged run can pass a simple continuity check while failing the crosstalk and return-loss limits that decide real performance.

This matters in Bay Ridge because so much cable here was put in by owners, handymen and internet installers who checked it with exactly that light. Years later, the run that “always worked” turns out to have been marginal from the start, and one more disturbance pushes it over the edge.

A certification tester settles it. It tests each pair against the category limits and reports where along the run a problem sits, which is the difference between a confident repair and guesswork.

“A damaged section of Cat6 can be cut out and joined”

Joining two pieces of Cat6 with a coupler, a crimp connector or a twist of tape is common advice and a common sight in Bay Ridge cellars. It may carry a connection for a while, but every such joint breaks the pairs’ twist and adds connection points the link was never designed for.

Industry troubleshooting guidance treats poorly twisted terminations and in-line couplers as typical causes of crosstalk failures, and a hidden joint inside a wall is also a future fault that nobody will know is there.

The proper repairs are re-termination at a jack or panel where there is enough slack, or a replacement run between proper end points. Where a joint already exists, we tell you, test it, and recommend replacing it if it fails or sits somewhere it cannot be reached again.

“Old masonry houses can’t take new cable without major demolition”

This one puts people off repairs they could easily have. Bay Ridge row houses and detached houses are masonry outside, but most of their interior partitions, closets and floor openings give workable routes, and a measured fault distance lets us open in one place rather than many.

Typical access openings are small and patchable. Closets that stack floor above floor, old pipe routes, the stair wall and the cellar ceiling all carry cable well, and where nothing hollow is available, a neat surface raceway is a perfectly respectable answer.

What old houses do need is a careful hand: plaster cut cleanly so it can be patched, painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes handled lead-safe, and stone or brick entries sleeved and sealed. That is a matter of method, not demolition.

“Whoever set up the internet can fix the wall jacks too”

Many can make a connection work. Fewer can prove it meets its category, find a fault inside a wall by distance, or tell a cable problem from a failing switch or power injector. Those are different skills and different tools from power wiring or setting up an internet service.

Internet installers in particular are usually responsible for their own equipment up to the modem or terminal; the cable inside your walls is yours. That gap is exactly where many Bay Ridge repairs begin, after an install visit that moved the router and left half the house’s jacks on the wrong side of it.

Ask any contractor three questions: will you test with a certification tester, will you give me the result, and are you licensed for low-voltage work in New York State? AESS answers yes to all three, under licence #12000287431.

National cost guides such as Fixr’s January 2025 update quote $125–$250 per new Cat6 drop and $45–$75 an hour for labour. Bay Ridge Cat6 Repair FAQ

Third Avenue closes for parades and street events several times a year. Can a repair at our shop be booked around those days?
+

Yes. Third Avenue hosts several parades a year as well as its everyday trade, and on those days the street is closed to vehicles and the pavement is crowded, which makes it hard to bring in ladders and reels of cable. Give us the event dates when you book. Testing needs only a hand-held tester and can be done on almost any day, but a visit that may involve replacing a run is better booked on an ordinary weekday, or early enough to finish before the street closes.

The plaster around our wall jack has crumbled and the jack keeps falling back into the wall. Can that be fixed?
+

Yes. In older Bay Ridge houses the jack was often screwed to plaster or to a box that was never fixed firmly, and after years of plugging and unplugging the plaster gives way. We fit an old-work bracket or a new box that grips the wall from behind, re-terminate the cable if it was strained when the jack fell in, and fit a matching plate. If the plaster around the opening is badly broken, we tell you whether a small patch is needed first.

Can you compare your test results with the readings the original installer left us?
+

Yes, if you still have them. In a house networked over several renovations, the only record of how a run performed when new is often a printout or file from the installer who put it in. If those results came from a certification tester, we compare length, loss and crosstalk figures link by link with ours; a run that has grown longer, or lost margin since, points to damage rather than a poor original install. If the old results came from a simpler tester, we explain what can and cannot be compared.

Only one of the two jacks on our wall plate works. Is the dead one fed by a separate cable?
+

It may be, or both jacks may share one cable. Some installers ran two cables to every plate; others split the pairs of a single cable between two jacks, which halves what each jack can do and often leaves one of them unreliable. We test both jacks from the cabinet end and look behind the plate. If there are two cables, the dead one is repaired like any other link; if there is one split cable, we explain the options, from one full-speed jack to a second run.

The way into our row house's cellar is a low door under the front stoop. Can the technician still get the equipment down there?
+

Yes. Almost everything a repair needs is hand-carried: the tester, tools, a few jacks and plates, and cable in boxes or short coils, so a low door and a tight cellar stair are not a problem. What helps is a clear path from the stoop to the network shelf, a working light down there, and anything stored in front of the cabinet moved aside before we arrive. If the only way in is through a tenant's space, tell us when you book so access can be arranged first.

A previous owner split our house into extra units, and we are turning it back into one home. What happens to the separate switches and runs each unit had?
+

We test every run and work out which unit and which switch it used to serve. Sound runs are brought back to one cabinet beside the single internet service, the small switches each unit relied on are removed or replaced, and runs that now serve nothing are taken out where they can be reached, since accessible abandoned cable is meant to be removed rather than left in the walls. Everything that stays is labelled at both ends, so the house ends up with one network and one list of which jack goes where.
