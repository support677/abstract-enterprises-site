The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need TV Installation

Bushwick is old housing with new people in it, and that combination is what makes hanging a TV here different from hanging one in a new tower across the river. The Department of City Planning's Bushwick Neighborhood Plan says nearly 80 percent of all buildings in the neighborhood were built before 1947. The same chapter notes that more than half of Bushwick's apartments sit in buildings with fewer than six units, which means the typical TV job is not in a doorman building with a service elevator. It is on the second or third floor of a narrow walk-up, up a stair that turns at every landing, in an apartment whose walls were finished by plasterers working a century ago.

Those walls are good walls, but they are not the walls the bracket instructions were written for. Every TV bracket in a box assumes you will find two wood studs sixteen inches apart and drive lag bolts into them. In a Bushwick walk-up you may find wood lath under the plaster, or plaster laid straight onto brick, or a party wall that, as one Brooklyn row-house owner put it on Brownstoner's forum, has "no studs, only plaster over brick." A stud finder tends to beep everywhere or nowhere on walls like that. The work is not harder because the walls are weak; it is harder because a fixing that is perfect in brick is useless in a hollow and a fixing that is perfect in a wood stud is useless in brick, and you have to know which one you are drilling into before you start.

The people in those buildings shape the job as much as the plaster does. City Planning counts about 90 percent of Bushwick households as renters, and only about 26 percent of households live in rent-stabilized apartments. For a TV, that means a landlord, a managing agent or a loft manager stands between most households and the drill, and many tenants expect to move within a few years. A good Bushwick TV installation is planned for the day you leave as much as for the day it goes up: holes that land behind the bracket, a cord cover that unscrews cleanly, and nothing in the wall that a landlord will call damage.

Condition matters too. The same city plan reports that roughly one in six Bushwick homes has three or more maintenance problems, and that between 2015 and 2018 the city's housing agency carried out more than 37,000 inspections and issued more than 50,000 violations in the neighborhood. A separate study by the Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development found that Bushwick buildings had more immediately hazardous violations per apartment than any other NYC neighborhood, 284.5 per 1,000 units. You do not need to know any of that to book a TV. But it explains why our technicians press on the plaster around a proposed spot before they mark it, and why we will sometimes tell you a different wall is the better choice.

Then there is the other Bushwick: the factory and warehouse buildings near Flushing Avenue and around Morgan Avenue that now hold studios, workshops and live-work lofts. A building like 195 Morgan Avenue, which is marketed as 79,352 square feet of creative loft and studio space, has walls of block and brick, concrete columns, exposed pipes across the ceiling and very few framed partitions. Screens in those spaces are often for work rather than for the couch: a monitor for reviewing footage, a display in a gallery, a TV over a bar. They need masonry anchors and careful cable routes instead of in-wall kits, and they come with a landlord who cares a great deal about what gets drilled.

Finally, Bushwick keeps building. Rheingold Gardens put attached brick townhouses on part of the old Rheingold brewery site, and the rest of that land was rezoned for new rental buildings. The neighborhood plan lists projects such as a building of up to 16 stories at 1531 Broadway and the 13-story Linden Court senior building. Newer construction like this usually means drywall, and often drywall on steel studs, which hold a TV well only with the right fasteners. So within a few blocks we may hang one TV on 1890s plaster, the next on a brick party wall and the next on steel. That range is the reason this page exists.

Same-Day Repair in Bushwick

Need Repair in Bushwick? A Bracket Creeping Out of Plaster, a Screen Hung on Mortar, or a Mount the Last Tenant Left Behind

The urgent calls from Bushwick tend to come in three shapes. The first is a screen that has started to droop at one corner or lean forward from the top. In plaster, that is usually a sign that the bracket was hung on plastic anchors in the plaster alone, and the plaster around them has started to crumble under the weight. Plaster is hard and brittle; it holds a picture hook for decades, but it does not hold forty or sixty pounds hanging forward off a bracket arm. The fix is to take the TV down, remove the bracket, find what is behind the plaster, and re-hang it into wood lath and framing or into the brick underneath.

The second is a bracket fixed into mortar on a brick wall. This is common in buildings where the brick is exposed and someone lined the bracket holes up with the joints because the mortar drills easily. This Old House puts the problem plainly: "Mortar is much softer than brick, and the anchor's grip will eventually deteriorate." In a Bushwick building with mortar that may be a century old, that day can come sooner than anyone expected. We move the fixings into the brick faces, which sometimes means shifting the bracket an inch or two sideways, and we fill the old mortar holes so they do not invite water on an exterior wall.

The third is the mount somebody else left behind. A new tenant moves in, finds a bracket on the wall and a hole with a cable coming out of it, and wants to hang their own TV on it. Sometimes that works. Often the bracket does not fit the new TV's bolt pattern, nobody knows what it is fixed into, and the cable in the hole turns out to be the old TV's power cord pushed through the wall, which the electrical code does not allow. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our list, and we tell you honestly whether the old bracket is worth keeping.

If your screen is tipping, do not push it back against the wall. If two people can lift it safely, take the weight off, unplug it and lay it face-up on a blanket or the bed. Then call (347) 934-8335 or send photos on WhatsApp. We give same-day estimates and book requests within two hours, and we will tell you on the phone whether the repair is a re-hang on the same wall or a reason to choose a different one.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Bushwick's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every Bushwick TV call. They differ in what the walls are made of, how far a boxed screen has to travel to reach the room, and who besides you gets a say in what is drilled. Those three things decide the plan well before anyone opens the bracket box.

The prewar walk-up

Three- and four-story apartment houses on the side streets off Knickerbocker, Wilson and Irving Avenues: stairs only, plaster walls, brick along the outside and between buildings.

The brick row house

Attached houses like the 32 on Linden Street from 1885 to 1901, with plaster over brick party walls, a parlor, and often a mantel that everyone wants the TV above.

The older frame house

Wood-framed houses scattered through the side streets, many now covered in siding, where the walls are plaster on lath over wood framing and studs actually exist, just not where the scanner says.

The converted factory loft

Industrial buildings turned into studios and live-work space, like 195 Morgan Avenue: painted block, brick, concrete columns, high ceilings and pipes overhead.

The Rheingold Gardens townhouse

Attached brick townhouses on the old brewery site, two-families like 26 Noll Street, built as a modern take on the Brooklyn row house and newer than almost anything around them.

The new rental or elevator building

Taller new buildings on the rezoned brewery land and along Broadway, with drywall partitions that are often on steel studs and an office that wants to know who is coming.

The prewar walk-up: a long carry, a short wall and plaster that has seen a century

The walk-up is the building most Bushwick TV calls come from, and for an installer the stair is half the job. A 65-inch TV in its box is more than five feet long, and a 75-inch one is longer still. In a walk-up stair with a tight turn at every landing, the box often has to be carried upright and tipped around the turns, which is exactly the handling the manufacturer warns against. We bring the moving blankets and a second pair of hands when the size and the stair call for it, and we ask you about the stair when you book, not when we are standing at the bottom of it.

Inside, the question is what sits behind the plaster. Partitions between rooms in a walk-up of this age are usually wood-framed with plaster on wood lath. The wall along the street, the wall at the back and the wall shared with the building next door are usually brick with plaster laid straight on it. That matters because the best-looking wall for the TV, the long unbroken one, is very often the party wall, and it is masonry. A bracket on that wall goes into brick with masonry anchors and the brick add-on applies; a bracket on the framed partition goes into the framing through the lath and the plaster add-on applies. Some living rooms have one of each, and the photos you send usually tell us which wall is which before we arrive.

Plaster this old also carries its history. It may have been patched, skim-coated, painted many times or cracked and filled. A technician presses on the plaster around the planned spot and taps along it, listening for the dull sound of plaster that has come loose from its lath. Where it has, a bracket can still go up, but the fixings have to reach sound framing or masonry and the bracket plate has to span the weak patch rather than sit in it. If a wall is in poor shape, we say so and suggest another wall rather than hang a screen on it and hope.

The brick row house: party walls, parlors and the chimney breast

Bushwick's row houses, including the 32 in the Linden Street Historic District, were built with brick party walls carrying the floors, plaster finishes inside and, in many parlors, a fireplace with a chimney breast standing proud of the wall. Party walls are the strongest place in the house for a heavy screen as long as the anchors go into the brick itself. This Old House's rule of thumb is to "drill into the brick, but not within a half-inch of its edges as it can crack," and to move the bracket if a mark lands on a mortar line. We follow it, which is why the first thing we do on a party wall is find the brick courses under the plaster.

The chimney breast is a separate case. Above a mantel, the breast is masonry, often brick, and behind its face there may be a flue that runs up through the house. In a fireplace that still works, heat rising past the TV is a real problem, a point Brooklyn owners raised on Brownstoner's forum when one asked about mounting above a parlor fireplace. In a fireplace that has been sealed or never lit, heat is not the issue, but the flue still is: anchors have to be short enough to stay in the solid face of the breast and not break into the flue behind it. Above-fireplace mounting is a $130 add-on on our list, and on a brick breast the $65 brick add-on applies too. Height is the other question. The same forum thread noted that a screen over a mantel can sit high enough to cause neck strain, so we tape the outline and have you sit down before we drill.

Owners of row houses in the historic district sometimes ask whether a TV inside needs permission from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. When the commission designated Linden Street, its chair told Brooklyn Paper, "We regulate changes to protect historic building fabric," and that designation covers the row houses' exteriors. Mounting a TV on an interior wall is not the kind of change it was set up to review. An outdoor screen on a front facade, or a dish visible from the street, is a different matter, and that is a conversation to have with the commission before anyone drills outside.

The older frame house: real studs, hidden under plaster and siding

Not every Bushwick house is brick. Some side streets still have wood-framed houses, many of them now covered in vinyl or aluminum siding, standing between brick buildings. Inside, these houses have plaster on wood lath on wood framing, and that is good news for a TV: there really are studs to find. The trouble is finding them. Lath and decades of plaster confuse electronic scanners, nails in the lath show up as metal every few inches, and framing in an older house is not always on the regular spacing a bracket expects.

We find the framing with a combination of a deep-scan pass, a strong magnet along the wall to locate the nails that hold the lath, and a single small pilot hole placed where the bracket plate will cover it. Once the studs are confirmed, a bracket fixed into two of them with lag bolts is about as strong as it gets. If the studs are not where the TV needs to be, a wider bracket plate or a full-motion arm with a long wall plate can reach them while the screen sits where you want it.

The exterior walls of a frame house are framed too, which means an in-wall power and cable kit is often possible on them, but they may hold insulation, old wiring or fire blocking. We look before we cut, and if there is anything in the bay that should not be disturbed, a paintable cord cover is the clean alternative.

The converted factory loft: block, brick, concrete and a landlord who reads the lease

Many of Bushwick's old industrial buildings now hold studios, workshops, offices and live-work spaces behind the same walls that once held machinery. A listing for 195 Morgan Avenue describes its 79,352 square feet as loft and studio space for artists, makers and creative businesses, and that is typical of what we find inside: walls of painted block or brick, concrete or steel columns, high ceilings with sprinkler pipes and ductwork running across them, and a handful of framed partitions that tenants or landlords added later.

Block needs care. Hollow concrete block has thin faces with voids behind them, and an anchor that lands in a void holds very little. We locate the solid webs and the mortar-filled cores and place anchors where they can grip, or use anchors designed for hollow block. Brick and poured concrete take masonry anchors with a hammer drill, and they are loud, which matters in a building where the next studio may be recording. We agree a time for the drilling before we start.

Framed partitions in lofts deserve a second look. Some were built properly with studs and drywall; some were put up by a past tenant from whatever was at hand. Before we hang a screen on one, we check what it is made of and how it is held to the floor and ceiling, because a light partition can flex under a screen on a full-motion arm. And because loft landlords often forbid opening walls or ceilings, cable routes usually run in a paintable cord cover or neatly along existing surfaces instead of inside them.

The Rheingold Gardens townhouse: newer brick, newer framing

Rheingold Gardens is a different generation of Bushwick housing. It went up on part of the 6.7-acre Rheingold brewery site, which Brownstoner notes was demolished in the 1980s, and the architects, Magnusson Architecture and Planning, designed it as "a modern update to the Brooklyn row house idiom." The houses are attached brick townhouses, many of them two-families like 26 Noll Street, with mansard roofs and dormers on the upper floor.

Newer construction usually means drywall inside, which is good for a TV, but it does not tell us what the drywall is fastened to. Interior partitions may be wood or steel studs, and the walls between houses are typically masonry. We confirm with a scan and a pilot hole as we do everywhere else. On wood studs the base price applies with no wall add-on; on the brick walls the $65 brick add-on applies. Rooms under the mansard roof have sloped ceilings and short walls, so we check the viewing height and bracket depth on those floors before choosing a spot.

In a two-family, the TV wall in one unit may back onto a bedroom in the other. We plan drilling times with that in mind and set soundbars up so the bass stays on your side of the wall.

The new rental or elevator building: steel studs, glass and a management office

The newest buildings in Bushwick, on the rezoned brewery land and along Broadway, are a different job again. The neighborhood plan lists new buildings such as one of up to 16 stories at 1531 Broadway, and Brownstoner reports that the remaining Rheingold land was rezoned for buildings that are 80 percent market-rate and 20 percent affordable rentals. Buildings of this generation are commonly framed inside with light-gauge steel studs and finished in drywall, with concrete slabs and concrete or block around stairs and shafts.

Steel studs are thin, so a lag bolt has nothing to bite into. A TV on steel studs is fixed with fasteners made for sheet steel, spread across more than one stud, or with a backer fixed across the studs first. We confirm the stud type with a magnet and a pilot hole. Where the wall is hollow and framed, an in-wall power and cable kit is usually possible, but many rental buildings do not allow tenants to open walls, so we ask the management office first.

Management offices in these buildings often want to know who is coming, when and with what insurance before they let a contractor up. We provide a certificate of insurance when asked, book the service elevator if there is one, and stay inside the building's work hours.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Bushwick Wall Before the First Hole

These notes describe how our technicians approach walls like Bushwick's. They are our standing practice, not a story about a particular address.

Find the brick courses first. On a plastered party wall, the technician drills one small test hole low behind where the bracket will sit. Red or orange dust after the white plaster dust means brick; grey dust and a sudden drop in resistance means a mortar joint. Knowing the height of one course lets us lay out the bracket so all its anchors land in brick faces.

Press, tap and listen. Old plaster that has lost its grip on the lath sounds dull and gives slightly under a firm palm. A bracket can span a patch like that, but its fixings cannot sit in it. If the patch is larger than the bracket plate, we suggest another wall.

Ask about the neighbors. If the wall is shared and the next apartment has a bedroom against it, the drilling is done in one burst at a time you agree, after the neighbors have been told. Nothing noisy happens early in the morning.

Check the carry. Before the TV leaves its box, the stair, the doorways and the turn into the room are checked. A large screen comes out of its box only in the room where it will hang, and lies face-up on a blanket until the bracket is ready.

Leave the move-out in mind. In a rental, every hole is placed where the bracket plate will hide it, and the technician tells you exactly how many there are and how large, so you can plan the patching when you leave.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick, Block or Steel: What Holds a TV on a Bushwick Wall and What Each One Adds

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. That wall exists in Bushwick, mostly in renovated apartments and newer houses, but it is the exception. Here is what we find instead, how each one is handled and what it changes on the bill.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The classic wall of a Bushwick walk-up partition or frame house: narrow wood strips nailed across the framing, covered in two or three coats of plaster that can add up to close to an inch. It is strong in compression and brittle under a point load. Anchors that grip only the plaster crack it. The bracket goes through the plaster and lath into the framing behind with lag bolts, or into anchors rated for the load that bear on the framing. We drill plaster slowly with sharp bits and without the hammer setting, and painter's tape over each mark keeps the edges from chipping.

Plaster laid on brick (+$65 for the brick behind it). On party walls and exterior walls, the plaster is often applied straight to the brick with no gap, and the brick is what carries the screen. Once a test hole confirms brick, the job is priced as a brick wall. The anchors go through the plaster into the brick faces, with sleeves or masonry screws sized for the TV, and the plaster around each hole is protected so it does not spall.

Exposed brick (+$65). Renovated Bushwick apartments and lofts often have a wall stripped back to the brick. It looks good behind a TV, and it holds well if the anchors go into the brick and not the mortar. On old brick that has been sandblasted, the faces can be softer than they look, so we test one hole first and choose the anchor length from what we find. Because nothing can be fished inside a solid brick wall, cables run in a paintable cord cover painted to suit, or straight down behind a console.

Concrete block and poured concrete. Block shows up in lofts, in basements and around the stairs of newer buildings; poured concrete appears in columns and in the newest buildings. Hollow block takes anchors placed in its solid webs or anchors made for hollow block; concrete takes masonry anchors with a hammer drill. Our list prices brick and stone at +$65; for block and concrete we confirm the add-on with you from the photos before booking, so it is settled before the visit.

Drywall on steel studs. Common in new rental buildings. Steel studs are thin, so lag bolts strip; fasteners made for sheet steel, spread across two studs, or a backer fixed across the studs first, solve it. Hiding the wires the legal way in a building that will not let you open the wall

After "put it on the wall," the most common request is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way. On Bushwick's brick and block walls, and in buildings where the landlord says no openings, the answer is a paintable cord cover at $59: a slim channel fixed to the wall that runs from the bottom of the TV to the outlet or console, painted to match. Renters and Owners

Leases, Loft Landlords and Move-Out Day: Mounting a TV on a Wall You Do Not Own

In a neighborhood where about nine in ten households rent, permission comes up on most calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what public guidance says and what we have learned from doing this work in rented apartments and studios.

Read the alteration clause. Brick Underground's Realty Bites column asked New York real estate attorney Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas about landlords who forbid hanging things. "It's not common for a landlord to ban you from hanging items," he said, and he pointed tenants to the lease's alteration provision. If it says nothing about wall fixtures, he said, a ban would not be enforceable; if it does, it governs. A broker in the same column advised asking permission before installing anything that leaves bigger holes, and TV brackets were his example.

Know what counts as damage. The same column quoted a property manager who said a nail-sized hole is generally fine, while larger holes count as damage that a tenant must repair or pay for. If a landlord keeps part of a deposit for repairs beyond normal wear and tear, the column notes that the owner must provide an itemized list of charges and return the rest within two weeks of the tenant moving out. A bracket leaves four to six holes about the width of a pencil, all behind the TV while it is up. We tell you how many there will be before we drill, and we can leave you the patching materials.

Get it in writing, briefly. A short message to your landlord with the wall, the TV size, the bracket type and the cable route is usually enough. If you choose an in-wall kit, say so, because it means two neat openings that will need patching later. If you choose a cord cover, say that it is screwed on and comes off.

Or do not drill at all. If the lease is strict or you expect to move within a year, a TV placed on its own stand on a solid console is a proper job, not a compromise. On our list that is $119.99, with a soundbar placed in front for $79, and it leaves nothing on the wall.

If you rent a studio or loft. Commercial and live-work leases in converted buildings are often stricter than apartment leases, and some landlords want to approve every fixing in masonry. Send the loft manager the same short note, ask whether they want a certificate of insurance, and confirm the hours when drilling is allowed. In a building full of studios, a neighbor may be recording, rehearsing or shooting, and quiet hours matter.

If you own the building. Owners of Bushwick walk-ups and two-families sometimes ask us to mount a bracket in each unit between tenants. The advantage is control: one bracket in the right place on solid fixings, one tidy cable route and a wall that stays sound from tenant to tenant, instead of a new set of holes every time someone moves in.

Lofts, studios and painted block

In our studio off Morgan Avenue, a sprinkler pipe runs across the block wall about a foot below the ceiling. How close to it can a screen and its cables go?

The screen itself can usually sit well below the pipe, because most people want a TV at seated eye level, not up by the ceiling. The rules we follow are simple: nothing is fixed to the sprinkler pipe or its hangers, no cable is tied to or draped over it, and nothing we install blocks a sprinkler head's spray. If the cables have to travel up the wall and across the ceiling, they go in their own supports or a cord cover that stays clear of the pipe. If your studio has sprinkler heads on the wall itself rather than the ceiling, send a photo, because the screen may need to move along the wall to keep the head clear. Drilling block near a pipe is done carefully, after we have looked at how the pipe is supported.

The outlets in our loft run in metal conduit on the face of the wall. Can the TV's HDMI and power follow that conduit to the screen?

They can follow its path, but they cannot go inside it. Electrical conduit belongs to the building's wiring, and low-voltage cables like HDMI are not pulled into it with power conductors; opening it or adding to it is electrician's work, and we do not do electrical work. What we can do is run the TV's cables in a separate paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, alongside the conduit so the two read as one clean line on the wall. The TV plugs into an existing outlet with its own cord. If the nearest outlet is too far or in the wrong place, that is the moment to bring in a licensed electrician, and we will tell you before booking rather than on the day.

A previous tenant built the partition we want to use out of plywood and two-by-fours. Will it carry a 65-inch TV?

Sometimes yes, sometimes not without help. A partition like that can be surprisingly strong if the studs are fixed firmly to the floor and the ceiling, because the plywood face gives the bracket a solid surface to bite into across its whole width. It can also be flimsy if it was only braced at one end or stands on a loose floor plate. We check how it is held at the top and bottom and push on it at screen height. A fixed or tilting bracket puts less leverage on a light wall than a full-motion arm pulled out into the room. If the partition flexes, we suggest a masonry wall or a TV placed on its stand at $119.99, and we tell you which before we drill.

Our gallery is showing video work for three weeks and the walls have to go back to white and unmarked afterward. What is the least damaging way to hang the screens?

Plan the holes before the show, not after. On drywall, a bracket fixed into the framing leaves a few small holes that fill and paint over in minutes. On brick or block, holes are larger and take more care to fill, so we keep them to the fewest the bracket needs and place them where they will be easiest to patch. Cables run in a paintable cord cover that unscrews at the end, or straight down to a plinth so nothing touches the wall at all. Taking each TV down at the end of the run is $85 on our list. If a work needs a screen at a particular height or angle, send us the artist's install notes with the booking so we can bring the right bracket.

"Use a stud finder, mark two studs, and screw the bracket in"

This is the first step of nearly every online guide, and the bracket instructions say the same thing. It assumes a wall of drywall on wood studs at regular spacing.

City Planning counts nearly 80 percent of Bushwick's buildings as built before 1947. Their walls are plaster on lath, plaster on brick, or brick alone. This advice appears regularly in answers about exposed brick, on the reasoning that mortar is softer, easier to drill and easier to patch later.

Softer is exactly the problem. This Old House puts it directly: "Mortar is much softer than brick, and the anchor's grip will eventually deteriorate." In a Bushwick walk-up or loft where the mortar may be a century old and already crumbling, an anchor in a joint can loosen within a few years under a screen's weight.

We drill into the brick faces with masonry bits, at least half an inch from the edge of each brick, and shift the bracket sideways if a mark lands on a joint. Mortar joints are where cord covers run, not where anchors go.

"Hide the power cord inside the wall with a pass-through plate"

Plenty of online answers suggest cutting a hole behind the TV and one near the floor and dropping the TV's power cord through the wall between them, often with a plate kit sold for the purpose.

The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords, such as a TV's power cord, to be concealed inside walls. A plate kit for low-voltage cables does not change that. And on Bushwick's brick and block walls, there is no hollow space to drop anything through in the first place.

On a hollow framed wall we fit an in-wall power and cable kit, $169, which carries power in its own rated cable. On masonry or where the landlord says no openings, we use a paintable cord cover at $59. Neither adds a circuit or moves an outlet; that is electrician's work, and we do not do it.

Design-focused answers often present the mantel as the obvious spot, because the furniture already faces it and the wall above is usually empty.

In a Bushwick row house, three things complicate that. A working fireplace sends heat up past the screen. The chimney breast is masonry with a flue behind it, so anchors must stay in the solid face. Bushwick TV Installation FAQ

Is the bracket included in the $179.99 base price for a Bushwick wall mount?
+

No. The $179.99 is the labor to mount a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, and the bracket is sold separately. If you already own a bracket rated for your TV's weight and size, we install it at the same labor price.

We booked our party wall as plaster, but the test hole shows brick underneath. What changes?
+

The wall is priced as brick, which is a $65 add-on instead of the $45 plaster add-on, so the difference is $20. The technician tells you before drilling any further and you agree to it first. If you would rather not, we can look at a different wall.

Can you hang a TV larger than 55 inches in a Bushwick walk-up?
+

Yes. The base price covers screens up to 55 inches, and larger screens are priced on your quote from the TV's model number. Tell us about the stairs when you book, because a big screen in a narrow walk-up stair may need a second technician for the carry.

Will the technician take the old TV off the wall before hanging the new one?
+

Yes. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our list. We also look at the old bracket and tell you honestly whether it is fixed well enough and fits the new TV, or whether it should come off.

Can the soundbar be added in the same visit as the TV?
+

Yes. A soundbar mounted on the wall under the screen is $129, and a soundbar placed on the console is $79. If you rent, the console option leaves two fewer holes to patch when you move.

How soon after I send photos can a Bushwick TV job be booked?
+

Requests are booked within two hours of reaching us. The appointment date depends on the schedule that week, and we never promise a same-day installation. Every appointment comes with a two-hour arrival window.

Could the technician show up before my arrival window starts?
+

No. Our technicians arrive inside the two-hour window you booked and never before it, so you are not woken early or caught out before you are ready.

Do I pay the technician at the door?
+

No. A job goes on the schedule once it is paid, so nothing changes hands on the day unless the wall turns out to need a different add-on, which is agreed with you before any drilling. Card payments carry a 3% card fee shown as its own line.

I already bought a full-motion arm online for my plaster living room wall. Will you install it?
+

Yes, if it is rated for your TV and the wall can take it. A full-motion arm puts more leverage on the wall than a fixed bracket, so on plaster we fix it into the framing or the brick behind, never into the plaster alone, and we tell you before booking if the wall is not a good match.

Does someone need to be home during the TV installation?
+

Yes, an adult who can approve the position, the cable route and any change in the wall add-on, and who can test the TV, the soundbar and the remote with the technician before we leave.

Is there a charge for looking at my wall photos and quoting?
+

No. Looking at your photos and giving you the total is free, and same-day estimates are available on most weekdays. You only pay once you decide to book.

Can you set up streaming apps and the game console if the TV is staying on its stand?

Yes. Placing the TV on its stand is $119.99, and if the TV is already in place, a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour, then in 30-minute blocks. Have your app passwords ready, because only you can enter them or approve sign-in codes on your phone.
