The Neighborhood

Why East New York Properties Need Security Camera Installation

East New York was built and rebuilt in layers, and almost every layer is still standing, which is why no single camera plan fits the whole neighborhood. Dutch colonists settled the eastern edge of what became Brooklyn in the 1650s and formed the town of New Lots. The name East New York came in 1835 from John Pitkin, a Connecticut merchant who planned a town here, and whose development was cut short by the panic of 1837, according to CityNeighborhoods and Localize.city. The area was annexed to the City of Brooklyn in 1886. A building boom followed the Brooklyn Bridge and the Atlantic Avenue elevated railroad in the 1880s, and growth came again after the Williamsburg Bridge opened in 1903 and the IRT subway reached New Lots in 1922. Brick rowhouses and modest frame homes went up along Liberty, Pitkin and Sutter avenues. Decades of decline left empty lots across the neighborhood, and from 1983 the Nehemiah Housing Program built thousands of affordable single-family brick homes on them. Then, in 2016, East New York became the first neighborhood rezoned under the city's Mandatory Inclusionary Housing rules. Since then, The Neighborhoods newsletter reports, it has added nearly 6,000 new housing units, more than 60 percent of them affordable.

Each of those layers puts a camera on a different kind of wall, with a different person in charge of it. A Nehemiah home usually belongs to the family that lives in it, so the owner decides. An older rowhouse or frame house is often a two- or three-family, with an owner upstairs or down and tenants who use the same front door, the same yard and sometimes the same cellar. The eleven NYCHA developments in the neighborhood belong to the Housing Authority, and their cameras are NYCHA's to plan; the Pink Houses alone are 22 eight-story buildings with 1,500 apartments, finished in 1959, and the Cypress Hills Houses were completed in 1955. Starrett City, now Spring Creek Towers, has 46 towers of 11 to 20 stories across 153 acres, and Wikipedia notes that its primary security is handled by its own Department of Public Safety. The new mid-rise buildings from the rezoning have developers and managing agents, often a ground floor of stores that are leased separately, and tenants who arrived through a housing lottery. A camera job here begins with sorting out which of these you are, because it decides what can go on the outside of the building, who sees the footage and who signs off.

The incidents and numbers in the sources point at the parts of a property a camera should cover first. CBS New York reported that earlier in 2026, armed men forced their way into an apartment in a building near Vandalia Avenue and Fountain Avenue, and that a neighbor received a Ring doorbell alert with what could be a picture of one of the suspects; one suspect was described as disguised as a cleaner. In the 75th Precinct, which covers East New York and Cypress Hills from its station house at 1000 Sutter Avenue, NYPD CompStat showed 124 burglaries year to date by the week ending September 13, 2026, down from 167 at the same point in 2025. An analysis of NYPD data published by Southwest Journal counted 226 vehicle theft reports in the precinct through July 26, 2026. And in October 2026, the Police Commissioner said shootings in the 75th Precinct were down 47 percent from five years earlier, NY1 reported. The trend is better than it was, but cars, doors and yards still account for most of what a private camera can help with. We found no source that ranks East New York blocks by crime, and we do not offer one.

There is a second side to cameras here that residents raise, and it matters for how we set systems up. In 2021, Amnesty International mapped camera coverage across Manhattan, Brooklyn and the Bronx and found East New York the most surveilled neighborhood of the three, with 577 cameras counted at intersections. Those are not cameras an owner can pull footage from, and they were not placed to watch anyone's back door. They do mean people here have thought hard about who is being recorded and why. So every system we install is framed on the owner's own frontage, doors, yard and rooms, with anything beyond that masked out in the camera's settings, recording stays on a recorder the owner controls, and nothing is shared with anyone, police included, unless the owner chooses to export and hand over a clip. That approach also keeps neighbors on good terms, which on a block of attached houses is worth as much as any camera.

Who Owns the Wall

Nehemiah Home, Two-Family, NYCHA Apartment or New Rezoning Building: Sorting Out Who Decides on an East New York Camera

In some Brooklyn neighborhoods nearly every camera job looks the same: a brownstone owner, a facade and a stoop. East New York is different. On one walk from Atlantic Avenue down to Linden Boulevard you can pass a new eight-story building with a leasing office, a block of single-family brick homes, a row of older two-families with tenants upstairs, a NYCHA campus and a strip of stores with apartments above. The camera hardware is similar in all of them. What changes is who is allowed to put it up, where it may point and who gets to watch. Getting that wrong is the most common reason a camera comes down a month after it goes up, so it is the first thing we settle on every job.

Single-family owners: freedom, with one limit

A family that owns a Nehemiah home or a single-family rowhouse outright has no board and no landlord to ask. That makes the job quick to start, but it does not mean anything goes. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a neighbor sue over a camera that records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, and on blocks where houses stand close together with small yards side by side, a careless rear camera can take in three gardens at once. We frame every outdoor view on the owner's own curb, walk, doors and yard, stop it at the fence line, and mask out any neighboring window or yard that still falls in the frame. The owner gets a better picture of what matters and the neighbors have nothing to complain about.

Two- and three-family houses: one system, separate views

The older brick rowhouses and frame houses along Liberty, Pitkin and Sutter avenues are often divided into two or three apartments. In those houses the owner controls the outside, the shared hall, the stairs, the yard and usually the cellar. We put all of those cameras on one recorder that belongs to the owner, then set up separate app accounts: the owner sees everything, and each tenant household can be given the front door, the shared entrance or the mailbox view, and nothing else. A tenant who wants a camera inside their own apartment can have one on its own account. When a tenant moves out, their account is switched off the same day, which takes a minute and does not touch the recordings.

Public housing and Spring Creek Towers: inside your door

NYCHA told the City Council in March 2025 that it has more than 20,000 cameras across its portfolio and an active CCTV project in construction at Cypress Hills. Those cameras, their placement and their recordings are NYCHA's. A tenant's own options are inside their apartment, and anything on a door, in a hallway or on the grounds is a question for NYCHA management. Spring Creek Towers works the same way in practice, with its own management and public safety department deciding what goes outside an apartment. We are happy to help a tenant set up a camera inside their home in either place, aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door, and to say plainly when something they want is the landlord's decision rather than ours.

New buildings from the rezoning: design it in, not bolt it on

The buildings filed since 2016, from four stories on Liberty Avenue to eight on Atlantic, are the easiest places in the neighborhood to install cameras well, if the cable goes in while the walls and ceilings are still open. A building at that stage can have every camera location roughed in, a locked room for the recorder and switch, power on a circuit that does not share a breaker with a hallway light, and spare cable to the storefronts for future tenants. Once tenants have moved in, the same work means opening finished ceilings in occupied halls. We work with the developer's electrician on the schedule so the low-voltage work lands before the ceilings close.

Cars, Doors and Yards

Building an East New York House Plan From the Precinct Numbers: The Car First, Then Every Door

The figures from the 75th Precinct tell a homeowner where to start. Burglaries were down about a quarter year to date in September 2026 compared with 2025, and shootings, by the Commissioner's account, were down 47 percent over five years. Vehicle theft reports, though, kept coming, with 226 counted by late July. For most houses in East New York, that makes the car at the curb or on the driveway pad the first camera, the front door the second and the back door the third, with the yard, the side passage and the cellar door after that, depending on the house.

The trap with a curb camera is trying to cover too much. A single wide camera aimed across the whole street turns a person at a car door into a smudge, and at night under streetlights it records movement rather than faces. The camera that helps is one aimed along your own frontage and your own car, zoomed so the car fills most of the frame and a person standing at the driver's door is large enough to recognize. It records continuously, not only on motion, because a person walking up slowly may not trip a motion setting and because the seconds before a window breaks are often the ones that show a face. Alerts are a separate setting, drawn tightly around the car itself so that every passer-by on the sidewalk does not ring your phone.

The curb or driveway pad

We mount the curb camera on the front wall, a porch post or the soffit, usually eight to ten feet up, high enough that it is not easily knocked off and low enough to see under a hat brim. A varifocal lens lets us set the zoom on site rather than guess, and we set a faster shutter for night so that someone moving quickly does not blur. If the house has a pad or a short driveway, a second, tighter view at the house end looking out toward the street catches the face of anyone walking up to the car, because they are walking toward the lens. A plate-reading camera is a separate, specialized job and only makes sense where every car must pass a narrow point, such as a gate or a garage ramp; on an open street it rarely earns its cost, and we say so.

The front door and the gate

Many East New York houses have a short front walk and a gate, and a video doorbell at the door is often already there. A doorbell sees a face as someone steps up, then mostly sees their back as they turn to leave. A second camera at the gate or the corner of the porch, looking back toward the door, records the face on the way out, which is the moment a package thief or a visitor who should not be there turns toward the street. That pairing also catches the person who never rings the bell at all.

The back door, the yard and the side passage

The back of a house faces fences and other yards, not the street, which is exactly why it matters. We place the rear camera on the back wall looking down the yard toward the back door and the gate, so anyone coming in or going out walks toward it, and stop the frame at your own fence. On semi-detached houses, where a side passage is the only route from the street to the yard, a camera at the front corner looking along that passage covers both the passage and anyone heading to the back. On attached rowhouses with no side passage, the rear camera's cable comes through the house rather than around it, usually along the cellar ceiling to the back wall.

Nehemiah homes: check the route before choosing the camera

Because the program built thousands of these homes on cleared lots, stretches of a block can look much alike from the street, and that has two effects on a camera job. The first is that every house is not the same inside, whatever the front suggests. Owners have finished attics, closed up crawl spaces, added rear rooms and changed the siding, so we never assume a cable route from the house next door; we look for an attic hatch, a cellar or crawl space, and an interior closet that lines up with the front wall. Where there is an open path, cable for the curb, door and yard cameras runs inside and comes out through a small sealed hole behind each camera. Where there is not, the run goes along the outside in a slim paintable cover tight under the soffit or beside a downspout, where it is hard to see and hard to pull. The second effect is that a clip has to prove which house it came from, which we deal with in the questions section below.

Older brick rowhouses: the cellar does the work

The attached rowhouses from the early building booms almost always give us a cellar that runs the full depth of the house, and that is the best cable route in the neighborhood. Runs for the front and back cameras travel along the cellar joists and come up through small holes at the front and rear walls, so nothing is stapled across the brick. Mounts go into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, because mortar is softer, easier to drill cleanly and easy to repoint if a camera ever moves. The shared party wall with the next house is never used for a mount, and the rear camera sits on the back wall that belongs to this house alone. If the cellar is damp, the recorder does not live there; it goes up to a closet on a living floor, on a battery unit, with the internet modem beside it if possible.

Frame houses: find the framing, keep the siding whole

A camera screwed straight into vinyl or wood siding will work loose and crack the siding within a season. On frame houses we fix a mounting block or base through the siding into the sheathing and framing behind it, seal around it, and mount the camera to the block. Cable goes through the center of the block into the wall cavity, so the hole is hidden behind the camera and sealed against water. Frame houses here often stand very close to the next house, so side cameras are aimed along the passage rather than across it, and an infrared camera aimed at a light-colored wall a few feet away is set with its night light turned down so the picture does not wash out white.

New mid-rises: rough-in, a locked room and power that stays on

In a new building we work from the plans. Planning for cameras someone will try to disable

Store owners anywhere in Brooklyn can tell you about cameras being covered, knocked down or ripped out during a break-in. The answer is overlap and a recorder nobody can find. One high corner camera that sees the other cameras as well as the floor records anyone reaching up to cover one. Hard-cased domes are harder to knock off than exposed bullets. Cable runs above the ceiling or inside conduit rather than down the wall in reach. Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk an East New York Two-Family, From the Parked Car to the Cellar Stairs

This is how we work on these houses, not a story about one job. We begin at the curb, standing beside wherever the owner parks, because the car is the most common reason for the call. From the car we look back at the house and find the corner, post or soffit from which a camera would see a person at the driver's door large in the frame. Next we walk the front path to the gate and the front door, ring the bell if there is a doorbell camera, and look at its picture on the owner's phone to see what it already covers and what it misses. Then we ask who lives where: which floor the owner uses, which apartments are rented, and whether tenants share the front door, the hall or the yard, because that decides how many accounts the recorder needs and which views each household gets. We go through the shared hall and down the cellar stairs, look at the ceiling for the clearest route front to back, note any old wire that will need tracing, find where the internet line comes into the house, and check whether the cellar is dry enough for equipment. If it is not, we find a closet on a living floor. Finally we go out the back door to the yard, stand at the rear fence and look back at the house, which shows where a rear camera sees the whole yard and which neighboring windows and yards it must stop short of. By the end, we know the camera count, every cable length, where the recorder will live and who gets which view, and the written quote is built from that.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Landlord, Tenant, Housing Authority or Neighbor: The Rules Behind an East New York Camera Job

East New York has only one city landmark, so the rules that shape most camera jobs here are about ownership, privacy and who is allowed to do the installing. This is how they play out on the houses, buildings and stores in the neighborhood. It is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

Who may install. The NYC Business portal explains that a license from the New York State Department of State is needed to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and camera systems used to watch for intrusion fall under that. Homeowners putting a camera on their own property are not in the business of installing, as a long-running electricians' forum discussion of the rule puts it, so they do not need a license; a contractor or handyman doing it for pay does. A licensing course provider says unlicensed installers can face fines of up to $10,000. Some installers' blogs say NYC requires a licensed electrician for any wired camera; the official sources we found point to the state installer license as the requirement, with an electrician needed when a new outlet or circuit is added. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to anyone who asks.

Tenants and shared spaces. In a privately owned building, a tenant generally needs the landlord's permission before putting equipment in hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces, and the outside of the building belongs to the owner. Inside their own apartment, a tenant can usually place a camera aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door. In a two- or three-family house, the owner decides about the outside, the yard and any shared hall. In NYCHA developments and at Spring Creek Towers, management decides everything outside the apartment.

Neighbors. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a resident a claim against a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. On blocks of attached and semi-detached houses with small yards side by side, every outdoor camera we install is framed on the owner's own curb, walk, doors and yard, and anything else that falls in the frame is masked out in the camera settings.

Police and registration. NYC 311 says owners can register the locations of their cameras with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org, so detectives know whom to ask for footage after an incident. That registration is voluntary. A bill to create a mandatory "Safe Streets Security Camera Registry" was introduced in Albany several times between 2011 and 2019, including as S487, and we found no evidence it ever passed. Registering a location does not give anyone access to your recordings; you decide whether to export a clip when asked.

Public property. Cameras go on your building, your porch, your fence or a post inside your own lot, never on a lamppost, street tree, sign pole or anything else that belongs to the city.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, block or siding is sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in an apartment or mixed-use building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

"East New York already has more cameras than anywhere else, so a home camera adds little."

The starting fact is real: Amnesty International's 2021 study found East New York the most surveilled neighborhood of the three boroughs it covered, with 577 cameras counted at intersections.

Those cameras watch intersections. They are not aimed at your back door, your yard, your parked car halfway down the block or the inside of your store, and you have no way to pull their footage when something happens to you.

"Crime is falling here, so cameras are not worth the money anymore."

The trend is genuinely better. Burglaries in the 75th Precinct were down about a quarter year to date in September 2026, and the Police Commissioner said shootings were down 47 percent over five years.

But vehicle theft reports still reached 226 by late July 2026, and 124 burglaries is still 124 households and businesses. A camera's value is not a prediction about crime rates; it is having a clear record on the day something happens to your property.

Falling numbers are a reason to spend sensibly, starting with the curb, the front door and the back door, rather than a reason to leave them uncovered.

"Public housing tenants can just ask and get cameras installed in their building."

Tenants can and do ask, and NYCHA says it has more than 20,000 cameras across its portfolio, with a CCTV project under construction at Cypress Hills as of March 2025.

Many installers advertise a single four-camera package price. Why does AESS write up East New York camera jobs line by line instead?

Because four cameras on a brick rowhouse with an open cellar and four cameras on a frame house with no attic access are very different amounts of work, and a package price either overcharges one of them or leaves something off the other. Burglaries in the 75th Precinct were down about a quarter year to date in 2026 while car theft reports kept coming. Should that change which cameras we put up first?
+

For most houses, yes, it points to the curb first. CompStat showed 124 burglaries year to date in mid-September 2026 against 167 a year earlier, and an analysis of NYPD data counted 226 vehicle theft reports through late July 2026. That makes a camera zoomed on your own parked car the first priority, the front door second and the back door third, with the side passage, yard and cellar door after that. A burglary is still the more serious event for a household, so the back door should not be left out for long; if the budget allows only two cameras at first, we would usually choose the curb and the back door and rely on an existing doorbell at the front.

We own our Nehemiah house outright, with no board or landlord. Is there anyone else whose permission we need before mounting cameras?
+

Usually not for the cameras themselves, but your neighbors' privacy still sets limits. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, so every outdoor camera should be framed on your own curb, walk, doors and yard and stop at your fence line. We mask out any neighboring window or yard that still falls in the picture. Cameras must also go on your own house, fence or a post on your own lot, never on a city lamppost, tree or sign pole. If a camera needs a new outdoor outlet, a licensed electrician installs it.

We heard the city will force everyone with a camera to register it with the police. Is that a law now?
+

Not that we could find. A bill to create a mandatory Safe Streets Security Camera Registry was introduced in the state legislature several times between 2011 and 2019, including as S487, and we found no evidence it ever passed. What exists today is voluntary: NYC 311 says owners can register the location of their cameras with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org so detectives know whom to ask after an incident. Registering a location does not give the police access to your recordings. You still decide whether to export and hand over a clip when someone asks.

We already have a Ring doorbell at the front, like the one that sent an alert in the Vandalia Avenue case. Does a wired system replace it or work alongside it?
+

Usually alongside it. A doorbell is good at the face of someone stepping up to the door and at letting you answer from your phone, so there is rarely a reason to remove one that works. The wired system adds what the doorbell cannot see: the car at the curb, the gate or porch view that catches a face on the way out, the side passage, the back door and the yard, all recording continuously to a recorder you own. Some doorbell brands can also record to the recorder and some cannot; we check yours during the walk-through and say plainly whether it can be joined or will stay on its own app.

The older people in our household are used to a simple Wi-Fi camera app. Will a wired recorder system be too complicated for them?
+

It does not have to be. A Yelp reviewer of a Brooklyn installer noted that a new system was more complicated than the Wi-Fi cameras they had before, which is exactly why we set accounts up by person. An older relative can be given a simple account that shows only live views of the front door and the curb, with no settings to change and nothing to delete, while the owner keeps a full account for playback and exports. Cameras are named in plain words, the app's main screen is arranged so the most important views come first, and a screen in the living room can show the cameras with no phone at all. We walk each person through their own view at handover.

Does keeping footage in the cloud add a cost for every camera on an East New York house?
+

It can, depending on the service. Many cloud plans charge by the camera or by the amount of video stored, and those fees go to the cloud provider, not to us. With a recorder you own, everything records locally with no monthly fee from AESS. Where an off-site copy makes sense, for example a store worried that a burglar could take the recorder, we can send only the alert clips from one or two important cameras to a cloud service, which keeps any subscription small. The quote names the service and says whether it carries a fee before you agree to anything.

Our store on Pitkin Avenue cannot close for a day. Can the noisy part of the camera install happen before the gate goes up?
+

Yes, that is how we prefer to run store jobs. Drilling through walls and floors, pulling cable through the ceiling and mounting the high cameras are scheduled before opening or after closing, and the quieter work, such as connecting cameras, setting up the recorder and adjusting views, can happen while you trade. If the store shares a building with apartments, we also agree with the landlord on what hours the work in shared spaces can happen.

Does AESS put up the cameras in NYCHA developments such as the Boulevard or Pink Houses?
+

No. Cameras on NYCHA buildings and grounds are planned and contracted by NYCHA, usually after funding from an elected official, consultation with the NYPD and sign-off from community leaders, as Pix11 has described. What we can do for a NYCHA resident is set up a camera inside their own apartment, aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door, and explain what is the Housing Authority's decision rather than theirs. Residents who want cameras added in their development's halls, elevators or grounds should take it to their tenant association and their Council member.

Five dead cameras from an old system are still bolted to the brick on our East New York house. Will taking them down leave holes in the front?
+

Taking them down leaves the old anchor holes, and we deal with them rather than leave them open. Where a new camera belongs in the same spot, it often goes on a base that covers the old holes. Where an old camera is not being replaced, the anchors come out and the holes are filled and sealed so water does not get into the wall, and old cable that is no longer needed is removed or cut back and capped. If the old mounts were drilled into the face of the brick rather than the mortar joints, a mason may be needed for a perfect cosmetic match, and the quote says so in advance.
