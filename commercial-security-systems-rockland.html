TERMINOLOGY

Rockland Commercial Security Terms, Translated

The vocabulary that decides what a Rockland quote actually contains.

PoE / PoE+. Data and power on one network cable per camera, reader or intercom — one battery-backup point in the closet instead of a wall-wart behind every device.

NVR vs DVR vs VMS. The recorder generations: DVR for analog coax, NVR for IP, VMS as the software layer above recorders and sites. A 2026 Rockland quote built around a DVR deserves a follow-up question.

ONVIF. The interoperability standard keeping cameras and recorders brand-independent — the difference between owning a system and renting a vendor.

OSDP. The encrypted reader-to-panel protocol replacing legacy Wiegand wiring, which can be skimmed at the door with a pocket device. Ask for it on every new opening.

Fail-safe vs fail-secure. What each locked door does when power drops — release or hold. Egress and fire code decide per opening; preference does not.

Electric strike vs maglock. Two ways to electrify a door: the strike cooperates with the latch; the maglock holds by force, suits storefront glass, and carries strict egress rules.

REX. Request-to-exit — the sensor or bar letting people leave freely while the street side stays locked. Skipped, the door either alarms all day or traps someone.

Door position switch. The contact reporting whether a door is actually shut — the cure for the rear door propped since the morning delivery.

Mobile credential. The phone as key: revoked in seconds, rarely lent out — which across retail and warehouse turnover is exactly the point.

Dual-path communicator. Alarm reporting over cellular and internet together, so one cut line or ISP outage never silences monitoring.

Video verification. A clip rides with the alarm signal so the central station escalates on evidence — the single best false-alarm reducer a business can buy.

Retention. Days of footage held before overwrite. Claims timelines and insurers set the number; the drives just have to obey it.

Edge analytics. Person, vehicle, line-cross and loiter detection on the camera itself, alerting live instead of after a scrub session.

Chapter 250 license. Rockland licenses electrical work at the county level under Chapter 250 of its code, and the five towns and their villages layer building departments and alarm-registration rules on top. Which set reaches your scope depends on your exact address; we flag it at the survey, and your own town gets the final word.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Real Questions from Rockland Business Owners

What does a security system realistically cost a Rockland business?

County working numbers: a Route 59 or Main Street storefront package of cameras plus a monitored alarm lands in the low four figures; an office or medical suite with cameras and a few doors on credentials runs mid four figures to low five; an integrated mid-size build — sixteen to twenty-four cameras, four to six controlled doors, monitored intrusion — commonly falls in the $15,000–$45,000 class; 303-belt warehouse, yard and portfolio work starts around $50,000 and climbs with gates, lots and trenching. The spread between two quotes is rarely the cameras — it is doors, gates and what got quietly excluded. A fair quote itemizes all of it.

How do I confirm an installer holds the right licenses for Rockland?

Two layers, both checkable in minutes. Statewide, security and fire alarm installation is licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D — verify any company with the Department of State; our number is 12000287431. Then the county layer: Rockland licenses electrical work under Chapter 250 of the county code, and your town or village building department adds its own rules. Ask which licenses the installer carries at your address, confirm with the town directly, and require a COI naming your business and landlord before anyone opens a wall.

Is an alarm permit required before monitoring starts in Rockland?

In several jurisdictions, yes — alarm registration requirements and false-alarm fee schedules vary across the five towns and their villages, and an unregistered system can mean fines or reduced response at exactly the wrong moment. Because the requirement is address-specific, we identify it for your property at the survey, handle the paperwork where it applies, and tell you plainly to confirm the current rule with your own town or police department — including against this page.

Is paying the whole install up front ever a good idea?

No. Deposit-and-balance is the professional structure — ours is fifty percent down, balance at completion — and full prepayment removes every ounce of your leverage on the punch list. It is also the common thread in the installed-and-vanished stories behind our takeover calls, which in a county this close to the city are mostly downstate installers who stopped making the drive. A written scope, a deposit structure and a named warranty are the floor of a professional proposal.

Am I allowed to run my own camera wiring at my Rockland business?

On property you own, generally yes — Article 6-D licenses businesses installing for others, and owners on their own premises sit outside it. The lane narrows fast: Rockland’s county electrical licensing governs who you can hire, leased strip and park units put alteration language and COIs in play, monitored certificates need a carrier-recognized installation, electrified hardware on egress and fire-rated doors is code territory, and anything near a building fire alarm is off the table. A camera over your own counter in a building you own is a fine weekend project; a leased unit on Route 59 is where DIY gets expensive quietly.

We inherited the last owner's cameras and panel — worth keeping?

Audit before you rip — an hour on site answers it. Existing coax frequently carries IP video over converters, sound Cat5e survives for cameras, and pathway across a lot is worth more than the cable inside it. The usual verdict is hybrid: keep pathway and some cable, replace the head end, rebuild access at the doors where legacy reader wiring is the skimmable old protocol. The proposal says keep, convert or replace line by line, because billing you to remove working infrastructure is billing you twice.

Does wireless ever beat wired on a Rockland property?

As the documented exception, not the backbone. Wired PoE remains the commercial default — one cable per device, centralized backup, no radio gamble through block construction. Rockland’s real exceptions: a lot pylon where the trench is absurd, a detached building at a 303-belt yard, a short-term lease. Engineered point-to-point links handle those positions properly, feeding wired devices at the far end. The honest design is a wired core with wireless spurs decided position by position at the survey.

What retention window keeps a Rockland business covered?

Thirty days is the working floor — slip-and-fall claims from strip lots, carrier investigations and police follow-ups reach back weeks, and discovering the recorder overwrote the incident is the most expensive storage lesson there is. Cash-heavy, regulated and higher-liability operations commonly hold sixty to ninety. Retention is drive math against resolution and camera count, so we spec the number in writing and size the recorder to deliver it.

If a storm cuts power on our block, what does the system do?

A properly specified one shrugs. Cameras keep recording to a battery-backed recorder, door controllers keep enforcing cached credentials, and the alarm fails over to cellular so the central station never goes deaf. What pauses is remote viewing and cloud sync, which catch up on reconnect. Each door’s fail-safe or fail-secure behavior in an outage is a deliberate, code-driven choice made per opening — and cloud-only cameras plus internet-only reporting are the two specs we talk Rockland owners out of most often.

Our shop closes on a fixed weekly schedule — can the system arm itself?

Yes, and it should — that is exactly what scheduled and auto-arming exist for. The panel arms itself at your posted closure time or off the last badge-out, treats the entire closure window as an any-entry-is-an-event state, and disarms only for named credentials at reopening. Pair it with video verification so the central station escalates on evidence during the dark hours everyone can read off your storefront sign. Predictable closures are only a vulnerability on systems that were never told about them — we build the actual calendar into the configuration at commissioning.

A landlord at a Route 59 center wants insurance papers first — normal?

Completely standard, and a useful filter. A certificate of insurance naming the landlord as additional insured, at their stated limits, is routine alteration language across the county’s strip centers and parks, and any commercial installer should produce one within a day without drama. Hesitation usually means thin coverage. We issue COIs before mobilization as a matter of routine and can walk your lease’s alteration clause at the survey so the design sits cleanly inside it.

The company monitoring our alarm got rolled up — who fixes things now?

That consolidation story is half our Rockland takeover pile: accounts sold, support moved to a national queue, locally installed systems orphaned with a monthly bill. The path out: we audit what exists, recover or factory-rebuild locked recorders and panels, replace only what is actually dead, migrate monitoring month-to-month, and put the system under our health monitoring and published rate. Check the old agreement for term and auto-renewal language before canceling anything — then bring whatever paperwork survives. We have rebuilt from none.

Why do owners end up consolidating onto one security platform?

Because pieces fail in pieces. Cameras from one vendor, an alarm from another, a locksmith keypad on the office door — and three phone numbers when something breaks at 6 a.m. On one platform the forced rear door pulls its clip, the alarm arms off the closure schedule, the yard gate logs plates onto the same timeline, and one service agreement covers all of it. On a multi-subsystem property, integration is usually cheaper than the pieces, because the survey, the cabling and the mobilization get paid for once.

How much calendar should we set aside for the install itself?

On-site time from county jobs: one to two days for a storefront package; two to four for a suite with controlled doors; one to two weeks for an integrated mid-size build; two to five weeks, phased, for 303-belt warehouse, yard and portfolio work. Rockland moves fast on access — parking at the door, reachable ceilings — while lot trenching and gate hardware add days. The pre-crew calendar at managed centers (landlord consent, COIs) is where delay actually lives, so we open it at the survey.

Our lease pushes 'all systems' onto the tenant — what does that capture?

Almost everything inside your unit: cameras, access, alarm and the cabling that feeds them, installed at your cost under the alterations clause — consent and COI first. What it does not settle is ownership at the end: many leases convert improvements to landlord property at surrender, which can capture the system you paid for. Paper your equipment as removable trade fixtures now, in writing, and confirm whose cameras cover the shared lot your customers actually cross. We read both clauses at the survey when owners bring the lease.

Someone with a master key just left the company — what's the sequence?

Same-day, in order: revoke every credential they held — fobs, mobile and PINs die in one click on a managed system, logged; change the alarm codes they knew, and retire shared codes for per-person codes so the next departure is administrative; pull their remote camera and monitoring logins; then read the recent audit trail with fresh eyes. The master key is the expensive part: every lock it opened gets rekeyed or stays a known hole — the recurring bill that is the entire business case for credentials over brass.

Our cameras live entirely in a cloud account — should we worry?

About two things, both silent. Outage risk: when the internet drops, a cloud-only system records nothing for the duration, discovered weeks later when footage from that window is needed. Cost risk: per-camera licensing forever, so the sticker was not the price. The fix is architecture, not brand: local recording — an NVR or camera-edge storage — as the source of truth, cloud as the access layer sized to bandwidth measured at your address. One question filters any proposal: where does video live when the internet is down?

Which monitoring-agreement terms should stop a signature cold?

Four of them: a multi-year term when month-to-month exists industry-wide; auto-renewal for another full term unless cancelled in a narrow window — calendar it the day you sign or refuse it; hardware that stays the company’s property or runs locked firmware, which is how exit fees become ransom; and unstated mid-contract service rates. None of those protect you; all of them protect the seller’s revenue. Our structure is the counterexample — owned equipment, month-to-month monitoring, a published $195/hr rate — and asking any bidder for the same is the fastest read on their model.

Does being ten minutes from New Jersey change anything about security?

It changes the exit math and the vendor pool. The Thruway, the Palisades and the state line give a crew three fast directions out, which raises the value of plate-reading coverage at gates and curb cuts — a timestamped plate is what survives a cross-border case. On the vendor side, Jersey installers work the county freely, but the licensing that governs the work is New York’s: Article 6-D at the state layer and Rockland’s Chapter 250 at the county layer, whatever the truck’s plates say. Verify both before the state line becomes an excuse.
