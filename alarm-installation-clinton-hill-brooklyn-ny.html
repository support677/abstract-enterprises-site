The Neighborhood

Why Clinton Hill Properties Need Alarm Installation

Clinton Hill is a neighborhood of big houses on a small piece of Brooklyn. The blocks between Flushing Avenue and Atlantic Avenue, and between Vanderbilt Avenue and Classon Avenue, hold brownstone and brick rowhouses four stories tall over a cellar, mansions on Clinton and Washington Avenues that were built for single families and later divided, prewar apartment houses, a large co-op complex, wood houses near the Navy Yard end, and the campuses of Pratt Institute and St. Joseph's. For an alarm, the common thread is size: there are a lot of doors, windows, hatches and stairs between the street and the bedroom, and a lot of plaster and brick between the cellar and the top floor.

The second thread is time. A Clinton Hill house is empty more often, and for longer, than most owners count. Garden-floor and top-floor apartments are rented to students who leave at the end of the spring term and come back in late summer. Owners travel at the holidays. Houses go through gut renovations that last a year, during which nobody sleeps there and the front door is opened by whichever trade arrives first. The most expensive break-in we found in recent police reports for the area happened in exactly that kind of gap: a South Portland Avenue home near Atlantic Avenue was entered through a window at some point between December 16 and January 6, and jewelry, a printer, a speaker, several phones and a projector worth at least $26,000 were taken. Brooklyn Paper's 88th Precinct blotter noted that the home had no security system or cameras.

Burglars here have also been patient enough to wait for the moment a house stops being watched. DNAinfo reported in December 2015 that thieves took two stoves, two refrigerators and a dishwasher, all high-end brands, from an apartment on St. James Place in a building under renovation, sometime between when the workers left at about 11 p.m. and 8:30 the next morning. The front door was found slightly open. In an April blotter, Brooklyn Paper described a Fulton Street construction site where burglars walked in at about 5:45 p.m. and were gone by 5:50 with more than $12,000 in tools. Neither of those needed a crowbar. They needed a door nobody was watching at the end of the working day.

Crime overall is far lower than it was, and we would rather say that plainly than sell fear. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 88th Precinct, which covers Clinton Hill and Fort Greene, counted 66 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 72 at the same point in 2025, and 79 for all of 2025. In 1990 the same precinct recorded 1,029. The 28-day count, though, was 10 against 8 a year earlier, a reminder that numbers this small move around, and that the burglaries which do happen tend to land on houses and businesses that were empty, under construction or left unlocked.

That is why our plan for a Clinton Hill property starts with three questions before any equipment: when is the building empty, who has a key while it is, and which floors can a stranger reach from the yard, the cellar, a neighboring roof or an unlocked street door. The answers decide whether the alarm needs one area or four, whether it can be wireless or needs wiring on some floors, and what it should do on the nights the house is full and the weeks it is not.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Clinton Hill's Architecture

Clinton Hill was built mostly in the second half of the nineteenth century for people with money, and the houses show it: high ceilings, deep lots, solid brick party walls, plaster on every wall and ceiling, and ornament that nobody wants drilled. Each building type below asks something different of an alarm, mostly in three ways: how many openings a stranger can actually reach, how far the radio signal has to travel, and where cable can run without touching anything original.

The four-story brownstone

A stoop to the parlor floor, an areaway door under it to the garden floor, two upper floors, a cellar and a roof hatch. Twenty or more windows, but usually only eight to twelve that anyone can reach, and a single stair that connects all of it.

The brownstone with rental floors

An owner's duplex with one or two apartments above or below, sharing a street door, a vestibule and often the cellar. The alarm has to serve several households without any of them seeing the others' comings and goings.

The divided Clinton Avenue mansion

Freestanding or semi-detached houses on wide lots, many now split into apartments or used by institutions. Long distances, thick interior walls, tall windows on several sides and, often, a carriage house or garage at the back.

The prewar apartment house

Walk-ups and elevator buildings from around 1900 onward, with a lobby, a mail area, a cellar, fire escapes at the back and a roof door. The owner protects the common spaces; each tenant protects their own flat.

The co-op complex

Large postwar co-ops such as the Clinton Hill Co-ops, with doormen, package rooms and their own security staff. Here the alarm is for the apartment alone, and the building's rules decide what can be mounted.

The house under renovation

Any of the above with the walls open, a dumpster outside, scaffolding on the front, and a door opened and closed by electricians, plumbers and delivery drivers for months. The most exposed building on the block, for as long as the work lasts.

The four-story brownstone: the stair is the spine

A brownstone looks like it has endless windows, and owners often assume an alarm means a contact on every one. It rarely does. The windows that matter are the ones a person can reach: the areaway windows under the stoop, the parlor windows if the stoop rail gives a foothold, every window at the back on the garden and parlor floors, any window above a one-story rear extension whose roof sits just below the sill, and the top floor if it opens onto a roof that runs level with the houses next door. Upper front windows facing the street, with nothing to climb, usually stay off the list.

What every intruder has to use, whichever window they come through, is the stair. A single motion detector on each landing of a brownstone stair covers the path from the cellar to the bedrooms, and the detectors on the floors where the family sleeps can be left out of the night arming so nobody trips them on the way to the kitchen. The cellar gets a contact on its street-side door and its yard door, and the roof hatch gets one too, even though it is four floors up, because a roof that connects to a row of houses is a path, not a dead end.

The panel's location matters more in a tall house than in a wide one. A panel in the cellar is easy to install but leaves the top floor at the edge of a wireless system's range and puts the siren as far as possible from the bedrooms. We often put the panel on the parlor floor in a closet, a keypad at the door the family actually uses, which is often the garden door under the stoop rather than the parlor door at the top of it, and a second keypad or a key fob for the top floor.

The brownstone with rental floors: one panel, several households

Most Clinton Hill rowhouses with tenants have a shape like this: the owner lives on the garden and parlor floors, one apartment on each floor above, a shared stoop and vestibule, and a cellar the owner controls. An alarm for that house is one panel split into areas. The owner's area includes the owner's floors, the cellar and the yard door. Each rental floor is its own area with its own door contact, its own code and, if the tenant wants it, its own app. The shared street door can chime for everyone without setting off anyone's siren.

The arrangement has two advantages here. Tenants change often, especially when the apartments go to Pratt students, and with areas a departing tenant's code is simply deleted and a new one issued, without touching the owner's system. And the owner can arm the cellar and the yard while tenants come and go through the front, which matters in a house with a double-door vestibule: in a 2008 Brownstoner thread about brownstone front doors, one poster described being robbed in their own vestibule, between the two doors.

If you rent out a floor, the alarm is not the only device that law asks about. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors within 15 feet of sleeping rooms in buildings with fuel-burning equipment, including rented one- and two-family homes. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors can be part of the alarm, but they do not replace whatever detectors the law requires in each unit.

The divided Clinton Avenue mansion: distance, thick walls and mirrors

The big houses on Clinton Avenue were built as single residences with servants' stairs, long halls and rooms on every side of the house. Divided into apartments, they leave each flat with an unusual mix: tall windows on two or three sides, a door onto a shared stair, sometimes a second door onto the old service stair, and interior walls of brick or heavy plaster that weaken radio signals. An apartment in one of these houses can need more sensors than a whole small rowhouse.

Wireless sensors can work here, but we test before we promise. Our technician carries a signal meter and checks each planned sensor location against the planned panel location before the price is written. One veteran installer on the Brownstoner forum warned in 2008 that "Mirrors also play hell with wireless," and the big gilt mirrors that survive in some of these parlors are exactly what he meant: a large mirror between a sensor and the panel can reflect enough of the signal to make a zone drop out. Where a reading is weak, the answer is a repeater, a different panel location, or a wired contact on that one opening.

Many of these lots also have a carriage house or garage at the back, sometimes rented separately. If it belongs to the same owner, it can be its own area on the same panel, reached by a wireless repeater across the yard or by cable buried along the garden wall. If someone else lives there, it should have its own system, because they need to arm and disarm without asking anyone.

The prewar apartment house: the owner's spaces and the tenants' flats

An apartment house owner and an apartment tenant need different alarms. The owner's concerns are the cellar, the boiler room, the roof door, any storage rooms, and the lobby mail area after hours. Those can be protected with contacts and a cellular panel in the cellar, reporting to the owner or the super, with no siren at all inside the building, so a 3 a.m. trip does not wake every apartment. A tenant's concern is their own door and any window on a fire escape. Their alarm is wireless, battery-powered, fixed with removable mounts, and goes with them when they leave.

In the building, the two should stay separate. A tenant's system should never be wired into the building's, and an owner's system should never depend on a tenant remembering a code.

The co-op complex: inside the apartment, by the board's rules

The Clinton Hill Co-ops, centered on 210 Clinton Avenue, are a 1940s complex whose 13-floor building at that address has 104 apartments, a full-time doorman, package rooms and, according to the listing we read, 24-hour security. In a building like that, the front door is not the shareholder's problem. An apartment alarm here is about the apartment door, a terrace or low window, and the things inside that matter: a safe, a closet, a home office. Smoke, water and low-temperature sensors are often more useful than burglar sensors, because a leak in a high-rise co-op reaches the neighbors below within hours.

Co-op boards generally want to know before anything is fixed to a door or wall. We give the managing agent a one-page outline of what goes where, how it is mounted, whether it uses the building's power or batteries, and our insurance certificate, before the visit.

The Empty Weeks

Planning a Clinton Hill Alarm Around the Calendar, Not Just the Floor Plan

Most alarm advice is written as if a house is either occupied or briefly empty: you arm it at night and when you go to work. Clinton Hill does not live like that. Its calendar has long, predictable gaps. When the spring term ends at Pratt and St. Joseph's, student renters leave garden-floor and top-floor apartments empty for the summer. Families leave between Christmas and New Year's, and the South Portland Avenue burglary in the blotter above happened during a stretch of three weeks over exactly that period. A gut renovation empties a house for a year. Each of those gaps is an invitation, because a house that is visibly unlived-in is the easiest one on the block.

An alarm planned around that calendar does a few things a basic system does not. It arms the empty parts of the house even when the occupied parts are not: if the top floor is between tenants for the summer, its area stays armed every day while the owner comes and goes downstairs. It reports things that are not burglaries, because in an empty house the more likely disaster is water: a burst pipe on the top floor, a sump that stops in a cellar, a radiator valve that fails in January. Low-temperature sensors on the coldest wall, water sensors under the boiler and the top-floor bathroom, and a power-loss alert from the panel cover the problems that turn a three-week trip into a ceiling replacement.

It also changes who gets told. When the house is full, an alarm reports to the family. When it is empty for three weeks, the first call should go to someone who can be at the door in twenty minutes: a neighbor, a relative in Brooklyn, the super of the building next door. The panel's notification list can change with the season, and so can the monitoring station's call list. We set that up at installation, with the dates written down, so nobody has to remember to do it the night before a flight.

And it makes the house look lived in without depending on a neighbor to pick up the mail. Smart plugs and light modules on the same app can turn parlor and bedroom lamps on and off on a varied schedule. A video doorbell at the garden door answers deliveries. None of that stops a determined burglar, but the South Portland Avenue thief went through a window into a house that, as far as the blotter says, had nothing at all. A loud interior siren and a phone alert in the first minute would at least have turned a three-week loss into a three-minute one.

Renovations

Alarming a Clinton Hill House While It Is Being Gutted, and Wiring It Before the Walls Close

Clinton Hill has been renovating for decades, and a house mid-renovation is the softest target in the neighborhood. The St. James Place case shows why: new appliances delivered before the owners moved in, a front door left slightly open, and a crew that went home at 11 p.m. The Fulton Street construction site lost more than $12,000 in tools in five minutes at the end of a working day, with no forced entry. And when the Brooklyn Eagle covered the 2019 rise in Fulton Street burglaries, police told merchants that scaffolding and a dumpster were giving burglars cover. A house with scaffolding on its front has, for the length of the job, a ladder to every window on the facade.

The fix is a temporary system that goes in the week demolition ends. It is wireless and battery-backed, reports over its own cellular link because the house may have no internet and no reliable power, and puts contacts on the street door, the cellar door, the yard door and any opening the scaffolding reaches. A couple of motion detectors cover the rooms where appliances, fixtures and tools are stored. Each trade gets its own code, so the owner can see which code disarmed the house and when, and can delete a code when that trade's work is finished. The last person out at the end of the day arms it from the app or a key fob, and if nobody does, the owner gets a reminder at a set hour.

The temporary system is not wasted money. The panel, contacts and motion detectors move into the finished house, or into the garden apartment, or come back to a closet as spares. What changes at the end of the job is the wiring. A gut renovation is the one time a Clinton Hill house is open from cellar to roof, and the cheapest moment to run cable for a hardwired alarm, cameras and data. One Brownstoner thread about a limestone renovation that included a security system ended on the right advice: hire an architect, because "a contractor cannot file plans with DOB." We do not file plans or pull renovation permits. We give your architect and electrician a simple drawing of where each cable should run, a home run from each door and reachable window to a panel closet, a run to each keypad location, and a run to the roof hatch, so the cable is in the walls before the plasterers arrive.

That cable costs very little to pull while the studs are bare and a great deal to fish later through finished plaster and restored cornices. A fully hardwired Clinton Hill brownstone on our Brooklyn list runs $2,000 to $6,000, depending on the number of openings and how much of the cable was left by the renovation crew.

Wired or Wireless

Wired or Wireless in a Four-Floor Plaster House: What Brownstone Owners Have Argued About for Fifteen Years

The question every Clinton Hill owner asks first is the one Brownstoner forum members were already arguing about in 2011. An owner of a four-floor brownstone with plaster walls posted two quotes: wireless was far cheaper to install, wired far more expensive, with monitoring priced differently, and asked whether wireless would even work in such a house. The alarm professional who answered was blunt: "Avoid wireless if at all possible." Another member said her mother had lived with a wireless system in a four-story brownstone for about twenty years, with constant false alarms and failing units. A third chose wired because there were too many batteries to keep track of.

Those complaints were fair for the equipment of the time, and some still hold. Wireless sensors run on batteries; a 2008 thread estimated "about 2-4 years" for the transmitter cells, and one member described a fading battery triggering a false alarm. Signals do weaken through brick party walls and old plaster on metal lath, and a cellar panel trying to hear a sensor on the top floor of a brownstone is working at the edge of its range. What has changed is that current panels report low batteries weeks ahead in the app, supervise every sensor so a dead one shows as a fault instead of silently disappearing, and use radio formats designed to pass through more material. In most Clinton Hill rowhouses we can now build a reliable wireless system, but we prove it with a signal reading on every floor before the price is written, not after the install.

Our honest answer for a lived-in house is usually a mix. Wireless contacts and detectors on the floors where fishing cable would mean opening plaster or touching ornament. Wired contacts where cable is easy: the cellar, along the cellar ceiling to the areaway and yard doors, and up through a closet stack or an old dumbwaiter or gas riser if the house has one. A panel on the parlor floor, which puts it closer to the top of the house. For a house being gutted, the answer is wired throughout, because the walls are open and the cable lasts as long as the house.

Two cautions from the same old threads still apply. Do not sign a long contract to make an installation cheap; one installer advised signing for "no more than a year," and our monitoring is month to month. And do not let anyone tell you wireless needs no wiring at all. As another forum answer put it, even a wireless system still needs power for the panel and the keypad. What it saves you is cable to the sensors, which in a brownstone is most of the work.

"They find another way in": what the restaurant break-ins here have in common

When the Brooklyn Eagle sat in on a 2019 community meeting about Clinton Hill crime, Samantha Safer, who ran the Otway bistro, said her restaurant had been burglarized four times in three years, plus an attempted break-in that week. None of those came through the front gate. So a Clinton Hill restaurant alarm starts at the openings the owner thinks about least: the side door to the alley or the residential hall, the cellar door, the sidewalk hatch, every back window including the small one over the dish station, and the door from the kitchen to the yard. Each one gets a contact. The cellar gets a motion detector, because the safe and the stock are usually down there, and the office or safe gets its own zone that can stay armed while the dining room is open. A window that staff open for air during service needs a contact that tells the closer, on the keypad or in the app, that it is still open before the alarm will arm.

Compliance notes for Clinton Hill businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building's audible burglar alarm to be capable of terminating, and to terminate automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. We set our sirens well inside that, which matters in a building with apartments over the store.
  • Landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook says health, safety and utility equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," and should be as small as possible and painted to blend in. Inside the historic district we keep sirens, strobes and antennas off the front and put them inside or at the rear.
  • Licensing. The New York Department of State licenses anyone in the business of installing alarms for others. Its FAQ says an owner who installs a system on their own property does not need a license; a company doing it for you does.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. Monitored smoke detectors on a burglar alarm are useful, but they do not replace a fire alarm system where the building code requires one. Several Brownstoner members reported in 2024 that their monitoring company sends the fire department on every fire signal, even after the owner says it is false, so smoke detectors go away from kitchens and fryers.
  • Detectors in rental units. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors near sleeping rooms where there are fuel-burning appliances. A store owner who is also the landlord upstairs is responsible for those too.
  • No citywide alarm permit that we could find. Many suburban towns in New York require an alarm permit and charge for repeated false alarms. We found no equivalent citywide permit for a home or store burglar alarm in New York City, so this page does not claim one.

Alarm Installation in Clinton Hill: Questions Answered

These questions come from the way Clinton Hill's houses are built and lived in, from the break-ins reported in the 88th Precinct, and from the threads where brownstone owners have argued about alarms for years. Each is answered once here; the FAQ further down covers different ground.

Empty houses and empty floors

Our house will be empty for three weeks over the holidays, and the alarm can only go in the week before we leave. Is one week enough to know it works?

Usually yes, if the week is used properly. On installation day we trip every sensor and confirm each one reaches the panel, and if monitoring is on, that each signal reaches the central station. Then we ask you to live with it: arm it every night and every time you leave, and send us a screenshot of the app's event history after four or five days. Any sensor with a weak signal, a door that does not close firmly enough for its contact, or a motion detector that sees a radiator or a curtain shows up in that history, and we fix it before the trip. Over the three weeks, the panel's supervision reports a sensor that stops checking in, so an empty house does not hide a failed device.

Our top-floor tenants are Pratt students who leave at the end of the spring term and come back in late summer. Can their empty apartment stay armed all summer without us knowing their code?

Yes, if the arrangement is agreed in the lease or in writing beforehand. With the panel divided into areas, the owner's master code can arm any area, including the tenants', without the owner learning the tenants' personal code. Before they leave, the tenants confirm in writing that the apartment will be empty and that you may arm it. You arm their area from the app the day they go, it stays armed all summer, and an alert tells you if their door opens. When they come back, they disarm with their own code as usual. What the owner should not do is enter an occupied apartment's alarm history or disarm it while the tenants are away without notice; their area is part of their home.

Old and orphaned systems

Our Nest Secure keypad and sensors are still on the walls since Google ended support. Can any of that equipment work with a new panel?

Not as alarm sensors. Nest Secure's sensors spoke only to Nest's own hub, and with the service discontinued there is nothing for them to report to. What can often stay are the things that were never part of the Secure system: Nest thermostats, Nest Protect smoke detectors and Nest cameras, which run on their own. We remove the dead Secure devices carefully, fill the screw holes, and put the new contacts in the same places where the old ones were well chosen, so the plaster is not marked twice. If the old keypad sat in a good spot by your everyday door, the new keypad usually goes there too.

Mansion apartments and big houses

Our apartment in a divided Clinton Avenue mansion has a tall gilt pier mirror between the parlor windows. Could it interfere with the wireless sensors?

It can, depending on where the panel sits relative to it. A large mirror with a metal backing reflects radio signals much as it reflects light, so a sensor on the far side of the mirror from the panel may read weakly or drop out now and then. We check every planned sensor position with a signal meter before we write the price. If the mirror is in the way, the answer is usually to move the panel to a spot with a clear path, add a small wireless repeater, or wire that one window. The mirror stays where it is.

Our flat in a converted mansion still has a door onto the old servants' stair, which now serves two other apartments. Does that door need more than a contact?

It needs a contact and a decision about how it is used. If nobody in your household uses that door, it should be locked, its contact should be on the instant zone with no entry delay, and it should report even when the alarm is in night mode. If someone does use it, it gets its own entry delay and the keypad or a fob near it. Because two other households use that stair, it is also worth a motion detector in the room the door opens into, so a door forced quietly from a shared stair still trips something. Ask the building owner before changing the lock or the hardware; the door may also be a fire exit for the other apartments.

Co-ops and doorman buildings

We live in the Clinton Hill Co-ops, with a doorman downstairs and the complex's own security. What would an alarm in our apartment actually protect against?

Mostly against things that start inside the building rather than at the street. A doorman stops strangers at the lobby, but not a contractor working in another apartment, a visitor let in by a neighbor, or a leak from the unit above while you are away. An apartment alarm in a building like this is usually small: a contact on the apartment door, one motion detector, perhaps a contact on a closet or a safe, and water and low-temperature sensors under the sinks and by the radiator. It reports to your phone, so you know the moment your door opens while you are at work or on vacation. Many co-op owners skip central station monitoring for the burglary side and keep it for smoke and water.

Restaurants and stores

Our Myrtle Avenue restaurant opens a back window over the dish station during service. How does the closer know it is still open before arming?

The window gets a contact, and the panel will not arm with it open unless the closer deliberately bypasses it. When the last person enters their code at the back door, the keypad and the app show "Kitchen rear window open" by name. They can close it and arm, or, if it is stuck, bypass that one zone for the night, which the app records and reports to the owner. The Speedy Romeo burglary on Classon Avenue started with a back window that was left open overnight, so this is one of the few zones where we recommend that a bypass sends an alert to the owner as well as the closer.

The front door of our Fulton Street market has a low glass panel, like the one broken at a market near Vanderbilt Avenue. Will the door contact catch someone who crawls through the broken glass without opening the door?

No. A door contact reports the door opening, and a burglar who breaks the lower panel and crawls through never opens it. That is exactly what was reported at the Fulton Street market. The door needs a glass-break detector aimed at it, set to report the sound pattern of breaking glass, and a motion detector just inside that sees anyone coming through at floor level. With both, the alarm reports at the moment the glass breaks, and again when someone enters, before they reach the register or the ATM.

Our store's cellar can also be reached from the apartments' hallway in our building. Who should pay for the sensor on that hallway door, the store or the landlord?

That is a question for your lease, but here is how we usually see it settled. The cellar space you lease is yours to protect, so a contact on the door between the hall and your part of the cellar, and a motion detector in your storage room, belong on your alarm and on your bill. The hallway itself and the residential street door are the landlord's, and if the landlord wants them alarmed, that is a separate system on their bill. We put the plan in writing for both parties before any work, and we never wire the store's alarm into anything the landlord controls, or the reverse.

The owner of Otway told the Brooklyn Eagle that burglars "find another way in." How do we work out which way a burglar would try next at our restaurant?

By walking the building the way a burglar would, which is the first thing our technician does. That means standing on the sidewalk at 4 a.m. in your mind, not at noon: which doors and windows are out of sight of the street, which ones open onto an alley, a yard or a neighboring roof, where the cellar can be reached from outside or from the building's hallway, and where the cash and the stock sit. In the Myrtle Avenue reports, burglars used a side door, a cellar door and a forced entry, and on Classon Avenue a back window. Every one of those becomes a zone, and the room where the safe or the cash drawers are kept gets its own motion detector, so even a way in nobody predicted still crosses a sensor on the way to the money.

Fire signals and forgetfulness

Our old monitoring company sent the fire department every time the kitchen smoke detector went off, even after we called to say it was toast. Can monitoring be set up differently?

The dispatch rule is the monitoring station's, not ours, and several Brownstoner members reported in 2024 that their station called the fire department on every fire signal, even after the owner said it was false. We cannot promise that any station will treat a smoke signal as cancelable. What we can change is the cause. A monitored smoke detector does not belong in or beside a kitchen; it goes in the hall outside, and the kitchen gets a heat detector, which reacts to a fire's temperature rather than to smoke from cooking. In a brownstone with an open garden-floor kitchen, that one change stops most of these calls.

Two of the Clinton Hill burglaries we read about came through an unlocked front door and an open front window in daylight. Can the alarm do anything when we simply forget?

Yes, in two ways. First, it can remind you: the app can alert you if the alarm has not been armed by a set time on weekdays, or if a phone with the app leaves the neighborhood while the house is still disarmed, and it shows by name any door or window left open. Second, if you add a smart lock to the door you use every day, the same app shows whether it is locked, and can lock it when you arm. None of that replaces a habit, but in the Washington Avenue case the resident was away for more than ten hours with the front door unlocked; a reminder in the first ten minutes would have mattered.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Clinton Hill

These are claims that turn up in generic cost guides, national-brand marketing and machine-written answers about home alarms. Each is partly true somewhere. Here is how each holds up on a Clinton Hill block.

"A professionally installed home alarm costs about $1,400."

That figure is real; it is the national average in Fixr's cost guide, updated in January 2025, for a basic monitored system on a three-year contract, with a typical range of $750 to $2,000. Generic answers repeat it as if it applied to any house. It is a fair number for a small suburban ranch with two doors and eight windows.

A four-story Clinton Hill brownstone is not that house. Even counting only reachable openings, a brownstone usually has a street door, an areaway door, a cellar door to the yard, a garden door, the rear windows on two floors, any window over an extension, and a roof hatch, plus motion on a stair that climbs four floors. A mansion apartment can have more. The national figure also folds in a three-year monitoring contract, which our pricing does not.

Our Brooklyn list puts a basic system at $600 for four to six sensors, most wireless systems at $600 to $1,200, complete home protection from $2,500, and a fully hardwired house at $2,000 to $6,000, before sales tax, with monitoring optional and month to month. The honest answer for a whole brownstone is usually the upper part of the wireless range or the complete package, and we tell you which after one visit.

"Wireless alarms work the same in any home."

Marketing for self-install kits implies that a wireless system works anywhere you stick it. In a two-story frame house with drywall, that is close to true. Brownstone owners on the Brownstoner forum have been telling each other otherwise for fifteen years, from "Avoid wireless if at all possible" in 2011 to "Mirrors also play hell with wireless" in 2008.

The physics behind those complaints is real. Signals weaken through brick party walls, old plaster on metal lath, stone lintels, and the distance from a cellar panel to a top-floor bedroom, and large mirrors and radiators reflect them. A kit's hub placed by the router, often in the cellar or the parlor, may be at the edge of its range on the fourth floor.

Modern panels and supervised sensors fix most of this when they are placed with a meter in hand. The difference is testing before installing, not after a false alarm at 2 a.m. That is why every Clinton Hill quote we write is based on signal readings, and why some houses end up with a repeater or a few wired contacts.

"Attached rowhouses are safer because burglars can only use the front and back."

Generic advice often treats an attached house as a box with two doors. It sounds sensible: neighbors on both sides, no side windows, nothing to climb but the front.

A Clinton Hill rowhouse has more ways in than that. There is the areaway under the stoop with its own door and windows, a cellar that may connect to the yard, rear windows reachable from fences and extension roofs, and a roof hatch on a roof that runs level along the row. A house under renovation next door adds scaffolding and, often, an empty building with roof access. The Brooklyn Eagle's 2019 report quoted police warning that scaffolding gives burglars cover.

That is why our rowhouse plans always include the areaway, the cellar, the back of the house and the roof hatch, and why the motion detector on the stair matters more than any single window.

"In a historic district, any alarm needs a Landmarks permit."

AI answers sometimes say that a landmarked house needs Commission approval for an alarm, or, the opposite, that alarms are never regulated. Both overstate it. Most of an alarm is inside the house, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates exterior work.

What the Commission's Permit Guidebook does say, in its health and safety chapter, is that such equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," must be as small as possible and painted to blend, and that ordinary maintenance and repair of existing equipment needs no permit. It sets specific limits for cameras and for light fixtures and intercom panels at doors, and we found no specific rule for alarm sirens or bells.

So in the Clinton Hill Historic District we keep sirens, strobes, antennas and keypads inside, or on the rear wall, which almost never needs anything filed. If an owner wants something visible on the front, we treat that as an LPC question first and an alarm question second.

"If you have cameras, you don't need an alarm."

This claim shows up in camera marketing and in a lot of chatbot answers to "camera or alarm?" Cameras are useful, and we install them. But they record; on their own, most of them do not tell anyone in time to do anything.

The Fulton Street construction site in the 88th Precinct blotter had nearby cameras that recorded burglars walking in at about 5:45 p.m. and leaving by 5:50 with more than $12,000 in tools. When the blotter went out, police had no suspect. The footage existed; nobody had been alerted while it was happening.

An alarm reports the first second a door opens or glass breaks, to a phone or a monitoring station, and sounds a siren that shortens the visit. The two work best together, on one app, so the alert arrives with a picture. Neither replaces the other.

"Apartments in doorman buildings don't need alarms."

This one is half right. A doorman or a building security staff, like the one advertised at the Clinton Hill Co-ops, does keep most strangers out of the lobby, and Brick Underground noted in 2021 that alarms are less common in NYC apartment buildings for that reason.

What the doorman cannot see is the inside of the building. The Grand Avenue case DNAinfo reported in 2014, in which a man dressed as a delivery worker was let in by a neighbor and walked into an unlocked apartment, shows how a building's front door can be passed politely.

For a co-op apartment the useful alarm is small: a door contact, a motion detector, water sensors and phone alerts, with nothing mounted in the hallway. It costs a fraction of a house system, and it covers the things a doorman never could.

"Installing your own alarm without a license is illegal in New York."

Some generated answers read New York's installer licensing law as covering everyone who touches an alarm. It does not. The Department of State's own FAQ says that if a purchaser or owner personally installs an alarm system on their own property, a license is not required.

The license matters when you hire someone. Anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining alarms for others must be licensed by the Department of State, and Brick Underground advised in 2021 that the license can be checked with the Department before you sign. A handyman fitting an alarm in your brownstone for pay needs one.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Signal Readings on Every Landing, Starting at the Roof Hatch

This describes how our technicians survey a Clinton Hill brownstone, not a story about any particular job. The survey starts at the top, not at the front door. The technician climbs to the roof hatch or bulkhead, checks whether the roof runs level with the houses on either side, and looks down at the backs of the houses for extensions, decks and fences that bring an upper window within reach. On the way down, he stops on every landing with the signal meter and a test sensor, taking a reading back to the spot where the panel is planned. If the top-floor reading is weak, the plan changes there and then: a different panel closet, a repeater on the third floor, or a wired contact on the hatch.

Then the cellar: its street door under the stoop, its door or window to the yard, any coal chute or sidewalk opening, and whether cable could run along its ceiling to the areaway and garden doors. Last comes the front, which in the historic district is mostly a list of what will not go there. By the time he reaches the stoop, the quote is written floor by floor.

Does protecting one apartment in a divided Clinton Avenue mansion cost much less than alarming a whole brownstone?
+

Usually, yes. One mansion apartment with a door, a handful of tall windows and good signal readings often fits the basic system from $600 or the wireless range of $600 to $1,200 on our Brooklyn list. A whole lived-in brownstone, with an areaway, a cellar, a yard, a roof hatch and a four-floor stair, more often lands at complete home protection from $2,500. Both are before sales tax, and the written price follows one visit.

We are students renting a garden apartment near Pratt on a nine-month lease. Is an alarm worth it for one school year?
+

It can be, if it is the removable kind. A small wireless system with a door contact, one motion detector and phone alerts, fixed with removable mounts, protects a garden apartment for the year and comes down without marks when you leave. Ask your landlord first and get the answer in writing. Garden floors are the easiest to reach in a brownstone, and the summer you leave is when the apartment is most exposed, so tell the owner when you move out.

During a renovation, can the cable rough-in and the final alarm installation be two separate visits on separate bills?
+

Yes, and that is the usual way to do it. The first visit, while the walls are open, runs cable from each planned door and window to the panel closet and each keypad spot, and is billed when it is done. The second visit, after the plaster and paint, fits the panel, contacts, detectors and keypads and tests everything. If a temporary wireless alarm protected the house during the work, its parts are reused at the second visit wherever they fit.

The gas and electric meters are in our cellar. How do Con Edison readers get in without setting off the alarm?
+

The simplest answer is to keep the cellar in its own area of the panel. When a meter reading or a utility visit is due, you disarm only the cellar area from the app, or give the person letting the reader in a code that works only for that area, and the rest of the house stays armed. The app records when the cellar was disarmed and rearmed, so you can see the visit lasted as long as it should.

Can the keypad by our parlor door show nothing on its screen, so a visitor at the door cannot tell whether the house is armed?
+

Yes. Most current keypads can be set to dim or blank the display until a button is pressed or a code is entered, and to hide the status lights. We also mount the keypad so it cannot be read through the glass of the inner vestibule door. In a house where the everyday door is the garden door under the stoop, the main keypad goes there and the parlor floor gets a fob or a second, quiet keypad.

Do you install alarms for Clinton Hill churches, schools and community groups as well as homes and shops?
+

Yes. Institutions usually need an alarm on the offices, the sacristy or storage rooms, the cellar and the side doors volunteers use, with codes for staff and volunteers that can be added and removed easily. Many share a building with a rectory or apartments, so the panel is divided into areas. The work is priced from the same Brooklyn list, usually on the commercial line.

Can you design the alarm layout and let our own electrician pull the cable during the renovation?
+

Yes. We mark the panel closet, every keypad spot and every door, window and hatch to be wired on your architect's plan, with the cable type to use and where each run should end. Your electrician pulls the cable while the walls are open, and we check it before the plaster goes up. We then do the final installation and testing ourselves, because the system is installed under our state license.

Is the panel brand you usually fit the only one you install?
+

No. Most of our Clinton Hill systems use 2GIG panels on the Alarm.com service, because they report over cellular, work with wireless and wired sensors, and put the alarm, doorbell and cameras on one app. If you prefer a different brand, or want to keep a panel family you already know, tell us before the visit and we will quote that instead, with any difference in features explained.

Our old alarm stopped reporting when a cellular network it used was shut down. Will a new communicator have the same problem?
+

Every cellular device depends on a network that will eventually be retired, and older alarm dialers built for earlier networks have gone quiet as those networks closed. A new communicator works on current networks, and the panel reports a communication failure to the app if it ever loses its connection, so the house does not go silent without anyone knowing. When a network change is announced, the communicator can be replaced without replacing the sensors.

We own two attached brownstones side by side. Can one alarm protect both houses?
+

One panel can, with each house as its own area, as long as the signal carries through the party wall or a cable can be run through the shared cellar wall. It saves a panel and a monitoring line and puts both houses in one app. If either house is rented to someone else, or may be sold separately, two systems are the better choice, because each household needs to arm and disarm without the other.

Our cellar still has the old coal chute opening under the sidewalk. Should it be on the alarm?
+

If it can still be opened from either side, yes. An old coal chute is a way into the cellar that many owners forget, and a cellar connects to every floor by the stair. If the chute is sealed solid, a motion detector in that part of the cellar is enough. If it still has a hatch or a door at the bottom, it gets a contact as well, set as an instant zone with no entry delay.
