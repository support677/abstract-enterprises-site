The Neighborhood

Why Canarsie Properties Need Alarm Installation

Canarsie fills the southeastern corner of Brooklyn between Paerdegat Basin, Fresh Creek and Jamaica Bay. Wikipedia sets its edges at Ralph Avenue on the west, Linden Boulevard and the Bay Ridge Branch rail line on the north, Fresh Creek Basin on the east and the Belt Parkway along the bay, with East Flatbush, Flatlands, Bergen Beach, Brownsville and the Spring Creek part of East New York around it. The name comes from a Lenape word usually translated as fenced land. For most of the nineteenth century it was a fishing village and then a summer resort: a railroad reached a pier at Canarsie Landing in 1865, hotels and boat-rental yards followed, and the Golden City amusement park opened near the pier in 1907.

The houses came later, and that timing explains much of what an installer finds inside them. Wikipedia says large-scale residential building only began after the war, with marshland filled in and most of the present housing put up between the late 1940s and the 1970s. City Planning’s 2017 report adds the detail: the detached and semi-detached blocks between Rockaway Parkway and Fresh Creek south of Avenue L were built mostly in the 1950s and 1960s, while the strip between Remsen Avenue and Rockaway Parkway is older, from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Older zoning produced a house type that is everywhere here, “a garage at the front with a recreation room behind it,” and attached rows whose front yards slope down one to four feet to the garage door. Backyards often sit several feet below the street as well.

Those two features, the sunken garage and the den behind it, change the burglar alarm. On a typical Canarsie row the garage door is the largest opening in the house, it faces the street, and it is lower than the sidewalk, out of the line of sight of anyone walking past. Behind it, often through a single hollow door, is a finished room that leads to the stairs. Many alarms installed in these houses decades ago covered the front door at the top of the stoop, the back door and a motion detector in the hall, and nothing at the garage at all. We still find houses where the overhead door has no contact, the door from the garage into the den has no contact, and the den has no motion detector, which means the easiest way in is also the one the alarm cannot see.

The basement is the second issue. City Planning found that many basements and cellars in Canarsie have been converted into rental units, and that a lot of them sit below the flood elevation the city now uses for design. During Sandy, according to the same report, blocked sewers backed up and residents described up to seven feet of water in their basements; almost 30 percent of the households inspected said the whole basement level flooded. City Limits reported in December 2012 that thousands of Canarsie residents had one to six feet of water in basements and first floors, and quoted a Paerdegat Street homeowner whose niece rented his basement and “lost all her stuff.” Alarm panels, backup batteries and communicators were very often in those basements, next to the boiler and the electric meter, because that is where the phone line came in. When the water rose, the alarm was among the first things to die.

The third issue is how houses here get broken into. A Brooklyn Paper report on a burglary spike in the 69th Precinct, written after the precinct’s 2009 totals were in, quoted the precinct commander saying thieves “like private houses where there’s a driveway and no car in the driveway,” and that they knock or ring first, then come back through a back window or door. About a quarter of those burglaries went through open windows or doors, with screens cut out. Another Brooklyn Paper roundup described a burglar on Avenue L near Remsen Avenue who climbed onto a barbecue grill to reach an unlocked bedroom window, between seven in the morning and six in the evening. CBS New York reported in January 2020 on a serial burglar who went through windows in Canarsie “at all hours of the day and night,” five times in about two weeks, and later tried to use a stolen card at East 103rd Street and Avenue N. Windows at the back, a house that looks empty, and doors nobody can see from the street: that is the pattern an alarm has to answer.

Many Canarsie owners also want a camera over the driveway or the backyard by the end of the first conversation, and the two systems work best when they are planned together: the alarm decides that something is wrong, and a camera clip shows what it was. We describe camera work on our Canarsie security camera installation page, and both can be wired on the same day.

Canarsie in numbers that shape an alarm design

  • About 12,000 residential buildings, roughly two-thirds of them inside the floodplain under FEMA’s preliminary post-Sandy flood maps, which raised the count of buildings in the floodplain from about twenty-six to 5,000 (Department of City Planning, Resilient Neighborhoods: Canarsie, May 2017).
  • Floodplain buildings by type: about 500 detached one- and two-family houses, 650 semi-detached, 680 attached houses with a ground-floor unit and 1,890 attached houses with living space below the design flood elevation (same report).
  • Two-family houses lead the sales: the Department of Finance’s 2024 summary lists 150 two-family, 85 one-family and 27 three-family sales in Canarsie, with a median two-family price of $791,625.
  • Police: the NYPD 69th Precinct, 9720 Foster Avenue, whose own page describes the area as mainly residential with commercial strips along Avenue L, Rockaway Parkway and Remsen Avenue.
  • Fire: FDNY Engine 257, Ladder 170 and Battalion 58 at 1361 Rockaway Parkway (Wikipedia); Forgotten New York dates the firehouse at Rockaway Parkway and Farragut Road to 1908.
  • Public housing: Breukelen Houses (30 buildings of three or seven stories, 1,595 apartments, completed 1952) and Bay View Houses (23 eight-story buildings, 1,609 apartments, completed 1956), per Wikipedia.

Water in the Basement, a Garage Zone Stuck Open or a Silent Panel: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Canarsie

The repair calls we get from Canarsie have a local flavor. After a heavy rain, a panel or keypad in the basement starts beeping because water reached its transformer or its battery, or because a wired zone in the cellar shorted. A garage zone shows open all the time because the overhead door has settled and the contact no longer meets its magnet, so nobody can arm the system without bypassing it, and before long nobody arms it at all. An app reports the system offline because the old communicator still depends on a landline that was cancelled years ago, or on a 3G cellular radio that stopped working when the carriers switched those networks off. And a siren keeps restarting because a sensor on a back door near the deck is wet or loose.

Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the brand on the keypad, what the display or the lights show, and whether the equipment is in the basement. If water is still standing near the panel, switch nothing on or off at the panel itself; wait for us. If the siren is sounding and you cannot silence it, most systems stop on their own when the programmed siren time ends, and New York City’s Administrative Code requires a building burglar alarm to be able to terminate within fifteen minutes of activation, so a siren running past that needs attention the same day. We work on most panels, including ones another company put in, and if a flood-damaged panel is not worth reviving we say so before any billable work starts.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Canarsie

Each service below is priced on its own line so the quote is easy to read, but a typical Canarsie house with a garage and a basement apartment ends up using four or five of them on a single panel.

Garage and driveway-level protection

A heavy-duty contact on the overhead garage door, a contact on the door from the garage into the den or the stairs, and a motion detector in the room behind the garage, all on their own zone so the household can tell from the app which door opened.

Alarms raised above the flood line

Panel, backup battery, cellular communicator and siren moved out of the basement to the first floor or higher, with only sensors left below. Old basement panels are retired or stripped to a junction box.

Basement apartment areas

A separate alarm area for the household living downstairs, with its own keypad or fob and its own app login, so the owner upstairs and the tenant below each arm only their own space while the shared doors protect both.

Water, temperature and sewer-backup sensors

Water sensors at the floor drain, beside the boiler and water heater, under the laundry and at the lowest corner of the basement, plus a temperature sensor where pipes run along an outside wall. Alerts reach the owner and, if wanted, the basement tenant.

Rear windows, decks and side alleys

Contacts on every window a person could reach from a deck, a grill, an air-conditioner cage, a fence or a sunken yard, glass-break detectors for rooms with sliding doors, and outdoor motion detectors aimed down side alleys.

Store and repair-shop alarms

Gate contacts, back-door and roof-hatch contacts, interior motion that catches a person who comes through a wall rather than a door, a hold-up button at the counter, and opening and closing reports for Avenue L, Rockaway Parkway and Remsen Avenue businesses.

Replacing old landline and 3G panels

Where the door and window wiring in an older house still tests good, a new panel and cellular communicator go onto the existing wires, which keeps walls closed and costs less than a full install.

Monitoring and app control

Self-monitoring through the phone app, or a central station plan from our partners with no long-term contract, plus video clips from a driveway or backyard camera when a zone trips.

Repairs and false-alarm fixes

Wet or corroded sensors, garage contacts out of alignment, motion detectors aimed at a heating vent, sirens with no timeout, dead batteries and communicators that went silent when the old networks shut down.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Canarsie’s Architecture

City Planning sorts Canarsie’s housing into a handful of types, and each one asks for a slightly different alarm. These are the ones we meet most, with the parts of each house that matter most for intrusion and water.

Detached one- and two-family houses

Lots twenty to fifty feet wide and about a hundred feet deep, according to City Planning, with a side driveway and often a detached or attached garage at the back. Four exposed sides mean more windows to judge, side doors on the driveway and a yard that neighbors cannot always see into. Common on the blocks south of Avenue L toward Fresh Creek.

Semi-detached houses

One shared wall and three open sides, frequently with a shared or paired driveway between houses. The side door on the driveway is often the family’s everyday entrance, so that is where the keypad belongs, and outdoor detectors have to be aimed to ignore the neighbor’s car on the other half of the drive.

Attached rows with sunken front garages

Lots up to twenty feet wide, front yards that slope down one to four feet to a garage under the house, a den behind the garage and living floors above. The garage door, the door from garage to den and the den itself need coverage, and the panel must not sit in the den if water has ever reached it.

Houses with basement apartments

A separate household downstairs with a side or rear door of its own. Their door and windows are part of the building’s perimeter, so the alarm design has to protect the tenant’s space and the owner’s floors without handing either household the other’s code.

Older frame houses near Remsen Avenue

The strip between Remsen Avenue and Rockaway Parkway predates the postwar building boom. Forgotten New York’s June 2026 walk noted attached wooden houses covered in siding on East 95th Street, low bungalow-style houses on eastern Conklin Avenue and the odd Italianate house south of Bedell Lane. Thin walls and irregular framing push these toward wireless sensors.

Condominiums and apartment developments

From the Seaview Estates condominium, which Wikipedia describes as a gated complex of five buildings completed in 2003, to the NYCHA towers at Breukelen and Bay View and the large middle-income developments nearby. Owners and renters here want an alarm inside their own unit that reports to their own phone and needs no building wiring.

Method notes for each type

  • Detached and semi-detached. Wire runs along the open joists of an unfinished basement where there is one, then up inside closets to first-floor openings. Second-floor windows facing a flat garage roof, a porch roof or a deck rail get wireless contacts. A keypad goes at the door the family actually uses, which on a driveway house is usually the side door, not the front.
  • Rows with a front garage. The overhead door gets a surface-mounted heavy-duty contact with an armored lead, mounted where a car bumper cannot reach it. The interior garage door gets a recessed contact. The den gets a pet-tolerant motion detector aimed away from the furnace or any heating vent. If the panel lived in the den, it moves to the first floor.
  • Basement units. The tenant’s exterior door and windows are wired to an area of their own; a basement keypad or fob arms that area. A water sensor at the floor drain reports to both households. Nothing that the whole house depends on, such as the panel, the communicator, the backup battery or the main siren, stays below the first floor.
  • Frame houses. Wireless contacts and detectors avoid drilling through siding and old lath. The panel and communicator are mounted on an inside wall away from the meter, and a second siren upstairs makes sure an alarm is heard in every bedroom.
  • Condo and apartment units. All-wireless, battery-powered sensors mounted with removable adhesive where the lease or the bylaws require it, a cellular communicator so nothing ties into the building’s network, and a self-contained siren inside the unit.

The Garage at the Bottom of the Driveway: Canarsie’s Most Overlooked Door

Stand on almost any block of attached houses between Rockaway Parkway and Fresh Creek and look down. City Planning’s report on Canarsie describes it plainly: front yards that slope down one to four feet to a garage, a layout that came out of older zoning rules that encouraged “a garage at the front with a recreation room behind it.” The garage door sits below the level of the sidewalk, partly screened by the stoop and the low front wall. A person standing at that door is hard to see from the street and almost impossible to see from the houses across the way. For a burglar that is a gift: a large door, out of view, that opens into a finished room with a door to the rest of the house.

The protection we put on this end of the house has three layers. First, a contact on the overhead door itself, of the heavy-duty kind made for garage doors, with a gap tolerance wide enough that a door that rattles in the wind does not cause false alarms. Second, a contact on the door from the garage or den into the house, which on most of these houses is an ordinary interior door with no deadbolt. Third, a motion detector covering the den or the garage interior, so that even if the inner door was left open, or the intruder comes through the overhead door and simply waits, the alarm knows a person is moving where nobody should be. The three are grouped into one zone label in the app, “garage level,” so the owner can see at a glance which end of the house was involved.

Two refinements come up often. Families who use the garage door every day, to bring a car down or to take out the bins, find it annoying when the alarm treats every opening as an event, so we program the garage contact with its own entry delay and pair it with the garage opener so the system knows when the door was opened by the household’s own remote. And where a driveway is shared with a neighbor on a semi-detached pair, any outdoor motion detector over that driveway is pointed at the garage face and the side door, not across the whole drive, so that the neighbor coming home at midnight does not send an alert.

Below the Flood Line: Where Panels, Batteries and Sirens Belong in a Canarsie House

For decades the basement was the natural home of a burglar alarm. The phone line came in there, the electric panel was there, and nobody minded a metal box on the cellar wall. In Canarsie that habit ran into the water. City Planning’s 2017 report says Sandy’s flooding came in through the Fresh Creek and Paerdegat Basin inlets and reached five to seven feet above grade, that blocked sanitary sewers caused backups, and that about one-third of residents in the study area reported damage, nearly all of it from flooding. The report also notes that many homes keep critical systems such as heaters in cellars and basements below the design flood elevation, and suggests that homeowners consider elevating them.

An alarm is a critical system in exactly that sense, and it is easier to elevate than a boiler. The panel itself, the backup battery, the cellular communicator and the main siren together fit in a space smaller than a kitchen cabinet. We move them to the first floor, usually inside a coat closet or a utility closet near the door the family uses, or higher still where the first floor itself is low. Only sensors stay in the basement: door and window contacts, a motion detector, water sensors. If the basement floods again, those devices may need replacing, but the system keeps running, keeps reporting and keeps the upstairs protected, and it can tell the family exactly when water arrived.

How high is high enough depends on the block. City Planning gives base flood elevations in Canarsie’s residential areas of roughly one to six feet above grade, higher only in small pockets along Fresh Creek and Canarsie Park, and design flood elevations that vary by zoning district. We do not survey flood elevations; that is a job for an engineer or the flood maps. What we do is ask where water reached in 2012 and in later storms, look for stains and replaced drywall, and put the alarm’s core well above the highest mark anyone in the house remembers, and above the design elevation if the owner knows it.

Wiring is the other half. A wired sensor in a basement that floods can short the zone it is on, and in older panels a shorted zone can make the whole system misbehave. Where a basement has a history of water, we put basement sensors on their own zones or use wireless sensors mounted high on walls and door frames, keep cable junctions off the floor, and leave a drip loop wherever cable comes down a wall. Water sensors themselves go at the floor, of course; that is their job. Their battery-powered transmitters sit on the wall above, connected by a short lead, so the part that sends the signal stays dry while the probe gets wet.

The Empty Driveway Problem: What the 69th Precinct Has Told Canarsie Residents

Two warnings from Canarsie police officers, each reported by Brooklyn Paper, describe the same routine. In one, an officer at a community meeting at the Hebrew Educational Society on Seaview Avenue explained that “people knock on the door and they ask for a fictitious name to see if you’re home,” with the burglaries then concentrated in the East 80s south of Avenue M. In the other, the precinct’s commander described daytime burglaries, mostly between about ten in the morning and two in the afternoon, in which thieves ring or knock, leave, and return through a back window or door, and said they favor private houses with a driveway and no car in it. About a quarter of those cases involved open windows or doors, and screens were simply cut.

An alarm cannot put a car in the driveway, but it can change what the knock achieves. A video doorbell or door station at the front door lets the household answer from work, so the knock gets a voice instead of silence. An alarm that is armed every weekday morning, which is easy only when arming is easy, turns the return trip through the back window into an event within seconds. And an app that sends a notification when the house is still disarmed at a set hour, or when the last phone leaves the area without arming, closes the gap that most alarm owners admit to: the system was there, but nobody set it that day.

The back of a Canarsie house also has more climbing aids than people notice. One Brooklyn Paper roundup described an intruder on Avenue L near Remsen Avenue who climbed onto a barbecue grill to get into an unlocked bedroom window. Decks, grill stations, air-conditioner cages, fences, sheds and the roof of a rear extension all put second-floor windows within reach, and where the backyard sits below grade, as City Planning says many do, a first-floor window may be at head height from the yard but at hip height from the deck. The survey therefore happens from the yard as well as from inside.

Arming habits that fit a Canarsie commute

  • Arm by the door you leave through. If the family leaves by the side door on the driveway, that is where the keypad goes, not in the front hall nobody uses.
  • Arm from the platform. If the house was not armed, the app lets anyone with a login arm it from the Rockaway Parkway station or the bus.
  • A reminder at a set hour. A notification at, say, nine in the morning on weekdays if the system is still disarmed.
  • Know who came home. Separate codes for each family member, so the app shows when a teenager got home from school and disarmed.

A Second Household Downstairs: Alarming a Canarsie Basement Apartment Fairly

City Planning’s report says plainly that many Canarsie basements and cellars have been converted into rental or residential units. A FEMA specialist quoted by City Limits after Sandy put the human side of it bluntly: “There is an overlap between immigrants and basement dwellers.” Whatever the legal status of a given unit, which is outside what an alarm installer decides, the practical situation is the same: two households, one building, one set of outside walls, and very different exposure. The downstairs household is the one with the most to lose in a flood and often the least say over the building.

The alarm design we use gives the basement its own area. The tenant’s door and windows report to that area, the tenant gets a code or fob that arms and disarms only their space, and the tenant’s alerts go to the tenant’s own phone. The owner’s floors are a second area with the owner’s codes. Shared items, such as the water sensors, any smoke and carbon monoxide detectors tied into the system and a door between the basement and the main house, are set up so that both households are told when they trip. Nobody needs the other’s code, and nobody’s daily routine sets off the other’s alarm.

A Storm-Season Alarm Routine for Canarsie Households

Before the season starts

Put the system in test mode, or tell the monitoring station you are testing. Pour a little water on each basement water sensor and confirm the alert reaches every phone that should get it, including the basement tenant’s. Check the battery report in the app for every wireless device. Make sure the backup battery for the panel is less than about three to five years old; if the app shows a low-battery or AC-loss history, replace it now rather than in the dark.

When a storm is forecast

Move anything stored on the basement floor around the water sensors so they are not blocked. Charge the phones that receive alerts. If the basement tenant is leaving for a relative’s home, agree who will watch the app. Do not unplug the alarm’s transformer to protect it; the panel’s battery is designed to carry the system through an outage, and an unplugged system cannot warn you about water or a break-in during the storm.

After the water goes down

If water reached any alarm device, assume it needs checking. Wet contacts and detectors can corrode and fail days later, and a panel or transformer that was submerged should not be powered up until it has been looked at. Call us before resetting a flood-damaged system, photograph any equipment that was underwater for the insurance claim, and ask your insurer what documentation they want for alarm parts. Once the basement is dry, we replace what failed and move anything that was low to somewhere higher.

Business Alarm Installation in Canarsie

The NYPD’s own description of the 69th Precinct names three commercial strips: Avenue L, Rockaway Parkway and Remsen Avenue. City Planning adds Flatlands Avenue and counts more than 400 retail properties along these corridors, in buildings of one to three stories, and notes that the corridors sit outside the floodplain and came through Sandy with little damage. That makes the stores a different problem from the houses: not water, but walls, gates, back doors and quiet hours.

Avenue L

One of the three commercial strips the precinct names, in the one- to three-story commercial and mixed-use buildings City Planning describes along the corridor. Brooklyn Paper once reported a butcher shop on Avenue L near East 93rd Street whose front door was forced after midnight and whose safe was jimmied open. Front glass, the safe and the door all need a sensor that reports quickly.

Rockaway Parkway

The street that leads from the L train terminal at Glenwood Road south toward Canarsie Pier, lined with shops, offices and fast food. Brooklyn Paper reported a cellphone store near Flatlands Avenue broken into at about 3 a.m. by men who smashed through the wall with a sledgehammer they had taken from a shed on Conklin Avenue. Forgotten New York notes the 1927 Jerry Building near the subway entrance.

Remsen Avenue

Older and more mixed: shops, houses and, as Forgotten New York observed in 2026, auto repair and tire shops among the residences, plus PS 114 at Glenwood Road. Repair businesses here have overhead doors, cars left overnight and outdoor lots, which call for door contacts sized for roll-up doors and motion coverage of the bays.

What a Canarsie store alarm should include

Rules that apply to every Canarsie alarm

  • Siren time. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says the owner of a building may not operate an audible burglar alarm unless it can terminate, and does terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. We program every siren inside that limit.
  • Who may install. Under New York General Business Law Article 6-D, a business that installs, services or maintains security or fire alarm systems needs a license from the Department of State. Ours is #12000287431. An owner installing on their own property is exempt.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm with smoke detectors is not a fire alarm system. Commercial and multi-family buildings that need a code fire alarm follow the fire code, FDNY review and licensed fire alarm work.
  • Permits. We found no primary source for a New York City permit requirement for an ordinary monitored burglar alarm, so we do not claim one. If your insurer, landlord or condo board has its own requirement, we work to it.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Canarsie: Questions Answered

Garages, driveways and the front of the house

Our driveway slopes down to a garage under the house with a den behind it. Which door does a burglar treat as the real way in?

Usually the garage. It is the largest opening, it sits below the sidewalk where passers-by cannot see it, and the door from the garage into the den is often a light interior door. We put a heavy-duty contact on the overhead door, a contact on the den door and a motion detector in the den, so any of the three tells the alarm. The front door at the top of the stoop still gets a contact, but it is rarely the weakest point on these houses.

Our old garage was turned into a family room years ago, but the overhead door is still in place. How should that be alarmed?

Exactly as if it were still a garage. A bolted or fixed overhead door can still be forced, and a room with a sofa behind it is no less an entrance. The overhead door gets a contact, the room gets a motion detector, and if the door no longer opens at all we program it with no entry delay, so any movement of that door is an instant alarm rather than a countdown.

We share a driveway with the house next door. Will an outdoor detector over the drive keep alerting us every time they park?

Only if it is aimed carelessly. Outdoor detectors have narrow, adjustable patterns; we point the one over a shared drive at your garage face and side door, not across the drive, and set it to need a person-sized target. Where a camera covers the drive too, we draw its detection zone around your doors only. The neighbor’s car becomes background.

Basements, floods and the equipment itself

When a downpour fills the trench drain at the foot of our sunken driveway, can a sensor warn us before the water reaches the garage?

Yes. A water sensor on the garage floor just inside the overhead door, set as a 24-hour zone, reports the moment water crosses the threshold, whether the alarm is armed or not. It will not stop the water; clearing or improving the drain is a job for a plumber. What it buys is time to move a car or lift boxes off the floor before the den behind the garage takes water too.

Our basement has a sump pump. Can the alarm tell us when the pit is filling faster than the pump can keep up?

It can watch for the result. A water sensor clipped just below the rim of the sump pit, or a high-water float switch wired to a spare alarm input, sends an alert when the level rises past the point the pump should hold it. Many pumps also stop when the power goes out, so the panel’s own power-loss alert is a useful second warning. The pump itself stays the plumber’s responsibility.

The basement tenant has a separate side door. Should that door’s alarm reach us, the tenant, or both of us?

The tenant first, because it is their home. We set the basement as its own alarm area: their door and windows, their code, their alerts. A break-in alarm in that area can also notify the owner, since it is the same building, but day-to-day arming and disarming stays private to the tenant. Water and smoke alerts go to both households.

Windows, yards and the hours the house is empty

The 69th Precinct once warned that burglars look for a private house with no car in the driveway. What can an alarm do for a house that looks empty all day?

It can make sure the house is never actually unguarded. Easy arming at the door you leave by, a reminder on your phone if the system is still off at nine, and arming from the app if you forgot. A video doorbell lets you answer the knock that comes first, so the house does not sound empty. And interior motion means a return trip through a back window is reported within seconds.

Police described thieves cutting window screens and being in and out in a minute. Does a sensor react fast enough to matter?

Sensors react in under a second. The minute that matters is what happens next: an alert on your phone and, if you monitor, a call from the central station, plus a siren that ends a quiet search of the bedrooms. The catch is the open window: a contact on a window that was already open behind a screen does not report. That is why we pair window contacts with motion detection and set the system to warn when a protected window is open at arming.

Our house sits on a corner with a side yard open to the street. Does a corner lot need more alarm coverage than a mid-block house?

Usually a little. A corner house has a whole side wall of windows that passers-by can study and a side yard that often has its own gate and door. We add contacts to the side windows that can be reached from the yard, cover the side door like a front door, and aim any outdoor detector along the side yard rather than at the sidewalk, so foot traffic on the cross street does not trigger it.

Stores, repair shops and condo units

A Rockaway Parkway store was once broken into straight through the wall with a sledgehammer. Which sensor catches someone who never opens a door?

An interior motion detector. Door and gate contacts protect openings; a wall breach skips them entirely. We cover the sales floor, the stockroom and the office with motion detectors, add a shock sensor on the safe, and set the motion zones to alarm immediately, with no entry delay, when the store is armed.

The thief at a Seaview Avenue laundromat lifted the metal gate before forcing the back door. Should the alarm react when the gate moves, or only at the door behind it?

When the gate moves. A gate contact that reports instantly, with no delay, gives the owner or the monitoring station a warning before anyone is inside. During business hours the gate zone is simply disarmed with the rest of the system, so it causes no trouble while the shop is open.

Our auto repair shop on Remsen Avenue leaves customers’ cars in the lot overnight. Can one system watch the bays and the lot as well as the office?

Yes, in layers. Overhead bay doors get roll-up door contacts, the bays get motion detectors that ignore the cars’ cooling engines once they have settled, the office gets its own zone, and the lot is covered by cameras whose person detection sends a clip to the owner’s phone. Outdoor alarm detectors can watch the lot too, but cameras usually give fewer false alerts outdoors.

We live in a gated condominium near Seaview Avenue. With a gate and a guard, is an alarm inside our unit still worth having?

The gate controls who enters the complex; it does not watch your door, your windows or a ground-floor patio, and it cannot warn you about water or smoke inside the unit. A small wireless system with door contacts, a motion detector and a water sensor under the sink reports to your phone wherever you are, needs no building wiring and comes with you if you move.

3. Draw the areas and price it in writing

5. Wire the basement high, wireless where finished

6. Program the areas and trip every device

Owner and tenant areas are programmed separately, water probes become 24-hour zones that ignore disarming, sirens are capped under fifteen minutes, and then every door, window, detector and probe is set off once to prove it reports.

7. Hand over, then come back

Owner and basement tenant are shown their own codes, the app and the storm-season water test, with the steps left in writing; a return visit a few weeks later fine-tunes the garage contact, entry times and any sensor that misfired.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Water Line Before You Pick the Wall

This describes how we survey in Canarsie; it is not the story of any particular house. Before a technician decides where anything goes, he goes down to the basement and looks at the walls, not the ceiling. Floodwater leaves evidence that outlasts a renovation: a band of newer drywall along the bottom of every wall, a tide line on unpainted block, rust on the lower legs of the boiler stand, a water heater newer than everything around it, outlets moved higher than usual, a dehumidifier that runs all summer. He asks the family where the water stopped in 2012 and whether it has come back in heavy rains since, and he believes the walls if the answers are vague.

That line sets two things. Everything the system depends on goes well above it, which in Canarsie nearly always means the first floor. And every sensor that has to stay below it is chosen and placed knowing it may get wet: wireless transmitters high on frames, wired basement sensors on their own zones, water probes on the floor with their transmitters on the wall. Then he goes outside, walks down the driveway to the garage, and repeats the exercise at the back of the house from the yard. Only after both walks does he count sensors.

Sources

Where the Canarsie Facts Come From

Every local fact, figure, rule and news item on this page was checked in October 2026 against the sources below. What carries no source is ours: the prices, which are the published AESS Brooklyn alarm list, and the descriptions of how we survey, design and install, which are our working practice and not claims about any particular building.

  • NYC Department of City Planning, Resilient Neighborhoods: Canarsie (May 2017) — about 12,000 residential buildings, two-thirds in the floodplain; floodplain count raised from about twenty-six to 5,000; counts by building type; lot widths; front garages with recreation rooms; sunken front yards and backyards; basements converted to units; Sandy water five to seven feet above grade via Fresh Creek and Paerdegat Basin; sewer backups; up to seven feet of basement water; elevating critical systems; base flood elevations of one to six feet; commercial corridors with over 400 retail properties outside the floodplain; construction eras
  • Wikipedia, “Canarsie, Brooklyn” — boundaries and neighbors; Lenape name; Canarsie Landing railroad 1865; Golden City 1907–1939; Canarsie Pier 1926; postwar development on filled marsh; mostly detached one- and two-family houses; Seaview Estates; 69th Precinct 9720 Foster Avenue and 2019 figures; Engine 257/Ladder 170/Battalion 58 at 1361 Rockaway Parkway; Canarsie Plaza and Brooklyn Terminal Market; Canarsie Cemetery; Canarsie Park; L and bus routes; Sandy basement flooding and post-Sandy flood maps
  • Wikipedia, “Canarsie–Rockaway Parkway station” — ground-level terminal at Rockaway Parkway and Glenwood Road, opened July 28, 1906; bus routes B6, B17, B42, B60, B82 and B82 SBS
  • Wikipedia, “Breukelen Houses” and “Bay View Houses” — buildings, stories, apartments and completion dates
  • NYPD, 69th Precinct — 9720 Foster Avenue; mainly residential with commercial strips along Avenue L, Rockaway Parkway and Remsen Avenue
  • NYC Department of Finance, 2024 Brooklyn neighborhood sales summary — Canarsie one-, two- and three-family sales counts and medians
  • Forgotten New York, “Canarsie 2026” (Kevin Walsh, June 27, 2026) — firehouse dated 1908; Jerry Building 1927; Holy Family Church; Remsen Avenue auto and tire shops among houses; attached wooden houses on East 95th Street; bungalow-style houses on Conklin Avenue; Italianate houses near Bedell Lane
  • Brooklyn Paper, burglary spike in the 69th Precinct (page shows no year; written after the 2009 totals) — daytime burglaries, knock first, return through back windows or doors, driveway with no car, screens cut, about a quarter through open windows or doors
  • Brooklyn Paper, “Increase in burglaries has Canarsie cops alerting residents” — knocking to ask for a fictitious name; East 80s south of Avenue M; meeting at the Hebrew Educational Society on Seaview Avenue
  • Brooklyn Paper, “Three hammer their way into a cellphone store” (no year shown) — Rockaway Parkway store near Flatlands Avenue entered through the wall with a sledgehammer; Avenue L burglary via a barbecue grill and an unlocked bedroom window
  • Brooklyn Paper, “Two Canarsie stores burglarized” (no year shown) — Avenue L butcher shop near East 93rd Street, front door forced and safe jimmied; Seaview Avenue laundromat, gate raised and back door forced
  • CBS New York, January 29, 2020 — five window burglaries in about two weeks, at all hours; card use at East 103rd Street and Avenue N
  • City Limits, December 21, 2012 — one to six feet of water in basements and first floors; Paerdegat Street homeowner and basement tenant; FEMA specialist on basement dwellers
  • Brooklyn Eagle, October 30, 2013 — flooded basements in the Paerdegat area; much of Canarsie in evacuation zones 3 to 5
  • National Park Service, Canarsie Pier news release (May 22, 2020) — Canarsie Pier managed as part of Gateway National Recreation Area
  • NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 — building burglar alarms must terminate within fifteen minutes of activation
  • NYS Department of State, Security or Fire Alarm Installer license and Article 6-D guide — license required for alarm installation businesses; owner exemption
  • NYC HPD, smoke and carbon monoxide detector requirements — detector rules for multiple dwellings and non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes

Where sources disagree, we say so. Wikipedia’s Canarsie article gives a 2020 population of 88,522 in its summary box and 92,227 in its demographics section, and City Planning’s 2017 report says more than 83,000 residents; this page avoids a single population figure for that reason. Forgotten New York says the subway has ended at Rockaway Parkway since 1920, while Wikipedia dates the station to 1906 and the conversion of the Canarsie Railroad to a subway line to 1928; we give only the station’s opening date. Wikipedia’s Canarsie article places Canarsie Plaza on Avenue D; we repeat that without an exact address.

Some sources could not be reached or found. Our build environment had no access to Google’s People Also Ask, related searches or AI Overview, Answer the Public, Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, or Yelp reviews. A search of Reddit found one excerpt in which a commenter from Canarsie said most people there worry about property crime such as break-ins, and no thread about Canarsie alarms or basement flooding. We found no 2025 or 2026 reporting on Canarsie house burglaries that we could open in full, and several Brooklyn Paper items used here carry no year, which we note where we cite them. We found no primary source for a New York City burglar-alarm permit, so the page makes no permit claim.
