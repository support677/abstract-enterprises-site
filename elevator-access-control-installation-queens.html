How It Works

How Elevator Card Readers Work in a Queens Building

An elevator card reader does one small job very reliably: it reads a credential, asks a controller whether that person is allowed, and closes or opens a set of electrical contacts that the elevator already understands. The reader itself does not move the car. It sits in the cab, usually on or beside the car operating panel, or at the lobby hall call station, and it is wired back to an access control panel that holds the list of people and the list of floors each one may reach. When a resident in a Hunters Point rental taps a fob, the reader passes the fob's number to the panel. The panel looks the number up, sees that this resident lives on the 31st floor and also has the gym on the 9th and the roof deck on the 44th, and for a few seconds closes the relays for those three buttons. The resident presses 31, the car accepts the call, and the relays open again. A stranger who rode in behind the resident and pressed 44 a moment later gets nothing, because 44 was only enabled for the person who presented the credential.

That is the core of every elevator access control system, whether the reader reads a plastic card, a key fob, a phone, a PIN code or a face. What changes from one Queens building to the next is how much of the elevator is controlled and where. In a six-story co-op in Pomonok with one small car, the whole job may be one reader in the cab that enables the car for anyone with a valid fob. In a forty-floor Court Square tower with three cars, a garage level and an amenity floor, it can mean a reader in every cab, a reader at the garage hall call, floor relays for every residential floor in every car, schedules for the amenity floor and a link to the lobby video intercom so a visitor can be sent to one apartment's floor only.

The three pieces inside every elevator access control system

  • The reader. A card reader, key fob reader, keypad, mobile credential reader or biometric reader, mounted in the cab or at a hall station. It reads the credential and sends the result to the panel. Where the reader and panel support a supervised, encrypted connection such as OSDP, we use it, which makes tampering with the reader wiring far harder to get away with.
  • The panel and the floor interface. The access control panel holds the users, the floors and the schedules. Attached to it is an elevator I/O module or a bank of floor-enable relays, one output per floor per car. Each output is a dry contact that the elevator contractor wires in series or in parallel with the floor button circuit, or into a dedicated security input on newer controllers.
  • The software. The management platform, in the cloud or on a local computer, where the property manager adds residents, removes people who move out, sets which floors each group can reach, sets hours, and reads the log of who used which car and when.

The cable between the cab and the machine room deserves its own mention, because it is the part people forget to ask about. A reader in the car has to reach the panel through the elevator's traveling cable, the flat bundle of conductors that hangs from the car and moves with it. Most Queens elevators installed or modernized in the last few decades have spare pairs in that cable, and the elevator contractor confirms which ones are free. When there are no spare pairs, or when a phone-credential reader or a video station needs a network connection, a new cable or a network extender over existing pairs is needed. That is why our quotes list the network or cable run to the car as its own line after the survey rather than hiding it in a flat price.

Car-enable or floor-by-floor: the decision that sets the price

The simplest system, which we call Level 1, uses a reader in the car that enables the whole car. The rider shows a credential, and for a short window the car will take any floor it serves. This stops people who have no credential at all, such as someone who slipped through the lobby door behind a resident, from riding up and wandering the halls. It does not stop a resident of the 4th floor from riding to the 20th. For a lot of Queens co-ops, especially the six- and seven-story elevator buildings that make up much of the stock in Jackson Heights, Elmhurst, Rego Park and Kew Gardens, that is exactly the protection the board wants. The neighbors know each other. The problem is outsiders, delivery people wandering, or people sleeping in stairwells, not residents visiting floors they do not live on.

Floor-by-floor control, which is part of Level 3, is a different animal. Each floor button is disabled until a credential enables it, and each credential only enables its own floors. This is the design that newer condo and rental towers in Long Island City and Flushing usually want, because they have amenity floors, storage floors, private roof terraces and, in mixed-use buildings, offices or hotel floors that residents should not reach and the public should not reach the residential floors from. It needs more outputs, more programming and more of the elevator contractor's time, so it is priced per building after the survey.

The Borough

Why Queens Properties Need Elevator Access Control

Queens is often described as a borough of houses, and in much of Bayside, Middle Village or Cambria Heights that is true. But the borough also holds a very large elevator building stock that grew in three waves: the prewar and postwar apartment houses along Queens Boulevard, 37th Avenue and the streets around the subway lines; the large union, Mitchell-Lama and cooperative complexes of the 1950s and 1960s; and the glass towers that have risen in Long Island City and downtown Flushing since the 2000s, with Jamaica next in line. The 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey counted 284,200 Queens housing units, 31 percent of the borough's housing, in buildings with an elevator. Only Manhattan has a larger share. That is a lot of elevators that were built to answer anyone who walks in.

The security problem in those buildings is rarely dramatic. It is the slow, everyday kind. A lobby door gets held for a delivery and a stranger walks in. Someone rides to the top floor and sleeps on the landing by the roof door. A package is lifted from a lobby table. In December 2025 QNS reported police looking for a man who took several packages from the lobby of a Woodside apartment building, and CBS New York has aired video of a man stealing packages from the lobby of another Queens building just after midnight. A locked front door is the first line, and the door side of a building is where most owners start. The elevator is the second line. If the car only answers residents, a person who gets past the door is stuck in the lobby, on camera, instead of free to roam twenty floors.

Queens buildings also have specific needs that a generic elevator security system does not cover. Many mixed-use towers put offices, a hotel, a medical practice or a community facility in the same building as apartments, and each group needs to be kept to its own floors. Many co-ops have a large share of older shareholders and residents whose first language is not English, so the credential has to be easy and the instructions have to be readable. Buildings near the water in the Rockaways and along Flushing Bay have had machine rooms and cellars flood, so where the panel goes matters. Hotels near the airports run around the clock with guest, crew and staff traffic on the same cars. And since 2021, residential buildings in New York City that use fobs, cards, phones or biometrics for entry fall under Local Law 63, so the way credential data is handled is a legal question as well as a technical one.

  • 284,200 housing units in Queens are in buildings with an elevator, 31 percent of the borough's housing, according to the 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey. Citywide, 71 percent of units built in 2000 or later have an elevator.
  • Over 21,000 housing units have been built within half a mile of the OneLIC project area in Long Island City since 2010, according to the NYC Department of City Planning, and the city's OneLIC proposal projects roughly 14,699 more over ten years.
  • The Jamaica Neighborhood Plan, adopted by the City Council on October 29, 2025, is expected to bring about 12,000 new homes, including 4,000 permanently affordable homes, and about 7,000 jobs to downtown Jamaica.

  • Electchester in Pomonok has about 2,500 apartments in 38 buildings, many of them six-story brick structures, built between 1949 and 1966 with Local 3 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, according to Urban Omnibus.
  • The Jackson Heights Historic District was designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on October 19, 1993, and covers the neighborhood's early twentieth century garden apartments and houses.
Long Island City

Elevator Access Control for Long Island City, Court Square and Queens Plaza Towers

The 2001 rezoning of the blocks between Queens Plaza and Court Square, followed by the waterfront towers of Hunters Point, turned Long Island City into the densest cluster of new elevator buildings in Queens. The Department of City Planning's OneLIC materials count more than 21,000 housing units built within half a mile of the plan area since 2010, and the pipeline keeps going: 30-25 Queens Boulevard has been reported as a tower of about 525 feet with 561 apartments and ground-floor retail. Most of these buildings are rentals, with a smaller number of condos, and almost all of them were built with some form of key fob or card access at the front door, the garage, the package room and the amenity floors.

What many of them were not built with is real elevator floor restriction. A common pattern we see in newer Queens towers is a reader in each cab that was installed at opening and programmed only to enable the car. The developer checked the box, the building leased up, and five or ten years later the managing agent finds that residents and their guests can ride to every amenity floor, every storage floor and the roof at any hour. Or the building added a coworking lounge, a children's playroom or a pool with posted hours and now needs those floors to open only during those hours and only for residents who signed the waiver. Turning a car-enable system into a floor-by-floor system usually means adding floor outputs, a second visit from the elevator contractor to land them, and a fresh permission chart. Sometimes the reader in the cab can stay. Sometimes the original platform cannot do per-floor rules and the cab reader has to be replaced. The survey tells you which.

Amenity floors, roof decks and garages

The amenity floor is where most Long Island City elevator projects begin. It may hold a gym, a lounge, a screening room, a terrace and a party room, each with different hours and different rules. The best elevator access control systems for an apartment building like this let the manager put residents in groups, such as "all residents," "residents who signed the pool waiver," "residents who booked the party room tonight," and give each group its own floor permissions and hours. The elevator then enforces the same rules the front desk would, without anyone standing at the door. Roof decks get the same treatment, often with an evening cut-off. Garage levels usually get a reader at the hall call button on the garage landing, so a person who walks in through the garage entrance cannot call the car without a credential.

Destination dispatch in the newest towers

A few of the newest towers use destination dispatch: the rider picks a floor on a lobby kiosk or touchscreen and the system assigns a car. In those buildings there are no floor buttons in the car to switch with relays. Elevator access control is done through a data link between the access control platform and the elevator group controller, which receives each rider's allowed floors and only offers those on the kiosk. This is the most elegant design, but it depends on the elevator manufacturer's interface and the access control platform supporting each other. We confirm compatibility with the elevator contractor before anyone buys anything, and destination dispatch integration is always quoted per building after the survey.

Flushing

Floor Separation in Flushing's Mixed-Use Towers

Downtown Flushing has built a type of building that is unusual for New York: large complexes where apartments sit on top of, or next to, a shopping mall, offices and a hotel, sometimes sharing a lobby, a garage and even an elevator core. Tangram, developed by F&T Group, fits two residential towers, Tangram House South (opened 2019) and Tangram House West (a 16-story tower of about 132 apartments that opened in 2022), together with the Renaissance New York Flushing Hotel and an office building around a central courtyard, above retail. Sky View Parc puts three condominium towers with 448 apartments on top of a mall on a 14-acre site, with a fourth tower, The Grand, added later. Flushing Commons has been listed with about 600 condominium units across two phases, alongside office and retail space.

In buildings like these, elevator access control is less about keeping strangers out and more about keeping each group of people on its own floors. Hotel guests should reach the lobby, their room floor and the hotel amenities, not the condo floors. Office tenants and their visitors should reach their office floor during business hours. Shoppers should reach the mall levels and the parking garage and nowhere else. Residents need their own floor, the residential amenity floors and the garage, and they should not have to go through a hotel lobby to get home. Where those groups use separate elevator banks, each bank gets its own rules. Where they share cars, for example between a garage and the floors above it, floor-by-floor restriction and hall-call readers do the separating.

One building, several owners

The hardest part of a Flushing mixed-use project is not technical. It is that the residential condominium, the office condominium, the hotel operator and the retail owner may each control different parts of the building and different budgets, and the elevator may belong to one of them or to a master association. Before we design anything, we ask who owns each car and each floor, who decides the rules for each, and who pays for which part. Then we design one system with separate permission groups and, where needed, separate administrator logins, so the hotel's front desk can manage hotel guests without seeing the condo residents' data and the condo's managing agent can manage residents without touching the hotel's rules. That separation also helps with Local Law 63, because residential tenant data stays with the residential side.

Co-ops

Elevator Access Control for Queens Co-ops: Garden Complexes and Elevator Buildings

Queens has more cooperative apartments in more kinds of buildings than any other borough outside Manhattan, and the kind of co-op decides what an elevator access project looks like. Lawyers who close Queens co-op sales describe a "garden co-op belt": low-rise prewar complexes built around shared courtyards in Jackson Heights, Sunnyside and parts of Forest Hills, most of them walk-ups. Beside them sit the large elevator co-op buildings of Forest Hills, Rego Park, Kew Gardens and Bay Terrace, many converted from rentals in the 1980s, and in many of those buildings the original sponsor still holds and rents a block of unsold apartments. Each of those facts changes the project.

Garden co-ops and the one building with an elevator

In a garden complex, most buildings have no elevator at all. Where elevator access control comes in is the occasional building in the complex that does, often a later six-story section or a building with a lift added for accessibility, and the complex's community rooms, laundry rooms and storage rooms that sit on lower levels. In these complexes the elevator is usually a single small car, and the board's goal is to keep it for residents, especially older shareholders who rely on it, and keep outsiders from riding it to the top-floor landing. A Level 1 reader in the car is usually enough. If the complex already has a fob system at the courtyard gates and building doors, the elevator reader should join that same system so residents carry one fob, and the board should check whether the building sits inside a historic district before anything is mounted on the outside. The Jackson Heights Historic District, designated in 1993, covers much of that neighborhood's garden apartment stock, and work visible from the street there goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. A reader inside an elevator cab does not, but a new reader post at a courtyard gate might.

Elevator co-ops on Queens Boulevard and the side streets

The six- to twenty-story elevator co-ops of Rego Park, Forest Hills, Kew Gardens, Briarwood and Jackson Heights are where most Queens elevator access requests come from. These buildings typically have one to three cars, a doorman only part of the day or not at all, a lobby with a package area, a laundry room in the cellar and sometimes a garage or a parking lot entrance at the back. The board's priorities are usually, in order: keep non-residents out of the elevator, keep the cellar laundry and storage for shareholders, give the super a way to open everything for moves and emergencies, and make it easy for elderly shareholders. Floor-by-floor restriction is less often the goal here than in a new condo tower, because shareholders do visit each other. A reader in each car, a reader at the cellar hall call and a well-run fob list cover most of what these boards want.

Sponsor units, sublets and renters in a co-op

Where a sponsor still owns apartments and rents them out, the board does not control who lives in those apartments, and renters turn over more often than shareholders. That is an argument for an elevator access platform with good credential management: the managing agent can issue fobs or phone credentials to a renter when the lease starts and switch them off the day it ends, without collecting a physical key. The same applies to sublets approved by the board. We set up user groups for shareholders, sponsor renters, approved subtenants, staff and contractors, so each group can be given and removed as a group, and the board can see at any time how many active credentials exist for each apartment.

How boards approve the project

A co-op elevator belongs to the corporation, so the board approves the project and the rules. What helps a Queens board decide is a short written scope it can read at a meeting, a one-page chart of who reaches which floors, a plain statement of what the elevator contractor will do and charge separately, and a draft of the resident notice and Local Law 63 privacy policy. We prepare those as part of the proposal. We also recommend that boards decide in advance how many credentials each apartment gets, what a replacement costs the shareholder, and how aides, dog walkers and cleaners are handled, because those are the questions that fill the next three meetings if they are left open.

Large Complexes

Elevator Access in Electchester, Big Six Towers and Other Large Queens Complexes

Queens also has a group of very large cooperative and union-built complexes that work almost like small towns. Electchester in Pomonok, built from 1949 to 1966 with Local 3 of the electrical workers' union, has about 2,500 apartments in 38 buildings, many of them six-story brick buildings. Big Six Towers in Woodside, a Mitchell-Lama cooperative completed in 1963 for members of Typographical Union No. 6, sits on Queens Boulevard next to Boulevard Gardens, which opened in 1935. There are similar towers-in-the-park complexes across the borough. What they share is a lot of identical buildings, a central management office, a long-serving maintenance staff and residents who have lived there for decades.

For these complexes, the elevator access question is about scale and consistency. Thirty-eight buildings with one or two cars each is a fleet, and it should be run as one system, not thirty-eight separate ones. That means one platform, one user list, the same reader model in every car so the maintenance staff carry one kind of spare, the same wiring layout in every machine room so any technician can find anything, and a rollout schedule that does a few buildings at a time so the elevator contractor's crew can work through them in order. It also means credentials that work at every building door and every elevator the resident is entitled to use, so a resident visiting a parent in another building of the complex can be given access to that building too.

Phasing a multi-building rollout

On a multi-building complex we normally recommend a pilot building first. The pilot shows the board and the residents exactly what the reader looks like, how fast it reads, how the language of the instructions works, and how the management office handles a lost fob. After the pilot, the remaining buildings follow in groups, with the cable and panel work done ahead of the elevator contractor's visit so the cars are out of service for as short a time as possible. Each building's floor chart, panel location and cable records go into one binder and one digital file for the management office.

The Rockaways

Rockaway Buildings: Flood-Safe Panels and Power Planning

The apartment buildings along the Rockaway peninsula, from Far Rockaway through Arverne, Edgemere, Rockaway Beach and Rockaway Park, carry a lesson most of the borough learned from news reports in 2012. After Hurricane Sandy, The New York Times reported on Queens high-rise residents who, weeks later, still feared getting stuck in the elevator as the power flickered several times a day. A study of public housing after the storm counted nearly 700 elevators out of service across the city's affected developments, leaving older and disabled residents stranded on upper floors for days and weeks. Elevator access control does not cause that problem, but a badly placed system can make recovery slower and a well-planned one can make it faster.

Where the panel goes

In many older buildings the natural place for an access control panel is the cellar, next to the electric service and the telephone frame. In a Rockaway building, or anywhere in the borough's flood zones along Flushing Bay, Jamaica Bay and the East River shore, the cellar is the worst place for it. We look for a location above the building's known flood level: the elevator machine room on the roof of a traction building, a ground-floor closet, the super's office, or a locked enclosure on an upper floor near the elevator shaft. The cable run gets longer, but the panel, its battery and the floor relays survive water that reaches the cellar. If the elevator itself is a hydraulic unit with the machine room at the bottom, we keep the access control electronics out of that room and run only the necessary wiring to the controller.

Power loss and getting home

Every panel we install has battery backup sized for the readers and relays, and the floor-release behavior on power loss is chosen with the elevator contractor and the building, not left to chance. In most residential buildings we set the floor outputs so that if the access control system loses power completely, the floor buttons return to normal operation rather than staying locked. A building with emergency power for the elevators should also have the access control panel on that emergency circuit so the restriction can keep running while the car runs. After a storm, the first thing the super should be able to do is open the elevator to everyone with one switch or one command, and that override is part of every job.

Jamaica

Downtown Jamaica: New Towers, Medical Offices and Government Buildings

Downtown Jamaica is the next big wave of elevator construction in Queens. The Jamaica Neighborhood Plan, which the City Council approved on October 29, 2025 and the Council described as the city's largest neighborhood rezoning in more than twenty years, is expected to bring about 12,000 new homes, including 4,000 that are permanently affordable, along with about 7,000 jobs and $413 million in community investments. Much of that housing will be in mid- and high-rise elevator buildings near the Jamaica Center and Sutphin Boulevard stations and the LIRR and AirTrain hub. Jamaica already has a dense mix of courthouses, government offices, medical practices and office buildings along Jamaica Avenue, Sutphin Boulevard and Parsons Boulevard.

Building elevator access into new Jamaica projects

For a building that is still on paper, elevator access control is far cheaper and cleaner when it is in the electrical and elevator specifications from the start. The elevator manufacturer can supply the controller with security inputs or a destination dispatch interface ready for access control, the traveling cable can be ordered with spare pairs and a network pair, and conduit to the machine room can be planned. We work with developers and their electrical engineers at that stage to write the low-voltage specification, so the elevator contractor knows exactly what will connect to its controller. Affordable units often come with their own lease-up and income-verification process, and credential issuing can be built into move-in so each new household gets its fobs or phone credentials on day one.

Medical and professional buildings

The medical and professional offices around downtown Jamaica, and similar buildings along Queens Boulevard and in Flushing, need elevators that are open to patients while practices are open and locked to the public after hours, with staff, cleaners and the building engineer still able to reach their floors. That is a schedule problem, and it is handled with time-based floor permissions: public floors open in the morning, close in the evening, and stay available to staff credentials around the clock. Floors with records rooms, pharmacies or equipment storage stay locked to the public at all times. The schedule follows the building's own hours, including holidays, and the log shows who entered which floor after hours.

Hotels

Hotel Elevator Access Control Near JFK and LaGuardia

Queens holds the city's two airports and the hotels that serve them, from the TWA Hotel, which has 512 rooms and is the only hotel on JFK airport grounds, to the hotels clustered around the Van Wyck Expressway, Jamaica and the streets near LaGuardia in East Elmhurst. Airport hotels have elevator traffic patterns that a residential building never sees: guests arriving at any hour, airline crews on contracted room blocks with their own floor and their own schedules, shuttle drivers, conference attendees and staff, all using the same lobby and often the same cars.

Guest key cards and the elevator

Most hotels already have an electronic lock system for guest rooms, and the guest key card is encoded at the front desk. Hotel elevator access control usually reads that same key card in the cab, so guests do not carry a second credential. The card's encoding tells the elevator which floor the guest's room is on, and the elevator enables that floor plus the shared floors such as the lobby, fitness center, pool and meeting rooms. Whether the elevator reader talks directly to the lock vendor's system or to a separate access control platform that shares data with it depends on the lock vendor. We work out with the hotel's lock vendor and the elevator contractor where each piece connects before quoting, and we only take responsibility for the parts we install.

Crew floors, club floors and staff

Airline crews are often housed on dedicated floors under contract, and the airline may ask that those floors be separated from other guests. Club or executive floors with a lounge need their own credential level. Housekeeping, engineering and security staff need every floor, but their credentials should be logged separately from guests. Time schedules can open the meeting room floor only during events and lock it overnight. All of this is set up as permission groups in the platform, so the front desk manager can change a group without calling us.

Offices & Lofts

Commercial Elevator Access Control in Long Island City Offices and Converted Warehouses

Long Island City's commercial buildings are mostly not glass towers. Many are former factories and warehouses that have been turned into offices, studios, labs and showrooms. The Factory on 47th Avenue was built in 1926 as a Macy's furniture warehouse and is now a large office and workspace building. The Falchi Building at 31-00 47th Avenue, a five-story building developed in 1922 as a warehouse, is now mixed use. Crain's New York Business has reported on Class A office space sitting vacant in Queens, which pushes owners to compete for tenants on amenities, convenience and security.

Multi-tenant office floors

The usual approach in a multi-tenant Long Island City office building is to give every tenant's employees a credential that enables their own floor and the shared floors, such as the lobby, the roof terrace and a shared conference center, during the hours the landlord sets, with the tenant able to extend its own floor's hours for its own staff. When several companies share a floor, the elevator lets all of them reach that floor, and the doors into each suite do the separating. Visitors are registered by the tenant and get a temporary credential or a mobile pass for their host's floor only. Many tenants already have badges from their own access control system; where the badge technology is compatible we enroll those badges so employees carry one card.

Freight elevators

Freight elevators are often the biggest gap in a converted building's security. They open onto loading docks, they may have manual gates, and they are run by building staff or by tenants' own people. A reader can be added to many freight cars so only authorized tenants and staff can call or run the car, with schedules that match the building's delivery hours. Whether that is practical on a particular freight car, especially an older one with manual doors or an attendant control, is a question for the elevator contractor, and we ask it on the survey before we promise anything.

Floor Restriction

How to Restrict Elevator Access to Certain Floors in a Queens Building

Restricting elevator access to certain floors comes down to four decisions, made in this order: which floors are controlled, who may reach each one, when, and where the credential is read. A Queens building that makes those four decisions on paper before the installer arrives gets a better system at a lower price, because the scope matches what is actually needed.

Step one: list the floors and mark each one

Start with a simple table of every floor the elevator serves, including the cellar, the garage levels, the lobby, the mezzanine, the amenity floors and the roof. Mark each one as open to everyone, residents only, specific residents only, staff only, or scheduled. In a typical forty-floor Long Island City rental, the lobby and the mail room level are open, residential floors are each limited to that floor's residents, the amenity floor is for all residents during posted hours, the roof is for residents until a set hour, the storage floor is limited to residents who rent a storage unit, and the mechanical floor is staff only. In a seven-story Forest Hills co-op, the answer may be: lobby open, all floors open to shareholders, cellar open to shareholders, roof staff only. Both are correct. They just need different systems.

Step two: decide who belongs to which group

Elevator access control systems manage people in groups. A group might be "residents of floor 12," "all residents," "pool members," "staff," "contractors," "movers this Saturday," or "the dental practice on floor 3." Each group has a list of floors and a schedule. A person can be in several groups, and the system combines their permissions. Good group design is what keeps a Queens building manageable over time, because when a resident moves out the manager removes one person, not twenty floor permissions, and when the pool closes for the season the manager changes one group schedule, not three hundred individual credentials.

Step three: set the hours

Time-based floor permissions let a floor open and close on a schedule. A medical floor in Jamaica opens to the public during office hours. A gym in a Flushing condo opens at five in the morning and closes at eleven at night. A rooftop in Astoria closes to everyone at ten. Holidays can follow their own schedule. Schedules can also work the other way: a floor that is normally restricted can open to everyone during a building event or a move, and close again automatically afterward.

Step four: choose where the credential is read

The reader can be in the car, at the hall call stations, or both. A reader in the car controls which floor buttons work. A reader at a hall call station controls whether the car can be called to that landing at all. Many Queens buildings want both: readers in the cars for floor selection, plus readers at the garage and cellar call buttons so a person who enters through the garage or the back door cannot summon the car. Each extra hall station adds a reader and wiring, so the survey counts them one by one.

How elevator floor restriction works electrically

Behind the scenes, elevator floor restriction works by inserting a contact the access control system controls into each floor's button circuit, or by giving the elevator controller a security input per floor. Older relay-logic and early microprocessor controllers typically need a relay contact in series with each car call button, landed by the elevator contractor. Newer controllers often have dedicated security inputs or a card-reader interface, where each floor input tells the controller to accept or ignore calls for that floor. The newest systems skip per-floor wiring completely and talk to the elevator group controller over a data link. Which of those your building has decides how much of the work is relays and wire and how much is configuration. The elevator contractor tells us which it is, and we design to match.

Credentials

RFID Cards and Key Fobs on Queens Elevators

Yes, an elevator can use RFID credentials, and most Queens elevator access systems do. The key fobs and cards that residents already carry for the front door are almost always RFID: a small chip and antenna that the reader powers and reads at a few centimeters. What matters is which kind of RFID, because the older and newer kinds behave very differently.

Older 125 kHz proximity fobs

Many Queens buildings that installed fob systems in the 1990s, 2000s and early 2010s use 125 kHz proximity credentials. They are cheap and they read reliably, but they have no encryption. The number on the fob can be read from a short distance and copied onto a blank fob with inexpensive equipment sold online, which is why "fob copying" kiosks exist. For an elevator, that means a copied fob works exactly like the original. If your building's fobs are this older type, the elevator project is a good moment to plan a move to encrypted credentials, at least for the elevator. Multi-technology readers can read both the old fobs and the new ones during a changeover, so residents can switch over a few months instead of on one day.

Encrypted 13.56 MHz smart cards and fobs

Newer systems use 13.56 MHz smart credentials, such as DESFire-based cards and fobs, with mutual authentication and encryption between the credential and the reader. These are much harder to copy, and they can carry more than one application, for example the elevator, the front door, the laundry room and the garage. For a Queens condo or rental tower installing elevator access control for the first time, we recommend an encrypted smart credential as the default, with mobile credentials as an option alongside it.

Phones

Smartphone Elevator Credentials for Queens Residents and Tenants

Elevator access control can use smartphones, and in new Queens rental and condo towers phone credentials are now common. The resident's phone holds a credential in an app or in the phone's wallet, and the elevator reader reads it over Bluetooth or NFC when the phone is held near the reader. Some systems also let the resident tap a button in the app to call the car or enable their floor, but for an elevator we usually prefer a design where the phone is presented at the reader inside the car, so the credential is tied to the person standing in the cab.

Why property managers like phone credentials

For a managing agent running a Court Square or Hunters Point rental with high turnover, mobile credentials remove a lot of work. A new tenant gets an invitation by email or text before move-in, accepts it, and the phone works on the front door, the package room and the elevator on day one. When the lease ends, the credential is revoked from the platform, and there is no fob to collect. Lost phones are less common than lost fobs, and when one is lost, the resident usually notices and calls quickly. Visitors can be sent a temporary mobile pass for one floor and one day.

Why some Queens residents will still want a fob

Not everyone in Queens carries a smartphone, wants an app, or can read the app's language. In the borough's older co-ops and in buildings with many elderly residents, a phone-only system shuts people out. The right answer in almost every Queens residential building is a multi-technology reader that reads phones and encrypted fobs, so each household chooses. Residents with a phone use the phone; residents without one, or who prefer something on their keyring, use a fob. Both appear in the same log, both are revoked the same way, and the board does not have to force anyone to change.

Residents

Credentials for a Multilingual Borough and Older Residents

Nearly half of Queens residents, 47.6 percent according to the Census Bureau, were born outside the United States, and a walk through a single building in Elmhurst, Flushing, Jackson Heights or Richmond Hill can turn up residents who read Chinese, Spanish, Korean, Bengali, Russian, Hindi or Greek more easily than English. Queens co-ops in particular have many shareholders who bought decades ago and are now in their seventies and eighties. An elevator access system that ignores either fact causes problems from the first week: residents who do not understand the notice, who hold the fob in the wrong place, or who are locked out of their own floor and do not know whom to call.

Notices and instructions people can actually read

We write the resident notice, the one-page "how to use the elevator reader" sheet and the Local Law 63 privacy summary in plain English, and we make them easy to translate. Buildings that already send notices in more than one language can drop our text straight into their existing translations, and we keep the wording short and concrete so the translation is accurate. Where the reader or the intercom has a screen with selectable languages, we set the languages the building asks for. A sticker beside the reader with a picture of where to hold the fob does more than any paragraph.

Choosing a credential for elderly residents

For older residents the credential should be something they already carry or can wear, that works with a simple tap, and that does not depend on a charged phone or a remembered code. In practice that is a fob on the key ring, a card in a wallet sleeve, or, where the building prefers, a wristband credential. Readers should be mounted at a height a seated rider can reach, with a clear light and tone so the resident knows it worked. Keypads are useful as a backup for staff, but asking an elderly resident to remember a PIN for the elevator is asking for calls to the super. Face recognition, offered at Level 2, works well for some buildings because nothing has to be carried at all, but it requires written consent under Local Law 63 and some residents are uncomfortable with it, so we always keep a fob option available.

Aides, family and caregivers

Many older Queens residents have home health aides, visiting family or cleaners who come on a regular schedule. Rather than lending out a resident's own fob, the building can issue the aide a credential tied to that apartment, limited to that floor and the lobby, valid only on the days and hours of the aide's shift, and cancelled with one click when the agency changes staff. Every use shows in the log, which gives the family and the managing agent peace of mind.

Local Law 63

Local Law 63 and Queens Apartment Buildings That Use Elevator Fobs

Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, applies to Class A multiple dwellings in New York City that use smart access systems, which include key fobs, key cards, phone apps and biometric readers for the building entrance, individual units or common areas. An elevator reader is a common-area smart access device, so most Queens rental and cooperative apartment buildings that install elevator access control fall under it. HPD summarizes the main duties: residents must give written consent before their data is collected through the smart access system, the owner must have a plain-language privacy policy, the data can only be used for granting access and monitoring the entrances, and reference data of tenants who move out must be destroyed within 90 days.

In practice, this changes how a Queens elevator access project is set up, not whether it can be done. Before go-live, the building collects signed consent from residents who want a smart credential. The privacy policy states what the elevator reader records, such as the credential number, the time and the car, how long logs are kept, who can see them and what happens when someone moves out. The platform is configured so logs are kept only as long as the policy says, and the managing agent has a written routine for deleting a departed resident's data. Residents who do not consent still need a way to reach their apartment, and the building should decide what that is, often a physical key alternative for the front door and an elevator arrangement that does not record personal data, before the project starts. Biometric enrollment, such as face templates for a Level 2 reader, deserves the most care.

Reddit threads from New York tenants show why this matters. Residents complain about landlords that limit how many fobs an apartment can have, about elevators that require a fob to reach their floor, and about the feeling that a fob log tracks their comings and goings. A clear policy, enough credentials per household and a log that is used only for security answer most of those complaints before they start. We help with the technical side and the plain-language description of what the system does. The legal review of your policy is for the building's attorney.

"A security company can install elevator access control on its own."

Search summaries often describe elevator access control as a simple add-on any security installer can complete. In New York City that is wrong. The connection into the elevator controller is elevator work, and since January 1, 2022 it requires New York City and New York State elevator licenses.

"Elevator access control is only for luxury towers."

AI answers tend to picture a doorman tower with a concierge. In Queens, the strongest demand comes from ordinary six- to twenty-story co-ops and rentals in Rego Park, Forest Hills, Jackson Heights, Woodside and Kew Gardens, many with no doorman at night.

"A reader in the car stops people from reaching other floors."
