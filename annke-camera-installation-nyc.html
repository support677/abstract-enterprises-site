The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need ANNKE Camera Installation

New York is an old city to wire, and that is the first thing anyone installing an ANNKE system here runs into. An analysis of city housing data by Residential Real Estate Analysis puts the median year built for the city's housing at 1931. Borough by borough, it gives 1910 for Manhattan, 1925 for Brooklyn, 1935 for Queens and the Bronx, and 1970 for Staten Island. Those dates matter because the buildings were planned for coal stoves, gas lamps, a single telephone line and, later, a doorbell. Nobody framing a Manhattan tenement in 1910 left a chase for eight Ethernet cables running from the cellar to the roof. So when an ANNKE PoE kit arrives with its recorder and its bundle of pre-made cables, the hard part of the job is not the cameras at all. It is finding a path for each cable through brick, plaster, joists, fire-stopping and other people's apartments, and doing it in a way that the owner, the board and, in a historic district, the city will accept.

The five boroughs were not even one city until recently in the life of their buildings. The New York City archaeology timeline records that on January 1, 1898, Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island joined into a single metropolis after a referendum that passed by 64,744 votes to 64,467. Each place had already built in its own way, and you still see it on a camera survey. Sky NYC's guide to prewar buildings describes four- to six-story walk-up tenements built before 1929, eight- to twelve-story elevator apartment houses from the 1910s and 1920s, and larger prewar doorman buildings from the 1920s and 1930s. Add the rowhouses and brownstones of Brooklyn and Harlem, the one- and two-family houses of Queens and Staten Island, the mid-century co-ops of the Bronx and the glass-and-steel commercial buildings of Midtown and Downtown Brooklyn, and an ANNKE installation in this city can mean five completely different jobs depending on the address. One installer's marketing, from Seneca Security, describes the range as running from 1890s brownstones with horsehair plaster walls to glass-curtain commercial towers. That is a fair summary of the building stock even if it comes from a competitor.

The age shows most clearly in Brooklyn. A history site citing the city's PLUTO dataset says roughly 57 percent of Brooklyn's residential buildings were built before 1940, and that most are three- and four-story walk-ups with original lath-and-plaster ceilings. We treat that as a rough figure from a low-authority source, but it matches what technicians find. Lath-and-plaster ceilings crack when a fish tape is forced through them, and the cavities above them are often blocked by old fire-stopping or a century of renovation. In the Bronx, the same housing analysis says 79.7 percent of homes are renter-occupied, the highest share in the city. For an ANNKE install, that number changes who we talk to first. A renter can usually put a camera inside their own apartment, but the moment a cable needs to cross a hallway, a lobby or the outside wall, the landlord's agreement is part of the job. Many of the calls we expect from the Bronx and from Upper Manhattan are from landlords and managing agents who want cameras on the entrances and in the halls of buildings full of tenants, which brings its own set of rules covered further down.

Then there are the landmarks. The Mayor's Management Report for 2025 says the Landmarks Preservation Commission has designated 1,464 individual landmarks and 157 historic districts and extensions. Brooklyn Heights became the first historic district on November 23, 1965, according to the New York Preservation Archive Project, and Village Preservation describes the Greenwich Village Historic District, designated in 1969, as the largest by number of buildings, with nearly 2,200. In the LPC's 2018 rules amendments, the Commission grouped security cameras with lighting and intercom panels as objects commonly used for security, and the proposed rules would prohibit installing many of these objects on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative. That one sentence changes the plan for thousands of rowhouses. A homeowner on a landmarked block who mounts an ANNKE bullet camera above the front door because that was the easiest spot may have just created a violation. We look up the building before we design the layout, not after.

ANNKE itself brings a second set of questions that most camera brands do not. IPVM, the security-industry research publication, lists ANNKE in its Hikvision OEM Directory of brands it says it verified as selling Hikvision-made hardware. In June 2026, the FCC issued a public notice, effective July 16, 2026, prohibiting the continued import and marketing of certain previously authorized covered equipment for public safety, government facility and national security purposes. Another database, CCTVinfo, describes ANNKE as China-based but not on the FCC Covered List, with some shared lineage with Hikvision-style hardware in older models. Those sources do not agree, and we say so plainly in the brand section below. What they mean in practice for an owner in Queens or a shop on Fordham Road is simpler than the headlines: the FCC notice says continued use of lawfully purchased equipment is not affected, but a building that answers to a government agency, or a buyer who wants to be sure replacement parts will be available for years, should know the brand's background before buying more of it.

The people asking about ANNKE online tell the same story. On Slickdeals, under listings for ANNKE PoE turret and panoramic cameras, buyers ask whether ANNKE is a reputable company for PoE cameras, whether a camera would work with a Uniview recorder they already own, and whether ANNKE cameras will work with a Lorex recorder. On IPCamTalk, a forum for camera enthusiasts, a thread asks whether ANNKE and Hikvision are the same company and whether the Annke Vision app uses the same cloud servers as Hik-Connect. Those are installation questions disguised as shopping questions. A New Yorker mixing an ANNKE camera into an older Lorex or Uniview system, or relying on a phone app for a storefront, needs someone to test that combination before the camera is drilled into a limestone lintel. That is a large part of what we do.

The renting city adds a third layer. A guide from Connextivity on NYC apartment camera law says Wi-Fi cameras that need no drilling and no permanent wiring generally raise no issue with a landlord or a board, but the building usually gets involved once the install becomes permanent, such as drilling into walls or running cable through the building. In 2012, AOL reported that a tenant at Parkside Houses, a public housing complex in the Bronx, received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing cameras. In the city's 2025 participatory budgeting process, a proposal for Gowanus Houses in Brooklyn asked for security cameras in elevators and near apartment doors to monitor activity and prevent trespassing. People want cameras, and the building decides how they get them. Our job is to find the version of an ANNKE system that the building will accept, whether that is a Wi-Fi camera inside a rented apartment, a wired system for an owner who controls the whole house, or a common-area system designed with the managing agent.

Why the disagreement matters less for a house than for a building with federal ties

For a family in a Staten Island house or a shop owner on Steinway Street, the practical effect of the 2026 notice, as reported, is limited. The FCC language reported by ICTally is aimed at imports and marketing for public safety, government facilities and national security purposes, and the notice says lawfully purchased equipment already in use is not affected. That means an ANNKE system recording the driveway is not suddenly illegal to run. It is different for a building that houses a government tenant, takes part in a publicly funded security program or answers to an agency that has its own purchasing rules. Those customers should ask their agency before buying more ANNKE hardware, and we will happily design the same layout on a different brand if that is the safer route. The cable, the mounts and the recorder location rarely change; only the cameras and recorder on the ends do.

Building a system that outlives any one brand

The way to protect yourself from supply questions is in the wiring, not the logo. A wired PoE system runs one standard Ethernet cable from each camera to a PoE switch or a recorder with PoE ports. If an ANNKE turret fails in five years and the exact model is gone, a different camera can usually go on the same cable and the same mounting box, provided it is compatible with the recorder or the recorder is replaced too. So on every ANNKE install we pull good-quality cable, label both ends, leave a little slack at the camera, and document the run. That way a future change of brand is a camera swap, not a rewiring of a prewar building. We also record the exact model numbers and firmware versions on the handover sheet, because a model number is the first thing anyone will need when the question of parts comes up.

The phone app and the cloud question

Some ANNKE owners worry less about the hardware than about where their video goes. On IPCamTalk, one user asked whether the Annke Vision app and Hik-Connect use the same cloud server. We found no reliable source that answers that, and we will not guess. What we can control is how much the system depends on any cloud at all. According to the ANNKE Help Center, you can view the stream without the internet when the NVR or DVR is connected to a monitor or TV. We set up every system to record locally to the recorder's drive and to show on a local screen, so it keeps working if the internet is down or if you choose not to use remote viewing. If you do want the app, we set strong unique passwords, change default logins, keep firmware current at install, and can put the cameras on their own network segment behind the recorder so they do not talk directly to the internet.

Get the paperwork moving early

The single biggest delay on New York camera jobs is not cable or weather; it is waiting for someone to say yes. If you live in a co-op or condo, ask your managing agent now what the building needs for an alteration of this kind: often a written request, a copy of the contractor's insurance, and a description of where cables will run. If you rent, send your landlord a short note with the camera locations and how they will be fixed. If your building is landmarked, tell us the address when you book, so we can look at it against the LPC rules before we design anything. We would much rather lose a week on approvals than drill a facade that has to be patched later.

The rowhouse type that, per the Architectural League, spread across neighborhoods between the Civil War and World War I. Stoop, garden-level door, rear yard and roof are the usual camera points; brick party walls block Wi-Fi.

Eight- to twelve-story elevator houses of the 1910s and 1920s and larger doorman buildings of the 1920s and 1930s. Longer runs, a lobby desk monitor, and boards or managing agents to satisfy.

Storefronts under apartments, offices above shops and newer commercial buildings with drop ceilings, where conduit, plenum-rated cable and fire-rated penetrations come into the plan.

Brownstones: the stoop, the garden door and the yard

A brownstone camera plan almost always covers the same four places: the stoop and the parlor-floor door, the garden-level entrance under the stoop, the rear yard, and sometimes the roof hatch. The trouble is that those four places sit at opposite ends of a long, narrow house with brick party walls on both sides. A Wi-Fi camera on the rear fence may have three or four masonry walls and a couple of floors between it and a router at the front. We plan these houses as wired PoE systems with the recorder in the cellar or a parlor-floor closet, where it is central. The stoop camera's cable usually comes in through the cellar under the stoop, which keeps the front facade clean. The rear cameras come through a small sleeved hole at the back, sealed against water. In a historic district, we keep the front cameras as small as possible and in places the LPC guidance contemplates, and we look at the rear and side first.

Walk-ups: one camera per landing and a long vertical run

The four- to six-story walk-up is the backbone of much of Manhattan, the Bronx and Brooklyn, and it is a straightforward building to put cameras in once you accept that most of its walls are solid. Owners usually want the front door, the vestibule mailboxes, each stair landing, the cellar door and the rear yard or air shaft. The cable for all of that has to climb the building. Where an old dumbwaiter shaft, a pipe chase or an unused chimney void is available and safe to use, we use it. Where it is not, we run neat surface raceway up the stair hall, painted to match, which is often the honest choice in a building with plaster on solid brick. A mid-run PoE switch on an upper floor can shorten the longest runs. We keep every landing camera aimed at the stairs and the landing as a whole, never at one particular apartment door.

Elevator buildings and co-ops: boards, desks and lobbies

Larger prewar and mid-century buildings bring a lobby desk, a super, a managing agent and, in a co-op or condo, a board. Their camera systems usually cover the lobby, the package or mail area, elevator landings, the laundry room, the garage or courtyard, and the service entrance. The recorder goes in a locked room off the lobby or the super's office, and a monitor at the desk shows live views. Runs can be long, so we measure every one and choose between direct runs to the recorder and a second PoE switch closer to a cluster of cameras. Most of these buildings want cable in conduit through common areas, insurance papers before work, and set hours for drilling. We send what the managing agent asks for and work inside the building's rules, because a board that trusts the contractor is a board that approves the next job.

Houses in Queens and on Staten Island

The city's newer housing, with a median construction year of 1970 on Staten Island and 1935 in Queens, is often the easiest place to install ANNKE PoE cameras. Wood-frame attics let us drop cables down to the eaves, where cameras can cover the driveway, the front walk, the side yard and the back. Basements let us run along joists to the rear. Many of these houses are detached or semi-detached, so there is no party wall and no shared hallway to negotiate. The challenges are different: long driveways that need a camera with enough resolution to see a face at distance, garages detached from the house, and two-family houses where the cable for the upstairs tenant's door camera has to pass through the downstairs apartment. We sort out who controls which space before we design the runs.

Masonry, sleeves and sealing

Wherever a cable has to pass through an outside brick or block wall, we drill with a masonry bit or a core bit sized for the cable or conduit, slope the hole slightly downward to the outside so water runs away, sleeve it so the cable is not chafed by brick, and seal it on both sides. The ANNKE camera's base or a junction box covers the outside end, so the hole is not visible. Inside, we fire-stop any penetration through a rated wall or floor with materials suited to it. In old brick, we place anchors in the face of the brick rather than in the mortar joint wherever the mount pattern allows, because joints hold less and crumble sooner. These are small details, but they are what keep a camera from loosening, leaking or letting water into a century-old wall.

What a store or restaurant camera has to do

A home camera mostly watches doors and yards. A business camera has to answer specific questions after something goes wrong: who took the money from the register, when the delivery actually arrived, whether the slip-and-fall happened the way the customer says, who opened the back door after closing. That changes the design. A register camera needs to be close and overhead enough to see hands and the drawer. A door camera needs to catch faces at the threshold, not the tops of heads. A sidewalk camera should see the approach but, for the owner's own protection, should cover the business's frontage rather than the apartment windows across the street. ANNKE PoE turrets and domes suit most indoor positions; panoramic dual-lens models can cover a wide shop floor from one point. We design commercial systems camera by camera with the owner, test each view with the owner at the screen, and keep recording on an on-site NVR so footage is there when the police or the insurer asks for it.

Rules and practical limits for business camera systems

  • State licensing. The New York low-voltage roadmap notes that burglar and fire alarm, CCTV and access control work requires an Article 6-D license. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • City permits for low-voltage wiring. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, in effect since December 21, 2025 under Local Law 128 of 2024, says an electrical permit is not required for wiring below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. One trade blog states that low-voltage work requires DOB permits; because the sources conflict, we check the specific job against the current code before work begins.
  • Conduit, plenum cable and fire-rated penetrations. One NYC installer writes that DOB regulations often mandate surface-mounted EMT conduit, fire-rated penetrations and plenum-rated cabling in commercial work. We treat that as a practical checklist: plenum-rated cable above return-air ceilings, fire-stopping through rated walls, and conduit wherever the landlord or the space calls for it.
  • New outlets are an electrician's job. PoE cameras take power from the switch or recorder, which avoids new outlets at each camera. Where a business wants a new circuit for the recorder or a rack, that is for a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • NYPD camera sharing. NYC 311 describes a safety initiative that lets any business with a security camera share live video feeds with the NYPD during emergencies, with cameras registered at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registration is the business's choice; we can make sure the system's network setup is ready if you decide to.
  • Buildings with government tenants or public-safety roles. Given the FCC's July 2026 notice on covered equipment for public safety and government facility uses, we talk through brand choice before proposing ANNKE hardware for those properties.
  • Working hours. Drilling and ceiling work in a shop is planned for hours when the store is closed or quiet, and for apartment buildings above, at times that will not wake the neighbors.

Placement and coverage

Which ANNKE cameras can face New York weather, and which belong indoors?

Outdoor positions need a camera built for outdoor use, sealed at the cable entry and mounted so rain runs off rather than into the junction. In New York that means the stoop, the rear yard, the roofline, the driveway and the storefront canopy, all of which see winter wind and summer sun. Indoor-only models belong in lobbies, hallways, stockrooms and living spaces. The ANNKE Help Center recommends mounting cameras more than 2 meters high, and we follow that wherever the layout allows, high enough to be hard to reach and low enough to see faces. We also avoid aiming outdoor cameras straight into low winter sun or a streetlight, which blinds the picture at the hours that matter most.

How far can an ANNKE camera sit from the recorder in a long city building?

For wired PoE cameras, the standard limit for one Ethernet run is about 100 meters, or 328 feet, between the camera and the switch or recorder port. Kits often ship with shorter cables; one install guide notes ANNKE kits include 60-foot PoE cables and that some locations need longer runs. In a brownstone, the run from a cellar recorder to the rear fence can approach that 60-foot length on its own once it climbs and turns. Where a run is longer than the standard allows, we add a PoE switch closer to the cameras. For Wi-Fi models, distance matters less than walls: brick and stone between the camera and the router can cut the signal to nothing in a short distance.

Can you put ANNKE cameras on a brownstone, a limestone front or a landmarked building?

Yes, with care for both the stone and the rules. On an ordinary block, we fix to brick or stone with anchors suited to the material, keep cable inside the building and seal every penetration. On a landmarked building or in a historic district, LPC rules would prohibit many security cameras on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and the Permit Guidebook's Chapter 11 sets out the criteria. Our Installation Process

Every ANNKE job in the city follows the same eight steps, whether it is two Wi-Fi cameras in a rented one-bedroom or thirty PoE cameras across a co-op's lobby, garage and landings. The order matters most in old buildings, where a cable run planned too quickly becomes a cracked ceiling or a wire in the wrong apartment.

1. Send the address, building type and existing gear

Borough and street, the kind of building, how many cameras and where, and whether you already own an ANNKE kit, recorder or older DVR. Photos of the cellar or closet help.

2. Check the building and its rules

Who owns the walls and the facade, landmark status, board paperwork, landlord approval, and whether any hallway or common area is involved.

3. Bench-test the ANNKE hardware

Every camera, the recorder and the drive powered up and checked, with model numbers and firmware recorded, before anything is mounted.

4. Plan cable paths and the recorder spot

A walk through the building to find chases, closets, ceilings and basements, every run measured, and a cool, secure place chosen for the recorder.

5. Pull, terminate and certify the cable

Cable pulled through the agreed paths, sleeved and sealed through masonry, terminated, labelled at both ends and tested before any camera goes up.

6. Mount and aim each camera

Fixed more than two meters up where the layout allows, sealed outdoors, and aimed with you watching the live picture on a screen.

7. Configure recording and viewing

Schedules, motion zones, time and date, strong passwords, fixed network addresses, phone viewing and a local monitor that works without the internet.

8. Walk through and hand over

Playback and export shown to you, the app set up on your phone, a labelled cable map and the recorder login left with the owner, and the site cleaned up.

Why the bench test comes before the ladder

It is tempting to mount first and test later, and on a suburban house with a short ladder it might not matter. In New York, the camera might be on a third-story cornice, over a storefront canopy on a busy sidewalk, or above a stair landing that tenants use all day. Finding a dead camera after it is mounted means another ladder setup, another hour of a blocked stair hall and, sometimes, another visit. So every ANNKE unit is powered and checked on the bench, every cable is certified before it is connected, and only proven parts go up.

Why we set fixed network addresses

Network cameras need an address on your network. Left to themselves, they take whatever address the router hands out, and after a power cut or a router replacement those addresses can change, which can leave a recorder looking for cameras that have moved. Setting fixed addresses during configuration, and writing them on the handover sheet, prevents a whole class of "cameras offline" calls after the next storm or the next internet provider switch.

FAQ

NYC ANNKE Camera Installation FAQ

If the internet goes down in our building, can we still watch the ANNKE cameras?
+

Yes, on site. The ANNKE Help Center says you can view the stream without the internet when the NVR or DVR is connected to a monitor or TV. Recording to the recorder's drive also continues without the internet. What stops is viewing on your phone from outside the building, which returns when the connection does. We connect a local monitor on every system for this reason.

Do the Annke Vision app and Hik-Connect use the same cloud servers?
+

We have not found a reliable source that answers that, and we will not guess. The question was raised on the IPCamTalk forum without a confirmed answer. What we can do is set your system up so it does not depend on any cloud: recording stays on your recorder, a local monitor shows the cameras, and remote viewing is optional, with strong unique passwords if you use it.

Is ANNKE a trustworthy brand for wired PoE cameras?
+

Buyers ask this on Slickdeals, and the fair answer has two parts. The cameras are widely sold and many owners use them for years, but the brand's background is debated, with IPVM listing it as selling Hikvision-made hardware and another database saying it is not on the FCC Covered List. We bench-test every unit before mounting and wire systems so another brand's camera can replace one later if needed.

Will an ANNKE camera record on the Lorex recorder we already have?
+

Sometimes, but we never promise it in advance. Mixed-brand setups can pass basic video through common network-camera standards, while features such as smart detection often work only on a matching recorder. We connect your Lorex recorder and the ANNKE camera on the bench, check live view, recording and playback, and tell you what works before anything is mounted.

Can a regular ANNKE camera go outside my apartment door instead of a video doorbell?
+

Physically yes, but in an apartment building the outside of your door faces a hallway, which sources describe as a common area that typically needs building approval in a co-op or condo. A video doorbell raises the same approval question. If the building agrees in writing, we mount a small camera on the door frame with a neat cable path; if not, an indoor camera watching your entry from inside is the usual answer.

As a landlord, do I have to tell tenants about cameras in the lobby and halls?
+

Security-company sources we found raise this question but disagree on a city notice rule, and we could not confirm one from an official source, so check with your attorney. Practically, visible cameras and clear signs at entrances avoid disputes, and cameras should cover shared spaces, never inside units. Tenants can raise building surveillance complaints with HPD or 311, so a clear, documented layout protects the owner.

Am I allowed to put cameras on the outside of my own house in New York?
+

Generally yes, on your own property and aimed at your own entries, yard and driveway. Two limits matter most here: in a historic district or on a landmark, LPC rules can restrict cameras on a primary facade, and covert recording of private spaces can be a crime under Penal Law 250.45. We aim cameras at your property rather than into neighbors' windows. This is not legal advice.

We have a working ANNKE system. How long does it take to add one more camera?
+

Usually far less than a full install, because the recorder, network and app are already set up. The time goes into the new cable run, which depends on the path through the building, and the mount. We check that the recorder has a free channel and enough drive space, then label the new cable to match the existing ones and add it to your cable map.

Why won't my ANNKE Wi-Fi camera see our 5 GHz network?
+

A third-party setup guide states that ANNKE cameras support only 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi bands, not 5 GHz. Many New York routers broadcast both under one name, which can confuse setup. We make sure a 2.4 GHz network is available to the camera, place the camera where that signal is strong, and recommend wired PoE where brick walls make the signal unreliable.

Can our store share its ANNKE camera feeds with the NYPD?
+

NYC 311 describes a safety initiative that lets any business with a security camera share live video feeds with the NYPD during emergencies, with registration at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Joining is the business's decision. If you choose to, we can make sure the recorder and network are configured properly and that the cameras cover the frontage you want to share.

Can new ANNKE cameras use the coax cable from our old DVR system?
+

It depends on which ANNKE system you choose. ANNKE sells DVR kits, which work over coax, and we can reuse sound coax runs with a DVR kit. Its PoE network cameras need Ethernet cable instead, so a PoE upgrade means pulling new cable. We test the old coax first; if it is damaged or the runs are poorly routed, new Ethernet is usually the better long-term choice.

How high should ANNKE cameras be mounted on a stoop or a storefront?
+

The ANNKE Help Center recommends an installation height of more than 2 meters, about six and a half feet. That keeps cameras out of easy reach on a busy sidewalk or a stoop. We balance height against angle, because a camera mounted too high sees only the tops of heads, and we confirm each view on screen with you before tightening the mount.

Where can a tenant complain about building cameras they think are misused?
+

Tenant-rights sources note that complaints about building security and surveillance can be made through HPD or 311. A tenant can also raise concerns directly with the landlord or managing agent first. For a legal dispute about a specific camera, a tenant attorney or legal aid organization is the right place to go.
