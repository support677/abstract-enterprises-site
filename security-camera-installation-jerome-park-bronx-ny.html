The Neighborhood

Why Jerome Park Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Jerome Park is a neighborhood shaped by a reservoir, and the reservoir shapes the camera work. CityNeighborhoods draws its edges at Mosholu Parkway to the northeast, the Grand Concourse to the east, Bedford Park Boulevard to the south and the Jerome Park Reservoir to the west. Other sources draw it more loosely: Wikipedia places Jerome Park in Community District 7 with Bedford Park, Fordham, Kingsbridge Heights and Norwood, NYC Parks files the park itself under Community Board 8, and some listings fold in the co-op buildings on the reservoir's western side along Sedgwick Avenue. Whichever map you use, the middle of the neighborhood is water, or right now the lack of it. Norwood News reported that DEP expects the reservoir to stay dry until 2028. Around it run Goulden Avenue, Sedgwick Avenue and a 1.5-mile paved path that links to the Mosholu Parkway greenway, and along its eastern edge stand some of the biggest public high schools in the city and Lehman College. The result is a strange mix for a camera installer: blocks that fill with thousands of students in the afternoon and then go completely still after dark, with a long, unlit stretch of fence and basin across the street from many front doors.

The history explains the buildings. NYC Parks records that Leonard Jerome, working with his brothers and the financier August Belmont, opened the Jerome Park Racetrack on September 25, 1866, and the Belmont Stakes ran there from 1867 until 1890. Sources differ on when the track ended: NYC Parks says the city condemned the property in 1890, while Wikipedia gives 1894 for the demolition. Either way the track became the reservoir, finished in 1906, and the Italian and Irish workers who built it settled nearby. The Grand Concourse was completed in 1914, the Jerome Avenue subway extension followed in 1917, and the Mosholu Parkway station opened on April 15, 1918. CityNeighborhoods describes the result as a wave of five- and six-story apartment buildings in Art Deco and Tudor Revival styles. That means most of the apartment stock here is now close to a hundred years old, with plaster over brick in the halls, cellars that hold the meters and the boiler, and cable from several generations of intercoms, phones and television tucked along the cellar ceiling. A camera plan here starts by finding out what is already in the walls.

Homes.com says co-ops make up most of the neighborhood's housing, alongside a few stately rowhouses and Italian Renaissance brownstones, and that on Villa Avenue many of the original homes gave way to postwar tenement buildings, with a few slatted Italianate rowhouses still standing. Metropolis Moving notes pre-war courtyard buildings and a small number of duplexes and single-family homes among the six-story apartment houses. Then there are the big cooperatives. Wikipedia describes the Park Reservoir cooperative, spread across three buildings west of the reservoir, as New York's first Mitchell-Lama cooperative; Apartments.com lists Amalgamated Co-op Towers at 3965–3977 Sedgwick Avenue under Jerome Park; and Tracey Towers, the Paul Rudolph co-op at 20 and 40 West Mosholu Parkway, sits over the Jerome Yard. Homes.com puts renters at 87.8 percent of residents, so even in a co-op neighborhood most people live under someone else's decisions about the common areas. Listings already mention cameras in some buildings: a 1937 co-op at 340 East Mosholu Parkway South, six stories and 56 units, advertises a video intercom and cameras watching the entrance and common areas, and a 1939 Art Deco co-op at 190 East Mosholu Parkway South lists video cameras among its security features. Systems like those age, change hands and eventually need someone to take them over.

New buildings keep arriving on the side streets. New York YIMBY's Jerome Park coverage lists permit filings for a six-story building at 3377 Sedgwick Avenue with 30 residences, an eight-story building at 233 East 202nd Street, 22 residences at 234 East 203rd Street and affordable senior housing at 2850 Webster Avenue. Norwood News reported a nine-story, 62-residence filing at 2895 Valentine Avenue, although that filing is several years old and we have not checked where it stands. Each of those buildings will open with entry hardware and, very often, a camera system that a managing agent inherits from the builder. The best time to get the pathways right for cameras is before the drywall closes; the second-best time is the first month after the building opens, before the first incident shows nobody knows the recorder's password.

Local reporting shows where the trouble lands. News 12 Bronx reported that around 3:30 a.m. on May 31, a business at 74 East 205th Street, Goals Plastic Surgery, was burglarized and a safe holding $85,000 in cash was taken, and that investigators released surveillance images and video afterward. News 12 also reported a bodega robbery on Villa Avenue around 3 a.m. on April 22, noting that NYPD data showed robberies in the 52nd Precinct up nearly 9 percent so far that year, and surveillance video captured a violent fight outside a deli on Villa Avenue. CBS New York reported a robbery suspect seen on surveillance video entering an apartment building on Bainbridge Avenue after apparently following a woman who lived there, and News 12 caught a package thief on camera taking deliveries from a Bedford Park building. A few blocks north, Norwood News reported a burglary at Our Lady of Angels Church in Kingsbridge Heights on May 24, 2026, with police releasing video. Several of these reports call the location Bedford Park or Kingsbridge Heights, and the line between those neighborhoods and Jerome Park depends on whose map you read. We are not ranking any block. What the reports have in common is the hour, very late at night, and the places: a shop door, an office, a lobby door that closed behind the wrong person. Those are exactly the views a camera plan has to get right.

Rules decide where the cameras can go and what else the building owes its residents. The state Attorney General's tenants' rights guide explains that under Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a, buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments need a two-way voice intercom; older buildings need them if a majority of apartments ask. The same guide says entrances, stairways and yards must be lit from sunset to sunrise. HPD's proactive self-closing door program issued 3,151 violations in fiscal year 2024 and 2,937 in fiscal year 2025, so nothing we install ever hangs on a door leaf, a closer or a latch. For cameras themselves there is no general city mandate, so the choice belongs to the owner or the board. Connextivity points out that in a co-op the hallway is a common area under the board's control, so a shareholder cannot put hardware there alone, and that hallway cameras should cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments when doors open. The same source notes that a hidden camera inside a unit can support a felony charge under Penal Law 250.45. We never put cameras inside apartments we do not occupy, we aim corridor views along the hall rather than at doors, and we leave audio recording switched off.

One more neighbor deserves a sentence of its own. DEP completed a $15.7 million project at the reservoir to rehabilitate gatehouses, put up new fencing and upgrade security infrastructure, including new security cameras and traffic-control bollards. Those cameras protect a city water-supply facility. They are not aimed at your lobby, and nobody in the neighborhood can call them up after a break-in on their block. A building that faces the reservoir still needs its own record of who used its doors, and that record should stay on the building's own property.

Jerome Park facts that shape a camera plan

  • A quiet western edge: the reservoir, kept dry until 2028 according to DEP, leaves many Goulden and Sedgwick Avenue fronts facing a fence and an empty basin, with little passing light or foot traffic at night.
  • Century-old apartment houses: the 1917 subway extension set off a wave of five- and six-story Art Deco and Tudor Revival buildings, so plaster stair halls and crowded cellar ceilings are the norm.
  • A co-op neighborhood of renters: co-ops hold most of the housing, yet 87.8 percent of residents rent, so boards and owners, not residents, decide almost every common-area camera.
  • Large cooperatives: the Park Reservoir cooperative, New York's first Mitchell-Lama co-op, spans three buildings, and towers such as Amalgamated Co-op Towers and Tracey Towers rise far above the walk-ups.
  • Late-night incidents: the reported East 205th Street burglary happened around 3:30 a.m. and the Villa Avenue bodega robbery around 3 a.m., the hours when a camera has only artificial light to work with.
  • A lighting rule already on the books: the state requires entrances, stairways and yards of multiple dwellings to be lit from sunset to sunrise, and that light is what a good night picture depends on.

What Jerome Park buildings tend to add next. A board that puts cameras on the lobby and cellar doors often comes back asking whether the same recorder can show the intercom's door panel, or whether the lobby door could move to fobs. Shop owners on Villa Avenue and Bedford Park Boulevard usually want a burglar alarm on the back door once the cameras are up. We leave a spare cable and an open, labeled port at the recorder so the next system does not mean opening the cellar ceiling again.

Light, Glare and the Late Hours: Planning Jerome Park Cameras for the Dark

Almost every incident in the local reporting above happened when the street was empty and the only light came from a fixture over a door. That is the test a camera in this neighborhood has to pass. A daytime picture of a Jerome Park lobby is easy; the sun does the work. The night picture depends on things a camera catalog never mentions: whether the vestibule light is on a working timer, whether the fixture over the street door shines down on faces or straight into the lens, whether the cellar stair has a bulb at all, and whether the camera will be staring across Goulden Avenue at a dark basin that gives it nothing to focus on. We plan each position around what it will see at night, then confirm it after sunset before the job is finished. The table shows how that plays out at the spots we expect to cover most.

The law already asks for the light, and the camera needs it too

The Attorney General's tenants' rights guide states that entrances, stairways and yards of multiple dwellings must be lit from sunset to sunrise. That requirement exists for people, but it also decides how good a camera's night picture will be. A camera working under a lit fixture can show color, clothing and a face; a camera relying only on its own infrared shows a gray image that gets grainier the farther the subject is from the lens. So during the survey we write down every fixture that each camera depends on, and when one is missing or dead, we tell the owner plainly. We do not sell a camera as a fix for a light the building should already have. In practice, a working bulb above the cellar door and a camera beside it will do more than the most expensive camera in the dark.

Infrared, color and the dark across the street

Many cameras switch to black-and-white infrared when the light drops, and that switch behaves badly in two common Jerome Park situations. The first is a front that faces the reservoir side. With almost nothing lit across the street, the camera decides it is night, turns on its infrared, and tries to light up an empty fence line it cannot reach, which floods the picture with noise and makes the steps right in front of it look worse. We narrow those views to the building's own entrance and steps and mask the far side, so the camera spends its effort where people actually walk. The second is a lobby full of polished stone, brass and mirrors, the kind these prewar buildings are known for. Infrared bounces straight back off those surfaces. There we often turn infrared off and let the lobby's own lighting carry the picture, which keeps it in color. Every one of these settings gets checked after dark on the day of the install, not guessed at.

Which hours deserve an alert

A camera on a Goulden Avenue building that sends an alert every time a student walks past in the afternoon will be muted within a week, and a muted camera might as well be off. We set alerts by door and by hour. The cellar door, the roof door, an office's side door and a shop's back door alert during the hours they should be quiet, and they alert the person who can actually do something: the super who lives on site, the managing agent's after-hours line, or the shop owner. The main street door and the lobby record around the clock without pinging anyone's phone. Recording is continuous on every camera, so when someone asks about a night three days ago, the footage is there whether or not an alert went off. The late-night timing in the local reports is the reason we treat the hours between midnight and dawn as the ones the system exists for.

Prewar co-ops: the cellar does the heavy lifting

In a five- or six-story Art Deco or Tudor Revival co-op, the cellar is almost always the place where the job is won or lost. It holds the electric meters, the internet provider's equipment and the boiler, and it sits directly under the lobby, which makes the lobby and vestibule cameras the easiest part of the work: a short run up through the cellar ceiling. The harder part is the stair hall. These halls are usually plaster on brick, and boards rightly do not want them opened. Before suggesting raceway, we look for the routes the building already has: a pipe chase that runs from the cellar to the roof, a stack of closets one above another, a dumbwaiter or service space that was closed off long ago, or a run beside existing risers with room to spare. Only when none of those exists do we propose slim raceway, painted to match, along the edge of the stair where it reads as part of the building. The recorder goes in a locked, dry spot in the cellar away from the boiler's heat and away from the cellar door, so whoever comes in that way cannot simply walk off with the footage. Each floor the cable passes through gets rated firestop.

Courtyard buildings: cover the way in, not the windows

Courtyard buildings bring a different problem. Everyone passes through the gate or archway from the sidewalk, and then splits off toward several doors around the court. The gate or archway is the single place where every person can be seen, so that camera is framed tight and at a height that shows faces. Each door off the court gets its own entry view, mounted inside the vestibule where the light is better. The court itself, if the owner wants it covered at all, is watched as an overview of the paths and the gate, with privacy masking over every window that faces the court, because a court is ringed by kitchens and bedrooms. Cable usually travels through the cellar, which in many courtyard buildings runs under all the sections. We write down which entrance each camera covers, by the street name and the door's position around the court, so nobody has to guess on a busy night.

Mitchell-Lama towers and multi-building co-ops: risers and distance

The big cooperatives are a different scale. A tower such as Amalgamated Co-op Towers or Tracey Towers, or a complex spread across separate buildings like the Park Reservoir cooperative, can put cameras hundreds of feet of cable away from a single recorder. The Paul Rudolph Institute notes that Tracey Towers' two towers have entrances at different elevations because one stands over the yard, which is a reminder that big complexes rarely have one simple front door. In buildings like these, camera cable goes up the telecom riser or another approved chase, never into an elevator hoistway, and small network switches go on intermediate floors so every run stays within what the cable can carry. Separate buildings are joined with links between their network rooms rather than one enormous run. We also keep the camera network apart from the building's office computers and any resident services that share the same internet line. Large cooperatives usually have a management office, a security desk or both, so we plan who sees what live, who can export a clip, and who holds the administrator account before anything is mounted.

Villa Avenue walk-ups, rowhouses and new buildings

On Villa Avenue, the postwar tenement buildings and the few Italianate rowhouses left between them are smaller jobs with tighter quarters. A walk-up with a store on the ground floor needs two separate systems: the residential one, covering the street door, stairs and cellar, and the store's own, covering its door and counter, so neither the tenants nor the shopkeeper can see the other's footage. Where there is no interior path upstairs, we look at the rear wall or a shaft for a covered outdoor run before opening any plaster. A rowhouse usually needs a camera at the front door framed for faces at the foot of the stoop, a view of the side passage if there is one, and the yard, with every neighbor's window a few feet away kept out of the frame. New buildings are the opposite case: the pathways are often already there, and the job is about ownership. We check that the builder's system is in the owner's name, that its views match how the building is actually used, and that the recorder can be updated and serviced by whoever comes next.

An office that is empty at 3:30 in the morning

The East 205th Street report shows the pattern that worries office owners most: a business closed for the night, nobody nearby, and a safe or locked cabinet that someone came specifically to take. Investigators later released surveillance images, which tells you footage existed, but the useful question for any office is whether its cameras would show a face, a direction of travel and the moment of entry. A plan for an office like that starts at every way in: the street door, any side or service door, and windows reachable from a fire escape or a low roof. Each gets a camera inside, set back so a person coming through walks toward it. The room with the safe gets a camera that shows the safe and the doorway together, so the footage ties a person to the act. The recorder does not go in that room; it goes somewhere else, locked, with a copy of key clips sent off site if the internet allows. Alerts run only during closed hours, to the owner or manager rather than a phone that stays on silent in a drawer.

Counters that stay open late on Villa Avenue

A deli or bodega that serves customers into the small hours is a different job. The clerk is often alone, behind a counter, with the door a few steps away. The most important camera is the one just inside the door at head height, framed so every customer walks into a clear face shot as they enter. The second sits above and slightly in front of the counter, showing the customer, the clerk's hands and the register. A third covers the aisles from the back corner, and a fourth covers the stock room or rear door. A monitor near the register, turned so the clerk can see the door and aisles without turning away from a customer, helps someone working alone notice trouble starting. Because these stores sit under apartments, the outside view of the sidewalk and gate needs the landlord's written agreement, and the store's recorder stays separate from the building's.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Jerome Park

  • Private rooms stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, exam or treatment rooms and any space where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we check for mirrors that might show one.
  • The building owner controls the outside. The facade, sign area, sidewalk canopy and shared halls belong to the landlord or the co-op, so cameras there need written agreement first.
  • Self-closing doors stay clear. No camera, bracket or cable is fixed to a door, closer or frame in a way that could stop it latching.
  • Rated walls stay rated. Every opening through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Audio stays off. Business cameras are set to record video only.
  • The installer should be licensed. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords, co-op boards and insurers usually ask to see before work starts.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Jerome Park: Questions Answered

Co-ops, owners and the rules

Our building is a Mitchell-Lama cooperative. Does anyone besides our board have to approve a camera project?

The board still decides on cameras in the common areas, the same as in any co-op. A Mitchell-Lama company also answers to the housing agency that supervises it, and larger capital projects or contracts can need that agency's review. Rules vary by company and agreement, so the board or managing agent should check with its supervising agency before signing. We give the board a clear written scope, item list and price so that review, if needed, has everything in one document.

Some apartments in our co-op are still held by renters who never bought their shares. Do they have a say in where lobby and hallway cameras go?

The board controls the common areas, so it makes the decision, but those residents live there under their own leases and deserve the same care as shareholders. We aim every hallway view along the corridor so no camera sees into any apartment when a door opens, whoever lives behind it. A short written notice to all residents saying what is recorded, that there are no cameras inside homes and no audio, and who can view footage, avoids most disagreements before they start.

State law lets tenants in an older building ask for an intercom and lets the owner recover the cost. Does the same work for cameras?

No. The Multiple Dwelling Law rule the Attorney General describes covers self-locking doors and a two-way intercom: required in newer buildings with eight or more apartments, and in pre-1968 buildings when a majority of apartments ask, with the owner allowed to recover that cost. Cameras are not part of it, so tenants cannot use that rule to require them. Whether any camera cost can be passed on depends on rent rules we cannot answer for, so ask the state housing agency or a lawyer.

The light over our cellar door has been out for weeks. Can an infrared camera stand in for it until it is fixed?

Only partly, and it should not be treated as a replacement. The state guide says entrances, stairways and yards must be lit from sunset to sunrise, so the light is the owner's duty whether or not there is a camera. An infrared camera will show a gray image of someone near the door, but faces, clothing colors and anything a few feet away get much weaker. Fix the light first; the camera beside it will then record a far more useful picture.

Inside the building

Our elevator building has two separate stairways. Does each one need cameras, or is one enough?

If both stairs connect the lobby or cellar to the floors, both are ways in and out, and leaving one dark means anyone who wants to avoid a camera just uses that one. The usual answer is a camera where each stair meets the lobby or the cellar, and a camera at the top of each stair near the roof door. Landing cameras on every floor can come later. That gives the board a record of every route through the building for a modest number of cameras.

Our stair doors have small wire-glass windows. Can a camera in the hallway look through the glass into the stair?

Not well. Wire glass is small, often scratched, and the wire mesh crosses the picture, and at night a camera's infrared reflects off the glass and blinds the view. A camera that needs to see the stair belongs on the stair side, on the wall or ceiling of the landing, never on the door itself. A hallway camera can still show the stair door from the corridor side, which records who goes in and out.

There is no chase from our cellar to the upper floors. Can camera cable run up the outside of the building instead?

Often, yes, on the rear or a side wall that faces a yard or a shaft rather than the street. Cable rated for outdoor use goes in a covered raceway or conduit fixed to the masonry, enters through small sealed openings at each floor, and is painted to match where that helps. On a co-op we need the board's written approval, since the facade is a common element. A rear-wall run usually avoids opening plaster in a finished stair hall, which is why boards often prefer it.

Out front

Our building is on the walk between the subway and the schools by the reservoir, and the sidewalk fills with students every afternoon. Should the entry camera record everyone going by?

No. The camera should be framed on your entrance, steps and the stretch of sidewalk directly in front, not on the whole crowd. A tight frame gives bigger, clearer faces of the people who actually use your door, and masking the far edge keeps passersby out of most of the picture. Alerts on that camera stay off during the day, because a stream of students would trigger them constantly; the camera still records continuously.

Our front faces the reservoir. Should our camera take in the fence, the gatehouses or the path on the other side?

We advise against it. The reservoir is a city water-supply facility with its own security, and DEP has added fencing, bollards and cameras there; tour notices have even asked visitors not to bring cameras. Your camera has no need to record it, and pointing at a dark basin also makes the night picture worse. We frame the view on your own frontage and mask out everything beyond the near sidewalk.

Shops and offices

Our deli on Villa Avenue stays open overnight with one clerk on shift. Where should the cameras go to watch over the person working alone?

Start with a camera just inside the door at head height, so every customer is recorded face first as they come in, and a camera above and in front of the counter showing the customer, the clerk and the register. Add a monitor beside the register showing the door and the aisles, so the clerk can see someone lingering without walking out from behind the counter. Keep the recorder locked away from the counter so it cannot be taken in a robbery.

Our store's stock room is reached through metal cellar doors in the sidewalk. Can a camera cover those doors?

Yes, usually from two directions. Outside, a camera on the storefront, mounted with the landlord's agreement, can look down at the sidewalk doors and show who opens them and when. Inside, a camera at the bottom of the cellar stair shows anyone coming down. The cable near that opening goes in metal conduit so nobody can cut it on the way in. Alerts can run only during closed hours, when nobody should be using the hatch.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Jerome Park

Our build environment could not display Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask results for Jerome Park, so we are not quoting them. Below are claims that turn up again and again in quick online answers, installer blogs and cost guides about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about this neighborhood. Cost pages from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers. They give a rough idea of scale for New York, but none of them has stood in a Jerome Park vestibule after dark.

"Jerome Park is the park around the reservoir."

Search for Jerome Park and many answers mean the park: the strip of green NYC Parks acquired in 1895 and opened in 1940, along Goulden Avenue between West 205th Street and Sedgwick Avenue, surrounding the reservoir.

The neighborhood is a residential area of apartment houses, co-ops and shops. CityNeighborhoods bounds it by Mosholu Parkway, the Grand Concourse, Bedford Park Boulevard and the reservoir, while other sources put it in Community District 7 or include buildings west of the water. The edges are genuinely disputed.

For camera work the difference matters. A Jerome Park job is a lobby, a stair, a cellar or a deli counter, not a park path, and when an answer lumps the two together it misses the buildings people actually live in.

"Residential buildings with common-area cameras must post notice under Local Law 50."

One vendor blog states that New York City Local Law 50 requires residential buildings with security cameras in common areas to notify residents. Another site says signage is not always required by statute. The sources disagree.

We could not confirm the Local Law 50 claim from any city source, so we do not tell Jerome Park boards that a specific sign is legally required. That would be inventing a rule.

What we do recommend costs nothing: a short, honest notice in the lobby saying which common areas are recorded, that there is no audio and no camera in any home, and who can see the footage. Boards that post one hear far fewer complaints.

"Wired security camera installation in NYC requires a licensed electrician."

At least one installer's cost blog says New York City requires licensed electricians for wired camera installation, and quick answers repeat it.

Camera cable carrying low voltage from a recorder or a PoE switch is not the same as new electrical wiring. New York licenses businesses that install and service security systems through the Department of State, which is the license AESS holds under NYS license #12000287431.

Where a Jerome Park cellar has no outlet near the best recorder spot, a new outlet or circuit is electrical work, and our quote says so on its own line. The right question to ask any camera installer is whether they hold the state security license and insurance.

"The reservoir's new security cameras keep the surrounding blocks safe."

DEP did complete a $15.7 million upgrade at the Jerome Park Reservoir that included new security cameras, fencing and bollards, and that news gets folded into answers about neighborhood safety.

Those cameras protect gatehouses and a water-supply facility. They are not aimed at apartment lobbies, store doors or the late-night corners where the reported robberies and burglaries happened, and residents have no way to pull footage from them.

A building facing the reservoir has the same needs as one a few blocks east: its own record of who came through its own doors. We plan that coverage on the building's property and leave the reservoir to DEP.

"Prewar buildings can't take wired cameras without tearing open the walls."

Quick answers often tell owners of older buildings to skip wired systems and use wireless cameras, on the grounds that plaster walls make cable impossible.

Jerome Park's Art Deco and Tudor Revival buildings have cellars directly under their lobbies, pipe chases, stacked closets and rear walls that can carry cable. Most lobby and vestibule cameras need nothing more than a short run up from the cellar ceiling.

Wireless cameras, meanwhile, struggle through thick masonry and still need power. Wiring takes more planning in an old building, which is why we trace and map first, but it is rarely the wall-wrecking job these answers describe.

"If the building has the required intercom, its entry security is covered."

Answers about NYC building rules sometimes treat the intercom requirement as the whole of entry security. The Multiple Dwelling Law, as the Attorney General summarizes it, asks for self-closing, self-locking doors and, in many buildings, a two-way intercom. It says nothing about cameras.

An intercom lets a resident decide whom to buzz in. It does not record the person who walks in behind a resident, as CBS New York reported happened in a building on Bainbridge Avenue, and it does not see the cellar door or the roof stair.

A compliant intercom and a working door are the foundation. Cameras are a separate decision for the board or owner, and the record they keep covers what the intercom never sees.

"Anything in a common hallway can be recorded."

Many answers say that because hallways are common areas, a landlord or board may point cameras anywhere in them.

Connextivity's summary of NYC rules is more careful: hallway cameras are fine when they cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open, and a hidden camera inside a unit can support a felony charge. Common areas are the board's to cover, but not without limits.

So in Jerome Park hallways we aim along the corridor, mount where an open apartment door does not fall in the frame, mask any edge that still catches a doorway, and leave audio off. That protects residents and keeps the footage usable if anyone ever questions it.

FAQ

Jerome Park Security Camera Installation FAQ

In senior housing, can a camera alert staff when a resident goes out the front door late at night?
+

A camera at the front door can be set to send an alert to the front desk or the staff on duty whenever someone crosses the doorway during set overnight hours. It cannot tell staff who the person is without face-matching software, which brings its own rules and is not something we recommend lightly. For residents who may wander, a camera alert is a helpful extra pair of eyes, but the building should also use a system designed for that purpose and its own care procedures.

Why is the labor on a camera quote for a prewar building often more than the cameras themselves?
+

Because most of the work is the path, not the camera. In a building from the 1920s or 1930s, the time goes into tracing old cable, finding a chase or a rear-wall route, drilling through masonry, sealing each opening with rated firestop, checking every view after dark and setting up the accounts. The camera itself takes minutes to mount. A written quote shows each of those tasks on its own line, so the board can see exactly where the time goes.

Can residents see the lobby camera from their apartments?
+

If the board or owner decides to share it, yes. The usual way today is a view-only account or shared link for the lobby or street-door camera, opened on residents' phones, with no access to any other camera and no ability to delete or export. Showing the lobby on residents' televisions is possible in some buildings but takes more equipment. The decision on who sees what belongs to the board or owner, and we set it up exactly as they choose.

Our deli has a cash machine near the door. Should a camera watch it?
+

A camera that shows the person standing at the machine, from slightly above and to the side, is worth having, because it ties a face to the time of each use and to anyone tampering with it. It should never be aimed so it can read the keypad, so customers' PINs are never recorded. If the machine belongs to an outside operator, check whether they have their own requirements before anything is mounted on or near it.

Turret or dome: which works better in a prewar lobby with a plaster ceiling?
+

Indoors, either can work. A dome is harder to knock out of aim and looks tidier on a finished ceiling, but its clear cover can catch reflections from bright fixtures. A turret has no cover over the lens, which avoids that glare and often gives a cleaner night picture. In a lobby with strong decorative lighting we often prefer a turret on the wall; in a cellar or landing where a camera might get hit, a vandal-resistant dome usually wins.

How long does the hard drive in a camera recorder last?
+

A recorder writes to its drive every minute of every day, so the drive is the part most likely to wear out over the years. Drives made for surveillance last far longer than ordinary computer drives, which is why we use them. The recorder can report drive errors, and we set it to send those warnings to the owner or managing agent, so a failing drive is replaced before recordings start going missing rather than after.

Can the super watch live views without being able to scroll back through old recordings?
+

Yes. Most recorders let the administrator set permissions account by account: live view only, playback limited to certain cameras, or full playback with export. Many boards give the super live views of the doors, cellar and stairs, and leave playback and export to the managing agent or a named board officer. We set those limits during handover and write them down, so everyone knows who can do what.

Our lobby lights stay on all night. Why does the camera still switch to black and white?
+

The camera judges light by its own sensor, and a dim corner of the lobby, or a dark street seen through the door, can make it decide it is night and turn on infrared. Once infrared is on, the picture drops to black and white. We change the switching setting, adjust the angle away from the dark glass, or lock the camera in color where the lobby lighting is enough. That is part of the after-dark check on install day.

Movers keep bumping the lobby cameras on move-in days. How do we stop that?
+

Mount height and position do most of the work: cameras go above the swing of a sofa or a mattress carried upright, and out of the path to the elevator or stair. Vandal-resistant domes with tamper-resistant screws hold their aim when something does hit them. It also helps to have the recorder alert the super when a camera's view changes suddenly, so a camera knocked off aim on a Saturday is caught that day rather than weeks later.

The vestibule camera fogs up on cold mornings. What causes that?
+

A vestibule sits between a heated lobby and a cold street, so warm, damp air meets a cold lens or cover every time the door opens in winter. The fix is usually a camera rated for the conditions, moved slightly away from the door's draft, and sealed properly where its cable enters. Some outdoor-rated cameras have built-in heaters. We check the spot during the survey, because a camera that fogs every winter morning misses the busiest hours.

Can new camera cable share the cellar raceway that carries our building's fire alarm wiring?
+

We keep camera cable out of fire alarm raceways and never attach it to fire alarm wiring. Fire alarm circuits have their own rules and their own inspections, and mixing other cable into their pathways can cause problems for the building. Camera cable gets its own supports or its own raceway in the cellar, labeled at both ends, so the fire alarm contractor and the camera installer never have to untangle each other's work.
