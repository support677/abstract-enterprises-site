The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Manhattan Beach is the small peninsula at the bottom of Brooklyn between Sheepshead Bay and the ocean, with Brighton Beach on its western side and Kingsborough Community College on its eastern tip. About 4,700 people lived there at the 2020 count, mostly in detached brick and stucco houses set back behind front gardens on the alphabetical streets from Amherst to Pembroke, with Oriental Boulevard running the length of the neighborhood and Shore Boulevard along the bay. Many of the original houses have been enlarged, and many have been torn down and replaced with far larger ones. There are rows and low-rise apartment buildings from the 1920s through the 1940s, a seven-story condo at The Wave on West End Avenue, a string of synagogues and schools along West End Avenue, and the college. It is a quiet place by city standards; a resident on r/Brooklyn called it "probably one of the calmer-feeling spots in Brooklyn." That calm is one reason camera systems here get neglected. Nobody looks at them for months, and the day someone does, the camera that mattered has been dark since spring.

The record shows why residents put cameras up in the first place, and why a working one still pays for itself. In the summer of 2008, News 12 reported that Manhattan Beach residents were "resorting to their own security measures" after a run of muggings and break-ins that included four robberies in four days. Around the same period, Brooklyn Paper reported that the Manhattan Beach Neighborhood Association's quality-of-life chair, Ron Biondo, wanted surveillance cameras at West End Avenue and Oriental Boulevard and at Shore Boulevard and Exeter Street, while members of the Manhattan Beach Community Group had unanswered questions about long-term costs and round-the-clock surveillance. The same report described a scam artist posing as a water inspector who got into the home of a 93-year-old woman on Beaumont Street and left with $300, three people arrested trying to break into a house on Hastings Street, and a car stolen on West End Avenue. By April 2010, the 61st Precinct was crediting residents' "penchant for pointing cameras just about everywhere" for its drop in felonies, and its crime was down 19.4 percent for the year, the largest drop in the city at the time.

Then the city added its own. In July 2014, NYPD cameras went up on light poles on West End Avenue, Oriental Boulevard and Shore Boulevard, covering what Bklyner called the community's two busiest entrances and exits, with state money set aside by then-Councilman Chaim Deutsch. Deutsch said they were "not really for the high crime area" but for cases such as a missing child or "an Alzheimer's patient," where footage could show whether someone left or came home. Those cameras are not watched live, and only NYPD personnel can see what they record. They do not see your front path, your side gate, your garden or your counter. That leaves private cameras doing the job they did in 2010, on systems that are now much older.

Recent cases show both what cameras catch here and what they miss. Just after midnight on October 29, 2024, News 12 reported, security footage at the pharmacy at The Wave on West End Avenue showed a person being dropped off out front, picking up a heavy rock and throwing it through the glass door; narcotics and other medications were taken, the owner put the damage at about $2,000, and police said they did not know how many people were involved. On February 21, 2026, at about 2:55 in the afternoon, four people robbed a 16-year-old and a 14-year-old near Ocean Avenue and Oriental Boulevard, part of a pattern police traced across four South Brooklyn precincts. The video that went to police that time came from a passing driver's phone. "I'm glad he caught it on camera and the videos were passed over to the police department," one victim's mother told News 12. A camera on a house facing that corner, recording and with its clock right, would have been one more record of the same few minutes.

The last pressure is water and power. The peninsula floods from both sides. After Hurricane Sandy, the president of the Manhattan Beach Community Group told City Limits that "every house was flooded," and power was out for more than a week. Smaller events keep happening: Council Member Inna Vernikov has posted photos of high-tide flooding on the bay side, with B49 service suspended between Emmons Avenue and Oriental Boulevard at Mackenzie Street. First Street's model counts 42 properties in Manhattan Beach, 3.2 percent of the total, with flood risk over the next 30 years. Recorders and power supplies on basement floors, outlets that trip when water gets near them, and drives that were writing when the power cut out all show up later as repair calls. Many houses with a front-door camera also have a video door station or a buzzer at the gate, and we can look at both on the same trip.

Attached rows and small apartment buildings from the 1920s to the 1940s, with a shared front door, a cellar, a laundry or trash room and a recorder put in by whoever managed the building at the time.

Brick houses from the 1920s to the 1940s: cameras added to a house that was never wired for them

The original Manhattan Beach houses were built long before anyone thought about cameras. When owners added them, the installer usually ran cable along the outside of the brick, over the side passage or down to the basement through a hole beside a window frame or the side cellar door, and put the recorder in the basement near the electric panel and the internet line. That works until something disturbs the path. Pointing and waterproofing crews work around old cables, gutters and leaders get replaced, and a basement that takes water once leaves the lowest stretch of every cable sitting where the water was.

On these houses, a repair starts by following each run from the recorder to the camera, not by replacing the camera. A front camera that fails only in heavy rain usually has a connector or a junction box that fills with water; a side-passage camera that died the week after a gutter job often has a cable that was pulled or cut where it crossed the leader. When the recorder sits in the basement, we look at how high it is, what it plugs into and whether a short outage would kill it, and we lift it or put it on a battery backup when that is the real cause of the trouble.

Stucco and tile-roof houses: anchors, eaves and water behind the wall

The Mediterranean-style houses on the peninsula look solid, but stucco is a thin skin over the wall behind it, and every camera mount is a hole in it. When one has to be remounted, we reuse a sound hole, seal it with a mounting box, and ask before drilling anything new. Over the years, a mount that was not sealed lets water in behind the stucco, the anchor loosens and the camera tilts down until it shows the lawn. Cables tucked behind downspouts or run under the eaves of a tile roof are out of sight, which is the point, but they are also where water collects and where a roofer or painter can damage them without anyone knowing.

Enlarged and rebuilt houses: big systems that nobody in the family can read

Many Manhattan Beach houses have been enlarged, and many lots now hold a new house several times the size of the cottage that stood there. Their camera systems are the largest on the peninsula: cameras on every side, at the garage, on the garden and along the passages, all wired back to a rack or a closet in the basement that also holds the recorder, one or more PoE switches, the router and sometimes a home-automation controller and a gate controller. The low-voltage contractor who built it usually left no drawing, and the family knows only that the screen shows cameras and the phone app sometimes does not.

The most common fault here is not a camera at all. One switch fails and every camera it powered goes dark at once; the internet company replaces the router and the recorder loses its connection to the app; the backup battery that was supposed to protect the rack died years ago, and the next flicker resets everything. Our repair begins with a map: every box on the rack and what it does, every camera and the port it lands on, and which outlet and backup each piece depends on. Then we fix what failed, label every cable, and give the cameras their own part of the network where the equipment allows it, so a busy evening of streaming does not knock them off the app.

The newer condo on West End Avenue: garage levels, a doorman desk and a cellar built to keep water out

The Wave at 102–104 West End Avenue went up between 2014 and 2016: seven stories, 48 units, a 20-foot lobby with a doorman, two levels of parking for 68 cars, and, according to its Department of Buildings filings as reported by Bklyner, commercial uses in a flood-proofed cellar. A building like this has more cameras in fewer kinds of places than any house: the garage entrance and ramp, the parking rows, the doors between the garage and the lobby, the lobby itself, the mailroom and the roof. The recorder usually sits in a management or equipment room, and the doorman sees a monitor at the desk.

Repairs in a building like this run through the board and the managing agent. They approve the work, hold the admin login, and decide who may export footage. We follow the building's rules for contractors, book any access needed to the garage or roof in advance, and leave a written record of what was repaired and what is still at risk.

Synagogues, schools and community buildings: many users, one recorder, irregular hours

West End Avenue and the nearby blocks have several synagogues, study halls and schools, including the Manhattan Beach Jewish Center at 60 West End Avenue, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2015. After Sandy, City Limits reported that the Zichron Aryeh Leib Kollel on West End Avenue needed major reconstruction, and that P.S. 195 on Irwin Street flooded. Buildings like these have cameras at the main door, the side doors, the yard and the parking area, used by a custodian, a rabbi's office, a school office and sometimes a volunteer, often with no single person in charge of the recorder.

A repair here includes deciding who holds the admin login, setting a separate view-only login for whoever watches the door, checking every outdoor camera at the hours the building is actually empty, and setting alerts so the office hears about a dark camera before the next holiday rather than after it. Listing on the National Register does not by itself restrict what a private owner may do to the building when no state or federal money or permit is involved, according to the State Historic Preservation Office, so replacing a camera on a listed synagogue is normally a decision for the congregation.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Working Through a Manhattan Beach Camera System From the Front Step to the Back Garden

These notes describe the order our technicians work in on a typical detached or rebuilt Manhattan Beach house when several cameras are out and nobody in the family is sure why. It is a working method, not an account of any one address.

Ask who uses it before touching it. On the peninsula the person who calls is often an adult son or daughter, and the person who lives with the system is a parent, a tenant or an aide. Before anything else the technician asks who watches which camera, on which screen or phone, and whether anyone needs footage from a recent date. If they do, that footage is exported first.

Start at the rack or the shelf. The technician checks the outlet and any battery backup, then every box: power supply, PoE switch, router, recorder. Temperature, drive health and the recorder's log of when each camera dropped out say more about the cause than any camera on the wall. A switch that died in an outage shows up as a group of cameras that all went dark at the same minute.

Walk the house in the same order every time. Street side and front door first, then the driveway side, then the narrow passage, then the back garden and anything facing the bay. At each dark or weak camera, power and signal are tested at the camera itself; then the connector, the junction box and the mount are checked for water, salt, a loose anchor in stucco or brick, or a cable that a contractor disturbed.

Play back, don't just look. Every camera is played back from the night before. A camera that shows a fine live picture can still be saving nothing, and the door camera that matters most is often the one set to record only when it detects motion, which it may never do if it is aimed too high.

Price before parts, and leave a map. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, the technician checks every camera by day and after dark, sets alerts for lost video and drive errors, leaves labeled cables and a simple list of what plugs into what, and makes sure the logins are in the owner's hands, not the installer's or a relative's who has moved on.

Fault by Fault

Old Systems, Long Runs, Wrong Phones and Low Shelves: Why Manhattan Beach Camera Systems Stop Working

Four causes account for most of the failures we find on the peninsula. None of them is unusual in Brooklyn, but the shape of Manhattan Beach, with its big detached houses, older owners and water on two sides, makes each one more likely here.

Old systems: the cameras of 2010 are still on the walls

When the 61st Precinct's commander told the City Council in 2010 that "in 2009 we identified a lot of bad guys" and that video "played a key role," the cameras he meant were mostly analog cameras feeding a DVR, with a picture that was good for its time. A lot of that equipment is still in place. Analog cameras of that age tend to fail slowly: the infrared lights that give the night picture wear out, the day-night filter sticks so the picture turns pink or stays black and white, the clear cover yellows, and the DVR's drive, which has been writing around the clock for years, starts to lose recordings before it dies.

An old system is not automatically a lost cause. Many coax cable runs on these houses are perfectly good, and newer HD cameras can often use them with a new recorder, which saves the cost and mess of pulling new cable through a finished house. What we tell owners plainly is which parts are worth keeping. A ten- or fifteen-year-old DVR with a failing drive is rarely worth a new drive; a run of sound coax to a good position on the house almost always is.

Long runs: big houses, deep lots and connectors far from the recorder

On a detached house with a deep back garden, the camera at the far fence may be a hundred feet or more of cable from the recorder, and the run often goes around the outside of the house, through a garage or under the eaves to get there. Every one of those runs ends in a connector outdoors, and on a peninsula with salt air and wind-driven rain, those connectors are where most single-camera failures start. A camera that drops out in wet weather and comes back when it dries is almost always a connector or junction box problem, not the camera.

Network cameras add one more limit: a single network cable carries power and data reliably only up to a certain length, and runs that were stretched to reach a garden corner or a garage at the back of the lot can work on a warm day and fail on a cold one. We test each long run end to end, re-terminate connectors in sealed boxes, and where a run is simply too long we add a small powered extender or relocate the switch, rather than replacing cameras that were never the problem.

Wrong phones: the system is fine, but the logins left with somebody else

A large share of our Manhattan Beach calls are about access, not hardware. The cameras record, but the app account belongs to a son who set it up before moving out of state, to the builder's contractor, to a former tenant or to a grandchild whose phone has since been replaced. The parent who lives in the house has a monitor in the kitchen and nothing else, and the family member who could pull a clip lives far away.

The repair is to recover the recorder, change every default and old password, put the account in the owner's name, and then decide on purpose who else gets access and what kind. An adult child may get full playback on their own phone; an aide may get a live view of the front door only; a neighbor who helps out may get nothing at all.

Low shelves: recorders and power supplies where the water goes

Most systems were installed with the recorder near the electric panel and the internet line, which in a Manhattan Beach house usually means the basement. Sandy showed what that means on this peninsula: the Community Group's president said every house flooded, and power was off for more than a week. Smaller tide events still leave water on the bay side, and a basement that took a few inches once leaves outlets, power strips and the bottom of every cable at risk the next time.

A recorder that survived a flood often fails months later, from corrosion inside the case or a drive that was damaged when the power cut out. When we repair a system in a basement that has taken water, we look at where the recorder, the switch, the power supplies and the battery backup sit, and we price raising them or moving them to a dry floor alongside the repair, so the next tide does not undo it.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or service security systems, and its rules cover closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. Cameras that watch staff. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get acknowledgment. If a repair moves or adds a camera covering a stockroom, a dispensing counter or a back office, update the notice.

Footage requests. Detectives from the 61st Precinct may ask for a copy of a clip. A copy on USB with original timestamps usually satisfies the request without the recorder leaving the building. Whether to share a clip with a neighbor or a community group is the owner's decision.

Historic listings. The Manhattan Beach Jewish Center is on the National Register of Historic Places. Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever installed it. These are the kinds of equipment we find on Manhattan Beach houses, condos, synagogues and shops, and the faults each one tends to develop on the peninsula.

Analog and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from the analog systems of 2008 to 2012 to newer HD models on the same cable. Typical faults: worn infrared, a stuck day-night filter, yellowed covers and corroded connectors at the end of long runs.

Network (IP) cameras

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with an adjustable zoom, common over front paths, driveways and lobbies. Typical faults: a lens left too wide to recognize anyone at the gate, focus that drifted after a knock, and covers clouded by salt and sun.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving cameras over back gardens, parking areas and school yards, and fisheye cameras over shop floors. Typical faults: lost presets, sticking motors and tours that leave the camera looking away from the gate.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

The switches that power network cameras, usually on a rack or shelf in the basement. Typical faults: dead ports or whole units after a surge, power budgets exceeded as cameras were added, and extenders on long garden runs that fail in the cold.

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, adapters plugged into basement outlets that took water, and backup batteries that died years ago without anyone noticing.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Manhattan Beach

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere, and each sends Manhattan Beach owners toward the wrong repair, a wasted purchase or a lost clip.

"In a quiet, low-crime neighborhood, a dead camera can wait"

General answers tend to rank camera repair by local crime rates, and Manhattan Beach is often described as one of the calmer parts of Brooklyn. On that logic, a dark camera on the peninsula is a low priority.

The record says otherwise. The neighborhood has had an impostor "water inspector" get into a 93-year-old's house on Beaumont Street, a break-in attempt on Hastings Street, a pharmacy door smashed on West End Avenue in 2024 and a daytime robbery of two teenagers near Ocean Avenue and Oriental Boulevard in 2026. A low crime rate means incidents are rare, not that they do not happen, and when one does, the footage is usually the only record there is.

A camera's value here is concentrated in a few nights over many years. That is an argument for making sure it records on those nights, with alerts that tell you when it stops, not for leaving it dark until something happens.

"The city's street cameras will have recorded it anyway"

Some answers suggest that public cameras at busy points make private ones less important, and that residents can request the city's footage after an incident.

The NYPD cameras that went up in Manhattan Beach in 2014 are on light poles on West End Avenue, Oriental Boulevard and Shore Boulevard, watching the ways in and out. Bklyner reported that they are not monitored in real time and that only NYPD personnel can access the footage. They do not see a front step on Falmouth Street, a side passage, a back garden or a counter.

A pole camera may help police follow someone leaving the peninsula. The camera that shows who rang your bell is yours, and the only way to be sure it recorded is to keep it working.

"Swap the old wired cameras for Wi-Fi cameras and skip the repair"

Consumer guides often treat a failing wired system as a reason to replace it with Wi-Fi or battery cameras, which are cheap and easy to put up.

On a long detached house with brick or stucco walls, Wi-Fi often does not reach the far corners of the garden or the garage at the back of the lot, and battery cameras drain fast in winter and record only short clips when they detect motion. Meanwhile, the existing cable runs, which are the expensive part of any wired system, are usually still good. Throwing them away to save a repair often costs more over a couple of years.

Find out what failed first. A wired system with a bad drive or a dead switch is usually cheaper to repair than to replace, and a Wi-Fi camera is best kept for the one spot a cable cannot reach.

"Indoor cameras are the best way to keep an eye on an aging parent"

Advice for families often suggests putting cameras in the living room, kitchen and bedroom so a relative can be checked on remotely.

Cameras inside private rooms raise real problems: the parent may not want them, aides and visitors have to be told about them, and hidden recording in bedrooms and bathrooms is a crime under New York's unlawful surveillance law. They also miss what families most often need to know on the peninsula, which is who came to the door and whether someone left the house at night.

A door camera that records continuously, a screen where the parent sits, and alerts for a door opening at night answer most of those questions with far less intrusion. Indoor cameras, if any, belong in shared spaces with the parent's agreement.

"A camera that has been through a flood should be thrown out"

After storms, quick answers tend to say anything that got wet is ruined, and that the whole system should be replaced.

Outdoor cameras are built to get wet; what usually fails after a tide or a flood on the peninsula is the equipment that was never meant to: the recorder, power supplies, outlets and power strips on a basement floor, and the low connectors of cables that run down into the basement. Throwing away twenty outdoor cameras because the recorder drowned is a costly mistake.

Test before replacing. We check each camera on a known-good power supply and recorder, replace what failed, and move the indoor equipment to a dry level so the next tide does not repeat the bill.

"A clip recorded off the screen with a phone is good enough for police"

Many people send police a video of their recorder screen filmed with a phone, and some answers suggest that is fine.

In February 2026, a passing driver's phone video of the robbery near Ocean Avenue and Oriental Boulevard went to police, and that was the right thing to do with a live event. Footage already on a recorder is different. Filming the screen loses detail, the original timestamp and any proof the clip has not been edited, and makes a face that was readable on the recorder much harder to make out.

Export the original file from the recorder to a USB drive, in its own format, with its timestamps, and note any difference between the recorder's clock and the real time. We show every customer how to do that before we leave.

"Any handy relative can fix a camera system"

Some answers suggest camera repair is a simple job for anyone with a ladder, and that the parts are all online.

An owner can work on their own system, and New York allows that. A company doing it for someone else needs a license from the Department of State, because the state treats CCTV used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement as a security system. More practically, the hard part of most Manhattan Beach repairs is not swapping a camera; it is reading a rack nobody documented, saving the footage first, and not resetting a recorder into a state that wipes its settings.

FAQ

Manhattan Beach Security Camera Repair FAQ

The microphone on our front camera picks up a steady hum that drowns out voices. Is that a repair?
+

Usually, yes. A hum on camera audio most often comes from the power supply or from the cable running beside electrical wiring, and less often from a failing microphone. We test the camera on a separate supply to find which it is, then replace the supply, reroute the cable or replace the camera. If you do not need audio at all, we can switch it off.

Our camera has two lenses in one housing, and now half of its picture is black. Is the whole camera finished?
+

Not always. On a dual-lens camera, one dead half can be a setting that was lost in a reset, a damaged sensor or water inside one side of the housing. We check the settings and the recorder first, then the camera. If one sensor has failed, the camera is normally replaced as a unit, and we tell you whether a single-lens camera would cover that spot just as well for less.

Our contractor took the cameras down for a renovation and left them in a box. Can you put them back up?
+

Yes. We test each camera on the bench first, check the cables the contractor left behind, and remount the cameras in their old positions or better ones, sealing every opening. If any camera or cable was damaged during the work, we show you which and price only those parts.

We own a condo with a private terrace. Can our own camera on the terrace be repaired, and does the board have to approve it?
+

A camera on your own terrace is usually yours to repair, but the building's rules decide what can be attached to its walls and railings, so check with the board or managing agent before anything new is drilled. We repair or replace the camera on its existing mount where possible, and we aim it at your terrace and door, not at neighbors' windows or the common areas.

How can we tell what resolution our cameras are before deciding whether to repair or replace them?
+

The recorder's menu usually lists each camera's resolution under its channel or camera settings, and the label on the camera or its box often shows the model. A photo of the recorder's channel screen and one camera label is usually enough for us to tell you whether your cameras are worth keeping and what a newer camera on the same cable would give you.

Two neighbors on our street want to be able to see each other's driveway cameras. Can separate houses share a view?
+

Yes, if both owners agree. Each owner can give the other a view-only login limited to the chosen cameras, so neither can change settings or see the rest of the other's system. We set it up, write down who has access to what, and show each owner how to remove the other's access later.

Our recorder came with a paid license for smart detection. If it has to be replaced, do we lose that?
+

It depends on the maker. Some features are built into the recorder and some are licensed to it, and a license tied to the old unit may not move. Many current recorders include person and vehicle detection without an extra license. Before replacing anything, we check what your recorder has, and the written price shows what the replacement will and will not do.

After rain, our dome cameras show a bright smeared halo at night until the dome dries. Is the camera failing?
+

Usually not. Water drops and salt film on the dome scatter the camera's own infrared light back into the lens. Cleaning the dome, replacing a scratched cover, adding a hood or a small shield, or turning down the infrared often fixes it. If the halo stays when the dome is clean and dry, the inner seal or the cover may have failed, and the cover can often be replaced on its own.

If the recorder has to be replaced, can the camera names, motion zones and schedules we set up be carried over?
+

Sometimes. A replacement of the same make can often import a saved configuration file from the old unit, if the old unit still starts. When it does not, or the new recorder is a different make, we write down every camera's name, zone and schedule before the old one goes, set them up again on the new one and check each camera in playback.

Can our cameras warn us when a car or a person lingers outside the house late at night, without sending an alert for every passing car?
+

Many newer cameras and recorders can. A loitering or intrusion rule can trigger only when a person or vehicle stays inside a marked area for a set time during set hours, which ignores traffic passing on the street. We check what your equipment supports, draw the area on your own property and test it before we leave.
