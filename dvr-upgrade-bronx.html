What A DVR Upgrade Is

Replace the Recorder, Keep Your Cameras

In a Bronx apartment building, the whole camera system usually runs back to one recorder in a locked basement room off the boiler or the meters — and when that box dies, every lobby, vestibule, and stairwell camera goes dark with it even though the cameras themselves are fine. A DVR upgrade swaps that recorder for a current unit and leaves the cameras and the coax exactly where a super or an old contractor mounted them years ago. For a building on the Grand Concourse or a two-family in Throggs Neck, it's the difference between one basement visit and a wiring project nobody wants to fund.

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems is a licensed New York low-voltage contractor (NYS Lic #12000287431), and the Bronx is our home borough — our office sits on Fordham Road. We work the pre-war rental stock of the Concourse, University Heights, and Morris Heights; the single- and two-family blocks of Riverdale, Country Club, and Pelham Bay; and the commercial density of Fordham Road, White Plains Road, and Southern Boulevard. On all of it we swap analog HD recorders (TVI, CVI, AHD, and older CVBS), fit hybrid XVRs that leave room for IP cameras, and migrate to NVR when the cameras themselves are due. Whatever goes in records to a local drive — no monthly fee, no cloud subscription, no contract.

The recorder tells you when the building was last wired. A Concourse Art Deco walk-up typically runs an eight- or sixteen-channel DVR bolted to a basement wall, covering the lobby, both stairwells, the mailroom, and the courtyard. A Parkchester or Co-op City section has larger shared systems tied to management offices. A Fordham Road storefront runs its box in a back stockroom next to the router. Most were installed by a big-box crew or a since-gone contractor a decade ago, lost firmware and app support years back, and now overwrite themselves in a day or two. The cameras keep working; the box in the basement is the part that timed out.

Two things make the swap the obvious call in the Bronx. First, the wiring is worth keeping: those runs through the risers and cinderblock are almost always solid-copper RG59, and they carry a modern HD signal without complaint, so re-cabling an occupied apartment building is effort no owner needs to spend. Second, the demand on these systems keeps climbing — a managing agent needs the lobby clip after a break-in, HPD or an insurer wants footage on file, a tenant reports a vestibule package theft. A recorder that holds two days can't answer any of it. Swap the box and the coverage the building already paid for is back the same afternoon.

What makes these failures dangerous in the Bronx is that no one is watching the recorder. It lives in a locked basement room a tenant never enters and a super opens once a month. A cooling fan seizes, a drive fails, or a summer brownout during a heat wave takes the power supply — and nothing changes on any monitor until someone actually needs footage. The dense pedestrian traffic through a Bronx lobby means the cameras are recording motion nearly around the clock, which wears drives faster than a quiet suburban system ever would. The call we get most is a managing agent the morning after a lobby incident, learning only then that the DVR quit weeks earlier and the drive is blank.

There's an access calculus, too, that only Bronx building owners will recognize. Re-cabling a multi-tenant property means coordinating a super's keys, getting into occupied units, fishing wire through pre-war plaster and cinderblock risers, and usually management sign-off — days of disruption for wiring that already works. A recorder swap collapses all of that into a single basement visit: the super lets us into the recorder room, the cameras stay on their brackets, and the one thing that changes is the box everything already feeds. For the large majority of pre-IP Bronx systems, replacing the recorder rather than the system is the proportionate fix that actually gets approved and done.

Recorder Types We Upgrade To

Which Recorder Is Right For Your System

The right recorder for a Bronx building is set by what already runs through the risers, not by whatever's newest. Choose wrong one way and you've talked an owner into re-cabling an occupied apartment building that didn't need it; choose wrong the other way and you've locked a co-op into dead-end analog when a few dollars more would have kept the door open. Three paths, in plain terms.

🔌

DVR (Analog HD)

The like-for-like replacement when a building's lobby and stairwell cameras still image well and the riser coax is sound. It reads your analog channels directly, and unlike the box it replaces, a modern DVR brings H.265 compression, a working phone app, and a drive big enough to actually hold a month.

🔁

Hybrid / XVR — Most Popular

Runs the building's existing analog cameras on BNC and also takes IP cameras over the network on one chassis. For a Bronx owner it's the practical choice: keep every lobby and stairwell camera now, add a 4K IP camera at the front entrance when the budget clears — without a second box or another basement job.

🌐

NVR (IP)

Records IP cameras over Cat6 with PoE — the move when a building wants 4K on every channel and face-level detail in a dim vestibule, and is ready to pull Ethernet through the risers. In a pre-war building that's a staged job, which is exactly how we phase it.

Our Recommendation

Why We Default to Hybrid/XVR for Bronx Upgrades

We steer most Bronx buildings to a hybrid XVR because of how upgrades actually get funded here — one line item at a time, often through a managing agent or a co-op board. A hybrid takes the building's existing analog cameras on BNC and also accepts IP cameras over the network, all on one box and one app. It costs only a little more than a plain DVR, often $20 to $80, and that small difference is what lets a building modernize the lobby camera this year and the entrance camera next year without replacing the recorder or scheduling a second basement visit. Where every dollar gets reviewed, an upgrade path you can spread across budgets beats the hardware premium every time.

Where a full IP move earns its cost in the Bronx: the one job analog struggles with is the dim, high-traffic entrance — a pre-war vestibule lit by one fixture where you need to actually recognize a face, not just register a shape passing through. If the goal is identifying who tailgated in behind a tenant at 2 a.m., that's a 4K IP camera with real low-light sensing, and it wants an NVR behind it. If the cameras are also failing — fogged domes, dead IR from years on an exterior wall — you're buying cameras anyway, so IP is the honest answer. We say so when that's the case rather than sell a swap that leaves the real problem in place.

How the staged path runs in an occupied building: day one, the dead DVR comes out and a hybrid goes in, so every lobby and stairwell camera is recording again before the technician leaves — no coverage gap in a building that can't go dark. From there the building replaces cameras on its own schedule: the entrance this quarter, the courtyard next, each new IP camera landing on the recorder's network side without another super appointment. Once most channels are IP, a dedicated NVR can take over. The whole thing happens without pulling the system offline, which in a managed building is the difference between a project that gets approved and one that stalls.

That sequencing sidesteps the two outcomes Bronx owners dislike most: a single large capital hit argued through a board all at once, and being stranded on obsolete analog with no exit. A landlord on a tight month modernizes one camera; a co-op aligns each upgrade with its budget calendar. The hybrid recorder is simply the hinge that keeps every future option open, which is why we install one even for buildings that arrive with nothing but analog on the walls.

How the upgrade actually goes

In a Bronx building the job runs in a set order. We start in the basement recorder room: read the signal type on each channel (TVI, CVI, AHD, or older CVBS), check the coax and BNC ends coming down the risers, and meter the existing power supply and drive — in a warm, unventilated basement the supply is often the part that's actually cooked, and catching it is what keeps the new recorder from dying the same way. Then we match a unit to what we found, almost always a hybrid so the building keeps an IP path. We pull the dead box, fit a surveillance-rated drive sized to the building's retention, and re-terminate any riser runs that fault. Finally we configure it: motion recording where it saves space, continuous on the lobby and entrance, view-only accounts for the super and management instead of one shared login, audio off where it shouldn't record, and the app set up and tested before we leave the building.

On a straightforward system with healthy cabling, that's a same-day job and your cameras are back online in an afternoon. Where there's BNC repair, added channels, or the start of an IP migration, the timeline stretches — but you know that up front, because we tested before we quoted.

Why we start with the recorder, not the whole system: when a Bronx owner calls with "my cameras aren't working," our first move is almost never to quote a new system. It's to figure out whether the cameras actually failed or whether the recorder took the whole system down with it. In the great majority of cases it's the latter — the cameras are fine, the coax is fine, and the box is dead or dying. Replacing just the recorder is faster, far cheaper, and less disruptive than pulling wire through an occupied building, and it keeps the equipment you already paid for doing its job. We only recommend going further — new cameras, an IP migration, fresh cabling — when the assessment actually shows you need it. That test-first, quote-second approach is the whole reason owners call us back for the next building.

When To Replace

Signs Your DVR Needs Replacing

A basement recorder rarely dies without warning — it drops hints for weeks while no one's down there to notice. In a building where the failure only surfaces when a tenant or the police need a clip, these are the tells a super or managing agent should catch early, before the lobby footage you need is the footage that already stopped saving.

Reboot loops

The recorder restarts on its own, often overnight, and you find gaps in the timeline right when incidents happen. Classic failing-drive or dying-power-supply symptom.

"No HDD" or missing footage

The unit boots but reports no hard drive, or footage only goes back a day or two when it used to hold weeks. The 24/7 write load has worn the drive out.

The app stopped connecting

Remote viewing that worked years ago now fails. Manufacturers retire the cloud servers old DVRs depend on — there's no fix on the old box, only a current recorder.

Black or "no signal" channels

Cameras that clearly still have power show nothing on the recorder. Sometimes a cable or BNC fault, sometimes the decoder in an aging box giving up channel by channel.

Dead fan / overheating

A recorder that runs hot or whose cooling fan has stopped is on borrowed time. Heat is what kills the drive and the board.

Discontinued & unsupported

The model is no longer made, firmware updates stopped, and you can't find compatible cameras. Even if it still runs, you're one failure from a scramble.

Decoded

DVR Upgrade Terminology

BNC

The twist-lock connector on the end of your coax cameras. If your cameras use BNC, you're on a DVR/coax system.

TVI / CVI / AHD

Three HD-over-coax signal standards. A pentabrid recorder auto-detects all of them, so mixed-generation cameras record on one box.

CVBS

Old standard-definition analog (480p). Still supported by modern recorders, so ancient cameras keep working during a phased upgrade.

H.265 / H.265+

Newer compression. Cuts storage use ~40–50% vs. H.264, turning days of retention into weeks on the same drive.

XVR / Pentabrid

A hybrid recorder that takes analog on coax and IP over the network at the same time.

Surveillance drive

WD Purple / Seagate SkyHawk — built for 24/7 writing. Desktop drives fail fast in a recorder.

DIY vs Professional

Swapping It Yourself vs. Calling a Pro

Honest take: on a single-family Bronx home where the cameras are one signal type and the coax is sound, a careful owner can swap a recorder. In a multi-tenant building — mixed camera generations, riser runs you can't easily open, a super's schedule to work around — a pro is what keeps it from becoming a second purchase and a callback. Here's where the paths split.

Signal-type match

DIY: you guess whether the building's cameras are TVI, CVI, or AHD and hope the box matches. Pro: read per channel in the recorder room and set to auto-detect, so every lobby and stairwell camera shows video.

Coax & BNC health

DIY: usually skipped — until half the channels read "no signal" and it's a riser you can't reach. Pro: runs metered and connectors re-terminated where they fault, before the new box goes in.

Power-supply risk

DIY: a supply cooked by years in a hot basement can fry the new recorder just like it stressed the old one. Pro: supply metered and replaced if needed before anything new connects to it.

Drive sizing

DIY: whatever drive shipped in the box, if any — rarely enough for a busy building. Pro: surveillance-rated drive sized to the channel count, resolution, and how far back the board wants to see.

Remote access

DIY: port-forwarding headaches and endpoints left exposed on the building network. Pro: secure app setup with view-only accounts for the super and management, tested before we leave.

Best for

DIY: a simple, matched, healthy single-signal system. Pro: anything mixed-generation, aging, multi-tenant, or already showing failure symptoms.

Repairing damaged coax or BNC connectors. Where a run faults or a connector has corroded, we re-terminate or repair just that run — restoring the channels that were dropping out without re-cabling the building.

Adding structured cabling for a staged IP migration. If you're planning to move toward IP over time, running Cat6 to a few key locations now lays the groundwork, so future IP cameras drop straight onto a hybrid recorder or a later NVR.

Connecting cameras with access control where appropriate. Retention & Compliance

What a Recorder Upgrade Affects Legally

In a Bronx building the recorder is quietly the most legally significant box on the property — it decides how far back footage reaches, who can touch it, and whether a lobby clip holds up when a tenant, an insurer, or the police ask for it. Here's what owners, boards, and managing agents should keep in mind. General guidance, not legal advice; confirm specifics with your attorney or board counsel.

How long you keep footage is set by your recorder

There's no statute setting a blanket retention period for most private residential buildings in New York, so the number is set by the building's own policy — and then physically by the recorder. Most boards land on 30 to 90 days, but a policy is only real if the drive can hold it, and a busy Bronx lobby records so much motion that an old DVR overwrites in a day or two. Sizing a current recorder's surveillance drive with H.265+ is what turns "we keep 30 days" from a line in the minutes into something the box can actually prove.

Insurance and commercial requirements

On the commercial side — the Fordham Road storefront, the Southern Boulevard shop, the ground-floor restaurant — the recorder often sits inside an insurance condition. Property policies frequently require working cameras and a defined retention window for theft and vandalism coverage, and some regulated operators, such as licensed cannabis retailers under the state Office of Cannabis Management, carry explicit footage mandates. If the policy assumes 30 days on the entrance and register and the box holds two, a denied claim is where that gap surfaces — after a loss, when it's too late to fix.

Audio, privacy & access control

Audio adds a wrinkle in shared spaces: New York is a one-party-consent state, and capturing conversation in a lobby or hallway raises questions video alone doesn't. A current recorder lets you switch audio off per channel wherever it shouldn't run — something old boxes handled clumsily if at all. The same recorders support layered accounts, so a super or managing agent gets view-only playback of specific cameras while the board's designated administrator keeps the rights to change settings or delete footage.

Chain of custody & footage that holds up

The day a clip becomes evidence — a hallway altercation, a slip-and-fall claim, an NYPD or HPD request — the question shifts from "do we have it" to "can we show it wasn't tampered with." A DVR where everyone shares one admin login can't answer that cleanly. A recorder with separate accounts, a defined access structure, and a straight MP4 export to USB produces a clip you can hand over with a clear story of who had access. In a building that might end up in front of an insurer or a courtroom, that's the line between video that helps and video that gets thrown out.

Board and management approval

Then there's the part unique to co-op and condo life: the approval itself. Common-area security work usually needs board or managing-agent sign-off, and the alteration agreement often names a licensed, insured contractor with a certificate of insurance on file before anyone starts. This is exactly where a recorder swap beats a re-wire — it's a contained, basement-scale job that's easy to approve. As a licensed NY low-voltage contractor (NYS Lic #12000287431) carrying insurance, we hand the managing agent the COI and documentation up front and work around the super's access hours, so the project clears the board and finishes in one visit instead of stalling between them.

Real Questions

What Bronx Owners Ask About DVR Upgrades

Cost

Management quoted the co-op $12k to "replace the building system." Real?

Get it checked before the board votes. If the lobby and stairwell cameras still image and the riser coax tests good, the actual need is usually a recorder and a drive — a fraction of that. The whole-system number is what you get when nobody tests the existing wiring first.

Cost

Does swapping the basement DVR need a special assessment?

Almost never. A recorder swap is a repair-scale expense, well within an operating budget — not the capital project that triggers an assessment. The five-figure numbers come from pulling new cable through occupied floors, which a healthy analog system doesn't need.

Cost

Will the building get stuck paying a monthly cloud fee?

No. The recorder writes to a drive in the basement and the app is free. That's the opposite of the cloud-camera brands a managing agent sometimes gets pitched, which bill per camera every month with no end.

Quality / Trust

A tenant wants footage of a hallway incident — can the old box even export it?

If it's still recording, maybe, and badly. Aged recorders often export a proprietary file nobody can open and cap quality on the way out. A current recorder drops a clean MP4 to USB that HPD, an insurer, or an attorney can actually use.

Quality / Trust

How do I know the installer isn't padding the job for the board?

Ask them to test before quoting — signal type per channel, coax condition, drive health — then give a written scope. An honest contractor will happily do a recorder-and-drive job when that's the real fix, and put in writing what they ruled out.

DIY / Pro

Can the super just swap the DVR himself?

If the replacement matches the camera signal type and the riser coax is sound, a handy super can. Where it goes wrong: mismatched TVI/CVI/AHD showing black channels, a cooked basement power supply that kills the new unit, and BNC ends that need re-terminating in a cramped recorder room.

DIY / Pro

New box is in and the lobby camera reads "no signal."

Almost always a signal-type mismatch on that channel or a faulted BNC — and it's always the one camera the building can't lose. A pentabrid set to auto-detect clears most of it; the rest is a connector repair on that riser run.

Technical

The system covers six floors of hallways — can one recorder handle that?

Yes — size by channel count, not floors. Six hallway cameras plus a lobby, vestibule, mailroom, and courtyard is a 16-channel recorder with headroom. The floor spread doesn't matter to the box; the number of cameras does.

Technical

Cameras were added over several years. Will they all run on one box?

They will. A pentabrid recorder auto-detects each channel, so the 2014 stairwell camera and the 2021 entrance camera record side by side on one drive with one app — common in buildings that upgraded piecemeal.

How do I tell if my building has a DVR or an NVR?

Look at where the cameras land on the box. Round BNC coax plugs mean a DVR; blue RJ45 network jacks mean an NVR. Most pre-2016 Bronx buildings are DVR on coax.

Does the super's monitor go dark during the swap?

Only for the few minutes the recorder is physically out, then it comes back on the new unit. We schedule the cutover for a quiet window so the lobby is barely unmonitored.

Can we keep the old lobby cameras and add a 4K one at the entrance?

Yes — a hybrid runs the old analog cameras and a new IP camera together. That's the usual first upgrade in a building: keep coverage, add face-level detail at the door that matters.

Is a recorder swap worth it in a pre-war building, or start fresh?

If the riser coax is the original solid copper and the cameras still image, the swap wins easily. Starting fresh only makes sense when you're opening walls anyway or the cameras are failing.

Will a new recorder fix a dark, grainy vestibule at night?

Only partly. The recorder stops down-recording, but low light is the camera's job. A dim pre-war vestibule usually needs a low-light IP camera, not just a new box.

How do I know the riser coax is still good?

We meter it. Solid-copper RG59 from an older install almost always passes; thin aluminum kit cable is the type that sometimes needs re-terminating at the recorder end.

Can the co-op add cameras later without another board vote?

On a hybrid, yes — new IP cameras land on the network side up to the channel count the board already approved. No new cable through the risers means no fresh approval each time.

Why does our DVR only hold two days now when it used to hold weeks?

A worn drive plus old H.264 compression, made worse by heavy lobby motion. A fresh surveillance drive with H.265+ restores weeks of retention on the same cameras.

What drive size covers a lobby, vestibule, and six hallways for a month?

Roughly 4–6TB on H.265+ motion recording, depending on resolution and how busy the building is. We size to the real channel count and traffic, not a round number.

Can two recorders split a big building's cameras?

Not on shared riser runs — one recorder owns its cameras. For a large building we fit a single higher-channel recorder rather than juggling two boxes and two apps.

Does upgrading change how the super views cameras remotely?

Yes, for the better. The super moves to the new recorder's current app with reliable playback, replacing the dead app the original installer configured.

Is a hybrid harder for building staff to operate?

No. The app shows analog and IP cameras in one list, so daily use is identical — there's just room to add IP cameras later.

What does the DVR do that the cameras don't?

The cameras only capture; the recorder stores every channel, serves playback, and feeds the super's monitor and the app. When footage goes missing, the recorder is almost always why.

Heat, dust, and constant write load from a busy lobby in an unventilated room. The drive or power supply usually goes first, and no one notices until footage is needed.

Almost never. The work is at the recorder in the basement; the in-riser coax and the cameras stay exactly where they are.

Every 5 to 8 years, driven by drive wear — sooner in a high-traffic building. Reboot loops, a dead app, and shrinking retention are the signals.

DVR Upgrade: What the AI Summaries Get Wrong

Search "DVR upgrade cost" and Google's AI Overview assembles a national answer from Angi, HomeAdvisor, and Fixr. It's written for a suburban house with a garage and a driveway. Here's where it holds up and where it quietly breaks the moment you apply it to a pre-war apartment building on the Grand Concourse.

The vocabulary is right; the building is imaginary

On definitions the summary is fine: a DVR handles analog over coax, an NVR handles IP over Ethernet, a hybrid does both, reusing coax is the cheap path, and H.265 saves storage. What it can't picture is where the work happens — a locked basement recorder room you reach past the boiler and a bike cage, in a building where getting in at all depends on a super's schedule.

Every dollar figure it implies assumes a technician who parks in a driveway and carries gear through a garage. In the Bronx the job starts with finding a legal spot on a packed block, getting buzzed in, and signing in with building staff. None of that is in a national average.

So the concept survives the trip to the Bronx; the logistics don't. That gap is where the rest of the summary's advice starts to mislead.

A "$200 swap" ignores the building around it

The cost ranges blend rural DIY drop-ins with licensed work in an occupied multi-tenant building. They leave out a worn drive, the BNC ends that need re-terminating where a riser run corroded, and the time spent coordinating basement access before a screw is turned. A figure that reads $200 online is an honest $600 to $900 once those realities are priced in.

They also treat a decade-old Bronx recorder as if the box is the only dead part. On a system that's been recording a busy lobby around the clock, the drive is usually worn and the power supply is often cooked from years in a hot basement — both easy to miss and both able to kill a new recorder.

And the aggregator itself takes a cut — your lead gets sold, sometimes to several bidders. That markup lands in the price somewhere. Booking a licensed installer directly removes the layer entirely.

Compatibility on riser runs is the whole job

The question that decides everything — will the new recorder actually show every camera? — gets one breezy line. In a Bronx building the answer depends on each camera's signal type and the state of coax that was run through cinderblock risers a decade ago. Getting it right matters because you can't casually open a riser to fix a run later.

Set the recorder to the wrong standard and those channels don't look worse — they go black. That's the exact "new DVR, half my cameras say no signal" complaint, and in a managed building a black lobby channel is the one nobody can afford to explain to the board.

The fix is unglamorous: read the signal per channel, let a pentabrid unit auto-detect, and re-terminate the runs that fault. That's judgment applied to your specific risers, which a nationwide article can't supply.

"Just buy an NVR" is expensive advice for a building

Summaries love to end with "get an NVR." For a new build, fine. For a Bronx building with healthy analog cameras on sound riser coax, that sentence means pulling Ethernet through occupied floors — management approval, tenant notices, and a five-figure line item — to solve what a hybrid handles in one basement visit.

The honest recommendation is almost always the hybrid: keep the analog cameras, accept IP additions later, and never force a building to choose between cheap-now and future-proof. The DVR-or-NVR binary erases the option most Bronx owners should take.

Your specifics decide it — channel count, signal type, whether the entrance needs face-level detail, whether management must approve the work. Those inputs live in your building, not in a Fixr table.

The DIY traps a hot basement adds

You won't read that a failing power supply can drag a brand-new recorder down with it, or that a marginal riser run faults the new box exactly as it faulted the old one. In a warm, unventilated Bronx basement the supply is often the part that's actually cooked — hang new hardware off it blind and you've bought a recorder twice.

Nor will it warn that big-box kit cable is frequently aluminum strand, not solid copper, and may not carry higher resolutions without re-termination. The summary treats "reuse the coax" as automatic; on a real Bronx job it's a test we run before trusting a single riser run.

These blind spots are how a confident DIYer ends up calling a pro anyway. We meter the supply and the runs before wiring anything new to them.

Recorder Upgrade vs. ADT, Ring & SimpliSafe

All three national brands want you on their cameras and their monthly cloud. Every one of them ignores the wired cameras already on your walls. A recorder upgrade is the only option that treats your existing equipment as an asset instead of something to replace.

vs. ADT

ADT is a monitoring company first — a contract and a monthly bill, usually paired with their own equipment. Older BNC coax cameras generally don't fit that ecosystem, so the ADT answer tends to be a new proprietary system rather than a recorder that keeps what you have. Our recorder upgrade reuses your cameras and coax, records locally, and carries no monthly fee. Where ADT can make sense is if you actually want 24/7 professional monitoring bundled in; we don't provide central-station monitoring, so if that's the priority, that's a real difference to weigh.

vs. Ring

Ring is built around Wi-Fi cameras and cloud video, billed monthly. Good for a renter or a single doorway; a poor fit for a wired multi-camera building. Ring won't record your existing BNC cameras at all, it leans on your Wi-Fi staying up, and footage lives in Ring's cloud on a subscription rather than on a drive you own. For a Bronx landlord with eight hallway and entrance cameras on coax, that's a downgrade in reliability and a new recurring bill.

vs. SimpliSafe

SimpliSafe is a DIY alarm and camera kit, again subscription-driven for meaningful camera features. Like Ring, it doesn't record your existing analog cameras. If you're starting from nothing in a small apartment, it's reasonable. If you already have a wired system and only the recorder failed, buying a whole new DIY ecosystem to replace working cameras is money spent to go backwards. The recorder swap is the proportionate fix.

What drives the price up or down

Three variables move the number more than anything else. Channel count: a 4-channel box is cheaper than a 16- or 32-channel unit, and you want to size for your current cameras plus a little headroom. Sizing your storage, roughly

A useful rule of thumb: about 2TB holds roughly 30 days of motion-only recording for four 4MP cameras under H.265+, or closer to a week on continuous. FAQ

DVR Upgrade Questions

Can I upgrade my DVR without replacing my cameras?
+

Yes. Most analog HD cameras (TVI, CVI, AHD) work with a new hybrid recorder over your existing coax. We confirm the signal type first, then match a recorder that keeps every camera you already have.

How much does a DVR upgrade cost in the Bronx?
+

Most residential DVR upgrades in the Bronx run roughly $450 to $1,200 installed, depending on channel count, hard drive size, and how much BNC re-termination is needed. Commercial 16 and 32-channel jobs run higher.

Should I replace my DVR or switch to an NVR?
+

If your coax and analog cameras are healthy, a hybrid DVR/XVR is the cheaper path and reuses your wiring. If you want 4K on every channel, color night vision, and license-plate detail, moving to an NVR with IP cameras is the better long-term choice.

Will a new DVR improve my camera image quality?
+

A new recorder lets older cameras record at their full native resolution and frame rate, and adds H.265 compression for longer storage. It can't turn a 1080p camera into 4K, but many aging DVRs down-record cameras below their real capability.

Can a DVR upgrade add remote viewing to my phone?
+

Yes. Every recorder we install supports a free mobile app for live view and playback with no monthly fee. Older DVRs often lost app support years ago; a new unit restores reliable remote access.

Will my old cameras work with a new DVR?
+

Almost always, as long as we match the signal type. Modern pentabrid recorders auto-detect TVI, CVI, AHD, and CVBS per channel, so a mix of camera generations records on one box.

How long do DVRs last?
+

A DVR typically lasts 5 to 8 years. The hard drive is usually the first part to fail because it writes 24/7. Rebooting loops, missing footage, and app dropouts are the common end-of-life signs.

What is the difference between a DVR and an NVR?
+

A DVR records analog cameras over coax and processes video at the recorder. An NVR records IP cameras over Ethernet, where each camera encodes its own video. DVRs reuse coax; NVRs need Cat6 and PoE.

Can a DVR upgrade increase my recording storage?
+

Yes. Newer recorders support larger surveillance drives (up to 10TB+ per bay) and H.265+ compression, which can multiply your retention from days to weeks or months without adding cameras.

My DVR keeps rebooting and losing footage. Is that fixable?
+

Reboot loops and gaps in footage usually mean a failing hard drive or a dying power supply inside the DVR. Sometimes a drive swap fixes it; often the recorder itself is at end of life and a replacement is more reliable.

Can you keep my existing coax cable?
+

In most Bronx buildings, yes. Solid-copper RG59 coax carries HD analog fine. We test the runs first. Thin pre-made cable from big-box kits sometimes needs re-termination or replacement.

Do you install the hard drive too?
+

Yes. We supply and install a surveillance-rated drive (WD Purple or Seagate SkyHawk) sized to your retention goal. Desktop drives are never used because they fail under 24/7 recording.
