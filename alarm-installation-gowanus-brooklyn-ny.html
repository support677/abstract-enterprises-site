The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Alarm Installation

Wikipedia draws Gowanus as a compact rectangle in what used to be called South Brooklyn: Wyckoff Street on the north, Fourth Avenue on the east, the elevated Gowanus Expressway on the south and Bond Street on the west, all inside Brooklyn Community District 6 and ZIP code 11215. The canal runs down the middle. It began as Gowanus Creek, where Dutch farmers settled in 1636 and tide-powered gristmills once turned, and in the 1860s it was dug out as an industrial waterway lined with factories, coal-gas works and shipping yards. The combined sewer system emptied into its head, and for more than a century the neighborhood was defined by a polluted canal and the industry along it. After the Second World War much of that industry left, and in the late 1940s the city built public housing nearby, partly for returning veterans.

What stayed behind is the building stock an alarm technician meets today. Brick and concrete factories and warehouses from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries stand beside one- and two-story garages, auto shops and yards, and the blocks at the eastern and western edges shade into the row houses of Park Slope and Carroll Gardens. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated five of the old industrial buildings in October 2019: the Somers Brothers tinware factory of 1884 on Third Street, later the American Can Company; the Brooklyn Rapid Transit Company’s central power station engine house of 1901–03 on Second Street; the 1911 flushing tunnel pumping station and gate house on Butler Street; the Montauk Paint Manufacturing Company building of 1908 on Second Avenue; and the ASPCA’s Brooklyn shelter and garage, opened in 1913, also on Butler Street. In the 2010s, Wikipedia notes, coworking spaces multiplied in the old buildings, followed by restaurants, bars and galleries.

Then came the rezoning. After years of planning, the City Council approved it in November 2021, and Wikipedia and Commercial Observer both put the plan at roughly 8,500 new apartments, more than 3,000 of them affordable. The list Commercial Observer published in January 2026 reads like a map of active scaffolds: a 27-story tower designed by Bjarke Ingels Group at 175 Third Street with more than 1,000 rentals, expected around 2029; Union Channel at 240 Third Avenue, finished in early 2025; Douglass Port at 251 Douglass Street, due in 2026; two Nevins Landing buildings at 310 and 340 Nevins Street; 360 units at 420 Carroll Street; a 20-story building at 450 Union Street due in 2027. The same article quotes a broker who says Gowanus land that traded for about $40 a foot in 2011 now sells for around $300. Meanwhile the EPA, which designated the canal a Superfund site in 2010, is building bulkhead supports along the lower canal before dredging it, work its December 2025 update expects to take two years.

For anyone protecting a house, a studio or a shop, all that construction does three things. It puts scaffolds, sidewalk sheds and temporary platforms next to buildings that never had anything climbable beside them. It fills the neighborhood with tools, copper and equipment that are worth stealing and with contractors and delivery crews who are hard to tell apart from people who should not be there. And it shakes, digs and dusts the buildings that stay, which is exactly the kind of disturbance that makes old alarm sensors misbehave. The theft risk is not theoretical. In January 2015, Brooklyn Paper reported, someone walked off with a DeWalt drill, a box of door handles, smoke detectors, strobe lights and a $1,000 security camera from a new office on Second Avenue near the canal before it had opened; police suspected an inside job. In July 2026, Hoodline reported an NYPD bulletin about a suspect linked to eleven commercial burglaries across brownstone Brooklyn, Gowanus among them, who got in through basement-level doors and took cash from offices and safes.

Water is the other constant. Gowanus sits low beside the canal, and its combined sewers still overflow when storms overwhelm them. On September 29, 2023, Hyperallergic reported, about fifty basement studios at 540 President Street, between Third and Fourth Avenues, took half a foot of water, ruining finished work artists had stored on the floor. A resident told BK Reader in 2026 that her Gowanus basement took about two feet during Hurricane Ida. The city has responded in pieces: a $54 million storm sewer along Third Avenue finished in 2023, two large overflow tanks on the canal due in 2029, and flood kits for basement residents handed out at a town hall in June 2026. An alarm here cannot pretend the basement is a dry room, which is why the panel never lives on a Gowanus basement floor and water sensors are offered alongside burglary sensors.

Many Gowanus customers combine the alarm with a camera on the street door or the yard, or with fob entry for a shared studio building. Camera work is on our Gowanus security camera installation page, and both can be done on the same visit.

Whole-house alarms for row houses

Temporary sensors for scaffold season

Extra contacts or glass-break detectors on windows a neighbor’s scaffold, shed or platform has brought within reach, grouped together so they can alert at night and be removed cleanly when the scaffold comes down.

Studio and workshop alarms

A panel for one studio inside a shared building, with a heavy-duty contact on the steel door, ceiling motion detection, optional tilt sensors on stored work, and codes only for the people who rent the room.

Construction-related fault fixes

Water sensors on alarm panels

Fit-out alarms before opening

Shop and restaurant alarms

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Gowanus’s Architecture

Few Brooklyn neighborhoods pack so many kinds of building into so few blocks. A single walk from Bond Street to Fourth Avenue can pass a brownstone, a garage, a converted factory full of studios, a new glass tower and a public housing development. Each one asks different things of an alarm.

Row houses on the edges

Brick and brownstone houses at the Park Slope and Carroll Gardens ends of the neighborhood, often with a garden apartment, a cellar door under the stoop and a deep rear yard that now backs onto a construction lot.

Converted factories and warehouses

Multi-story brick and concrete industrial buildings divided into studios, workshops and offices, with shared corridors, freight elevators, steel doors and large multi-pane windows.

One- and two-story garages and shops

Auto repair bays, supply yards, small manufacturers and storage buildings with roll-up doors, skylights and yards behind fences, many of them on lots the rezoning has made valuable.

New canal-side towers

Rental and condominium buildings finished since 2024 or still under construction, with landlord-run entry systems, large glass at the ground floor and waterfront walkways open to the public.

Mixed-use buildings on Fourth Avenue

Apartment buildings with ground-floor stores along the avenue’s subway corridor, where a shop’s back door, cellar and the apartments above all share one structure.

Studios below street level

Basement studio complexes in older buildings between Third and Fourth Avenues, reached down a stair from the street or from a lobby, often without windows and close to the water table.

How the method changes from one building to the next

  • Row houses: the rear of the house is re-assessed against whatever now stands behind it. A lot that was a parking yard and is now a scaffolded tower changes which upper-floor windows count as reachable, and those windows go into a separate, temporary group.
  • Converted factories: each tenant’s studio is a separate system or a separate area on a shared panel, never one alarm for the whole floor. Heavy-duty contacts go on steel doors, and ceiling-mounted motion detectors are chosen for tall rooms where a wall unit would look over everyone’s head.
  • Garages and shops: roll-up doors get overhead-door contacts at the floor, skylights and roof hatches get contacts or shock sensors, and yards get a camera rather than an outdoor motion detector, because cats, trash and wind in an open yard make outdoor motion sensors cry wolf.
  • New towers: nothing is wired into the building’s systems. A wireless panel with its own cellular communicator sits inside the apartment or store, glass-break detection covers the big fixed panes, and every part can be removed with no trace at the end of a lease.
  • Mixed-use Fourth Avenue buildings: the store’s alarm protects its own doors, cellar and back room, and its siren is kept inside and capped short, so it does not wake the apartments above on every false trip.
  • Basement studios: the panel and communicator go high on a wall or on the floor above, the siren is placed where people on the stair will hear it, and water sensors are offered at the drains, because in a Gowanus basement the next intruder may be the sewer.
Scaffold Season

Living Next to a Construction Site: Sensors That Come and Go With the Scaffold

Most alarm designs assume the outside of a building stays the same for years. In Gowanus it can change in a month. A three-story row house on the side streets between Third and Fourth Avenues was designed in an era when the lot behind it held a garage or a warehouse yard. Today that lot may hold excavation, then foundations, then a steel or concrete frame rising floor by floor, wrapped in pipe scaffold and netting that reach past every rear window of the house next door. The same is true of the old industrial buildings that remain between the new towers: a studio window that looked onto empty air can, for a year or two, look onto a working platform a few feet away.

We do not treat that as a reason to put a sensor on every window forever. We treat it as a temporary change in what is reachable. On the survey, the technician stands at each rear and side window and asks one question: could someone standing on anything outside this window, including a scaffold plank, a shed roof, a hoist landing or a neighbor’s new roof deck, open or break it? Every window that gets a yes goes into a group of its own on the panel. That group can be set to alert you even when the rest of the house is in stay mode at night, and when the scaffold comes down it is either removed or switched off in one step, with no rewiring.

Construction also creates false alarms, and an alarm that cries wolf every afternoon soon gets switched off. Three causes come up again and again. First, vibration: pile driving, rock breaking and heavy trucks can rattle loose windows and shake old shock sensors into tripping, so we avoid shock sensors on windows next to an active site and use glass-break detectors that listen for the specific sound of breaking glass. Second, settlement: excavation and underpinning beside an old house can move a door frame enough to misalign a contact, so we use wider-gap magnets on doors facing a site and check the gap again after the foundation work ends. Third, dust: demolition and cutting dust gets into smoke detectors and onto motion lenses, so we tell owners how to clean them and keep a log of trouble signals that shows when the dust began.

There is a paperwork side, too. If your house is damaged by work next door, the developer’s insurer will want dates. An alarm’s event history records when a door first began reporting faults, when a window sensor tripped and when the panel lost power. We show owners how to export that history before anything is claimed, and we note in writing any sensor we had to re-set because a frame moved. We do not give engineering opinions about the cause of cracks; we simply keep the record straight.

Finally, a building site is itself a target. The tools, copper and equipment brought onto a site are worth stealing, and once a new building is enclosed but not yet occupied, its ground-floor spaces are full of valuable fixtures with nobody inside. The 2015 theft from the unopened office on Second Avenue, where a drill, door hardware, smoke detectors, strobes and a security camera disappeared before the tenant moved in, is a small Gowanus example. For a shop or café being fitted out, we install a battery-backed cellular alarm as soon as there is a lockable door, then move the same panel to its permanent home when the store opens.

Studios and Makers

Studios Behind Steel Doors: Alarming One Room in a Building Full of Them

Gowanus has long been one of Brooklyn’s densest concentrations of artists and fabricators. In 2017 Arts Gowanus ran a survey to count how many artists worked in the neighborhood and how many had already left; DNAinfo reported about 400 responses within its first days. The rezoning tried to keep some of them: according to Commercial Observer, Arts Gowanus reached community benefit agreements with eleven developers for roughly 100 to 120 permanently affordable studios, with rent increases capped at two percent a year after the second year. Powerhouse Arts, which New York YIMBY reported opened in 2023 in the restored 1904 Brooklyn Rapid Transit power station at 322 Third Avenue, has wood, metal, ceramics, textile and print shops under one roof. And the old American Can Company factory on Third Street, a city landmark since 2019, was described by the Brooklyn Eagle in 2020 as an arts and cultural home when the Gowanus Canal Conservancy moved in.

An alarm for a studio in a building like that has to respect a basic fact: you control one room, and dozens of other people have a right to be in the corridor outside it at any hour. Artists work at night. Fabricators come in at six. Somebody is always loading out for a show. So a studio alarm is not built around a front door that should be closed after dark. It is built around your door, your room and, often, the work itself. A heavy-duty contact on your door, a ceiling motion detector covering the room, and your own codes for the people who share your space mean the system knows the difference between the building being busy and your studio being entered.

For finished work, small tilt and shock sensors can be fixed to crates, framed pieces or plinths and left armed even while you are working, so a piece that is lifted or tipped sends an alert without the rest of the studio needing to be armed. For shared workshops, each member can have a personal code, and the system records who disarmed and when. For a studio reached by a freight elevator that opens directly into the space, we protect the landing area on your side with a motion detector rather than touching the elevator, whose doors and interlocks belong to the building.

Old industrial buildings bring their own surfaces. Painted concrete block, rough brick and steel do not hold adhesive the way drywall does, so on masonry we fix small sensors with anchors in mortar joints, which are easy to fill when you move, and we agree each fixing before drilling. Signal is another question. Thick walls and basements can weaken cellular reception, so before the panel is mounted we test the signal where it will live and, if it is weak, place the communicator higher in the building, near a window, or on an external antenna where the owner allows it.

Studios below ground deserve one more paragraph. The fifty basement studios that flooded at 540 President Street in 2023 are a reminder that in this part of Brooklyn the panel, the battery, the siren and the communicator should never sit on the floor of a basement studio. We put them high on a wall, preferably on a stair landing or the floor above, and offer water sensors at the floor drains and at the bottom of the stairs, reporting through the same app as the burglary sensors. Hyperallergic quoted one painter who lost work wrapped in plastic and cardboard on the floor; an alarm cannot keep water out, but it can tell you at two in the morning that it has arrived.

Water From Below

When the Threat Comes Up Through the Floor Drain: Water Sensors on a Gowanus Alarm

Most of Brooklyn worries about water coming in the door. In the low blocks near the Gowanus Canal the more common story is water coming up from below. Wikipedia explains that the canal has received combined sewer overflows for generations, and when a storm overwhelms those sewers, the backup does not respect property lines: it can rise through floor drains, toilets and basement sinks well before the street itself floods. That is how a basement can take water on a day when the sidewalk outside looks only wet.

The city has been chipping away at the problem. The Department of Environmental Protection announced in July 2023 that a $54 million project had laid more than a mile of new storm sewer, 7,118 feet, under Third Avenue between Carroll and State Streets and along the cross streets to Fourth Avenue, and that there were no sewer backups or street flooding along Third Avenue during the following July 4 storms. BK Reader reported in 2026 that two large underground tanks on the canal, meant to hold excess combined sewage during storms, are expected to be finished in 2029. At a June 2026 town hall attended by about a hundred people, basement residents received flood kits worth roughly $300 each, with a flood sensor, a sump pump, door and window barriers and plugs for drains and toilets.

None of that removes the need to know quickly when water arrives. A flood sensor on the alarm panel sits on the floor at the lowest point, near a floor drain or at the foot of the basement stair, and reports through the same app and call list as the burglary sensors. For a studio, a shop’s cellar stockroom or a garden apartment, that alert can be the difference between lifting work off the floor and finding it ruined in the morning. We place the panel itself well above the floor, keep its power supply and battery out of reach of standing water, and test that the cellular communicator still reports with the basement door shut.

Water sensors are not a substitute for the city’s kit or for the steps the DEP recommends, such as keeping valuables on high shelves and avoiding running taps during storms. They are an addition. If you already have a stand-alone sensor that only beeps or only talks to its own app, we leave it in place and add an alarm-panel sensor beside it, so one wakes whoever is home and the other reaches whoever is not. And we always ask customers to check their insurance; as BK Reader noted, ordinary homeowners and renters policies generally do not cover flood damage.

New Buildings

The New Towers on the Canal: What an Apartment or Ground-Floor Store Still Needs

People moving into the new Gowanus buildings sometimes assume the building has security covered. Up to a point, it does: entry systems, lobby staff in some buildings, cameras in common areas and app-controlled locks on apartment doors. But each of those systems belongs to the landlord or the condominium, reports to management, and protects the building’s shared spaces. What happens inside your apartment or your store when you are not there is still your concern, and a resident usually has no say over how the building’s own systems are configured.

An apartment alarm in a new building is therefore deliberately separate. A wireless panel with its own cellular communicator plugs into an ordinary outlet. A contact on the inside of the apartment door works beside the landlord’s smart lock without touching it. Glass-break detectors cover the floor-to-ceiling panes that new buildings use, since tall fixed glass has no opening sash to put a contact on. Everything mounts on removable adhesive and comes away cleanly at the end of the lease. Nothing joins the building’s network, intercom or fire alarm.

Ground-floor stores in the new buildings are a different case. They face public sidewalks and, along the canal, public walkways: Commercial Observer describes a 5,700-square-foot esplanade at 450 Union Street and a 28,000-square-foot waterfront park at 175 Third Street. That means foot traffic past the glass at all hours, so outdoor motion detectors are rarely useful. The store alarm relies on glass-break detection behind the windows, contacts on the doors, an interior motion detector and, often, a panic button by the counter. During the fit-out, before opening, the same panel runs on battery and cellular to protect equipment as it arrives.

Compliance notes for Gowanus businesses

  • Siren limit: New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building burglar alarm to be able to stop sounding automatically within fifteen minutes. We set store sirens far shorter, especially under apartments.
  • Licensed installers: businesses that install alarm systems in New York State must hold a Department of State security or fire alarm installer license. Ours is #12000287431, and we show it before work starts.
  • Fire alarms are separate: a burglar alarm is never merged with a building’s fire alarm system. Fire alarm work is its own trade with its own approvals.
  • Landlord equipment: in multi-tenant and new buildings, we do not drill into or wire to the landlord’s doors, elevators, network or entry system without written approval. Your alarm sits inside your space.
  • Basement and cellar doors: after the 2026 burglary series Hoodline reported, in which a suspect entered businesses through basement-level doors, every Gowanus commercial survey includes the cellar door, the sidewalk stair and the office where cash or a safe is kept.

Alarm Installation in Gowanus: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up in Gowanus specifically: about living beside construction, working in shared industrial buildings, the canal and its water, and the new buildings going up along it.

When the lot next door becomes a tower

A twenty-story building is going up on the lot behind our row house, and its scaffold now rises past our back windows. Do windows nobody could reach last year need sensors now?

Usually, yes, for as long as the scaffold stands. A rear window on the third floor of a Gowanus row house was out of reach when the lot behind it held a one-story warehouse; with pipe scaffold and planking a few feet away, it is a step from a working platform. The 2017 case DNAinfo reported on the Upper East Side, where a burglar used façade scaffolding and fire escapes to reach five apartments, is the pattern to keep in mind, even though it happened in Manhattan. We add a contact or glass-break detector to each window the scaffold brings within reach, put those sensors in their own group so they can be removed from the plan when the scaffold comes down, and set the group to alert you at night even when the rest of the house is only in stay mode.

Since the excavation next door began, our front door sticks and the alarm keeps reporting it open at random. Is the construction moving the door?

It may be. Digging and underpinning beside an old brick house can shift a frame by a fraction of an inch, and a door contact only needs its magnet to drift a little to start flickering between open and closed. Before we touch the alarm we check the gap at the strike side with the door shut and compare it with the hinge side; if the frame has moved, the contact is re-set with a wider-gap magnet and the event history is saved so you have a dated record of when the faults began. That record belongs with any claim you make to the developer’s insurer. The door itself, if it no longer latches, is a carpenter’s job first and an alarm job second, because a sensor on a door that will not close protects nothing.

Demolition dust from the site across the street gets into everything. Does it affect the smoke detectors and motion sensors tied to the alarm?

It can affect both. Fine dust in a photoelectric smoke chamber is a known cause of nuisance alarms, and a film of dust on a motion detector’s lens makes it less sensitive. During heavy demolition or excavation nearby we suggest checking the panel’s trouble log weekly, blowing out smoke detectors gently with compressed air as the maker allows, and wiping the motion lenses with a dry cloth. If a smoke detector keeps false-alarming once the dusty phase is over, it is replaced rather than disabled. Never cover a smoke detector with a bag or tape and forget it; the HPD rules on smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in rentals assume they are working every day.

A sidewalk shed in front of our storefront will be up for months and hides the shop from the street. Does that change how the alarm should be set up?

It does, because the shed takes away the passer-by. A storefront in plain view of Third or Fourth Avenue relies partly on people noticing someone at the gate; under a shed, with plywood and posts in front of the glass, nobody sees much. We add glass-break detection behind the window so the alarm reacts before anyone is inside, move the panel and siren away from anything that can be reached through broken glass, and pair the alarm with a camera that sees the gate from inside so an alert can be checked in seconds. When the shed comes down, the camera stays useful and the extra detector simply keeps working.

Studios, shops and freight doors in the old industrial buildings

Finished work for a show sits wrapped and stacked against our studio wall for weeks. Can a sensor on the work itself tell us if a piece is moved?

Yes. Small wireless tilt or shock sensors, the same size as a window contact, can be fixed to the back of a crate, a framed piece or a plinth with removable mounting, and set to report if the object is lifted, tipped or knocked. They are useful in a shared studio building where the door may be opened by people with every right to be there, but nobody should be moving your work. We put them in a group of their own that can stay armed while you are in the studio, so you can work beside the stack without disarming it, and we name each sensor in the app after the piece so an alert tells you exactly what moved.

Our studio has no windows and one steel door onto a shared basement corridor. Is a single door contact enough?

It is the most important part, but it should not be the only one. A windowless basement studio has one ordinary way in, and a contact on that door catches it, but a steel door is often in a stud partition, and a thin partition wall can be cut through faster than a heavy door can be forced. A ceiling-mounted motion detector covering the room catches anyone who arrives some other way, and a heavy-duty contact on the steel door, rather than a small plastic one, survives being slammed by everyone on the corridor. If the studio shares a ceiling plenum with its neighbors, we say so in the quote, because a dropped ceiling can be a route too.

A freight elevator in our building opens straight into our space. How do you put an elevator door on the alarm?

Not by touching the elevator. The hoistway doors, their interlocks and their wiring belong to the building and its elevator contractor, and nobody else should drill or wire into them. What we protect is the opening on your side: a motion detector aimed across the landing area inside your space, or a wireless contact on a gate or partition door you control, so that the elevator arriving with someone in it is noticed the moment they step out. If the building can lock out your floor at night, ask the owner to do that as well; the alarm is the second line, not the first.

Our café will open in one of the new canal-side buildings, but permanent electric service is not on yet. Can an alarm run before it is?

Yes, for a while. A wireless panel with a cellular communicator runs on its backup battery for many hours, and on a construction-power outlet or a small battery pack for longer, so a fit-out space can be protected from the day expensive equipment arrives. That matters in Gowanus: in 2015 Brooklyn Paper reported that a drill, door handles, smoke detectors, strobe lights and a $1,000 security camera, about $3,800 in all, were taken from a new office on Second Avenue before it even opened. When permanent power arrives, the same panel is moved to its final spot and plugged in; nothing is thrown away.

Water, bridges and the canal edge

At a Gowanus town hall the city handed out flood kits with their own water sensor. Can that kind of sensor report through our alarm instead of a separate app?

Sometimes directly, more often by replacement. BK Reader reported that basement-unit residents at a June 2026 town hall received kits worth about $300 that include a flood sensor, a sump pump, barriers and drain plugs. A stand-alone sensor usually talks only to its own app or beeps on the floor. If you would rather have water alerts arrive the same way as break-in alerts, we add a flood sensor made for your alarm panel beside the kit’s sensor; keep the kit’s barriers and plugs exactly as the city intended. Two sensors on the same floor drain are not wasted: one wakes you, the other tells the people on your call list.

When the Union Street or Third Street bridge is closed for the canal cleanup, does it slow down the response to our side of the canal?

We cannot speak for the police or the fire department, and they plan their own routes. What the EPA’s December 2025 update does say is that bridge closures happen regularly at Hamilton Avenue and Ninth Street and occasionally at Third Street and Union Street while bulkheads are built, and that the Carroll Street Bridge has been shut for repairs since April 2025. What you can control is your own call list. Put a keyholder who lives or works on your side of the canal first, so that the person who checks on an alarm is not the one stuck on the wrong bank, and keep the schedule of closures, which the EPA posts online, in mind when you travel.

New apartments on the canal

The smart lock on our new apartment is run by the landlord’s app. Can a door sensor work alongside a lock we do not control?

Yes. A wireless contact on the inside face of the door, with its magnet on the frame, does not touch the lock, its battery or its wiring, and it reports to your panel regardless of how the door was unlocked. That separation is useful: the landlord’s system knows when the lock was operated and by which credential, while your alarm knows when the door actually opened and whether that was expected. We mount the contact on the hinge side or the top of the door, away from the lock body, with removable adhesive that comes off cleanly at the end of the lease.

Our new apartment has floor-to-ceiling windows facing a canal esplanade that will open to the public. Does a public walkway outside change the plan?

It changes it in two ways. Commercial Observer describes new buildings with public waterfront space, including a 5,700-square-foot esplanade at 450 Union Street and a waterfront park at 175 Third Street. A ground-floor or second-floor unit on a public walkway gets glass-break detection on the large panes, because tall fixed glass rarely has a sash to put a contact on, and outdoor motion detectors are usually left out, since a public path would set them off all night. Instead, the inside of the glass is the line: the alarm reacts when glass breaks or a door opens, not when somebody walks a dog along the water.

The affordable artist studio promised under the rezoning’s community agreements will be in a brand-new building. Who is responsible for securing it, us or the developer?

That is set by the lease, not by us, so read it before you buy anything. Typically a landlord secures the building entrances, the common corridors and the fire alarm, and a tenant secures what is inside its own space. An alarm for the studio itself should therefore be wireless, report over cellular rather than the building network, and come out without damage, so it stays yours whatever the building’s management decides. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Gowanus

We could not load Google’s AI Overview from where this page was built, so the claims below are paraphrases of advice that circulates widely in AI answers and on contractor-listing sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, not quotations. Each is followed by what we see on Gowanus jobs.

“An alarm design should be based on the building itself”

It sounds obvious, and for most of the borough it is good advice. The typical answer walks through doors, ground-floor windows, the basement and the back yard, and stops at the property line. In Gowanus that leaves out the most changeable factor: the lots around you. A house that was well protected in 2021 may now have a scaffold within reach of its rear windows, an excavation shifting its door frames, or a new building’s roof deck looking straight into its top floor.

The survey we run here therefore starts by walking the outside of the block, not just the building. We note every scaffold, shed, hoist and new roof that touches what a burglar could reach, and we ask how long it is expected to stay. Commercial Observer’s list of projects due between 2026 and 2029 is a reminder that for many Gowanus houses this is not a short phase.

The result is an alarm in two parts: the permanent design for the building, and a temporary layer for its surroundings, priced separately and removed when it is no longer needed. Advice that ignores the neighbors either leaves windows uncovered or adds sensors you will be paying to remove for no reason later.

“Wireless sensors stick anywhere”

Product listings and AI summaries often promise peel-and-stick installation on any surface. On drywall and painted wood trim, that holds. In converted Gowanus factories, studios are often painted concrete block, rough brick, steel or old plaster with flaking paint, and adhesive pads let go within weeks, usually in the first humid spell of summer.

When a sensor falls, a well-designed panel reports a tamper or a fault; a cheap one may simply report the window as open and refuse to arm. Either way the system is out of action until someone puts it back, which in a studio you visit twice a week may be days.

We fix sensors to masonry with small screws in plastic anchors, set in mortar joints wherever possible because those are easy to fill, or onto a small plate bonded to a smoothed patch. In rental studios every fixing is agreed with you in advance and recorded on the parts list, so move-out repairs are predictable.

“Shock sensors are the best protection for windows”

Shock or vibration sensors on window frames are often recommended because they react to a window being struck before the glass gives way. That is true in a quiet street. Beside an active construction site, with pile driving, rock breaking and loaded trucks, the same sensor can trip every afternoon, and an alarm that false-alarms daily is soon left off.

Old wood and steel windows in Gowanus industrial buildings rattle more than modern ones, which makes the problem worse. Lowering the sensitivity to stop the false alarms usually means the sensor no longer reacts to a real attempt either.

Near building sites we prefer acoustic glass-break detectors, which listen for the particular sound pattern of breaking glass rather than for any movement, combined with contacts on windows that open. When the construction ends, shock sensors can be added if a window really calls for one.

“Every room in a shared building should be on one system”

Advice written for single-family homes often suggests one panel for the whole property, with every door and window on it. Applied to a Gowanus building divided into studios, that would mean one alarm for many tenants who keep different hours, with whoever arms last deciding whether everyone else can get in.

It also raises the question of who holds the master code and who receives the alerts, and in a building with turnover every few months, nobody wants that responsibility.

The practical answer is one system per tenant, or a panel with separate areas, each with its own codes and its own alerts, and nothing that affects the common corridor. The building owner can protect the street door, roof and cellar on a separate system of their own if they wish.

“A new building doesn’t need a personal alarm”

Some answers suggest that a modern building with an entry system, cameras and app-controlled locks makes a personal alarm unnecessary. Those building systems are real, but they belong to the landlord, report to management and are configured for the building’s purposes, not yours.

Lock logs show which credential opened a door; they do not alert you when your door opens while you are away, or when someone breaks a ground-floor pane on the esplanade side. Lobby cameras watch the lobby, not your apartment or store.

A separate wireless alarm with its own cellular link, working beside the building’s systems without touching them, fills that gap and leaves with you when you move. For ground-floor shops in new buildings, the gap is wider, because those spaces face public sidewalks and walkways all night.

“Monitoring always means a long contract”

AI answers frequently present monitoring as a multi-year agreement with an installation fee folded in. That is one business model among several, and it is a poor fit for studio tenants on short leases or renters in new buildings who may move in a year or two.

With our installations you own the equipment outright from the day it is paid for. The app, alerts and remote arming carry no monthly fee from us.

Central station monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month and can be started or stopped month to month. Many Gowanus studios use phone alerts only and add monitoring when they store work for a show or travel.

“Anyone with basic tools can install an alarm”

Self-installation of a boxed kit in your own home is legal and common. Installing alarm systems as a business in New York State, however, requires a security or fire alarm installer license from the Department of State, which involves qualifying education, an examination and fingerprinting.

Asking to see a license matters more in Gowanus than in many places, because so much work here is done by people already on site for other trades. A general contractor’s electrician wiring a burglar alarm in a new store as a favor may be skilled, but licensing and responsibility for the system are separate questions.
