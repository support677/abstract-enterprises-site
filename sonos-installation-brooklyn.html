The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Homes Need Sonos Installation

In Brooklyn the hard part of a Sonos job is usually not one room. It is the shape of the house. A classic Brooklyn rowhouse is narrow and deep: a renovation guide from the New York architects Havard Cooper Architecture describes the brownstone footprint as "almost always 20 feet wide and 40 to 50 feet deep," and a long-running Brownstoner forum thread on house sizes puts 20 feet as the standard width, anything under 20 as narrow and 25 or more as wide. Stack three or four of those floors on top of a cellar and you have a building that is short in one direction and very long in the other two. A router that covers a suburban ranch house easily is asked to push a signal 45 feet from the street windows to the garden windows, and then up through several plaster ceilings.

The internet does not arrive in the middle of that building. It arrives wherever the provider's line enters, which in a rowhouse is frequently the cellar or the garden level. A Brownstoner member giving advice on a Fios installation described the result simply: the fibre terminal "goes in the cellar," and Ethernet cable is used to connect access points around the house. A poster on Reddit's r/HomeNetworking, writing about a four-floor New York brownstone, made the same point from the other side: the channel was already the least busy one, but "the problem is really that it doesn't reach far enough through the house." Sonos speakers sit at the end of that weak link. They drop out on the top floor, lag behind the parlor floor when grouped, or vanish from the app altogether.

Then there is the second household. Brooklyn has a great many two- and three-family houses where the owner lives on some floors and rents out the rest. Across the city, the Comptroller's office counts roughly 225,000 two-family structures, where the homeowner typically lives in one unit and rents the other. Sonos has a setting called Guest Access, which Sonos says lets any device on the same Wi-Fi network control playback, volume and grouping, and which Sonos switches on by default. In a house where the tenant shares the owner's Wi-Fi, that means the tenant's phone can see, and play to, the owner's speakers. Few people find this out until it happens. A Brooklyn Sonos plan has to decide early whose network is whose.

The fourth piece is the yard. Most Brooklyn houses have one, and plenty of condo conversions have a shared roof deck. People want music out there, which means outdoor speakers, an Amp somewhere indoors, a cable path through a brick rear wall, and some thought about the neighbors whose gardens are a few feet away on each side. In a historic district, the rear facade is part of what the Landmarks Preservation Commission looks after, so anything fixed to the outside of the house gets checked before the drill comes out.

The newer half of the borough brings the opposite set of problems. Since the 2004 rezoning, Downtown Brooklyn has added close to 27,000 housing units, and the 2005 Greenpoint–Williamsburg waterfront rezoning turned the East River edge into a line of towers. Those apartments are concrete boxes with drywall partitions, sometimes with a structured-media panel in a closet, often with dozens of neighbors' networks visible from the couch. Ceiling speakers are rarely possible there, but a well-placed soundbar, a Sub and a pair of Era speakers, on a network you control, can sound excellent.

So the Brooklyn Sonos visit starts with three questions before anyone opens a box: where does the network need to be on each floor, which speakers belong to which household, and does the music need to reach the yard or the roof? Answer those and the speakers themselves are quick work.

Brooklyn figures that shape a Sonos plan

  • 20 feet by 40 to 50 feet: the typical brownstone footprint described by Havard Cooper Architecture's renovation guide — a long front-to-back distance for Wi-Fi on every floor, multiplied by three or four floors.
  • 1,141,824: housing units in Kings County as of July 1, 2025, according to the Census Bureau's QuickFacts, with an owner-occupied rate of 29.5 percent for 2020–2024. Most Brooklyn Sonos systems are in rented homes, which shapes what can be drilled.
  • About 225,000: two-family structures across New York City, per the City Comptroller's housing market spotlight — the building type where owner and tenant most often share an internet line and, without planning, a Sonos system.
  • Close to 27,000: new housing units added in Downtown Brooklyn since its 2004 rezoning, as reported in 2025 — concrete towers where Sonos lives on wireless speakers and a private network.
  • On by default: Sonos's Guest Access setting, which lets any device on the same Wi-Fi control playback, volume and grouping. In a shared house it is the first setting we discuss.
  • 1965, 1973, 1978: the designation years of the Brooklyn Heights, Park Slope and Fort Greene historic districts — three of the borough's largest concentrations of rowhouses, where outdoor speakers on a facade need a landmarks check.

Those numbers describe the typical Brooklyn call: a tall, narrow house or a tower apartment, a network that ends before the house does, often a second household under the same roof, and a garden or deck that the owner wants to hear music in by next summer.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Brooklyn's Architecture

Brooklyn has more kinds of houses than most American cities, and each one changes the Sonos plan. These are the building types our crews meet most often, and what each one means for the network, the speakers and the wire.

Brownstone and Brick Rowhouses

Three or four floors over a cellar, plaster ceilings on wood joists that span from party wall to party wall, and stairs at one side. The network goes floor by floor; ceiling speaker positions follow the joists; cable rises in closets stacked above each other.

Two-Family Masonry Rowhouses

The Sunset Park, Bay Ridge, Crown Heights and Bushwick house with a flat on each floor. Two households, one internet entry point, and often one Wi-Fi network that both share. We split them before adding any speakers.

Wood-Frame Rowhouses and Victorians

Frame houses in Bed-Stuy, Greenpoint and Fort Greene, and the detached Victorians of Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South. Lighter walls for Wi-Fi, attics and crawl spaces for cable, and porches and side yards for outdoor speakers.

Pre-War Elevator Apartments

The older apartment houses along Ocean Parkway, Eastern Parkway and the avenues of Flatbush and Crown Heights. Plaster on masonry, a building with a super and sometimes a board, and an apartment that works much like a Manhattan co-op for Sonos purposes.

Waterfront and Downtown Towers

Concrete slabs, metal-stud partitions, glass walls and a crowded Wi-Fi environment in Downtown Brooklyn, Williamsburg and Greenpoint. Wireless Sonos on a private network, with the soundbar and surrounds placed around windows rather than walls.

Warehouse Lofts

DUMBO, Williamsburg and Bushwick conversions with timber or concrete ceilings, brick walls and open plans. Hard surfaces make Trueplay essential; cable usually runs in surface raceway or above the kitchen and bath.

How the floors of a rowhouse change the Sonos install

The cellar. This is where the internet usually arrives and where the cable for the rest of the house should start. A small wall-mounted switch or a shelf beside the provider's box becomes the hub: one cable up to each floor's access point, one to the Amp if the ceiling speakers are on the garden level, one to the wired Sonos product if the house needs one. Damp cellars need equipment off the floor and away from the water heater; we mount it on the wall or on a board.

The garden level. Often the kitchen and family room in an owner's triplex, or a separate rental unit in a two-family. If it is the owner's kitchen, it is the easiest place in the house for ceiling speakers, because the ceiling is shallower and sometimes already opened during a kitchen renovation. If it is a tenant's flat, nothing of the owner's system belongs there except, perhaps, a cable passing through a closet with the tenant's agreement.

The parlor floor. The tallest ceilings, the plaster cornices and centre medallions, and usually the TV. This is where people most want ceiling speakers and where we most often argue for wall-mounted or free-standing speakers instead, because cutting an 8.25-inch hole near decorative plaster risks damage that costs far more to repair than the speakers cost to buy. A soundbar under the TV with Era speakers on stands, or bookshelf speakers on an Amp hidden in a cabinet, preserves the room.

The upper floors. Bedrooms and a study under lower ceilings, often with closets stacked directly above each other on each floor. Those stacked closets are the natural cable riser: a hole through the closet floor, a cable through, a hole through the next. Each floor gets an access point on the hall ceiling and, where wanted, a pair of ceiling speakers or an Era 100 on a bracket.

The roof and the rear. Roof decks on rowhouses and condo conversions, and the garden behind the house. Outdoor speakers need a mounting surface, a cable path through a brick wall or a roof penetration, and an Amp inside. Roof penetrations are a roofer's work; we bring the cable to the inside face and coordinate with yours.

A brownstone renovation is the moment to plan Sonos for every floor, even the ones you won't finish this year. Our structured cabling crews in Brooklyn rough in network and speaker cable while the walls are open, and the AESS low-voltage team for Brooklyn handles the cameras, intercom and door hardware that usually go in during the same gut job.

One House, Two Households

Sonos in a Brooklyn Two-Family: Keeping the Systems Apart

This is the part of Brooklyn Sonos work that installers from elsewhere tend to miss. A two-family or three-family house has one roof, one cellar and very often one internet connection, but two or three separate lives going on inside it. Sonos is built around a single household per system. When the owner and the tenant share a network, their systems can collide in ways that range from mildly confusing to plainly intrusive.

The first collision is control. Sonos describes its Guest Access setting as controlling "whether devices connected to the same WiFi network as your Sonos system can control playback, volume, and grouping as guests without access to administrative features," and lists the default as on. In a single-family home that is a convenience: a visiting friend can queue a song without being added to your account. In a house where the tenant downstairs is on the same Wi-Fi, it means the tenant's phone can find your living room and turn it up. Most tenants would never do it on purpose; some apps and voice assistants will happily find the speakers for them.

The second collision is setup. If the tenant buys their own Sonos speaker and sets it up on the shared Wi-Fi, the app may offer to add it to the system that is already there — yours. Sonos community members who have tried to run two systems on one network describe the results bluntly: one long-time forum contributor wrote that when a phone is registered to both, "it could pick either of the systems to latch onto," and that "results will be frustratingly unpredictable." Sonos's own support article on connecting to more than one system describes it as a way to use Sonos at home and at a friend's or family member's home — in different places, on different networks.

The clean answer is a separate network for each household. In most two-family houses that does not mean a second internet bill. One modem in the cellar feeds a router that can run two isolated networks, or two routers on separate subnets, each with its own Wi-Fi name. The owner's access points and the owner's Sonos live on one; the tenant's devices live on the other. Neither household's speakers appear in the other's app. Guest Access can then stay on, because the only "guests" on each network are the people who actually live there and their visitors.

What we set up in a shared house

  • Two networks from one connection. Separate Wi-Fi names and passwords, isolated from each other, with the owner's Sonos on the owner's network only. If the tenant's lease includes internet, they get their own network name and nothing changes about their service.
  • A cable path that respects the tenant's flat. Where the owner's system needs a cable from the cellar to the upper floors, it rises through common areas or the owner's own closets. If the only route is through the rental unit, we ask you to get the tenant's agreement first, and we schedule around them.
  • Guest Access set on purpose. On for a family that likes visitors to play music; off for a household that wants its speakers to answer only to people signed in to the account.
  • Volume limits where floors meet. Sonos lets you set a maximum volume for each product in the app. On a floor that sits over or under the tenant's bedroom, we agree that ceiling with you on the day.
  • A written note of what belongs to whom. Which access points, switches and speakers are the owner's, and which network the tenant uses, so the next time someone moves out the handover is simple.

If the tenant wants Sonos of their own, the same visit can set up their system on their network. It is a separate setup, on their account, and they can book and pay for it directly.

Out the Back Door

Garden, Deck and Roof Speakers for Brooklyn Houses

Ask a Brooklyn homeowner which Sonos room they use most from May to September and many will name the one with no walls. The rear garden of a rowhouse, the deck off a kitchen extension, or the roof deck on a condo conversion is where people actually spend summer evenings. Getting good music out there is mostly a wiring and placement problem.

The speakers. Sonos sells an outdoor pair made with Sonance. Sonos lists it as IP66-rated and built for humidity, water, salt spray, heat, UV and freezing temperatures, from −13°F to 149°F, and designs it to be driven by a Sonos Amp. The speakers are passive: they need speaker wire back to an Amp, and the Amp lives indoors. Sonos's own guide to the Amp lists an operating range of 32° to 104°F, which in Brooklyn rules out an unheated shed or a rear vestibule in January.

The wire. In a masonry rowhouse the cable has to pass through the rear wall, usually from a closet or the back of a kitchen cabinet on the garden level. We drill through the brick at a slight downward slope toward the outside, sleeve the hole, seal it, and run the cable along the wall in a paintable raceway or behind a downspout to each speaker. On a wood-frame house the path is easier, through the rim joist or a window frame. On a roof deck the cable needs a roof or bulkhead penetration that a roofer seals; we bring the wire to the inside of the bulkhead and fix the speakers to the deck structure, never to the roofing.

The neighbors. Brooklyn gardens sit side by side with only a fence between them, and bedrooms face the yards. The city's Noise Code says no one may use a sound reproduction device in a way that creates unreasonable noise. We aim speakers down and into your own garden, away from the windows of the houses next door, and set a volume limit in the app for the outdoor room so the evening stays pleasant for everyone.

The landmarks question. In a historic district, most changes to the front and rear facades of a building come under the Landmarks Preservation Commission's review. Small brackets on a rear wall may still need a check. If your house is in Park Slope, Brooklyn Heights, Fort Greene, Carroll Gardens, Cobble Hill, Clinton Hill or any of the borough's other districts, we confirm what is needed before drilling, or mount the speakers on a fence, pergola or deck structure instead of the masonry. This is general information, not legal advice.

Myrtle Avenue and Fort Greene

The Myrtle Avenue Brooklyn Partnership's stretch of restaurants and shops, plus DUMBO's design studios and showrooms nearby. Brick walls, timber ceilings and owners who want the speakers to disappear.

Bay Ridge and Sunset Park

Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street in Bay Ridge, and the Sunset Park BID on Fifth Avenue: family restaurants, salons and long-established shops where staff run the music from a counter tablet.

Compliance callouts for Brooklyn shops and restaurants

  • Music under apartments. The city's Noise Code fact sheet limits music from a commercial establishment to 42 dBA measured inside any apartment that receives it. We keep speakers off party walls and away from the ceiling under bedrooms, and set the room's volume limit in the Sonos app so staff can't push past it.
  • No music aimed at the sidewalk. The Noise Code bars using a sound reproduction device to attract attention to a business from outside or through an opening onto a public street. Speakers face into the room, not out of the open front window.
  • Business Wi-Fi. Sonos needs the app and the speakers on the same subnet, and it doesn't support guest networks, portal log-ins or WPA/WPA2 Enterprise. Most shops get a small dedicated network for the music system, separate from the customer Wi-Fi.
  • Power. New outlets for an Amp or a Port are electrician's work. We tell you where they need to be and work with your electrician; we don't touch line voltage.
  • Landlord paperwork. Commercial landlords usually want a certificate of insurance naming them before work starts. Ours is free and usually sent the same day.

Music that opens and closes with the shop

Sonos's alarms are not only for waking up. Sonos's support page describes setting a time, a room, the music, the volume, how often it repeats and how long it plays, with up to 32 alarms per system. For a Brooklyn cafe that means the morning playlist can start at a set volume when the shutters go up and stop on its own at closing, and the bar's late playlist can be capped lower after 10 PM. We set these up during handover so nobody has to remember.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the questions Brooklyn owners and renters actually put to us before a Sonos booking, grouped by the part of the house they concern. Prices, scheduling and paperwork are covered in the FAQ near the bottom of the page.

The tall, narrow house

The provider's router sits in our cellar — should its own Wi-Fi stay switched on once each floor has an access point?

Usually not, or at least not on the same channels. A router radio broadcasting from the cellar mostly reaches the cellar and the garden level, and phones and speakers on the parlor floor can cling to its weak signal instead of moving to the strong access point a few feet away. The tidier arrangement is to leave the provider's box where the line enters, set its Wi-Fi off or hand the job to the access points, and let one access point per living floor do the broadcasting. A Brownstoner member advising another owner on a Fios installation described the same layout: the terminal "goes in the cellar," with Ethernet out to access points around the house.

If the house needs one Sonos product wired by Ethernet, it should be the one nearest the router's primary connection — Sonos says that on a mesh network the wired product must connect to the primary node. In practice that is often the soundbar or an Amp on the parlor floor, fed by the same cable that serves the parlor access point through a small switch. The top-floor bedroom then gets a strong signal from the access point a few feet away, not a weak one from four floors down.

Our joists run side to side between the party walls — does that limit where ceiling speakers can go?

It sets the rules rather than limiting the result. In a typical rowhouse the floor joists span the short way, from one party wall to the other, so every joist bay runs side to side across the house. A ceiling speaker has to sit between two joists, and its wire can travel easily along that bay to the side of the house where the stairs or a closet are. Moving a wire front to back means crossing joists, which means drilling each one carefully in the middle of its depth or finding a soffit or a closet to go around.

So in a Brooklyn kitchen, a pair of speakers usually lines up across the room rather than along it, and the Amp sits in a closet on the same side as the cable. Before marking anything we find the joists with a scanner and a small pilot hole, because joist spacing in a 19th-century house is not always the modern 16 inches, and an extension at the back may have joists running the other way.

Can a cable reach the top floor through the stacked closets, the old dumbwaiter shaft or a disused flue?

Stacked closets, usually yes, and they are our first choice: a hole in the closet floor directly over the closet below, a cable fished through, and the next floor the same way, with each hole fire-stopped afterwards. Many rowhouses have bedrooms laid out the same way on each floor, so the closets line up. A disused dumbwaiter shaft, where a house still has one, can also be a good riser once someone has confirmed it is empty and safe to work in.

Flues are different. We don't run cable inside a chimney flue, active or not, because a flue's job is to carry heat and gases and it may be reconnected later; a cable in it is a problem waiting for the next owner. Where a chimney breast is the only route, the cable runs beside it in a chase built by a carpenter, not inside it.

Owners, tenants and shared houses

Can a tenant on our Wi-Fi turn our Sonos up without ever being added to our account?

Yes, if you share one Wi-Fi network. Sonos's Guest Access setting lets any device on the same network control playback, volume and grouping without being added to your account, and Sonos lists it as on by default. A tenant on your network can therefore see your rooms in their Sonos app or in some music apps and play to them. Most never would, but the setting doesn't distinguish between your visiting sister and the people downstairs.

The proper fix is separate networks: yours for your floors and your Sonos, the tenant's for theirs, both from the same internet line if you like. Once the networks are separate, the tenant can't see your speakers at all, and you can leave Guest Access on for your own visitors. If separating the networks isn't possible right away, you can switch Guest Access off in the Sonos app's privacy settings as a stopgap; we show you where it is.

We bought the departing tenant's Sonos speakers — how do they move onto our system?

They have to leave the tenant's system before they can join yours. A Sonos product belongs to one system at a time, so the simplest route is for the tenant to remove them from their system before handing them over; then they are added to yours like new products. If the previous owner of the speakers isn't around to release them, a factory reset clears them so they can be added fresh. Once they're in, they appear as new rooms and can be grouped with the rest of the house.

If the garden unit is being folded back into the owner's house at the same time, the two separate networks become one, the access points on that floor join your network, and any Ethernet runs installed for the tenant are relabelled. If a new tenant is moving in instead, the speakers stay on your network and the new tenant gets a fresh network of their own. Either way we make the network change and the Sonos change on the same visit, so nothing is left behind on a network that no longer exists.

Where does the Amp for the garden speakers live if the garden level is someone else's apartment?

Somewhere on the owner's side of the house, with a cable route to the yard that doesn't depend on the tenant's rooms. Common answers in Brooklyn are the cellar, if it is the owner's space and stays above freezing, with the speaker cable leaving through a cellar wall below the garden; a closet on the parlor floor with the cable dropping down the rear facade in a raceway; or a deck off the parlor floor that has its own path to the yard below.

The Amp must stay indoors, because Sonos lists its operating range as 32° to 104°F. If the only practical route passes through the garden apartment, the tenant's written agreement comes first, and the cable runs inside a closet or along a ceiling edge in raceway, not across their living space.

Decks, roofs and rear walls

How does speaker wire get from the kitchen through a brick rear wall to the garden without letting water in?

Through one small, sleeved and sealed hole. We pick a spot inside — usually the back of a base cabinet, a closet or behind a radiator cover — and drill through the brick with a long masonry bit, angled slightly downward toward the outside so any water runs out, not in. A short sleeve lines the hole, the cable passes through with a drip loop on the outside, and the gap is sealed with an exterior-grade sealant that suits masonry.

Outside, the cable follows the wall in a paintable raceway, a downspout line or a mortar joint to each speaker. We drill brick, not mortar, for the speaker brackets, because old mortar crumbles. If the house is in a historic district, we confirm first what the rear facade work needs; often mounting the speakers on the fence or a pergola avoids the question.

Can speakers go on the shared roof deck of our condo conversion?

Possibly, but the deck usually isn't yours alone. In most small Brooklyn condo conversions the roof is a common element controlled by the condo association, even where one owner has exclusive use of it. Fixing speakers to the deck structure, running cable through the bulkhead and drilling anything on the roof generally needs the association's written approval, and the roofer who maintains the warranty on the membrane may need to do any penetration.

If approval is slow or unlikely, a pair of Move 2 speakers carried up for the evening gives good outdoor sound with no fixings at all. If approval comes through, we fix the outdoor speakers to the deck framing, never through the roofing, and bring the cable to the inside of the bulkhead where your roofer has sealed the opening.

Towers and new construction

The TV wall in our Downtown Brooklyn condo is solid concrete — how does the Arc Ultra get mounted and connected?

With concrete anchors and a cable route that stays on the surface. Many newer towers put the TV on a concrete shear wall or column rather than a stud partition. Concrete holds a soundbar bracket extremely well once it is drilled with a hammer drill and fixed with anchors rated for the load, and the $75 concrete line on our price list covers the extra time and hardware. Before drilling we scan for embedded conduit, because towers often run electrical pipe inside the pour.

What concrete won't do is hide a cable. The HDMI lead from the TV to the Arc Ultra is short and runs behind the screen, but power and any Ethernet line have to come down the wall in a slim paintable raceway ($59) or across to a nearby media console with a cable run ($99). Where an adjacent stud wall exists, we sometimes mount the TV and bar there instead so the in-wall kit can be used; we show you both options before choosing.

We're renovating our brownstone one floor at a time over two years — how do we make sure the later floors can join the Sonos system?

By putting the backbone in during the first phase, even on floors you aren't touching yet. When the first floor is open, we run network cable from the cellar to a junction point on every floor above — usually inside the stacked closets — and leave a labelled coil at each one. When the contractor reaches a floor later, the network is already there, and speaker wire for that floor only has to reach the closet, not the cellar.

We also write down where every cable and coil is, with photographs before the walls close, and give a copy to you and your contractor. On a two-year renovation that drawing is worth more than the cable, because the people who open the next floor won't be the ones who closed the last one.

The Brooklyn Network Plan Behind a Reliable Sonos System

Speakers get the attention, but in a Brooklyn house the network decides whether Sonos is a pleasure or a nuisance. These are the decisions we make, in order, before a single speaker is unboxed. Each one comes from Sonos's own support pages or from what we find inside the borough's houses.

Six rules we follow in every Brooklyn house

  • Wire the floors, don't hop them. A wireless hop through a plaster-and-joist floor loses signal at each level. Ethernet up the riser to each access point removes that loss, and Sonos's guidance on wired setups assumes the network behind it is solid.
  • 2.4 GHz stays on. Every Sonos product works on 2.4 GHz and Sonos doesn't support routers that broadcast only 5 GHz. In a rowhouse the 2.4 GHz band also travels further through plaster, which helps the garden speakers' Amp at the back of the house.
  • One subnet per household. Sonos requires the app and every product to be on the same subnet. We keep each household's devices on one network and never split a system across two.
  • No guest or portal networks for Sonos. Sonos lists guest networks and log-in portal networks as unsupported. The tenant's network is a full private network of its own, not a "guest" network on the owner's router.
  • Keep Sonos clear of other electronics. Sonos recommends at least 18 inches between its products and other electronic devices. On a parlor console crowded with a cable box, game console and router, that sometimes means moving the router rather than the soundbar.
  • Label everything. Every cable, access point and switch port is labelled with its floor and its household. In a house that changes tenants every few years, labels are what keep the next visit short.

What a floor-by-floor plan looks like on paper

For a typical four-storey Brooklyn rowhouse with a rental garden unit, the written plan we leave behind reads something like this. The cellar holds the provider's box, a small switch and, if the owner wants garden speakers and the cellar stays warm, an Amp on a wall board. The garden unit has the tenant's own access point on the tenant's network, fed by its own cable from the switch. The parlor floor has the owner's first access point on the hall ceiling, the soundbar under the TV and, if a room needs it, a wired connection to the soundbar from the same switch. The third and fourth floors each have an access point on the hall ceiling, reached through the stacked closets, and an Era speaker or a pair of ceiling speakers in the rooms that want music. The rear wall has one sealed hole for the garden speaker cable.

Every item on that list has a label, a location and an owner. It's the opposite of what we usually find on a service call: a router in the cellar, a mesh unit on each floor talking wirelessly through the plaster, a tenant on the same network and a Sonos system that works on Tuesday and not on Saturday.

In a tower, the plan is shorter

A Downtown Brooklyn or waterfront apartment rarely needs more than your own router in a sensible spot, perhaps one wired access point at the far end of a long layout, and the speakers joined to that network. The care goes into the router's channel choice, because the neighbors' networks are many and close, and into keeping the Sonos products off the building's amenity Wi-Fi. If the apartment has a media panel, we test its runs and use the ones that work.

Bass needs its own thought in a tower. Concrete slabs carry low frequencies into the apartments above and below more readily than people expect, so the Sub goes on a dense rug or an isolation pad, away from the column or shear wall that ties into the structure, and its level is set after Trueplay with you sitting where you watch. A Sub Mini is often enough in a one-bedroom; a Sub 4 suits a larger living room where the soundbar is an Arc Ultra. Either way, the night-listening setting on the soundbar is switched on for late films, and we show you where to find it in the app.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Brooklyn

A note on method: our research tools couldn't load Google's AI summaries, so nothing below is quoted from one. Each heading is a claim we found in published advice — forums, product pages, generic networking guides — of the kind answer engines repeat. Under each is what actually happens in a Brooklyn house or apartment.

"Buy a mesh Wi-Fi kit and Sonos will work in any house"

Mesh kits are sold as the fix for big houses, and wireless mesh does extend coverage. In a single-storey house with drywall, it may be all you need.

A Brooklyn rowhouse stacks three or four floors of plaster on joists over a cellar, and each wireless hop through a floor costs signal. Sonos's own requirements add a condition: in a mesh network with a wired Sonos product, that product must connect to the primary node. Brooklyn owners who have been through it say the same thing on Brownstoner — wire the access points with Ethernet where you can.

Our standard rowhouse plan is a wired access point on each living floor. It costs more on day one than a box of mesh units, and it's the version that keeps the top floor playing.

"Your Sonos speakers are private to your account"

The Sonos account is private: no one else can change your settings, add products or see your music services without signing in.

Control is a different matter. Sonos's Connection Security page says Guest Access lets any device on the same Wi-Fi control playback, volume and grouping, and that it is on by default. In a single-family home that's handy; in a two-family house on one shared network, it means the tenant's devices can reach the owner's speakers.

The fix is in the network, not the speaker: give each household its own network, or switch Guest Access off until you can.

"You can run two Sonos systems on one network and switch between them"

Sonos does support connecting one app to more than one system, and answers that skim the support pages repeat that.

Read the article and it describes using Sonos at home and at a friend's or family member's home — different places, different networks. Sonos community members who tried two systems on one network report that a phone registered to both "could pick either of the systems to latch onto," with "frustratingly unpredictable" results.

For a Brooklyn two-family, two systems are right; two systems on one network are not. Separate networks first.

"Outdoor speakers just need an outdoor outlet"

That's true of portable speakers and of powered outdoor speakers from some brands, and general advice often stops there.

Sonos's architectural outdoor pair is passive. It needs speaker wire back to an Amp, and Sonos lists the Amp's operating range as 32° to 104°F, so the Amp stays in a heated room. In a brick rowhouse, getting that wire from the Amp to the garden is the main job: a sealed hole through the rear wall, a tidy route outside, and in a historic district, a check before anything is fixed to the facade.

If you want no wiring at all, a Move 2 carried outside is the honest alternative; Sonos says its charging base is for indoor use.

"All brownstones are the same, so the same speaker plan fits every one"

Renovation writers often describe the brownstone as a standard type, and a 20-foot-wide house is common enough that it's thought of as standard.

The same Brownstoner thread that calls 20 feet standard also calls anything under 20 narrow and 25 or more wide. Add rear extensions with joists running the other way, kitchens moved down to the garden level, original plaster on some floors and new drywall on others, and stacked closets that line up in one house but not the next, and the plan changes from door to door.

We survey each floor before quoting built-in speakers. A soundbar and wireless speakers can be priced from photos; ceiling and outdoor work can't.

"A professional Sonos install runs about $500 all-in"

That figure comes from a single owner's post on Reddit's r/sonos, describing a quote from a local home theater company, and it gets passed around as a typical price.

It may have been fair for that job. A Brooklyn job could be $125 for setup alone, or several thousand dollars for a multi-floor network, ceiling speakers on two Amps and an outdoor pair through a brick wall. A single "all-in" number tells you nothing about which of those you're buying.

Our published lines are below, item by item, at Brooklyn's base rate. Built-in speaker rooms are quoted in writing after a walkthrough, before any work starts.

"Ceiling speakers are always the neatest choice"

Flush, paintable grilles are hard to argue with in a modern room, and design sites recommend them for a clean look.

On a Brooklyn parlor floor the ceiling may carry original plaster cornices and a centre medallion, and an 8.25-inch cut-out near either risks damage that costs a specialist plasterer far more to repair than the speakers cost. In a tower, the ceiling is often a concrete slab with no cavity at all.

Ceiling speakers are often the best answer in the garden-level kitchen and the bathrooms. In the parlor, a soundbar with Era speakers, or bookshelf speakers on an Amp in a cabinet, usually keeps the room as it was meant to look.

Long rows of brownstones and brick houses, many of them two- and three-family, plus wood-frame houses in parts of Bed-Stuy and Bushwick. This is where the owner-and-tenant network split is most common, and where the cellar is most often shared space with a boiler, meters and the tenant's storage. Cable routes are planned through the owner's closets.

Williamsburg, Greenpoint, DUMBO and Downtown Brooklyn

Two different jobs side by side. In the towers along the waterfront and Downtown, it's a wireless Sonos system on a private router, a soundbar on metal studs or a concrete column, and surrounds placed around glass. In the converted warehouses and the remaining frame houses of Greenpoint and Williamsburg, it's brick, timber, surface raceway and Trueplay working hard against echo.

Flatbush, Ditmas Park, Midwood and Kensington

Detached wood-frame Victorians and pre-war elevator buildings. The houses behave almost like suburban homes: attics and basements for cable, porches and side yards for outdoor speakers, and Wi-Fi that passes through wood more easily than through Park Slope plaster. The apartment buildings bring a super, sometimes a board, and plaster on masonry.

Sunset Park, Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights and Bensonhurst

Two-family masonry rowhouses in Sunset Park, pre-war co-ops and rowhouses in Bay Ridge, and detached and semi-detached houses further south. Families with a parent or grown child on the other floor often want one system for the whole house rather than two; others want strict separation. We ask before we design.

Southern and eastern Brooklyn: Canarsie, Marine Park, Sheepshead Bay, East Flatbush

Brick row houses, attached two-families and detached homes with finished basements, close to our Troy Avenue shop. Basements become the media room; the Sub goes there, with ceiling speakers in a drywall ceiling that is the easiest of any in the borough.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Brooklyn Rowhouse Sonos Jobs

The order a crew checks things in when it first walks into a tall Brooklyn house. This is our routine, described in general, not an account of any one client's home.

We start in the cellar, not the living room. The first questions are where the provider's line comes in, whether there's an outlet beside it, and whether the cellar is the owner's space or shared with a tenant's storage and the boiler. That decides where the network begins.

Then we climb. On each floor we open the closet nearest the stairs and look up: if the closet above sits directly over it, the riser is solved. We check the signal at the far end of each floor — the street end and the garden end are usually very different — and we ask who lives on that floor before touching anything on it.

Last comes the back of the house. We look at the rear wall from the garden, find the brick, the mortar and the downspouts, and note which neighbors' windows face the yard. Only then do we talk about which speakers go where, because by then the house has told us most of the answer.

FAQ

Brooklyn Sonos Installation FAQ

What does it cost to split an owner's and a tenant's Wi-Fi so the Sonos systems stay apart?
+

If the existing router can run two isolated networks and no new cable is needed, it is setup-only time: $195 for the first hour in Brooklyn, then 30-minute blocks, and most splits fit inside that first hour. If the house needs a second router, a new cable to the tenant's floor or access points on each level, that part is quoted in writing after we've seen where the internet comes in.

Do you charge extra when the apartment is up several flights with no elevator?
+

There is one flat line for it: $40 for a third-floor-or-higher walk-up with no elevator. It covers carrying ladders, tools and any brackets up the stairs. Inside your own house, climbing between your own floors doesn't trigger it.

Can you take out old wall speakers and dead wiring a previous owner left in the house?
+

Yes. Brooklyn houses often carry speaker wire, coax and phone cable from two or three owners back. We trace what is live and what is abandoned, remove old speakers and brackets you no longer want, reuse any cable that tests good, and cut back or label what is left. Filling the holes and repainting afterwards is for your painter or plasterer.

Can our tenant book their own Sonos setup for the same day as ours?
+

Yes. Once the networks are separate, the tenant's speakers are set up on their own network and their own Sonos account, as a separate booking that they arrange and pay for directly. Doing both households on one day means the crew from our shop at 1282 Troy Ave makes a single trip and each system is tested alongside the other.

Our restaurant already has ceiling speakers from the last tenant. Can Sonos run them?
+

Often, once we know what they are. Many restaurant ceiling speakers are ordinary 8-ohm models that a Sonos Amp can drive directly, within the limits Sonos sets for impedance and the number of pairs. Others are commercial speakers wired to a separate amplifier, and in that case a Sonos Port feeding the existing amplifier is usually the answer. We test the wiring and identify the speakers on a setup-only visit before you buy anything.

Will we get a record of where the cables, access points and Amps are in the house?
+

Yes. At handover you get a written list of every access point, switch, Amp and cable run, with the floor and closet each one is in, which network each belongs to and photos of anything that was closed into a wall. In a two-family house it also says which equipment is the owner's and which network the tenant uses.

Should we wait until the Sonos products are delivered before booking?
+

You can book first, but pick a date after the delivery. If the crew arrives and the products aren't there, the $150 return-trip fee applies. Moving the appointment with at least 24 hours' notice is free, so if a delivery slips, just call and move it.

We just closed on a house and the internet isn't connected yet. Can the Sonos work still go ahead?
+

The wiring can, the setup can't. We can run the riser cable, mount brackets, cut in ceiling speakers and wire the garden while you wait for the provider. Sonos needs a working home network and internet access to set up and update products, so the final setup and Trueplay are booked as a short second visit once the line is live.

Can the garden speakers play something different from the parlor at the same time?
+

Yes. Outdoor speakers on their own Amp form a separate room in the Sonos app, so the yard can play one thing while the parlor plays another, or the two can be grouped when you want the same music inside and out. The garden room also gets its own maximum volume, set with you on the day.
