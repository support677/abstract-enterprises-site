The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Sonos Installation

Pelham Bay was farmland, then it was the end of the line. The land once belonged to Thomas Pell and was known in colonial times as Pell's Point, though Wikipedia is careful to note that the neighbourhood was never part of the old Town of Pelham. Very little was built here until the subway arrived. On December 20, 1920, the IRT Pelham Line opened its stations at Middletown Road, Buhre Avenue and Pelham Bay Park, and houses followed the trains. Most of what stands today went up between the 1920s and the 1960s, and the neighbourhood guide Reinvent NY points to Tudor-influenced and Colonial Revival details on Wilkinson Avenue, Hobart Avenue and Crosby Avenue. NeighborhoodScout puts the share of homes built in 1939 or earlier at 57 percent. After the war, by the account of the neighbourhood history site City Neighborhoods, Pelham Bay settled into its reputation as one of the borough's steadiest family neighbourhoods, home to large Italian, Hispanic and Albanian communities and a sizeable Greek one.

That age and that construction are exactly what a wireless speaker system notices. A Pelham Bay house is usually brick on the outside, often plaster on the inside, and divided into small rooms by walls that were built to last rather than to let a radio signal through. Sonos publishes a table of how building materials affect its wireless products: wood, glass and synthetic materials are a low obstacle, brick a medium one, and plaster and concrete a high one. Put a router at the front window where the cable company's line comes through the brick, and a Sonos speaker in a back bedroom behind two plaster walls, and the speaker is working at the edge of what it can hear. It may play happily in the afternoon and stutter in the evening when every phone in the house is streaming.

The second thing that makes this neighbourhood different is how many households share one roof. Listing sites describe the housing as overwhelmingly low-rise: detached and semi-detached one-family houses, brick two-family houses and a smaller number of three-family houses, with modest apartment buildings of three to six storeys. Homes.com listings in Middletown and Pelham Bay show a brick semi-detached one-family on the Hutchinson River Parkway, a semi-detached two-family with three large units and a cellar with a private rear entrance, and a detached three-family brick house on an oversized 50-by-100 lot on Hobart Avenue. In those houses there are often two or three internet accounts, two or three routers, two or three families who all want music, and one cellar where every cable starts. A Sonos installer who treats the house as one home will build a system that nobody can share sensibly.

The edges of the neighbourhood add a third kind of job. City zoning has kept the taller buildings mainly to the edges and the wider avenues, according to the local newsletter The Neighborhoods. StreetEasy lists co-op apartments along Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road, including 1874 and 1910 Pelham Parkway South and 3121 Middletown Road, where a board has to approve anything cut into a wall or ceiling. Neighborhoods.com describes the rest of the apartment stock as brick walk-ups with hardwood floors and large windows, and no skyscrapers at all. And a new generation of buildings is arriving near the terminal: the Bronx Times reported permits for an eight-storey, 36-unit building at 1609 Crosby Avenue and a four-storey, 13-unit building at 1536 Gillespie Avenue, while New York YIMBY has covered a 17-storey building at 3250 Westchester Avenue. Steel studs and poured concrete bring their own rules.

There is one more local fact worth keeping in mind. In April 2025 News 12 reported Pelham Bay residents being woken late at night by bass, especially on Sundays and in the warm months; one resident described hearing only "boom, boom, boom, boom," and Councilwoman Kristy Marmorato urged neighbours to file 311 complaints. The source in that story was not anyone's living room. Still, it is a reminder that in a neighbourhood of close-set brick houses, a Sub placed against the wall shared with a semi-detached neighbour is part of the same conversation.

So the brief for a Sonos installation in Pelham Bay is plain. Decide which product is wired and which can stay wireless, using the walls you actually have. Work out where cable can run, which usually means the cellar, and agree that route with everyone who lives above it. Give each household its own system on its own network. Then do the ordinary craft well: soundbars level and connected properly, ceiling speakers cut in after the joists and pipes have been found, the Sub placed away from shared walls, and limits set before we leave.

Signal and Masonry

Brick Between the Rooms: How Pelham Bay Houses Treat a Wireless Speaker

Every Sonos product talks to the router, and to the app on your phone, over the house network. Sonos's system requirements are specific about that network: a router that offers 2.4 GHz, because every Sonos product supports 802.11b/g on that band; all products and the phone on the same subnet; personal security such as WPA2 rather than the enterprise kind used in offices; and no guest networks, no portal log-ins, no wireless range extenders and no Ethernet over power. A house that breaks one of those rules can still play music for a while, which is why so many people blame the speakers.

In Pelham Bay the rule people break by accident is the one about extenders. When the back bedroom of a brick house gets weak Wi-Fi, the obvious fix is a plug-in extender in the hall. Sonos lists extenders as unsupported, and in our experience a speaker that clings to an extender is the one that drops first. The better fixes are a cable to the speaker, a cable to a proper access point, or a wired Sonos product near the router so the others have a strong neighbour to lean on. Sonos also notes that in a mesh system using its wired setup, the wired Sonos product must connect to the primary node.

The table explains a pattern we see over and over. The front room, with the router on the windowsill, is perfect. The kitchen, one plaster wall back, is fine most of the time. The rear bedroom, behind the kitchen and a brick bearing wall, is the one that drops during a family dinner, because the signal has been weakened twice and the house is full of phones. The attic bedroom is worse still, and the cellar den, under a floor and behind foundation walls, is often unreachable by Wi-Fi at all.

The other things that crowd the air

Sonos's interference guidance lists the devices that compete with its products: cordless and DECT phones, wireless video senders and cameras, other routers and access points, wireless audio, baby monitors, wireless set-top boxes, smart-home hubs, some monitors and a microwave in use. It asks for at least 18 inches between a Sonos product and other electronics. In a two-family house the list grows, because the other household's router is often directly above or below yours. Rebooting the router so it picks a fresh channel is Sonos's first suggestion; in a multi-router house we go further and plan the channels so the floors stop competing.

Which products can use which band

Not every Sonos product sees the same networks. According to Sonos's list of supported Wi-Fi modes, newer products such as the Arc Ultra, Era 100, Era 300, Sub 4 and Move 2 can use 5 GHz networks up to Wi-Fi 6, while older ones like the Port, Sub Mini and Five use earlier 5 GHz modes, and products still on the older S1 app are limited to 2.4 GHz and cannot use WPA3. A household that mixes a decade of Sonos purchases can therefore be fine on one router and unhappy on another. We check each product against the router before deciding what needs a cable.

The answer is rarely to wire everything. In most Pelham Bay houses we wire one product and perhaps one access point, and the rest of the system stays wireless. The skill is in picking which ones, and in finding a route for that cable through a house with brick walls. That route usually starts downstairs.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Pelham Bay's Architecture

The Department of City Planning's 2006 rezoning study described a neighbourhood dominated by large one- and two-family detached homes on the inner blocks, with apartment buildings on the wider avenues, and moved most of it into a district that allows only detached one- and two-family houses. That is the housing we work in, and each type routes sound and signal differently.

Brick Semi-Detached Houses

Two houses sharing one masonry wall, often with a driveway on the open side. The shared wall stops some Wi-Fi from the neighbours and passes bass the other way, so the Sub stays off it and out of its corners. Router placement matters more here, because the open side is where the house leaks signal.

Detached 1920s–1960s Houses

Brick or frame houses with Tudor or Colonial Revival touches, a cellar, often a finished attic. These suit a cellar Amp feeding ceiling speakers on the first floor, a wired soundbar in the front room and an access point near the stairs for the upper floor.

Brick Two-Family Houses

One household upstairs, one down, frequently related to each other, frequently not. Each gets its own network and its own Sonos system. The cellar usually belongs to the ground floor, so any cable to the upper unit is planned with both households.

Three-Family Houses

Like the detached brick three-family on a 50-by-100 lot that Homes.com lists on Hobart Avenue: three stacked households, three routers, sometimes a shared hallway. Channel planning and labelled cable runs keep three systems from tripping over one another.

Prewar Brick Walk-Ups

Apartments with hardwood floors and large windows, as Neighborhoods.com describes them. Renters get no-drill layouts: soundbars on consoles, speakers on stands, the Sub on a rug away from the walls, and volume limits that respect the floor below.

Co-ops on the Parkway and Middletown Road

Apartments in the buildings StreetEasy lists along Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road. Any cut into a wall or ceiling is a board matter, so we work from the alteration rules, supply a certificate of insurance, and favour wireless rooms with one wired anchor.

Mixed-Use Avenue Buildings

Two-storey buildings like 3211 Westchester Avenue, built in 1927 with four units, according to StreetEasy: a shop below, homes above. The shop's system and the family's system stay on separate networks.

New Buildings Near the Terminal

Steel studs, concrete slabs and builder-installed networks. Era 300 mounts need studs that can carry them, ceiling work depends on the floor assembly, and the building's own Wi-Fi rules decide whether Sonos can live on it.

Houses with Finished Cellars

A cellar den, often below a drop or drywall ceiling, where Wi-Fi from upstairs barely arrives. A cabled soundbar or access point down there turns it into a usable TV room, and the equipment stays off the floor.

On ceilings in older houses: before any ceiling speaker goes in, Sonos's in-ceiling manual asks for an obstruction survey with a stud finder for joists, conduit, pipes, heating, ducts and air returns, and a small pilot hole probed with wire where there is doubt. The 6-inch speaker needs an opening of 8.25 inches and about 4.73 inches of depth; the 8-inch needs 10.125 inches and about 5.32 inches. The clamps grip ceilings up to 1-1/4 inches thick with the caps on, or 1-7/8 inches with them off. That last number matters in Pelham Bay kitchens where a newer layer of board was fixed over the original plaster, and we measure through the pilot hole rather than guess.

On wall mounts: Sonos describes the Era 300 wall mount as stud-mounting only and not for drywall, with about 2.5 inches of clearance from the wall and a joint that swivels 15 degrees. In a brick house, the interior walls that have studs are not always where the room needs the speaker, so we decide early whether the Era 300 goes on a framed wall, on a stand, or on a shelf. We do not improvise a fixing the maker does not describe.

The Cellar

The Cellar Is the Backbone of a Pelham Bay Sonos System

Homes.com describes many of the neighbourhood's 1910s and 1920s houses as having basements suitable for renovation, and in practice that cellar is where a Sonos installation in Pelham Bay is won or lost. It is usually where the internet line enters, where an old stereo or a camera recorder already sits, and the one place in a brick house where cable can travel freely along exposed joists. A Bronx customer on Yelp put the typical starting point plainly when praising an installer for sorting out "a basement with a messy TV / Stereo / Sonos system / Routers."

Why routes start downstairs

Exterior walls are brick and most interior walls are plaster, so fishing a cable horizontally across a finished floor means opening walls. Going down is easier. From the cellar ceiling we can usually come up into the front room behind the TV, into a kitchen wall, or into a closet that stacks above another closet, and from there reach an access point or a Sonos product upstairs. Where a closet route does not exist, a painted cord cover on a baseboard or along a corner is the honest fallback; it costs $59 on our list and keeps plaster intact.

Keep it off the floor

We never leave a router, an Amp or a Port on a cellar floor. Sonos lists the Amp's operating range as 32 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit, and the Port guide asks for at least 2 inches of space on every side and distance from radiators and other heat. A cellar floor is the coldest, dampest and most knocked-about surface in the house, and Pelham Bay sits beside a park that NYC Parks is studying for flooding in eight vulnerable areas. We are not saying your block floods; we are saying a shelf at waist height costs nothing and protects equipment from the mop bucket, the storm and the boiler room together.

Order on the shelf

The typical cellar we walk into has a provider's gateway, a separate router somebody bought, a camera recorder, an alarm panel and a tangle of power strips. Sonos asks for 18 inches between its products and other electronics, which on a wireless product is about avoiding interference. Our usual layout puts the provider's equipment and the router at one end, a small switch beside them, wired Sonos gear like an Amp or Port a little apart, and wireless gear further still. A small wall rack is worth it in larger houses; a sturdy shelf is enough in most.

Labels and a map

Every cable we pull is labelled at both ends, and the handover note lists where each one goes: "Front room soundbar," "Second floor access point," "Kitchen ceiling pair." In a two-family house the note also says which household each cable belongs to. When a new tenant moves in, or the provider swaps the router again, the next person in the cellar knows what they are looking at.

Shared Houses

One Roof, Two or Three Households: Sonos in Pelham Bay's Multi-Family Houses

The brick two-family is one of the defining houses of the neighbourhood, and three-family houses turn up in the listings too. Sonos was designed around a single household: one network, one account, one set of rooms that can be grouped together. When two families share a building, the easiest mistakes are giving everyone one system because the house has one cellar, or letting each family set up alone and discovering their speakers have joined the wrong network. Both are fixable, but they are much simpler to avoid.

One system per household, as a rule

Our default is a separate Sonos system for each apartment, on that apartment's own network and registered to that household's own Sonos account. A Sonos product only sees other products and phones on the same network, so separate networks keep the downstairs music in the downstairs app. If the families want to share, for instance a parent and adult children who want one system across both floors, that is possible on a shared network, but it is a decision to make deliberately and write down, not something that happens because one router reached both floors.

Who owns the cellar, and the route through it

In most two-family houses the cellar is reached from the ground-floor apartment, or from a rear entrance like the one in the semi-detached two-family Homes.com describes. The internet usually enters there for both units. If the upstairs owner wants a wired Sonos product or access point, the cable has to leave that cellar and climb past the ground floor. We plan that route with both households before the visit: a shared hallway, a stacked closet, the outside of the rear wall in a cord cover, or an existing opening. Nobody's apartment is drilled on the strength of the other household's permission.

Three routers in one brick box

A three-family house can have three routers within a few feet of each other vertically. Brick and plaster help separate the floors a little, but routers on the same channel still compete, and Sonos's guidance names other routers and access points among the sources of interference. With each household's consent we look at what channel each router uses and spread them out, keep each Sonos system on its own network, and avoid any extender bridging floors.

Landlord and tenant

Many owners live in one unit and rent the other. Equipment the owner installs for the tenant's apartment should be clearly the owner's, documented in the handover note, and on a network the owner controls only if the tenant agrees to that. More often we recommend the tenant's Sonos stays the tenant's: bought by them, on their internet, registered to their account. That keeps the music, the account and the responsibility in the same hands, and it keeps the owner out of the tenant's listening habits.

Bass and the floor between you

A Sub on a hardwood floor sends vibration into the joists that the other household lives on top of, or below. In a two-family we keep the Sub off shared walls and out of corners, put it on a rug or isolation feet, set Sub level after Trueplay rather than at the factory default, and set Night Sound for late viewing. The household upstairs will not hear whether the soundbar is an Arc Ultra or a Ray. They will hear the bass.

Dining rooms, bar areas and party rooms on one system, with TV sound for the game where wanted, separate zones and limits for each space, and a staff zone for the kitchen that never reaches the tables.

Family Businesses with Homes Above

Owners who live over the shop often want one internet line for both. We keep the shop's Sonos and the family's Sonos on separate networks so customers' devices and staff tablets never see the family system.

Compliance notes for business music on the avenues

  • Measured where it lands. NYC Administrative Code §24-231 limits commercial music measured inside a home that receives it: 42 dB(A) overall, 45 dB in any one-third-octave band from 63 to 500 hertz, or a 6 dB(C) rise over a loud background. The bass test is the one a big Sub fails, so business Subs stay modest, isolated and set low.
  • After 10 p.m. NYC311's guidance on noise from bars and restaurants says music may not exceed 42 decibels inside nearby homes between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. We save a late-evening setting for staff to choose rather than relying on someone to remember.
  • Licensed music, not a personal account. Spotify's terms, for one, restrict its service to personal, non-commercial listening and name bars, restaurants and stores as places it may not be played publicly, as recorded in our Bronx research. We connect a business music service to the commercial system.
  • Low-voltage wiring. Speaker and network cable under 50 volts installed by a qualified person does not need an electrical permit in New York City, under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 as recorded in our Bronx research. New outlets and circuits are an electrician's work, and we say so in the quote.
  • Above the ceiling tiles. Where a drop ceiling is used as a return-air space, cable run above it may need to be plenum-rated. We check the space before we price the run.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Pelham Bay: Questions Answered

These questions come from the houses, apartments and shops we described above. Each is answered fully here; shorter practical questions are in the FAQ further down.

Signal, brick and the neighbours' networks

We plugged a Wi-Fi extender into a hallway outlet and our Sonos got worse, not better. Why?

Because Sonos does not support wireless range extenders, and says so in its system requirements. An extender repeats the router's signal, but it adds a hop and can confuse which access point each speaker should talk to; Sonos products also rely on network traffic that extenders do not always pass cleanly. The result in a brick house is often a speaker that connects, then drops when the extender gets busy. Unplug it. The fixes Sonos supports are a cable from the router to one Sonos product, a cable to a proper access point on the same network, or a mesh system with the wired Sonos product connected to the primary node. In most Pelham Bay houses one cable run from the cellar does the job.

Our house is semi-detached and the family next door has Sonos too. Can their app see or control our speakers through the shared wall?

Not unless your speakers and their phones are on the same network. The Sonos app finds products on the network the phone is connected to, so their app sees their system and yours sees yours. The real risks are a shared or guessable Wi-Fi password, or a speaker that was once set up on their network during a friendly favour. We check that every product in your system is on your network, that the network uses personal WPA2 or WPA3 security with a password the neighbours do not know, and that your system is registered to your own Sonos account.

Our three-family house has a router on every floor. Do they interfere with each other, and does that hurt Sonos?

They can. Sonos lists other routers and access points among the devices that interfere with its products, and three routers stacked in one building, possibly on the same channel, compete for the same air. Brick and plaster separate the floors somewhat, but not completely. With each household's agreement we check which channel each router is using and spread them apart, keep each Sonos system on its own network, and make sure nobody is using an extender to reach across floors. Rebooting a router so it picks a new channel is Sonos's own first step; a planned channel layout is the lasting one.

Two-family houses and the floor between

The internet comes into the cellar of our two-family, but we live upstairs. Which route can a cable take that doesn't pass through the downstairs apartment?

There are usually three candidates, and we look at them in this order. A common stair hall, where a cable can climb in a corner under a painted cover or behind trim. A stack of closets or a chase that runs from the cellar ceiling to your floor without opening into the ground-floor rooms. Or the outside of the rear wall in an exterior-rated cover, entering your apartment through a sealed hole. If the only practical route does cross the ground-floor apartment, we explain that before booking and the downstairs household decides. The cable then lands at a wired Sonos product or an access point in your apartment, and is labelled in the cellar as yours.

For a family party, can the upstairs and downstairs Sonos systems in our two-family play the same music together?

Not as two separate systems. Sonos groups rooms within one household on one network; two systems on two networks cannot be grouped into a single synchronised stream. There are workable options. For an occasional party, one household's portable Move 2 or Roam 2 can be carried down and join that floor's music if it is on that floor's network. If the families often want the whole house playing together, the two floors can share one network and one system, with the trade-off that both households then see each other's rooms in the app. We set it up whichever way you choose and write down how to undo it.

Inside prewar rooms

Between the radiators and the windows, the only place for our TV is the corner of the front room. Can a soundbar work in a corner?

Yes, with some care. A soundbar wants a clear line to the seats and space either side, and a corner TV on a stand or an angled mount usually gives that. A compact Ray or Beam suits a corner better than the 46-inch Arc Ultra, which needs a wide base and, by Sonos's guidance, at least 4 inches between bar and screen. The Sub should not go into the same corner if that corner is a wall shared with a semi-detached neighbour. Trueplay, run from a supported iPhone or, for some newer products, an Android phone, helps balance a room where the sound reflects unevenly off the nearby walls.

Our sofa sits right against the back wall of a narrow front room. Where do rear surround speakers go?

Close behind or beside the sofa, a little above ear height, angled toward the seats. Sonos rears such as the Era 100 or Era 300 can sit on stands at the sofa's ends, on side tables, or on wall mounts if the wall behind is framed with studs. On a brick or plaster back wall we usually prefer stands or a shelf, because the Era 300 mount is described by Sonos as stud-only and not every wall in a prewar house has studs where you want the speaker. After the rears are placed we run Trueplay so the system accounts for how close they are.

Do the ceiling speakers have to be the Sonos-branded ones, or will a discounted Sonance pair work with a Sonos Amp?

Sonos's own in-ceiling speakers are made with Sonance and are optimised for the Amp; Sonos says the Amp reliably powers up to three pairs of them in parallel and supports Trueplay with them from a supported iOS device. Other passive speakers can be driven by an Amp too, provided their impedance suits it; reviewers rate the Amp at 125 watts per channel into 8 ohms. What you may lose with a different model is Sonos's tuning for that speaker and the published cut-out templates. Before you buy, send us the model, and we will confirm it fits your joist bays and the ceiling thickness we measure.

Businesses on the avenues

Can the kitchen crew at the back of our restaurant play their own station without it reaching the dining room?

Yes. The kitchen becomes its own Sonos room with its own speaker and its own volume limit, separate from the dining room and bar zones. Staff choose the kitchen station from a tablet without touching the front-of-house music, and the kitchen speaker is placed and aimed so it does not throw sound through the pass. We also check the network: commercial kitchens are full of stainless steel, which Sonos rates as a very high obstacle, so the kitchen speaker often needs a cable or an access point of its own.

Our family lives above our Westchester Avenue store. Should the shop's Sonos and our apartment's Sonos share one network?

We advise against it. The shop's network is used by staff devices and sometimes a point-of-sale system, and if the shop offers customer Wi-Fi, that should be a separate guest network that Sonos does not use anyway. Keeping the shop's Sonos on the shop's network and the family's Sonos on the family's network means staff never see your living room in their app, your teenagers never change the shop's music, and a problem in one does not take down the other. One internet line can feed both with a router that supports separate networks, or two routers on separate connections.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Pelham Bay

Google's AI Overview could not be viewed from the system we built this page on, so nothing below is presented as its wording. These are seven statements about Sonos and installation that appear in cost guides, product marketing, forums and templated installer pages, the kind of thing an answer engine tends to repeat, each checked against a named source and against what Pelham Bay houses are actually like.

Claim one: "Sonos is plug-and-play, so nobody needs an installer"

For a single speaker on a shelf near the router, this is close to true, and we say so to customers who ask. Sonos built its reputation on products that set themselves up from a phone, and many Pelham Bay households run a Beam or an Era 100 happily without ever calling anyone.

Sonos's own guides are more careful about the rest of the range. Its guide to ceiling speakers says that in-ceiling speakers require special installation and that architectural speakers often require a professional installer; its in-wall guide describes cutting holes, running speaker cable and installing an Amp.

In a brick house there is a third case the marketing does not mention: products that set up perfectly and then drop out because of the walls. Plug-and-play describes the setup screen, not the network the speaker has to live on afterwards.

Claim two: "A Wi-Fi extender will fix a dead spot"

It is the most common advice on the internet for weak Wi-Fi, and for a laptop in a back bedroom it may help. Extenders are cheap and they plug into any outlet.

Sonos lists wireless range extenders as unsupported, along with Ethernet over power. In practice we find extenders are behind a large share of the "speaker keeps disappearing" calls we take in houses with brick and plaster between rooms.

The supported answers are a cable to one Sonos product or a proper access point, or a mesh system with the wired Sonos connected to its primary node. In a house with a cellar, that cable is usually an afternoon's work.

Claim three: "Any router will do"

Retail advice often treats the router as interchangeable. Most will work, and Sonos supports the usual home security standards, WPA and WPA2 Personal, with WPA3 on newer products.

The exceptions are specific. Sonos does not support routers that offer only 5 GHz, enterprise security, guest networks, captive-portal networks, or VPNs that block local traffic, and products on the older S1 app are limited to 2.4 GHz. Two-family houses add another wrinkle: a household that joins the other floor's router "temporarily" usually stays there.

So we check the router and every product against it before the install, not after. A household mixing ten years of Sonos purchases is the one most likely to find a gap.

Claim four: "A stronger router solves a brick house"

Router packaging promises coverage measured in thousands of square feet, and it is tempting to think more power will push through masonry.

Sonos's interference table rates brick as a medium obstacle and plaster as a high one. A prewar house with both, plus a concrete cellar, weakens the signal at every wall, and a more powerful router at the front window does nothing for the phone in the back room that has to answer it. The speaker and the phone both have to be heard, not just the router.

Moving the router toward the middle of the house, adding a wired access point upstairs, and wiring the busiest Sonos product all work better than turning up the power.

Claim five: "Trueplay tunes the room from any phone"

Trueplay is one of Sonos's best features, and reviews describe it as automatic. It measures how sound reflects off walls and furnishings and adjusts each speaker to the room.

Sonos says most of its products need an iOS device for Trueplay. The Arc Ultra, Beam Ultra, Era 100, Era 100 Pro and Era 300 offer a quick tuning option on iOS or Android; the more thorough advanced tuning remains iOS-only. Each room takes about three minutes and needs quiet.

In an Android household we bring an iPhone for tuning. In a busy two-family house we time it for when the other floor is quiet, because footsteps and a TV upstairs make the measurement less reliable.

Claim six: "The Sonos app problems are just a reason to avoid installers"

The app has had a rough patch, and nobody should pretend otherwise. CE Pro reported that the redesigned app launched on May 7, 2024 left out or broke features including volume control, saved stations and grouping, and that Sonos's chief executive apologised publicly.

The fixes Sonos has shipped since have restored most of what people missed, but app trouble has a way of showing up first on weak networks, where discovery and grouping fail silently. A system with a wired anchor and a clean network is much less likely to look broken.

An installer cannot change Sonos's software. We can make sure the network underneath it is one Sonos supports, which removes the most common reason the app cannot find a speaker.

Claim seven: "Any 'Pelham' installer covers Pelham Bay"

Search for Sonos installers here and some results are templated pages that name the neighbourhood but no streets or building types; one we found carries a New Jersey phone number. Others mix the Bronx neighbourhood up with Pelham in Westchester.

They are different places. Wikipedia notes that Pelham Bay was never part of the old Town of Pelham; it is a Bronx neighbourhood in Community District 10, patrolled by the 45th Precinct, built around the 6 train. Its housing is brick two-family houses, semi-detached pairs, prewar walk-ups and co-ops, not the large suburban homes a Westchester page assumes.

Sonos recommends keeping its products at least 18 inches from other electronic devices to reduce interference, and lists wireless cameras and other routers among the sources. A wired Amp or Port can share the shelf with some spacing, as long as it has ventilation; Sonos asks for 2 inches of space around the Port. Wireless Sonos products should sit further away. We lay out the shelf so everything has room and nothing rests on the floor.

Our kitchen ceiling had new board laid over the old plaster. Is it too thick for a ceiling speaker?
+

It may be, which is why we measure first. Sonos's in-ceiling speakers clamp ceilings up to 1-1/4 inches thick with the caps on, or 1-7/8 inches with the caps removed, according to the manual. A layer of board over original plaster can approach those figures. We drill a small pilot hole during the obstruction survey, measure the actual thickness, and only then confirm the speaker. If the ceiling is too thick, we suggest a different location or a different kind of speaker rather than forcing it.

There are network jacks in a few rooms but nobody knows where they lead. Can you trace them first?
+

Yes. We use a tone generator and probe to find where each jack's cable ends, usually in the cellar or a closet, and a tester to see whether it is network cable in working order or old telephone wire. Working network cable can give a soundbar or access point the wired connection that brick walls would otherwise block. Old phone wire is not suitable for that, but it sometimes shows us a route a new cable can follow. Tracing is billed as setup-only time.

Is a small rack in the cellar worth it for the router, switch and Sonos gear?
+

In a house with more than a few wired devices, often yes. A small wall rack keeps the provider's gateway, the router, a switch, Amps and Ports off the floor, spaced and ventilated, with every cable labelled. In a one-family house with a single Amp, a sturdy shelf does the same job. We recommend what the cellar and the system actually need, and quote the rack separately after the walkthrough so it is never a surprise.
