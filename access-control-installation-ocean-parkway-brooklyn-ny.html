The Neighborhood

Why Ocean Parkway Properties Need Access Control

Ocean Parkway is a road before it is a neighborhood. Wikipedia describes it as about five and a half miles long, running from the circle at Prospect Park down to Sea Breeze Avenue and Surf Avenue near the ocean, and passing Windsor Terrace, Kensington, Midwood, Homecrest, Gravesend, Sheepshead Bay, Brighton Beach and Coney Island on the way. What ties those blocks together, for anyone who installs door systems, is the building that keeps repeating along the service roads: a six-story apartment house set back behind a front lawn, with an elevator, a live-in super, a street door, an inner lobby door, a row of mailboxes and, behind or beneath it, a cellar and very often a garage. The questions people bring us here are about that building, and about how a stranger gets from the sidewalk to the mail area, the cellar or the parked cars inside it.

The two most recent reports we found are both about mail areas rather than apartments. Boro Park 24 reported that on October 31, 2024, at about 3:37 in the afternoon, a person “entered a residential building” near Ocean Parkway and Cortelyou Road, in the 66th Precinct, took a credit card from a mailing envelope in the building’s mail area and then ran up about $1,859 in purchases. Hoodline reported that early on August 7, 2025, at about 4:40 in the morning, a person got into an apartment building near Ocean Parkway and Avenue M, pried open a mailbox and took a checkbook. Neither report says how the person got through the street door. That gap is the point. In a building of sixty or seventy apartments, the street door is opened hundreds of times a day by residents, aides, couriers and visitors, and a lock that works perfectly still lets in whoever is standing close behind the last person through. Access control is how a building decides which of those openings it wants, at which door, and how it finds out about the rest.

The building stock explains the rest of the work. Openigloo’s summaries of HPD data describe 1609 Ocean Parkway as a 1929 rent-stabilized building of 59 apartments on six floors, 1777 Ocean Parkway as a 1926 building of 75 apartments, 1850 Ocean Parkway as a 1938 building of 70 apartments with 86 open violations, and 2901 Ocean Parkway as a 1926 building of 60 apartments with rent-stabilized units. These are large rentals with one main entrance and a busy cellar, and in a rental the rules about fobs are stricter than most owners expect: a 2016 DHCR ruling summarized by Landlord v. Tenant required free fobs for occupants, capped replacements at $25 and kept working metal keys for emergencies. Further along, the co-ops add staff to the picture. A listing for 820 Ocean Parkway describes a six-story, 72-unit co-op with a full-time doorman, a live-in superintendent, a common garage and a bike room, and a listing for The Park Tower at 370 Ocean Parkway describes a 12-story, 131-unit co-op with a 24-hour doorman, outdoor spaces rented at $280 a month and a waitlist for indoor parking. And there are newer buildings, such as the 2019 Ocean 24 Condominium at 379 Ocean Parkway, which its listing describes as 24 residences on eight levels with a video intercom, a garage, and parking and storage units sold separately from the apartments.

The garages are not an accident. The Special Ocean Parkway District in the city’s Zoning Resolution says, in section 113-21, that for all developments having frontage on Ocean Parkway, “all required or permitted accessory off-street parking spaces shall be completely enclosed,” and section 113-12 keeps a 30-foot landscaped front yard along the parkway. Put those together and many parkway buildings end up with their parking in a garage under or behind the building, reached from a side street, with a door from the garage into a stairwell or a cellar corridor. That inside door is one of the busiest entrances in the building and one of the least watched. It is where we start more often than the front door.

Finally, the parkway itself shapes how the work is done. NYC DOT lists Ocean Parkway among the roads where commercial vehicles are not permitted, the main roadway is flanked by one-way service roads, and the scenic landmark designation stops at the outer curbs of those service roads. None of that stops a building from adding readers to its own doors. It does mean that installation day on Ocean Parkway is planned around a side street, a service road and residents who have used one key for decades, and the rest of this page is about those details.

What the materials mean for the install

The prewar buildings on the parkway were built with heavy entrance doors in masonry openings, and many still have them. An electric strike in a steel or bronze frame set into brick takes more time than one in a modern hollow-metal frame, because the frame is often filled with mortar and the strike pocket has to be cut cleanly without cracking it. Where a door is original and the board wants it untouched, we use a lock that fits the existing mortise or put the control on the inner lobby door instead, so the street door keeps its looks and the building still gets a controlled entrance. In every case we check that the door closes and latches on its own before any electronics go on, because a reader on a door that drifts open is a log entry, not a lock.

Postwar co-ops and the newer condos are easier in one way and harder in another. Their frames are usually standard hollow metal, which takes a strike quickly, but their lobbies are finished in stone, tile or panels that nobody wants opened, so the cable route matters more than the door. We look for the path the old intercom cable took, the ceiling of the garage or the cellar under the lobby, and the chase beside the elevator before we suggest anything that would mean opening a wall. AESS does not patch or paint walls, so if a route needs an opening we say so before the work is priced, and the building decides whether it wants that route or a surface raceway along a corner.

Garage doors into the building are often fire-rated steel doors in steel frames, set between the garage and a stair or a corridor. Those doors carry a label, and a strike or electrified lock on them has to be one that is listed for that kind of door, fitted without cutting the door beyond what its listing allows. We read the label before we quote, and if it is painted over or missing, we tell the building so that the door can be checked rather than guessed at.

Between the Street Door and the Stairs: Mail Areas in Ocean Parkway Buildings

Walk into most six-story buildings on the parkway and the order of rooms is the same. The street door opens into a vestibule, sometimes only a few feet deep. An inner door leads to the lobby. Somewhere between the two, or just inside the second, is the mail area: a bank of mailboxes in the wall, a ledge or table where larger envelopes and small parcels get left, and in many buildings a corner where packages pile up until their owners come down. The mail area is the room every resident visits every day and the room a stranger reaches first. In both of the reports we found from the last two years, that is where the trouble was.

The October 2024 case near Cortelyou Road is the clearer example. The person did not break into an apartment. According to Boro Park 24, they entered the building, went to the mail area, took a credit card out of a mailing envelope and used it at several stores. The August 2025 case near Avenue M, reported by Hoodline, was a pried mailbox at about 4:40 in the morning. Neither report describes a forced street door. In our experience that usually means one of four things: someone walked in behind a resident, a door was propped or left on its latch, an old key or an unencrypted fob was copied, or the mail area sat on the street side of the only door that locks. Each of those has a different fix, and only some of them are electronic.

The first question we ask about a parkway lobby is therefore not which reader to buy. It is which side of the controlled door the mailboxes sit on. If the mailboxes are in the vestibule, outside the inner door, then anyone who gets through the street door has reached them, and the street door is the one that has to be controlled, closed and watched. If they are inside the lobby, the inner door matters most, and the vestibule can be treated as a weather lock. Where the layout allows a choice, we would rather put both doors on the system and have one tap release them in sequence, so that the outer door is locked again by the time the inner one opens. That small delay is what turns a vestibule into a place where someone following a resident in has to stop and be seen.

Mail carriers, couriers and the package shelf

Mail carriers have their own way in. The city’s own standard specification for mailboxes and postal releases describes mailboxes built to Postal Service rules with an arrow lock on the master door, and an “electro-mechanical micro-switch activated door opening system for Postal Carriers” that works from the carrier’s master key and is interfaced with the building’s entry intercom. When a building moves to fobs, that release has to keep working, and we wire it to the new controller so the carrier is not left waiting for someone to buzz. What the carrier’s release opens is a building decision: in most parkway lobbies it should open the door to the mail area and nothing further.

Couriers are different. They do not carry a master key, they arrive many times a day, and they are the people most often let in by a resident who does not want to come downstairs. The cleanest arrangement is a package shelf or room that couriers can reach through a door the building controls, with a code or a call from the panel that releases only that door, and a camera over it. In a building where packages are left on the floor of the lobby, every courier is a visitor who has to be admitted to the whole ground floor. A fob system cannot fix that by itself, but planning the doors around where the packages actually land usually can.

What a door system adds to a camera in the mail area

Many parkway buildings already have a camera over the mailboxes. A camera is the right tool for finding out afterwards who did something, and the 66th Precinct’s request for help identifying the October 2024 suspect is a reminder of how much depends on a clear picture. What a camera does not do is decide who reaches the mailboxes in the first place, or tell anyone at 4:40 in the morning that a door has been forced or left open. A controlled door with a contact does both: it releases only for a valid credential, and it reports when it is opened without one or held open too long. Paired with the camera, the alert gives the super or the managing agent a time to look at instead of a whole night of footage.

Enclosed by Zoning: Garage Entrances on the Parkway

Section 113-21 of the Zoning Resolution is one sentence long, and it explains a great deal about Ocean Parkway buildings. For every development with frontage on the parkway, accessory off-street parking “shall be completely enclosed.” Together with the 30-foot front yard required by section 113-12, it means parking along the parkway is not a lot in front of the building. Where a building has parking at all, it is usually a garage under the building or behind it, with an overhead door on a side street and a pedestrian door from the garage into a stair or a cellar corridor. Listings for parkway buildings show how common this is: 820 Ocean Parkway lists a common garage, The Park Tower at 370 Ocean Parkway rents outdoor spaces and keeps a waitlist for indoor parking, and Ocean 24 at 379 Ocean Parkway lists a garage with parking spaces sold separately from the apartments.

From a door point of view, a garage is a second lobby with a car-sized hole in it. The overhead door opens for a remote or a reader on a post, stays open for the time it takes a car to clear, and closes slowly. Anyone on foot who wants to get into the building can walk in under it. Once inside the garage, the only thing between that person and the stairs is the pedestrian door, and in many buildings that door is the one residents complain about most, because it is heavy, it is used by people carrying groceries and car seats, and it is often found propped. The street door at the front can have the best reader on the parkway, and the building is still only as closed as this door at the back.

So we plan garages as entrances in their own right. The pedestrian door gets a reader, a fail-secure strike or an electrified lock suited to its fire-rated frame, a door contact and a held-open alert with a short delay and a local chirp before anyone’s phone rings. The overhead door gets a contact so the building knows when it has been left up, and where the garage operator allows, its own reader or tag reader tied to the same list of people. Credentials are grouped so that the people who park and the people who live upstairs are not assumed to be the same: a monthly parker who does not live in the building opens the garage and nothing else, a resident without a car space does not need the garage at all, and staff get what their work requires.

When the parking space is property

In a condominium that sells parking spaces and storage units separately, as Ocean 24’s listing describes, the right to reach a space can change hands without any apartment changing hands. That is easy to get wrong. If garage access is attached only to a resident’s fob, the seller of a parking space can keep opening the garage after the closing, and the buyer may have no way in until someone remembers to add them. We set the system up with groups for each space and storage unit, so that when the board or the managing agent records a sale, the credential moves with it and the old one ends on the same day.

Co-ops have the same issue in a different form. Where spaces are rented, as at The Park Tower, garage access should start and stop with the rental, not with the shareholder’s membership in the building. A space that moves from the outdoor lot to the indoor garage when a name reaches the top of the waitlist is a change of group, not a new fob, and the system should make it a one-minute job for the office rather than a visit from us.

Fobs in a Rent-Stabilized Six-Story: What Owners Along the Parkway Should Plan For

Many of the largest buildings on the parkway are rentals, and many of those contain rent-stabilized apartments. Openigloo’s summaries list rent-stabilized units at 1609, 1777 and 2901 Ocean Parkway, among others. Owners of those buildings can modernize their doors, but a fob rollout in a stabilized building is not the same as one in a co-op, and the differences are worth knowing before anyone orders hardware.

The first is cost to tenants. In a 2016 decision summarized by Landlord v. Tenant, DHCR approved a building’s switch from keys to fobs but attached conditions: free fobs for every occupant, including children; up to four more free fobs for a tenant’s guests or employees; replacements at no more than $25; entries recorded but not exits; proof of identity checked but not recorded; metal keys that still work in an emergency; a 48-hour battery backup; and no equipment installed inside apartments. That was one case, and every building’s situation is decided on its own facts, but it is a fair picture of what DHCR has expected, and we design rentals so that an owner can meet those conditions without changing the hardware later.

The second is data. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, as summarized on HPD’s page, applies to smart access systems in multiple dwellings, including fobs, cards, apps and biometrics. Owners must get tenants’ consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only a minimum of information, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to track tenants outside the building or to harass or evict them. Tenants also cannot be required to use smart access to enter their own apartments. We set retention and reporting to match the owner’s written policy, and we leave biometric readers out of rentals unless the owner has taken separate legal advice.

The third is reliability, which tenants care about more than anything else. In November 2018 Brick Underground published a letter from a tenant whose new fob system failed when the internet went out or it rained, leaving people locked out of their own building; a tenant lawyer it quoted said that if you are “being deprived of the ability to get into your own apartment, your lease is being breached,” and another lawyer said that when the fob fails, “there needs to be a back-up system.” A rental on the parkway gets a controller that keeps every credential on site, a battery sized for the doors it runs, readers built for an outdoor entrance, and a keyed override held by the super and the owner. Rules that apply to Ocean Parkway business and building doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code’s rules for electrically locked egress doors (section 1010.1.9.8), a sensor on the egress side releases the lock, a PUSH TO EXIT button between 40 and 48 inches high and within five feet of the door cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds, the lock releases on loss of power and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and the hardware is listed to UL 294.
  • Magnetic locks. Section 1010.1.9.9 lets a door with a magnetic lock be released by hardware with a built-in switch that works with one hand and releases immediately; we use maglocks only where those conditions can be met and the door’s use suits them.
  • Apartment entrances. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and an intercom with voice and a remote release for buildings with eight or more apartments; older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants request it in writing.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, cards, apps or biometrics on residential doors, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies; an office’s or shul’s own doors are not covered by it, but we still keep records to the minimum the owner needs.
  • Electrical work. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431. The outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician, and we tell you before the job if one is needed.
  • Leases and boards. Commercial tenants usually need the landlord’s written consent, and co-op and condo units need the board’s. We give a one-page description of the devices, the holes and the cable route to send with the request.

Access Control in Ocean Parkway: Questions Answered

Mail areas and the space inside the street door

Police said the person who took a credit card from a mail area near Ocean Parkway and Cortelyou Road had simply “entered a residential building.” How does a stranger usually get as far as the mailboxes?

Rarely by breaking the door. The report did not say how the person got in, so we do not guess about that building, but in buildings like it the usual routes are the same few. Someone walks in close behind a resident, who holds the door out of courtesy. A door is propped for a delivery or a move and nobody closes it. The street door closes but does not latch, because its closer is out of adjustment or its strike was moved when the door was rehung. An old key, of which the building has long since lost count, or an unencrypted 125 kHz fob, which can be copied with cheap devices, is in the wrong hands. Or the mailboxes sit in the vestibule, on the street side of the only door that really locks.

Each route has its own fix. Closers and latches are mechanical and come first. Copied keys and fobs are solved by encrypted credentials issued to named people. Propped doors are solved by a contact and a held-open alert that tells the super within minutes. Following someone in is the hardest, and the best answer is a vestibule with both doors controlled, so the inner door releases only after the outer one has closed, with a camera that shows who came in with whom.

A mailbox was pried open before dawn in a building near Avenue M. What should a door system have told that building the next morning, or sooner?

At minimum, which door was opened around that time and how. A controlled door with a contact records every opening and whether a valid credential came before it. An opening with no credential is a door that was forced, opened from inside, or let open by someone holding it, and the system can say which of those is most likely from the sequence of events. If the mail area sits behind its own controlled door, the record narrows further to the door nearest the mailboxes.

Sooner is possible too. We can set the street door and the mail-area door to send an alert when they are opened without a credential during the night hours the building chooses, to the super’s phone and the managing agent’s, with a camera clip attached where the building has cameras. An alert at 4:40 in the morning does not stop someone already inside, but it can mean a call to 911 while the person is still in the lobby rather than a discovery at breakfast.

Our lobby already has a camera pointed at the mailboxes. Why would we spend money on a reader as well?

Because the camera and the reader answer different questions. The camera answers who it was, after the fact, provided the picture is clear and someone looks. The reader answers whether that person should have been able to reach the mailboxes at all, and it answers at the door, before anything happens. A building with only a camera has a record of the people it let in; a building with both has fewer people to record and a time to look for when something goes wrong.

The two also help each other. When the door reports an opening without a credential, the camera clip from that minute is the first thing the super sees. When the camera shows someone walking in close behind a resident, the reader’s record shows whose fob opened the door, which is often enough to find out whether the second person was a guest or a stranger. In a large building we usually put the money into the door that leads to the mail area before adding more cameras.

Garages the parkway zoning keeps indoors

The parkway’s zoning says parking for buildings on the parkway has to be completely enclosed. Why does that make our garage door one of the building’s entrances?

Because an enclosed garage connects to the building. Section 113-21 of the Zoning Resolution requires accessory parking for developments fronting the parkway to be completely enclosed, so instead of a lot in front of the building there is a garage beneath or behind it, and people who park there walk into the building through a door from the garage. That door leads to the stairs, the elevator or a cellar corridor, and from there to the lobby and every floor.

Anyone who gets into the garage, by walking in under the overhead door as it closes behind a car or through a side door left open, is one door away from the inside of the building. So that door needs what the street door has: a reader, a lock that is right for its fire-rated frame, a contact, an alert when it is held open, and a closer that pulls it shut every time. In many parkway buildings it is the first door we put on the system.

Our condo sells parking spaces and storage units separately from the apartments. When one of those is sold, how does the access move with it?

By attaching access to the space as well as to the person. We create a group for each parking space and each storage unit, or for each block of them, and the person who holds the space is placed in that group. When the board or the managing agent records a sale, the seller is removed from the group and the buyer added on the same day, without touching either person’s fob for the lobby. The record shows when each holder had access, which is useful if there is ever a question about a car or a storage cage.

If garage access is attached only to residents’ fobs, sales get missed. The seller keeps opening the garage after closing, the buyer cannot get in until someone remembers, and a buyer who does not live in the building may be given a lobby fob just to reach a car. We set the groups up at installation and show the administrator how to make the change, so it takes a minute in the software rather than a service call.

Some people who rent spaces in our garage do not live in the building. Can their credential open the garage and stop there?

Yes, and it should. A monthly parker who does not live upstairs needs the overhead door and perhaps a pedestrian door from the garage to the street. They do not need the door from the garage into the building, the lobby, the laundry or the elevator. We put them in a parking-only group that opens exactly those doors, during the hours the garage is open to them, and nothing else.

The same applies in the other direction. Residents who do not rent a space usually do not need the garage at all, which keeps the garage’s list short and the people in it accountable. Staff, such as the super, the porter and the garage attendant if there is one, get the doors their work requires. When a parking rental ends, the office ends that credential, and the building does not have a former parker with a key to the stairwell.

Reviews of 1609 Ocean Parkway on Openigloo describe a super who “will schedule appointments and will not show up,” and complaints like that are not unusual in large rentals. A fob system makes the problem worse if the only way to get a replacement is to find the super. It makes it better if the owner keeps a small stock of unassigned fobs in a locked place, two people can enroll them from a phone or a computer, and tenants know whom to call and in what time.

We set up at least two administrators, usually the managing agent’s office and the super, with the ability to cancel a lost fob and enroll a new one remotely. A tenant who reports a broken fob can have the old one switched off at once, and the new one is ready to pick up as soon as someone with the stock is on site. Until then the tenant still has a way in, because the building keeps working keys for emergencies or an intercom the tenant can be buzzed in from.

Residents carrying shopping in from a car keep wedging the lobby door. Can the door stay unlocked for a few minutes on request and then lock again by itself?

Yes, within limits. Most controllers can give a credential a longer unlock time, or treat two quick taps as a request to hold the door unlocked for a set number of minutes, after which it locks again without anyone touching it. On a lobby door with a closer, the door still closes; it simply stays unlocked so a resident can push it open with an elbow on the second trip. On a door with a powered opener, the opener can hold it open for the same period.

We keep the time short, usually a few minutes, and we leave the held-open alert in place, so that a door wedged with a box rather than held by the system still reports. We also leave the feature off for doors where it makes no sense, such as the door from the garage into the building. Most buildings find that once there is a legitimate way to keep the door unlocked for a minute, the wedges disappear.

Small shuls on the side streets

Two people, not one, and not the person who happens to be most comfortable with phones. What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Ocean Parkway

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers, vendor blogs and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each one is set against what a parkway building actually needs. We do not attribute a claim to any of those sources unless we quote it.

“Putting fobs on the front door makes an apartment building secure.”

A fob reader decides who can unlock a door. It does not close the door behind them, stop a second person walking in, or protect a mail area that sits on the street side of the lock. Fobs are part of the answer. The closer, the latch, the vestibule and the alerts are the rest, and we price them together because a reader on a door that does not latch is money spent on a log.

“Keyless entry for an apartment building is a cheap, one-day job.”

National guides show the real range is wide: Safe and Sound Security’s 2026 cost guide lists keypads at $500 to $2,500 per door and card or fob systems at $1,500 to $3,500 per door installed, and an installer on Reddit’s access control forum suggested budgeting about $2,500 for an interior door and $3,000 for an exterior one.

“A landlord can charge tenants whatever the new fobs cost.”

Not in a rent-stabilized building, where many parkway tenants live. In the 2016 decision summarized by Landlord v. Tenant, DHCR required free fobs for all occupants including children, up to four more free fobs for guests or employees, replacements at no more than $25, and working metal keys for emergencies.

Brick Underground’s reporting on DHCR rulings points the same way, and a 2020 settlement between the New York Attorney General and a security company over a Flushing rent-stabilized building involved a fob rollout that recorded driver’s license numbers and capped fobs at two per apartment.

Every case turns on its facts, and owners should take their own advice. We simply design rental systems so those conditions can be met without changing the hardware later.

“Smart entry data is the building’s business and nobody regulates it.”

In New York City it is regulated for residential buildings. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires owners of multiple dwellings using smart access to obtain consent, give tenants a written privacy and retention policy, collect only minimal data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it or use it to track tenants outside the building.

The same law bars owners from requiring smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment and from using the data to harass or evict. Biometric readers are on its list of covered technology.

That is why we ask on the first call whether a building has rental apartments, and why we set the system’s retention to match the owner’s written policy instead of leaving it on a vendor default.

“Cloud access control is always better than a local system.”

Brick Underground’s 2018 letter from a tenant whose building’s fob system failed whenever the internet went out or it rained is the cautionary tale, and one lawyer it quoted said simply that “there needs to be a back-up system.”

We install controllers that store every credential on site and open doors with the internet down, with the cloud used for management and alerts. The building gets both, and the door does not depend on the router in the super’s office.

“A garage door is a parking matter, not part of building security.”

Anyone who walks into the garage under a closing overhead door is one door from the stairs. Generic advice treats the garage as the parking operator’s problem and the lobby as the board’s, and the door between them belongs to nobody.

We treat that door as an entrance, with a reader, a listed lock for its fire-rated frame, a contact and a held-open alert, and we put garage renters, residents and staff in separate groups so a parking space is never a key to the lobby.

“A building with a doorman does not need access control.”

The garage door, the service entrance, the cellar door from the rear court, the bike room and the laundry are out of his sight.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a single office door and a 131-unit tower. What changes on Ocean Parkway is steps two and six: we walk the building the way a stranger would, from the service road to the mail area and the garage, and we enroll people by group, because parkway buildings hold residents, aides, staff, parkers and sometimes a whole congregation.

1. Photos, the door list and who decides

You send the street door, lobby door, mail area, garage door and any cellar or side doors from both sides, and tell us who approves the work: a board, an owner, a managing agent or a congregation.

2. Walk the building the way a stranger would

We follow the route of a visitor, a courier and someone walking in behind a resident, from the sidewalk to the mail area, the garage and the cellar, and check every door, frame, closer and cable path.

3. Written door plan and Brooklyn price

Every reader, lock, contact and controller, each door’s schedule, what each door does in a power cut, and the price from the Brooklyn list, in writing.

4. Make every door close and latch

Closers, hinges and strikes adjusted so the street, lobby and garage doors shut and lock by themselves before any electronics go on.

5. Cable, controller and battery

Controller and battery in a locked, dry room, cable run to each door, and a power supply on an outlet a licensed electrician provides where none exists.

6. Enrollment by group

Fobs, codes and phone keys issued to residents, garage renters, staff, vendors and congregants, with schedules and end dates checked with whoever runs the building.

7. Test with the power off

Every door tested on battery and with all power removed, to prove the inside always lets people out and the override key works where it should.

8. Handover and a follow-up visit

Logins, a door list and a one-page guide for the board, owner or administrator, and a return visit after the first weeks to adjust closers, schedules and alerts.

Once a year: a fifteen-minute check on a parkway building’s doors

Pick a quiet morning. Open the street door, the lobby door and the door from the garage into the building, and let each close by itself: does it latch without a push? Stand in the mail area and ask whether a stranger could reach it without a fob. Look at the system for the battery status and any held-open alerts that have been ignored. Find the override keys, try each one, and make sure the people who hold them still work for the building. Then print the list of active fobs and cancel every one that belongs to a former tenant, a former parker, a contractor whose job is finished or an aide who no longer comes. A list reviewed once a year stays a list the board can trust.

What moves an Ocean Parkway price up or down

  • The door and frame. A modern hollow-metal frame takes a strike quickly; an original steel or bronze frame set in masonry, or a labeled fire door between a garage and a stair, takes more time and listed hardware.
  • The cable route. An open cellar or garage ceiling under the lobby makes the run short; a finished lobby with stone walls and no old intercom path adds labor or calls for surface raceway.
  • How many people. A house needs a few codes; a 131-unit co-op needs enrollment time, more credentials, separate garage and staff groups and a phased handout.
  • The existing intercom. A video panel that can use the building’s existing riser costs less than one that needs new cable to every apartment or a move to phone answering.
  • Mechanical repairs first. Closers, latches and door hardware that need fixing before the electronics are priced separately and shown in the written plan.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start Where the Mail Lands

This describes how we start in an Ocean Parkway apartment house, not a particular customer. We do not begin at the reader. We begin on the sidewalk on the service road, with the super if he is free, and walk in the way the mail does. Through the street door: does it close by itself, and does it latch, or does it stop an inch short and wait for a push? Into the vestibule: are the mailboxes here, on the street side of the inner door, or past it? Where does the carrier’s release open, and where do couriers leave the boxes that do not fit? We stand in the mail area and ask the plainest question on the job: could a stranger reach this without anybody letting them in?

Then we go down, not up. The cellar tells us where the old intercom power supply lives, where cable can run, and whether there is a dry, lockable place for a controller and a battery. If the building has a garage, we walk from the overhead door to the pedestrian door and count how many seconds the overhead door takes to close, because that is how long someone on foot has to walk in behind a car. We open the pedestrian door, let it go and watch whether it latches. Only after that do we go back up to the lobby door. By then the board or the owner usually knows which door they want on the system first, and it is often not the one they called about.

FAQ

Ocean Parkway Access Control FAQ

In an Ocean Parkway co-op that rents out some apartments, who owns the entry records: the board, the managing agent or the installer?
+

The building does, through whoever the board names. The installer should not hold the only copy of anything. We set the board or its managing agent up as the account owner, with administrators named in writing, and we keep only what we need to service the system. Where apartments are rented to tenants, the Tenant Data Privacy Law also sets what may be collected and how long it may be kept, so the retention setting should match the building’s written policy.

Our Art Deco lobby has a pair of doors, but residents only ever use one leaf. Does the second leaf need an electric lock too?
+

Usually not. The leaf people use gets the reader and the electric lock, and the inactive leaf is held shut top and bottom by its flush bolts, with a door contact so the system reports if someone opens it. What matters is that the inactive leaf cannot be pulled open from outside when the active one releases. We check its bolts and the meeting point of the two leaves before quoting, because worn bolts are the usual weak spot in an old pair.

Roughly where does a fob system for a 60-unit, six-story rental on Ocean Parkway fall on your Brooklyn list?
+

Most land on the multi-door building line, $4,000 to $15,000, because the job is rarely one door. A typical scope is the street door, the lobby door, the cellar door and perhaps a garage door, with enough fobs for every occupant. Where the old buzzer panel is also being replaced, the video panel with fob access is $1,500 to $3,000 within that total. The figure is fixed in writing after one visit, with sales tax added.

Can a fob reader be built into the video intercom panel our condo’s developer installed, instead of a second box beside it?
+

Often, yes. Many video panels have a reader built in or a reader module made for them, and some only need the reader feature turned on and connected to a controller. We read the panel’s make and model before quoting. If the developer’s panel cannot take a reader, or its software is no longer supported, we say so plainly, and the choice is a matching reader beside it or a new panel that does both.

A long-time resident will spend several months in rehab or with family. Can her fob be paused and switched back on when she returns?
+

Yes. An administrator can suspend a credential without deleting it, so the fob stops opening doors while she is away and works again the day she comes back, with nothing to re-enroll. If a relative needs to check on the apartment in the meantime, the relative can be given a credential of their own with an end date. That way the record shows who actually came in, rather than her fob being used by someone else.

Our building and the one next door use the same brand of fob. Could a neighbor’s fob open our door by mistake?
+

Not if the system is set up properly. A controller opens a door only for credentials enrolled on that controller, so a fob from the next building is unknown to yours even if it is the same brand. Encrypted credentials go further, because they are tied to a site key and cannot be read as valid by another system. The risk is with older unencrypted fobs on systems that accept any card of a type, which we replace.

If someone opens our lobby door with the override key instead of a fob, will the system show it?
+

Yes, as an opening with no credential. The door contact reports that the door opened, and with no valid fob, code or exit request just before it, the controller records it as a forced or unexplained opening and can send an alert. Some locks also have a switch that reports the key being turned. Either way the board learns when the override was used, which is one reason we keep the override keys to a few named people.

Do you need to get into every apartment during a lobby fob installation?
+

No. Readers, locks, contacts and the controller are all in the common areas, so a fob installation at the street and lobby doors does not need access to apartments. We may ask to test the door release from a few apartment stations if the new system is connected to the existing intercom. Only a full intercom replacement, where every apartment station changes, needs entry to each unit, and that is planned and announced separately.

What should residents bring when the building hands out the new fobs?
+

Whatever the board or owner has asked for, and in rentals that should be very little. Usually it is the apartment number and the names of the occupants who need fobs, checked against the building’s records. In the 2016 DHCR decision we cite above, identity could be checked but not recorded. We set up a table in the lobby at hours that suit working residents and older ones, and hand each household a short guide with whom to call.

Can the system tell the board how many times a day the street door is used, so it can plan closer and lock maintenance?
+

Yes. The controller counts every opening at every controlled door, and a monthly total by door, without names, is an easy report to set up. A busy street door may open many hundreds of times a day, and closers, hinges and strikes wear in proportion. Knowing the counts lets the super adjust the closer before the door starts drifting open, and lets the board budget for hardware on the door that actually takes the wear.

How soon after the site visit does an Ocean Parkway building get its written price?
+

Usually within a day or two of the visit for a single door or a small building, and a little longer for a large co-op where the board wants options for phasing. The written plan lists every door, the hardware, the credentials, what each door does in a power cut and the price from the Brooklyn list. If a mechanical repair has to come first, it is priced on its own line so the board can see it.

Our managing agent’s office is in another borough. Can staff there add and cancel fobs without coming to the building?
+

Yes, with cloud management turned on. The controller in the building keeps every credential on site so doors work if the internet drops, and the agent’s staff can add, suspend and cancel fobs from a browser or an app. Physical fobs still have to be handed over, so most buildings keep a small locked stock on site with the super. We set up the agent’s logins at handover and show them how on the day.
