The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Greenwood Heights sits between the Prospect Expressway and 39th Street, from the Gowanus Canal and the harbor edge east toward Eighth Avenue, with Green-Wood Cemetery taking up most of its southeastern side. Developers named it partly after the cemetery, and for decades the blocks were simply counted as part of Sunset Park or South Slope. Mail here mostly goes to ZIP code 11232, though the northern blocks near the expressway use 11215, which is one reason city records for the neighborhood have to be read street by street.

For a door buzzer, the housing is what matters. The Department of City Planning described the area's side streets, when it rezoned them in 2005, as rows of two- to four-story houses built in the late 1800s and early 1900s as worker housing, with three- and four-story mixed buildings along Fifth Avenue and large lots of one-story warehouses and commercial buildings on the Fourth Avenue edge. Brownstoner's architecture writer Suzanne Spellen put it more plainly in 2014: "Greenwood Heights is better known for its wood framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding." Most of those houses hold one, two or three households behind a single street door. Many still have the bell pushes they were wired with generations ago, and many have an audio system that a previous owner put in and nobody has touched since.

The rezoning also allowed something the side streets never had: tall buildings on Fourth Avenue. The R8A district on the avenue, with inclusionary housing, has brought buildings like the 14-story, 187-unit rental at 737 Fourth Avenue, and in April 2026 Community Board 7 was pressing the developer of the 214-unit St. Rocco's project for more large affordable apartments. Those buildings need a lobby panel, a directory or code entry and a way for hundreds of households to answer, which is a different job from the three-button panel on a frame house a block away.

The city's housing records show where the trouble is now. When we pulled every HPD violation since January 2023 that mentions an intercom at addresses on 18th through 38th Streets and the Fourth Avenue frontage between them, we found 26, written against buildings of every size from a three-family on 23rd Street to a walk-up on 29th Street with apartments on the fourth floor. Thirteen were still open in November 2026. One building on 18th Street accounts for nine of them by itself. Three neighboring buildings on one block of 30th Street account for six more, every one still open. That is not a neighborhood without buzzers; it is a neighborhood whose buzzers are wearing out.

The people answering the door matter too. Greenwood Heights is home to older Polish and Italian families who have held the same houses for decades, to younger owners who bought and restored frame houses, to tenants in the walk-ups on Fifth Avenue and to new renters in the Fourth Avenue buildings. A dead return buzzer means the same thing to all of them: walking down the stoop stairs for every delivery, or pressing release without knowing who is outside. Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith, quoted in Brick Underground's intercom repair guide, summed up what most of them ask for now: "They want a camera to see in front of the door before allowing someone in."

Six facts that shape a buzzer job in Greenwood Heights

  • The violations are at house scale. Of the 26 intercom violations we found on these streets since January 2023, 21 named an apartment on the first three stories or the building as a whole; the other five were on a fourth floor, and none were higher.
  • Half are still open. 13 of the 26 were open when we checked on November 12, 2026, including all six on the one block of 30th Street.
  • One building can dominate the record. A single 18th Street building was cited eight times in 2023 alone, across five different apartments and once for the system "at building front to all apartments," and once more in 2024.

  • The law draws its line at eight apartments. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercom in class A buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after 1968; most Greenwood Heights houses are smaller and put one in by choice.

Install or Repair in Greenwood Heights

Need Repair in Greenwood Heights? How We Decide Between One More Fix and a New System

Plenty of installation work here starts as a phone call about a repair. A tenant on the second floor has complained, an inspector has written up the return buzzer in the kitchen, and the owner wants to know whether one visit will clear it. Brick Underground's repair guide gives the honest starting point: the faults in most building intercoms sit in a handful of places, the base unit at the entrance, the station in the apartment, the door strike or the power supply in the cellar or the lobby ceiling. A burned-out transformer, a strike whose screws have pulled out of a soft frame, or a single handset with a cut cord is a repair, and we price it as a repair.

The record on these blocks shows when it stops being one. The 18th Street building that was cited eight times in a single year, across five apartments, is the clearest example of a system where each repair cleared one violation and the next one followed within weeks. The three buildings on one block of 30th Street that have carried open violations since 2023 and 2025 are another kind of warning: when an order to repair has sat open that long, the owner is usually being told the parts are gone or the cable is bad. In both cases a new door station and power supply on pairs that test sound, or a new system on new cable where they do not, is what ends the cycle. Our technician tells you which it is only after metering every pair, and if a repair is genuinely all your building needs, our Brooklyn door buzzer team will price it that way and nothing more.

If you are not sure, the cheapest first step is a set of photos. A picture of the panel at the door, one of the bell transformer or power supply in the cellar and one of a station inside an apartment usually tells us the age and make of the system, which in turn tells us whether parts still exist for it.

They differ in the things that decide a buzzer job: how many households share the door, what the walls are made of, where the cellar is and who can reach it, and what more than a century of use has done to the street door and its frame.

Two- to four-story frame houses from the late 1800s and early 1900s on the side streets, some restored to clapboard and trim and many under aluminum or vinyl siding, with hollow walls and narrow door casings.

Brick and brownstone rows

Three- and four-story mixed buildings along Fifth Avenue, many facing the cemetery fence between 24th and 34th Streets, with a residents' door squeezed beside a storefront and one long stair.

Roosevelt Court and Woodrow Court near 30th Street and Fifth Avenue, rows of houses built in 1922 that face each other across a shared walk, with their back yards toward the cemetery.

Wood-frame worker rows: easy walls, tired doors

Brownstoner's 2014 look at 207A to 209 18th Street, a pair of Queen Anne rowhouses built before 1888, noted that on the 1888 maps "wood framed row houses dominated both sides of the block." That is the building we see most. In a frame house the stud bays run from the cellar to the attic, so a new cable can usually be dropped or fished inside a wall from the cellar to each floor with only small openings at the top and bottom. That is why a first talking system in a two- or three-family frame house so often lands in the lower half of our ranges.

The difficult part is the street door. Frame houses settle, doors get planed and rehung to keep them closing, and the wood around the latch has often been patched after decades of replaced locks. An electric strike needs sound wood to hold its screws and a door that meets its frame squarely so the latch drops into the strike every time. Where the frame cannot give that, a reinforcing plate or frame repair comes first, and it is named on the quote before you approve, never found on installation day.

Siding changes how the door station is fastened. On a house under aluminum or vinyl, the panel goes on the door casing or on a solid backing block set behind the siding, not on the siding itself, which flexes with every change in temperature and would work the screws loose within a winter. On a restored clapboard front, we choose a slim station and a mounting spot that puts the fewest holes in new trim.

Brick and brownstone rows: the cable route is the price

The masonry rows mixed in among the frame houses look similar from the street but behave differently once a drill comes out. Solid brick party walls and plaster on brick inside mean new cable cannot simply be fished. If the house has an old multi-pair bell cable running up a closet, a chase or the stair hall, and its pairs test sound from the cellar to each floor, a new system can reuse it, which saves both money and dust. If not, the route is planned with the owner floor by floor, through closets, beside the stair or in a slim raceway painted to match.

Many brick rows here also have a vestibule with an inner door. The release normally goes on whichever door closes and latches best, and the mailboxes are kept where the carrier can reach them, which we settle at the walkthrough rather than assume.

Fifth Avenue walk-ups: two front doors that must never share a system

The Brooklyn Eagle's 2020 walk along Fifth Avenue described the stretch from 24th to 34th Street as cemetery on one side and Greenwood Heights homes on the other, including siding-covered rowhouses such as 822 Fifth Avenue at 34th Street. The mixed buildings along that frontage put a narrow residents' door beside a shop. That door gets its own panel, its own release and its own power supply, separate from anything the store uses, so a shop renovation or a change of tenant never leaves the apartments without a working door. The panel has to sit where a roll-down gate, a sign or a sidewalk display can never cover it, and its cable climbs the residents' stair rather than passing through the store.

The 1922 courts: the door is not on the street

Forgotten New York's walk around Green-Wood described Roosevelt and Woodrow Courts, built in 1922, as rows of houses that seem "turned away from the cemetery," their back yards facing the fence. Their front doors face a shared walk instead of a street. For a buzzer, that means the station has to be where a courier coming in from Fifth Avenue or 30th Street will look for it, clearly numbered, with a camera aimed down the walk rather than straight out at the house opposite. We decide the spot standing where a delivery driver would stand.

Fourth Avenue buildings: lobby, side street and garage

The newer buildings on Fourth Avenue are a different scale of job: a lobby panel with a directory or code entry, stations or phone answering for every apartment, and often a package room, a parking entrance and a service door. On the stretch of the avenue north of 24th Street, the Department of City Planning's 2011 Fourth Avenue rules generally limit curb cuts to the side streets, so on those blocks the secondary doors usually face a numbered side street rather than the avenue; farther south we check each building's plans before assuming where they are. We plan them together on one system so the building's staff or managing agent manages one directory, not three.

Mixed buildings between Third and Fourth: workshop below, homes above

The blocks closer to the canal still carry warehouses, workshops and small manufacturers, and new buildings like the one filed in 2026 for 156 21st Street mix manufacturing space and parking with other uses. Where people live above a business, the residents' door and the business door are planned as two systems that share nothing but a wall, and the residents' station sits where the noise of a loading dock or a roll-up door does not drown out a visitor's voice.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What Our Technician Looks at on a Greenwood Heights Stoop Before Anything Is Priced

On a Greenwood Heights walkthrough our technician does four things before any equipment is mentioned. They take roughly half an hour on a frame house and longer on a walk-up, and they decide most of the price. This is our standing practice on every door here, not one particular job.

The stoop and the door come first. The technician climbs the stoop the way a visitor does, notes where a panel can be fastened to something solid, and checks whether the light at the door is enough to see a face at night. Then the door is opened fully, let go and watched. It should swing shut, latch on the first try and not bounce back. A frame house door that has to be pulled shut, or one that has been shaved so thin it rattles in the frame, is noted before anything electronic.

Next, the households. The technician counts bell pushes, mailboxes and apartment doors, and asks who actually lives where. A house listed as a two-family may have a third household on the top floor or in the garden level, and every household that gets visitors needs its own button.

Then the cellar. On a frame house the old bell transformer and the wiring usually start there, sometimes beside a newer phone or cable TV run that crosses the same joists. Each pair to the floors is traced and metered, and the result goes on the quote: kept, or replaced. Last, the record: the technician compares what the building's HPD violations say with what the meter shows, because an apartment cited three times usually points straight at its own run.

The City's Record

What 26 Intercom Violations Since 2023 Say About Greenwood Heights Doors

NYC Open Data publishes every violation HPD writes under the Housing Maintenance Code, with the address, the apartment, the story, the date and the inspector's own description. Because Greenwood Heights is split between two ZIP codes and shares 11232 with Sunset Park, we did not count the whole ZIP. We pulled every violation since January 1, 2023 whose description mentions an intercom, in ZIP codes 11232 and 11215, and kept only the addresses on 18th through 38th Streets and on Fourth Avenue between them. That left 26 violations: 11 in 2023, 2 in 2024, 8 in 2025 and 5 in 2026 through early November. Thirteen were still open on November 12, 2026.

They cluster. One building on 18th Street, on the 11215 side, accounts for nine. Inspectors wrote it up in January 2023, twice in September 2023, once more that September for the intercom "at building front to all apartments," three times in October 2023 and again in May 2024, across apartments on the first, second and third floors. Every one of those has since been closed or dismissed, which tells you the owner kept repairing, and the repairs kept failing, for well over a year. On 30th Street, three neighboring buildings in 11232 carry six violations written between October 2023 and December 2025, and all six were still open when we looked. On 29th Street, one building has three open violations for its fourth-floor apartments, from September 2025, May 2026 and July 2026.

The inspectors' wording says what failed. They write that the "return buzzer" is broken, meaning the button in the apartment no longer opens the street door; that the "bell buzzer" is defective, meaning the apartment no longer rings; that the system is broken "from building front entrance" to a particular apartment; or, as on a 23rd Street building in October 2026, that the "bell, buzzer and return intercom system" is defective "in the entire apartment." One 29th Street violation describes the system as running "from building front entrance to tenant phone," a reminder that some older buildings here still answer the door on wall-mounted handsets. Every one of the 26 is an order under § 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, the owner's duty to keep what exists in good repair.

The record also shows what the neighborhood is not. Across all of ZIP 11232 since 2023, the streets with the most intercom violations were 39th, 41st and 43rd Streets, six each, on the Sunset Park blocks south of the cemetery's edge, where larger walk-ups are more common. Greenwood Heights' own violations come from smaller buildings: 21 of the 26 named an apartment on the first three stories or the building as a whole, and the five others were fourth-floor apartments in the 29th and 30th Street walk-ups.

How we use the record on a Greenwood Heights job

  • Before the visit. We look up the building's intercom and door violations, so the technician arrives knowing which apartments have failed and how often.
  • When choosing repair or replacement. One violation for one station is usually a repair. The same apartment cited again within months, or several apartments cited within a year, points to the cable, the power or the whole system, and the quote says so.
  • When scheduling. An open violation has a correction period. Where the equipment allows, we plan the installation date inside it, and we tell you if a special-order part makes that impossible.
  • At handover. We leave a written test sheet listing every apartment, what was tested and the result. Certifying the correction to HPD is the owner's or managing agent's job; the test sheet is what they certify with.

What the record cannot show is how many frame houses here have no talking system at all. A building of fewer than eight apartments is not required to have an intercom, so its absence draws no violation. Those houses only appear in the data after something is installed and later breaks, which is why we put as much care into how a first system is built in a frame house as into replacing a failed one on 30th Street.

The Door Comes First

A Greenwood Heights Street Door Has to Shut, Latch and Open From Inside Before It Gets a Release

Every entry system is two systems. One is electronic: the panel, the stations, the cable and the power. The other is mechanical: the door, its hinges, its closer, its latch and the strike in its frame. Owners shop for the first. Most of the failures we find at street doors in Greenwood Heights are in the second, and on a frame house more than a century old the second needs the most attention.

What the city expects of an entrance door

Where the rules apply, meaning class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments, Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a and the Buildings Department rule 1 RCNY §42-01 require automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at the entrances, a door that can be opened from inside without a key, and a bell or speaking device for each apartment with a return buzzer that releases the lock. The rule also sets heights: push buttons no more than six feet above the floor and the speaking device between four and five feet. Most Greenwood Heights houses are below the eight-apartment line, so these are not legal requirements for them, but they are a sound standard for any street door, and we build to them as a matter of course.

Inside, Local Law 111 of 2018 required doors to interior corridors and stairs in residential buildings to be self-closing, and HPD expects those doors kept in working order. A release must never stop any door from closing on its own.

What that means on installation day

Before a strike is cut into the frame, the door has to swing freely, close from any position and latch without help. If the closer is worn, too weak or so strong that a visitor cannot push the door once buzzed in, it is adjusted or replaced. If the latch misses its keeper because the door has dropped on its hinges, the hinges are tightened or the door rehung. Only then does the release go in, so the electronic half has a mechanical half it can rely on.

On residential street doors we use fail-secure electric strikes. Allegion's guide explains the difference: fail-safe products unlock when power is removed and fail-secure products stay locked, and with an electric strike a person inside can always leave by turning the lever, whichever type it is. A fail-secure strike keeps the house locked through a power cut and never traps anyone inside. Magnetic locks, which Allegion notes are only available fail-safe, are a last resort on a street door and come with the release devices and signage the building code requires.

The same thinking applies while the work is going on. When the old panel and strike come off, the street door is never left unlocked and unattended. Either the old lock stays working until the new release is tested, or a crew member stays at the door until it is.

Rules and Responsibilities

Which Greenwood Heights Buildings Must Have an Intercom, Who Has to Keep It Working and Who Decides

  • Eight or more apartments: required. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to have self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system allowing voice communication with each apartment and remote release of the door.
  • Older buildings of eight or more: on tenants' request. For buildings put up before 1968, §50-a ties the duty to a written request or consent from tenants occupying a majority of the apartments, with installation to begin within 90 days and finish within six months. If your building falls in this group, ask HPD or your lawyer how it applies to you.
  • A working intercom is treated as essential. Habitat Magazine's February 2024 column, drawing on lawyers who handle co-op disputes, noted that courts have treated intercoms as essential services and that a broken one can breach the warranty of habitability. As one of them, Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group, put it about a co-op with a failed system: "the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom."
  • Anything that exists must be maintained. All 26 violations we read for Greenwood Heights were written under § 27-2005, the owner's general duty to keep the building in good repair. A three-family with a buzzer is cited for a broken one just as a larger building is.
  • Small houses are the owner's choice. A one-, two- or three-family frame house with no buzzer draws no violation. Most first systems we install here go in because owners and tenants want them.
  • Conversions count. Adding apartments that bring a building to eight or more brings the door and intercom requirements with it, so a conversion is the moment to plan the entry system, not after.
  • Tenants can complain; owners decide the work. Habitat's column lists a letter to the board, a 311 complaint and an HP proceeding in Housing Court as tenants' routes. The installation itself is ordered by whoever controls the building, and we take instructions from the owner, the board or the managing agent.

None of this is legal advice for a particular building. If you are unsure which rules apply to yours, HPD, the Buildings Department or your own lawyer can tell you. What we do is install a system that meets those rules where they apply and leave a written record of what was installed and tested.

Rules that apply to business entry installations in Greenwood Heights

  • Anyone inside can always get out. A business door with a buzzer, code or reader still opens from inside with one motion and no key. With an electric strike, the lever or push bar on the inside works whatever the release is doing.
  • Controls within reach. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum and the low at 15 inches minimum for operable parts, so the button a customer presses and the release a receptionist uses go inside that range wherever a business serves the public.
  • Tenants need the landlord's written approval. A shop or workshop that rents its space orders a door buzzer only with the owner's written permission, and its cable stays clear of the residents' system.
  • Magnetic locks come as a package. Where a door cannot take a strike, a magnetic lock comes with a sensor and a push-to-exit release on the egress side, power-loss unlocking and a fire alarm connection where the code requires one, and we say so before anyone chooses it.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Greenwood Heights: Questions Answered

Frame houses built as worker housing

Our wood-frame row on 20th Street went up before 1900 and still has three households behind one door. Is a house that old worth wiring, or is wireless the sensible choice?

Wiring is usually the better and cheaper choice in a frame house of that age. The stud bays in a wood-frame row run from the cellar to the attic, so a new cable to each floor can generally be fished inside the walls with a few small openings at the top and bottom. Wired stations get their power from one supply in the cellar, never need batteries and do not depend on a radio signal getting through floors, plaster and lath.

The electric release in the street door is wired whatever the stations are, because it needs dependable power. Wireless earns its place where a run truly cannot be made, such as a studio at the back of the yard. For three households, a wired audio system usually sits in our $400 to $900 range and a video system in the $700 to $1,800 range, with the door and the cable test deciding where.

Our 1880s frame house's street door has been planed and rehung so often that it barely meets the frame. What has to happen before a release can go in?

The door has to be made to close and latch on its own first. That can mean resetting or replacing worn hinges so the door hangs square, shimming or rebuilding the frame on the latch side so there is sound wood for the strike's screws, and fitting or adjusting a closer. A door that has lost wood from repeated planing sometimes needs a reinforcing plate or a new strike jamb section.

We name every one of those items on the quote, with its price, before you approve. If the door itself is too far gone to take a strike reliably, we tell you that too, and the release waits until a carpenter has replaced or rebuilt it, because a buzzer on a door that drifts open protects nothing.

Our parents have lived in our house since the 1970s and read Polish more easily than English. Can the new system be set up so they can use it without help?

Yes, and it is mostly a matter of choosing simple equipment and labeling it clearly. For residents who want nothing complicated, an audio or video station with one talk button and one door button, marked with symbols rather than words, is easier than any app. Directory labels at the door can carry the family name, the apartment number or both, and labels inside can be printed in whatever wording the family prefers.

At handover the technician shows each household how to answer, talk and release, standing at their own station, and the family keeps the test sheet. If a younger relative wants calls on a phone as well, phone answering can be added alongside the station without changing how the parents use theirs.

What the city record shows on these blocks

Three buildings next to each other on our block of 30th Street all have intercom violations open at the same time. Is that a problem with the block or with each building?

Almost always with each building. Intercoms are not connected between houses, so three open violations side by side usually mean three systems of a similar age, installed around the same time and wearing out together, often under owners who have been told the same thing about parts. The violations on that block run from October 2023 to December 2025 and include apartments on the second, third and fourth floors, which is the pattern of old systems, not of one shared fault.

Each building gets its own system, door station and power supply, priced on its own quote. Where neighboring owners want the work done in the same week, we can schedule it that way, and choosing the same equipment for adjoining buildings means one set of spare parts and one way of managing them.

The violation for our 29th Street walk-up describes the intercom as running to a "tenant phone." Can a new system keep a handset on the wall, or does everyone have to move to cell phones?

A new system can keep a wall handset or a wall station in every apartment, and in a building with older tenants we usually recommend it. Wired systems ring a station on the wall of each apartment; most can also ring residents' phones as an option. Nobody has to use an app if they do not want to.

The "tenant phone" wording in an HPD violation usually describes an older system in which each apartment answers on a phone-style handset. The violations at that building are on the fourth floor, cited three times between September 2025 and July 2026, which points at the runs to that floor. A new installation meters those runs first and replaces the ones that fail.

The avenues and the newer buildings

At the person standing at the door, from slightly above, with enough light on their face. On a quiet frontage like that the camera's job is to show residents upstairs exactly who is ringing, so the station goes beside the residents' door at a height where its lens sees a face rather than the top of a hat, and the doorway light is checked at night as well as during the day.

In every system we install, yes: one lobby panel serves every apartment, and each apartment gets the same station or phone answering the building has chosen. What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Greenwood Heights

Search summaries and AI assistants answer buzzer questions with figures lifted from national cost guides and from the blogs of companies that sell entry systems. Some of those figures are fair for the places they were written about. Here are seven that come up again and again, each traced to where it comes from and set against what we actually find on the frame rows, Fifth Avenue walk-ups and Fourth Avenue lobbies of Greenwood Heights.

"An intercom installation runs anywhere from $1,500 to $15,000"

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost page gives that spread, with hard-wired systems at $1,000 to $15,000 and wireless at $150 to $1,500. It is a national range that mixes room-to-room house intercoms with apartment entry systems.

For most Greenwood Heights houses the bottom of that range is too high. A two-family frame house with a sound door and usable bell pairs often lands inside our $400 to $900 audio range, and a three-family with video inside $700 to $1,800. The top of the range belongs to big buildings, not to the side streets.

Where the wide range is useful is as a warning: what separates a $1,500 job from a $15,000 one is the number of apartments and the cable, which is exactly what our walkthrough measures before we give a figure.

"Modern video entry costs $1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door"

Swiftlane's apartment intercom cost guide prices modern IP or video systems that way, per door. Swiftlane sells cloud entry systems, and its examples are generic apartment buildings.

Per door is the wrong unit for a frame row with one door and three households. The cost there follows the households and the condition of the cable and the door. Per door makes more sense at a Fourth Avenue building with a lobby, a parking entrance and a service door, but even there the side doors often need a keypad or a release on the same system, not a second full panel.

So the right question for a Greenwood Heights owner is not how many doors, but how many households, how many floors and what is in the walls.

"Wireless is the way to go if you plan to do it yourself"

HomeAdvisor says wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY, and for a one-family house with a doorbell that is a fair statement.

A street door shared by several households is different. The release in the frame needs wired power whatever the stations are, and the stations have to work through floors, plaster and, in the brick rows, solid masonry. In a frame house, where new cable climbs easily inside the walls, a wired system often costs little more than a wireless one and lasts far longer without batteries.

We use wireless where a run genuinely cannot be made and say so on the quote. For the main system, wiring is what keeps it out of the violation record.

"Each intercom station takes only an hour or two to install"

HomeAdvisor gives that time per station for a whole-house system. Brick Underground's repair guide reports installers estimating about a day for a five-apartment building and about two days for a hundred units.

The station itself is quick. What takes time in Greenwood Heights is everything around it: making a planed, rehung frame house door latch reliably, fitting a strike into a patched jamb, fishing cable past fire blocking in old stud bays, or finding a route through a brick row without opening finished walls.

That is why we price after a walkthrough rather than per station, and why the quote lists door work and cable work as their own lines.

"A cellular intercom needs no building wiring at all"

ButterflyMX describes its intercom as one that "requires no building wiring," and cellular call boxes do avoid running a network cable to the door.

They still need power at the door and a release in the frame, and both are wired. They also ring only residents who have the app and a phone, so the older households on the side streets, or anyone who wants a station on the kitchen wall, need something else as well.

For some Fourth Avenue lobbies an app-based panel makes sense. For a three-family frame house, a wired system with no subscription is usually simpler and cheaper over a few years.

"Most intercom problems are just user error"

The line comes from a Brooklyn locksmith quoted in Brick Underground's repair guide, and it is true that some service calls end with a button that was not held long enough.

The violation record for these blocks tells a different story. Nine violations at one 18th Street building in little more than a year, six open violations across three neighboring buildings on 30th Street, and three for the fourth floor of a 29th Street walk-up are not people pressing buttons wrong. They are cable, power and systems at the end of their life.

When one household has an occasional problem, a check of how the station is being used is the right first step. When several households, or the same apartment again and again, have it, the system needs to be tested.

"Subscriptions only add a few dollars per apartment"

Swiftlane's guide says software subscriptions commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month. For a single apartment that sounds small.

Multiplied out, it is not. At those rates a Fourth Avenue building with 187 apartments would be paying hundreds to thousands of dollars every month, indefinitely, and a three-family house would pay a monthly bill for a door that a wired system answers for nothing after installation.

We show any subscription as its own line on the quote, next to the wired alternative, so an owner or board can compare the cost over five or ten years rather than the first month.
