The Neighborhood

Why Staten Island Homes Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Door by Door

Most of the keyless-entry advice written about New York City assumes an apartment: one door, a landlord, a board, a lobby. Staten Island mostly isn't that. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council's 2024 look at the city's housing typologies found that 83 percent of the island's homes are in one- or two-family buildings. Census QuickFacts puts the owner-occupied rate for Richmond County at 67.7 percent for 2020–2024. In practice that means the person deciding what goes on the front door is usually the person who lives behind it, and the person who pays when a lock is fitted badly is the same person.

It also means the house has several doors, and they are not equal. A detached colonial on a Mid-Island street may have a front door that guests use twice a month, a side door off the driveway that the family uses every day, and a door from the attached garage straight into the kitchen. A semi-attached two-family built after the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge opened in 1964 often has a garage under the house, an outside door to the lower unit, and a separate stair door for the upper unit. A house on the East Shore rebuilt after Hurricane Sandy may have its main entrance at the top of a long run of steps, with the old ground-floor door now opening into flood-vented storage. Putting one smart lock on the front door and calling the house keyless rarely matches how anyone in these homes actually comes and goes.

So we start with a question most installers skip: which doors does each person in the household really use, and when? The answer decides where the smart locks go, which door gets a keypad you can read in the dark, which door locks itself every time it closes, and which door keeps an ordinary key because nobody should be opening it remotely. It also decides how the locks connect. A router in the upstairs hallway of a brick-faced two-family may reach the front door easily and the door at the back of an attached garage not at all, and a detached garage at the end of a driveway is a different radio problem again.

Then there is the weather. A good part of the island faces open water — the Lower Bay along the East Shore, Raritan Bay at the southern tip near Tottenville, the Kill Van Kull on the North Shore and the Arthur Kill to the west. Exterior hardware here lives with wind-driven rain and, close to the shore, salt in the air. One commercial lock maker, dormakaba, defines an “ocean front” application in its lock-finish guide as an exterior lock on a property within 3 miles of a saltwater body, and states that using a lighter finish there voids the lock's warranty. Residential brands set their own terms, so we check the paperwork for the exact lock you choose, but the point stands: on this island the outside face of the lock deserves as much thought as the app.

Finally, many Staten Island homes are shared across generations, or split between an owner and a tenant. A parent who doesn't use a smartphone, a home health aide who comes on weekdays, a tenant in the basement unit who needs their own door and their own privacy, a teenager who forgets to lock up — each of those is a code-planning decision, not a hardware decision, and getting it right on day one is what makes a smart lock feel like an improvement instead of a new chore.

Door and lock details we check on every Staten Island house

  • Door thickness and bore. Most smart deadbolts are listed for doors around 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick with a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset; Schlage's published door-prep figures, for example, run from 1-3/8 to 2 inches. Older North Shore doors can be thicker, and doors that have had three different locks over the years may have bores that need filling before anything new goes in.
  • Swing direction. Side and basement doors on the island often swing outward over steps. Outswing doors expose hinge pins to the outside and change which smart locks fit; we check before you buy.
  • Glass near the lock. Sidelights and glass panels within reach of the thumb-turn affect both lock choice and placement. A double-cylinder deadbolt is not the answer on a required exit door (more on that below).
  • Self-closing garage-entry doors. The door between an attached garage and the house is a fire separation in the model residential code — solid wood or steel at least 1-3/8 inches thick, or a 20-minute rated door, with a self-closing device. A smart lock on that door has to let it close and latch on its own.
  • Storm doors and screens. The gap between a storm door and the entry door decides whether a bulky interior or exterior escutcheon fits at all.
  • Exposure. Which way the door faces, whether there is an overhang, and how close the house is to open water decide the finish and whether the keypad needs extra protection.
  • Signal at the door. We test Wi-Fi and Bluetooth at every door with a phone before recommending a connection method, especially in garages and basements.

Compliance callouts for business doors

  • Free egress. The 2022 New York City Building Code, section 1010.1.9, requires egress doors to be “readily openable from the egress side without the use of a key or special knowledge or effort.” A keypad outside is fine; a code or key needed to get out is not.
  • Main-entrance key locking. Section 1010.1.9.3 lets certain main entrance doors in business, mercantile and storage occupancies be key-locked under the conditions in section 1010.1.4.4. Where a shop relies on that, the smart lock has to fit the same conditions, and we leave that call to the code and your landlord's requirements rather than guess.
  • One motion to open. Section 1010.1.9.5 says unlatching a door shall not require more than one operation, outside the listed exceptions. A smart deadbolt plus a separate latch that both need turning can fail that test on a business exit.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure. Electric strikes and magnetic locks either release or stay locked when power is lost. We match that choice to the door's role and confirm free egress either way.
  • Mixed-use buildings. Where a shop and a home share a building, the residential door and the commercial door are planned separately so staff codes never open the family's entrance.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Staten Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Staten Island homeowners bring to a walkthrough that the generic guides don't cover. They are grouped by the part of the house, and by the people in it, that the question is really about.

Garage, side and basement doors

Should the door from our garage into the house lock itself every time it shuts?

For most families, yes. That door is the one people treat as an inside door, because the garage feels like part of the house, and it is the one most often found unlocked at night. A smart deadbolt or keypad lock with auto-lock throws the bolt a set time after the door closes — we usually start at around thirty seconds so someone carrying shopping in two trips isn't locked out between them, then adjust it after a week of real use.

Two conditions have to be met first. The door must close and latch fully on its own, because in the model residential code the door between an attached garage and the living space is a fire separation with a self-closing device, and a bolt that fires against a door resting half an inch open will jam or strain the motor. And the lock needs a door-position sensor or a lock that can tell the door is actually shut; better models will refuse to throw the bolt into thin air. We adjust the closer and strike as part of the install so both are true.

If the garage is where the household's cars are charged or where a teenager parks a bike at 11 p.m., we also look at whether the keypad side should face the garage at all. Some families prefer the keypad inside the garage and only a thumb-turn on the kitchen side; others want the keypad hidden and rely on phones. Either works; what matters is that the door can't be left open by accident.

Our basement apartment has its own door down a stairwell — how do we lock it securely without anyone getting trapped?

Start with the exit. The New York City Building Code's egress chapter requires doors that people leave by to be readily openable from the inside without a key or special knowledge or effort, and the exception for dwelling-unit doors allows a deadbolt, night latch or chain only if it opens from the inside without a key or tool. For a basement unit, that door may be the occupant's main way out in a fire. So the inside is always a thumb-turn or lever, never a second keypad, never a key cylinder.

The outside is where the smart part goes. A keypad deadbolt rated for exterior use, with a keyed override as a backup, gives the occupant their own codes and means the owner never has to hand out copies. Stairwell doors get rain, snowmelt and leaves pooling at the threshold, so we favour models whose electronics sit on the inside of the door and whose outside face has a gasket, and we check that water drains away from the threshold rather than toward the lock.

Finally, think about who controls it. If the unit is rented, the tenant should hold the admin role for their own door, and the owner should have whatever key or code the lease provides for, not a live view of every entry. We set that up explicitly so there is no argument later about who can see what.

Our side door's hinges are on the outside — is a smart lock on it worth having?

Only once the hinges are dealt with. Many side and basement doors on the island open outward because the landing inside is too small for the door to swing in, and that puts the hinge pins on the outside. If those pins can be tapped out, the door can be lifted off its frame with the bolt still thrown, and the best lock in the world won't matter.

The fix is cheap and quick: non-removable hinge pins, or security studs on the hinge edge that hold the door in the frame even with the pins removed, plus longer screws through the hinge leaves into the framing. We do this as part of the door work before fitting the lock. We also confirm the strike suits an outward-swinging door, since some smart deadbolts list a different strike or limited support for that arrangement.

Placement matters on these doors too. The keypad shouldn't sit where someone standing on a narrow step has to lean over a drop or around a railing to reach it, so we check the approach before deciding which side of the stile it goes on.

Weather and the waterfront

We raised the house after Sandy and the front door is now at the top of a long stair — does that change which lock we should choose?

It changes several things. The door is now fully exposed: wind-driven rain reaches it from every direction, often with no overhang, and on the East Shore it faces open water. That pushes us toward locks whose outside face is sealed and whose finish is meant for exterior use, and toward placing the keypad where water running down the door won't sit on it. The dormakaba finish guide's three-mile rule is one maker's definition, but it's a useful reminder of how close “waterfront” really is on this island.

It also changes how the door is used. With the main entrance a full storey up, families often start using the ground-level door into the storage area, or the garage, as their everyday way in. Those doors then need locks too, and a ground level designed to flood needs hardware that can be wiped down or replaced without drama. We usually recommend keeping the smart lock upstairs, where the electronics stay dry, and a robust keyed or mechanical keypad lock below.

And it changes deliveries and visitors. A long stair means you don't want to run down to let someone in, so a video doorbell at the top, with remote unlock limited to people you can see, becomes more valuable than it would be on a door at street level.

Our front door faces south with no overhang — does direct sun and rain matter for a keypad?

It matters more than most spec sheets suggest. Published operating ranges are generous — Schlage lists its Encode keypad deadbolts for outside temperatures from about −31°F to 150.8°F — but a dark-finished keypad on a south-facing door in July, or one that sits in a sheet of rain every storm, ages faster than one under a porch roof. Touchscreen keypads can also be harder to read in direct sunlight.

On fully exposed doors we lean toward keypads with physical buttons or good contrast, lighter or weather-rated finishes, and installation that respects the maker's gasket and drain details. Where the storm door allows it, closing the storm door over the lock is the simplest protection of all.

If you already own a lock that is mostly black glass and plan to put it on an exposed door, we will still install it, but we will tell you what to expect and where to watch for water ingress, so a slow failure doesn't become a lockout.

Should a smart lock that faces the water get regular maintenance, and what does that involve?

A little, and it's worth doing. Commercial hotel-lock guidance for salt-mist areas goes as far as wiping salt off the lock weekly with a damp cloth; a homeowner doesn't need to be that strict, but a wipe of the keypad face and the bolt every few weeks during the windy months, and a check that the bolt throws smoothly, catches most problems early.

Twice a year we suggest a slightly fuller check: replace batteries before they reach the low-battery warning rather than after, test the keyed override so the cylinder hasn't seized from never being used, and look at the strike for corrosion or for the door having shifted. A key that has never been turned in three years is often the thing that fails on the one night it's needed.

If you'd rather not think about it, a combined visit for all the doors on the house can be booked at the setup-only rate, and doors that need hardware work are quoted before anything is replaced.

Family, caregivers and everyday codes

What should happen to the codes when a grown child moves out or a relative stops visiting?

Remove them, the same week. Households change: a grown child moves into their own place, a relative who used to stay over stops coming, a former partner still knows the side-door code. A smart lock only stays secure if the code list matches the people who should still be coming in.

Because each person should have their own code, removing one is a few seconds in the app and nobody else is affected. If the household has been sharing a single family code, this is the moment to switch to individual ones and retire the shared code. Phone credentials work the same way: remove the person from the lock's account, not just the code.

Do the keys as well. If the person had a key to the override cylinder, either get it back or rekey the cylinders. We include a written code-and-key list with every installation precisely so that this clean-up is easy years later.

Does everyone in the house need the app, or can some people just use a code?

Only one or two people need the app. Everyone else can live entirely with a keypad code and, if they want one, a key. Most keypad smart locks are designed so the app is for the administrator — adding and removing codes, setting schedules, reading history — and the code is for everybody.

That suits Staten Island households where a grandparent, a younger child or a tenant has no interest in another app. Each of them gets a personal code that works on the doors they use. The administrator handles the rest, ideally with a second admin on another phone so the house is never dependent on one device.

When choosing locks, favour real buttons or large, high-contrast digits with a backlight, and a simple wake-up. We test each person's code at their own door before we leave, with them standing there, so nobody discovers a problem the first night they come home late.

Our teenager keeps leaving the side door unlocked — what can the lock actually do about it?

Quite a lot. Auto-lock will throw the bolt a set time after the door closes, so “forgot to lock it” stops being possible on that door. Many locks can also send a notification when a particular person's code is used, so a parent at work knows when someone got home, without anyone having to text.

Give the teenager their own code rather than sharing the family one. If friends start turning up at odd hours or the code gets passed around, you can change one code without changing everybody's, and the history shows exactly which code opened the door.

Some families also set an evening check in the app: if any door is unlocked at, say, 11 p.m., the phone says so and the door can be locked remotely. That needs the lock to be online, so we confirm the signal at that door during installation.

If an elderly parent lives alone, how do we plan for emergency entry around a smart lock?

Think it through before it's needed. A smart lock can make emergency entry easier — a family member can unlock the door remotely for a neighbour or a responder, or read out a temporary code over the phone — but only if the lock is online, the person with admin access is reachable, and the code works on the door that can actually be reached quickly.

We suggest at least two people with admin access, a spare code kept by a trusted neighbour, and a key that still works in the cylinder. Where a medical alert service is involved, ask the service how they want entry handled; some keep a code or key on file. Avoid chain guards or secondary bolts that can only be released from inside, since they defeat every remote option.

The inside of the door should be as simple as possible for the parent: one motion to open, no key, no code. Rapid exit and easy entry by helpers are the two halves of the same plan.

Two-family houses

In a two-family with one shared side door, should owner and tenant share a code or have separate ones?

Separate ones, always. Many Staten Island two-families have a shared vestibule or side door that leads to each unit's own door. That shared door is the owner's to manage, like a small lobby, but the tenant's code on it should belong to the tenant: personal, permanent, working at all hours, and never changed without notice.

The tenant's own unit door is a different matter. That is their home, and they should control codes for their guests. If you want a key or code for emergencies or agreed access, put it in the lease and keep it sealed or unused day to day. Under New York City's Administrative Code section 26-521, disabling or changing the lock on someone's home without giving them a key is an unlawful eviction once they have occupied it for 30 consecutive days, and the section is written for dwelling units generally, not only apartment buildings — so a code deleted in anger is a legal problem, not a technical one.

A two-family house is a “private dwelling” under the state Multiple Dwelling Law's definitions — the law's multiple-dwelling rules start at three families — so many lock rules written for apartment buildings don't apply in the same way. The lease and the city's eviction protections do the work instead. We set up the locks so the arrangement you agree with your tenant is what the system actually enforces.

Is it fair to keep entry logs on the door my tenant uses?

Be careful with it. A shared door's log tells you every time your tenant came and went, and many locks keep that history by default. For a shared vestibule door in an owner-occupied house, a short log helps if there is ever a question about a stranger getting in, but there is a difference between having a record and reading it every day.

Our recommendation is to keep logging on the shared door to the shortest retention the app allows, turn off per-person arrival notifications for the tenant's code, and tell the tenant plainly in writing what is recorded. On the tenant's own unit door, the owner shouldn't receive logs at all.

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Act is written for multiple dwellings rather than two-family houses, but its principles — collect only what you need, delete it on a schedule, don't use it against the tenant — are a sensible standard for any landlord, and they keep a good tenancy good.

Guests, buying and renovating

Can I put smart locks on bedroom doors if I host short-term guests in my house?

Not key locks that let guests lock their room behind them. The city's Office of Special Enforcement says in its guidance for hosts that internal doors “cannot have key locks that allow guests to leave and lock their room behind them,” because those locks create barriers to escape in an emergency, and that every guest must have free and unobstructed access to every room and exit. Under Local Law 18, adopted January 9, 2022, a host must register with the office, must be present during stays of under 30 days, and may have up to two paying guests at a time.

So a smart lock on the front or side door, with a time-limited code for each registered guest, is fine and useful. Smart locks or keyed locks on internal bedroom doors for guests are not. Privacy latches that open freely from both sides in an emergency are a different product, and you should check the current rules with the Office of Special Enforcement before relying on anything on an interior door.

A smart lock is not a way around the registration law. If the setup you want is renting the whole house without being there, that is a legal question about short-term rentals, not a hardware one, and we won't configure locks for an arrangement the rules don't allow.

We just bought a house on the South Shore — how do we make sure the previous owners can't still open the doors?

Assume every lock is still enrolled to someone else until proven otherwise. Smart locks keep codes, phone credentials and an admin account; garage-door openers keep remotes; and mechanical cylinders open to whatever keys were cut over the years. Start by factory-resetting every smart lock and enrolling it to your own account, which removes old codes and phones in one step.

Then deal with the keys. Rekey or replace every keyed cylinder, including the override on each smart lock, the door from the garage, and any basement or shed door. Reprogram the garage-door opener and any keypad on the garage, and ask whether an alarm company account or a video doorbell is still registered to the seller.

This is one of the jobs we do most often as a single visit: walk every door, reset what can be reset, rekey what can't, and leave you a written list of every lock, every code and who holds it. It is also the right moment to decide whether the house should move to a single keyless system across all its doors.

We're replacing the front door itself — should the new door and the smart lock be chosen together?

Yes, and before the door is ordered. Pre-hung doors come with their bores and backset already cut, and fibreglass and steel doors in particular are hard to re-drill cleanly afterwards. If the door arrives prepared for a lock format the smart lock you want doesn't fit, the choice is a compromise lock or a damaged new door.

Tell your door supplier the lock you plan to use, or ask us to check its door-prep requirements first: thickness, cross-bore and edge-bore sizes, backset, and whether a handleset and deadbolt are drilled at the spacing the lock needs. Sidelights and glass inserts change where a thumb-turn can safely sit, so settle those at the same time.

If a doorbell camera or a powered device is part of the plan, the new frame is also the moment to run a low-voltage cable to it. Once the door is hung and trimmed, we fit the lock and adjust the strike to the new frame.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Staten Island

“Expect to pay about $300 to have a smart lock installed”

That figure comes from national cost guides. Fixr's page, updated in January 2025, gives a national range of $250 to $400 with an average of $300, for a Wi-Fi lock and one accessory, and other guides publish similar per-lock numbers. A Staten Island house rarely needs one lock on one ready door. The realistic job is three or four doors, at least one of them a self-closing garage-entry door that needs its closer and strike adjusted, a side door that swings out, and codes for five or six people. The number that matters is the price for the whole house, quoted after someone has looked at every door.

That is how we price residential locks: flat, after a free walkthrough, with every door on one quote. Where AESS does publish figures — keypad or fob readers with electric strikes, strike work, setup-only visits — they are in the pricing table below with the Staten Island adjustment already applied.

“It's a 30-minute job with a screwdriver”

For a retrofit on a perfect, modern door, close to true. Bob Vila's testers, for example, reported needing only a Phillips screwdriver and about 30 minutes for a DIY install. That is the best case, and it is real.

The worst case is also real on this island: a garage-entry door that doesn't latch on its own, a North Shore door thicker than the lock is listed for, a side door with an old bore that has been drilled out three times, a storm door that leaves no room for the escutcheon. Each of those turns thirty minutes into an afternoon of door work before the lock goes on.

Our time is mostly spent on that door work and on making sure every lock is online and every person's code works. The screwdriver part is the short part.

“Wi-Fi reaches every door in a normal house”

In a compact one-storey house, often. In a Staten Island two-family with the router upstairs, a brick or block garage under the house and a basement unit below grade, often not. Garages are full of metal — the door, the opener, the shelving, the car — and basements sit behind foundation walls.

Manufacturers are clear about this in their own support pages. August asks for its Wi-Fi bridge to be within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and checks for full Wi-Fi bars at the bridge's location; Yale's Matter-over-Thread locks need a Thread border router set up before they will join. Neither instruction assumes the router will reach the garage on its own.

So we test the signal at every door with a phone before recommending a connection method, and if a door can't be reached, we say so and solve it — a bridge, a border router closer to the door, or a properly placed mesh point — instead of leaving a lock that only works when you are standing next to it.

“Any exterior finish is fine for a front-door lock”

Not near salt water. dormakaba's lock-finish guide treats any exterior lock on a property within 3 miles of a saltwater body as an ocean-front application, recommends specific heavy or ocean-front finishes there, says gaskets must be fitted at installation or the warranty is invalidated, and states that using a lighter finish in an ocean environment voids the warranty.

That guide is for a commercial hotel-lock range, and residential brands write their own terms. But a large share of Staten Island homes sit within a few miles of the Lower Bay, Raritan Bay, the Arthur Kill or the Kill Van Kull, so the question is worth asking before you buy.

We read the warranty and finish notes for the lock you are considering, fit every gasket and seal the maker supplies, and tell you plainly if a door is exposed enough that a different model or finish would last longer.

“A smart lock makes a house much harder to break into”

A smart lock is a good lock that is never left unlocked by accident and that tells you who opened it. That is valuable. It is not a wall. In June 2022, CBS New York reported a Todt Hill break-in where the suspects threw a large stone through the glass of a back door and rushed in while the family slept, part of a pattern police linked to Tottenville, Prince's Bay and Lighthouse Hill.

No lock, smart or otherwise, stops glass from breaking. What helps on doors like that is lighting, cameras, an alarm with glass-break or contact sensors, and sometimes laminated glass — and, on the lock side, a strong strike with long screws into the framing.

We install smart locks for convenience, control and accountability, and we fit them with reinforced strikes. If the walkthrough shows a door whose weak point is the glass, we will say so rather than sell you the lock as the fix.

“A double-cylinder deadbolt is safer next to glass”

Older security advice often says to use a key on both sides of a deadbolt near glass, so a burglar can't reach in and turn the thumb-turn. Some smart locks are even sold with a keyed or coded inside.

On a door people need to escape through, it's the wrong trade. The 2022 New York City Building Code requires egress doors to open from the inside without a key or special knowledge or effort, and allows a deadbolt on a dwelling-unit door only if it opens from the inside without a key or tool. A key that has to be found in a smoky hallway is the problem the rule exists to prevent.

The better answers are to move the lock away from the glass where the door allows, use glass that is harder to break, add sensors, or choose a lock whose inside turn can't be reached through a broken pane — and keep exit simple.

“Put locks on every room and run it as a rental”

Smart-lock marketing aimed at hosts often shows a keypad on every bedroom door. In New York City that runs straight into the short-term rental rules.

The Office of Special Enforcement's guidance for hosts says internal doors cannot have key locks that let guests lock their room behind them, and that guests must have free and unobstructed access to every room and exit. Local Law 18 requires hosts to register, to be present for stays under 30 days, and to keep to two paying guests.

A smart lock on the entrance, with a time-limited code per registered guest, fits the rules. A keypad on each bedroom door for guests doesn't, and we won't fit one for that purpose.

Connectivity

Getting Every Door Online: Garages, Basements and Detached Buildings

The most common complaint we hear about smart locks on Staten Island is not about the lock. It's that the lock on the garage door or the basement door “keeps going offline.” Almost always, the router is on the opposite side of the house, a floor away, behind a block wall or a steel garage door, and the lock is sitting at the edge of what its radio can reach.

How we pick the connection for each door

  • Bluetooth only: fine for a door you only ever open while standing at it, by keypad or phone. No remote control, no away-from-home alerts. Often the right answer for a shed.
  • Bluetooth with a Wi-Fi bridge: the bridge plugs in near the door and relays to your router. August's guidance is to place it within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and check for full Wi-Fi bars where it sits. Good for a garage-entry door with an outlet nearby.
  • Wi-Fi built into the lock: no bridge, but the lock itself needs a strong 2.4 GHz signal at the door, and Wi-Fi locks tend to use batteries faster. Best on front and side doors near the router.
  • Matter over Thread: the lock joins a low-power mesh through a Thread border router, such as the smart speakers and hubs manufacturers list. Mains-powered Thread devices around the house can strengthen the mesh toward a far door.
  • Z-Wave: where the house already has an alarm panel or hub that speaks Z-Wave, the lock can join that network instead, and locking can be tied to arming.

The detached garage problem

A detached garage at the end of a South Shore driveway, or a workshop at the back of a Todt Hill lot, can sit well beyond the reach of a router inside the house. Three approaches work: a lock that simply runs offline by keypad, with no remote features; an outdoor-rated access point or mesh node that brings real Wi-Fi to the garage, which also helps any camera there; or, where there is already a cable to the garage, a wired access point inside it. We test before recommending, and we don't sell a mesh system to a house that doesn't need one.

Whatever the method, we finish by testing remote lock and unlock from mobile data, not the home Wi-Fi, so you know it works when you're actually away.

Before You Buy

A Homeowner's Checklist Before Ordering Smart Locks

Plenty of Staten Island homeowners buy the locks first and call us second. That works fine if a few things are checked before the boxes are opened, because most locks can't be returned once they've been fitted and enrolled.

  • Count the doors you actually use. Walk the house for a week and notice which doors open every day. Those are the doors worth making smart; a front door that opens twice a month may be fine with a matching keyed deadbolt.
  • Measure each door. Thickness at the lock, the backset (edge of door to centre of the bore), and the bore size. Write down which way each door swings and whether a storm door sits in front of it.
  • Look at the latch on its own. Close each door gently without turning anything. If it doesn't latch every time, it needs adjusting before any smart deadbolt will behave.
  • Check the signal at each door. Stand at the garage-entry door and the basement door with your phone on Wi-Fi only. If a video call drops there, the lock will struggle too.
  • Choose one family of locks. Locks from the same maker usually share one app, one code list and one bridge or hub. Mixing brands means mixing apps.
  • Decide who gets codes. List everyone who comes and goes, and whether their access should be permanent, scheduled or one-off. That list shapes which lock features you actually need.
  • Read the finish and warranty notes. For a door facing the water or standing in the rain, look for the maker's guidance on exterior and coastal use, and keep the gaskets and seals that come in the box.
  • Keep a key. Pick models with a keyed override, and test that key twice a year. It's the only way in when batteries, motors or apps fail together.

If you're unsure, send photos of each door, inside, outside and edge-on, before ordering. We'll tell you which formats fit and which doors need work first, and that advice costs nothing.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Staten Island House Doors

How our installers read a house before recommending locks — general working practice, not a story about any particular customer.

We don't start at the front door. We start at the door with the most wear on its threshold, because that's the one the family really uses, and on most Staten Island houses it's the side door or the door from the garage. We open and close it ten times without touching the handle on the way shut. If it doesn't latch on its own every time, no smart lock will work well on it, so the closer and strike come first.

Then we stand at each door with a phone and look at the Wi-Fi bars, because the garage door and the basement door are where locks go offline. We look at which way each door faces, whether rain hits it directly, and how far the house is from the water. We look for glass within an arm's reach of the thumb-turn, for storm doors that leave no room, for outswing doors with exposed hinge pins.

And we ask who lives here and who visits. The best smart lock plan for a house is usually less about which brand and more about who gets which code on which door — and making sure that, on every one of those doors, getting out takes one turn of the hand and nothing else.

FAQ

Staten Island Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Why are Staten Island prices 10 percent above the Brooklyn base?
+

AESS prices each area from a Brooklyn base using a fixed multiplier, and Staten Island's is 1.10. It applies to our published figures, such as the single-door keypad or fob reader with electric strike at $1,650 to $2,750 here, and it is already built into any flat quote we give you after a walkthrough.

Will drilling for a new lock damage a fibreglass or steel door?
+

Not if the door is prepared properly. Fibreglass and steel doors need sharp hole saws, slow speeds and support behind the cut so the skin doesn't chip or dent, and some steel doors have reinforcement plates that change where a bolt can go. Where a door is already drilled for a standard deadbolt we use the existing holes; we only cut new ones after confirming the lock's door-prep figures.

Will the lock still work properly if the door has dropped on its hinges?
+

Not for long. A sagging door makes the bolt rub the strike, which drains batteries and can stall the motor. We tighten or replace hinge screws, add longer screws into the framing where needed, and adjust the strike so the bolt throws freely before the lock goes on.

Can the new smart lock match the brushed nickel or bronze hardware already on the house?
+

Most major brands offer their smart deadbolts in several finishes, and some sell matching handlesets. We note your existing finish at the walkthrough and suggest models that come in it. On exposed or waterfront doors we may recommend a more durable finish over an exact match, and we'll explain why.

Do you fit keypads on side-yard gates as well as house doors?
+

Yes. A gate usually needs a weather-rated keypad or reader with an electric strike or magnetic lock, a power supply with battery backup, and a free-exit button or handle on the inside. That is priced from our published single-door figure, $1,650 to $2,750 on Staten Island, rather than as a residential lock.

Do smart locks need a monthly subscription?
+

Most don't. Codes, auto-lock, history and remote control through the maker's app are generally included. Subscriptions usually come from linking the lock to a monitored alarm or a camera service, where the alarm or camera company charges for its own features. We tell you before installation if anything you want depends on a paid plan.

Can our house and our parents' house nearby use the same codes?
+

Yes, if both houses use locks from the same family and you want them to. Each lock is enrolled separately, so you can give one person the same code at both houses while keeping other codes house-specific. Many apps let one administrator manage two homes from one account, which suits families who look after a relative's house down the street.

How long does a whole-house smart lock installation take?
+

It depends far more on the doors than the locks. A door that already latches well and takes the lock as drilled is quick; a garage-entry door that needs its closer reset, or a door with an old bore to fill, takes longer. After the walkthrough we give you a time estimate with the written quote, so you know whether to plan for a morning or a full day.
