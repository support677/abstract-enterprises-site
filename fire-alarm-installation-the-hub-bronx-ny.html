The Neighborhood

Why The Hub's Old Department Stores, Storefront Rows and New Towers Need Careful Fire Alarm Work

The Hub is the shopping district at the bow-tie crossing where East 149th Street, Third Avenue, Willis Avenue and Melrose Avenue meet in the South Bronx. Wikipedia places it mostly in Melrose, along the northern edge of Mott Haven, inside Bronx Community Board 1, and calls it the borough's oldest major shopping area, sometimes nicknamed "the Broadway of the Bronx." The district now runs north along Third Avenue toward East 156th Street, where the Hub Retail and Office Center opened in 2007.

The age of the place is the first thing a fire alarm designer notices. The 3 Av–149 St station on the 2 and 5 lines opened on July 10, 1905, and the old Third Avenue elevated line ran overhead from 1887 to 1973. Department stores, boutiques and theaters followed the trains. The Bronx Opera House at 436 East 149th Street opened on August 30, 1913, to a design by George M. Keister, with seats for 1,892 people, and served as a vaudeville house, a movie theater, a Latin dance club and two churches before it reopened as a hotel in August 2013. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it an individual landmark on June 13, 2023. Every one of those uses rearranged walls and exits, and each change is a question for the fire alarm drawings.

The retail buildings tell the same story. Welcome2TheBronx reported that the Alexander's store on Third Avenue was founded in 1928 at that location, passed to Conway after Alexander's bankruptcy in 1992, and saw its upper floors closed for years before a 2016 renovation that took the top fourth floor off and added new elevators and escalators. The Real Deal identified the building as 2948 Third Avenue and reported in 2017 that Burlington would take 55,000 square feet and Marshalls 25,000 square feet. When a multi-story store is cut down, split and re-let, the fire alarm is redrawn along with it.

Street life is intense. The city's Commercial District Needs Assessment for The Hub-Melrose, run by the Department of Small Business Services with the Third Avenue BID and published in 2025, counted 613 storefronts on East 149th Street, Third Avenue, Morris, Courtlandt, Melrose and Brook avenues, with 17.5 percent vacant and 40 percent rated in poor condition. It measured 5,744 weekday afternoon pedestrians on one block of Third Avenue, more than 50,000 commuters passing Roberto Clemente Plaza each day and 12,027 average weekday riders at the 3 Av–149 St station in 2024. Of the 124 merchants surveyed, 86 percent rent their space. That means crowded sales floors, frequent re-lettings, and landlords and tenants who must agree on who owns which device.

Fires here are not abstract. News 12 Bronx reported in January 2008 that a three-alarm fire that began in the basement of a wireless store near Third Avenue and East 149th Street damaged a dozen businesses. In June 2024, ABC7 and FOX 5 reported a four-alarm fire on Third Avenue near East 157th Street, just outside the BID, that burned through a Dunkin' and attached shops including a Chinese restaurant, a smoke shop, a furniture store and a 99-cent store; about 140 firefighters responded, three had minor injuries, and residents on the other side of the building were told to keep their windows shut. Neither report says what any building's fire alarm did, and we draw no conclusion about it. They show the pattern a designer plans for: cellar stockrooms, shared walls, and apartments next to or above the stores.

This page covers building-wide systems: a control panel that supervises its own wiring, detectors and manual stations laid out under NFPA 72 and the city code, and a link to an approved monitoring center. The single-station smoke and carbon monoxide alarms inside apartments are a separate set of rules for owners and occupants, and we leave them out.

The Hub at a glance

  • Where: East 149th Street, Third Avenue, Willis Avenue and Melrose Avenue converge in a bow-tie; mostly in Melrose, bordering Mott Haven; Bronx Community Board 1 (Wikipedia).
  • Who maintains it: the HUB Third Avenue Business Improvement District, which its own site says was established in 1988 and describes as the Bronx's first BID (Third Avenue BID; NYC SBS).
  • Storefronts: 613 counted, 17.5 percent vacant, 40 percent in poor condition, 86 percent of surveyed merchants renting (NYC SBS, 2025).
  • Transit: the 3 Av–149 St station on the 2 and 5 lines, opened 1905, with 3,698,741 riders in 2024 (Wikipedia); buses including the Bx4, Bx15, Bx41 and M125 (Wikipedia).
  • Big new buildings: La Central at 556 and 600 Bergen Avenue, five buildings with more than 1,000 affordable apartments planned, a YMCA, a BronxNet studio and 45,000 square feet of retail (NYC HPD); The Jacqueline, 15 stories and 69 apartments at 2980 Third Avenue (Welcome2TheBronx).
  • Nearby institution: NYC Health + Hospitals/Lincoln at 234 East 149th Street, a Level I adult trauma center with more than 144,000 emergency visits a year (Wikipedia).

Store Panel Beeping on Third Avenue? What to Tell Us When You Call

A panel in the back office that will not stop chirping before opening, a trouble lamp that came on after a delivery truck knocked the gate, or horns sounding in a full store on a Saturday: call (646) 490-0629 and the visit is booked within 2 hours. Have ready the address, where the panel is, the brand on its door, the exact text on its display, and whether anyone has called the monitoring company.

Until we arrive, photograph the display, let the monitoring company know a technician is on the way, and press reset once at most. Do not unplug the panel, pull its batteries, tape over a detector or take a device off the ceiling to quiet it. In a store with a cellar stockroom, a detector removed downstairs is the one that would have found the fire nobody upstairs could see.

Hub buildings fail in their own ways. Cellar stockrooms under Third Avenue storefronts collect dust from cardboard and moisture after rain, which shows up as detector trouble and fire alarm ground fault messages. Roll-down security gates and awnings get replaced, and wiring to an exterior strobe or a pull station by the door gets cut in the process. Re-let stores often carry devices from two or three earlier tenants on one panel, so a fire alarm open circuit repair begins with sorting out which wires still serve anything. When the panel reads communication failure, the problem people describe as a fire alarm system not communicating, the usual cause is a phone line the last tenant cancelled. An actual fire alarm panel failure repair is the exception.

Searches like emergency fire alarm repair, urgent fire alarm repair, same day fire alarm repair and 24 hour fire alarm repair come from store managers who cannot open with a panel in trouble. For commercial fire alarm emergency repair we confirm a booking within 2 hours and give a straight arrival estimate. Emergency fire alarm service, after hours fire alarm service and fire alarm system emergency service are scheduled around store hours and the condition on the panel, and a fire alarm system down repair, where the building has no working protection at all, may also require the owner to follow the FDNY's fire watch rules until it is restored.

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for The Hub's Buildings, From a 1913 Theater to 2020s Towers

What is a commercial fire alarm system? A network built around one control panel. Detectors, manual pull stations and sprinkler switches tell the panel something has happened; horns, strobes or speakers tell the people inside; batteries keep it running through a power cut; and a communicator reports every event to a monitoring center that calls the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work when something trips? The panel identifies the device, runs whatever the approved design says should follow, such as recalling elevators, stopping air handlers or releasing held-open doors, and sends the signal off site. It also reports on its own health, so a broken wire or a dead battery appears as a trouble condition instead of silence.

When does a building need a fire alarm system? It depends on the occupancy group on the Certificate of Occupancy, and The Hub has most of them on a few blocks. For stores, the 2008 edition of the NYC Building Code, section 907.2.7, required a manual and automatic system in a Group M occupancy when any one fire area was larger than 12,000 square feet, when a fire area was more than three stories above grade, when all Group M fire areas together, mezzanines included, exceeded 24,000 square feet, or when a below-grade fire area was larger than 1,500 square feet. That last line matters on Third Avenue, where many shops sell upstairs and store goods in a cellar. For apartments, section 907.2.9 requires a system once a building passes 16 units or puts a unit three or more stories above the lowest level of exit discharge, and detectors in those buildings report to a central station rather than sounding horns everywhere. The editions change, and existing buildings are judged by their records and the scope of work, so the design professional confirms which rule governs before anything is drawn.

Former department stores re-let to chains

The building at 2948 Third Avenue is the clearest example. It opened as Alexander's in 1928, became a Conway with a failed second-floor mini mall, sat partly closed, lost its fourth floor in 2016, and was re-let with Burlington and Marshalls taking 80,000 square feet between them, according to Welcome2TheBronx and The Real Deal. A building like this usually has two layers of fire alarm: the landlord's base building system covering stairs, elevators, the sprinkler riser and common areas, and each tenant's devices on its own floor. Getting both layers onto one approved set, with the FDNY's letter of approval for the base building attached when tenant devices connect to it, is most of the work.

Low-rise storefront buildings with apartments above

Much of East 149th Street, Willis Avenue and Melrose Avenue is lined with older buildings that have a shop or two at grade, a cellar below and a few floors of apartments upstairs. These are mixed-use buildings in the code's eyes. The store side follows the mercantile rules, the residential side follows its own, and the FDNY's tips ask that systems for Group R-2 occupancies be filed separately from other occupancy groups. The design question is how the two systems are separated, where each panel sits, and how a fire in the shop is reported to the people living above it.

Theaters, the Opera House Hotel and assembly rooms

The Hub grew up around theaters. Wikipedia lists the Bronx Opera House and the former Jackson Theatre among the district's 1930s movie palaces and vaudeville houses. The Opera House building is now a hotel, which brings in hotel fire alarm installation rules for guest rooms and corridors, inside a landmark whose historic features constrain where cable and devices can go. Other former theater and hall spaces along the corridor have served as churches, clubs and stores. Whenever one of those spaces changes use, its occupant load and its fire alarm requirements change with it.

Retail and clinic centers

Triangle Plaza Hub at 459 East 149th Street is a two-story, 88,000-square-foot commercial and community building by Triangle Equities, designed by S9 Architecture, whose tenants have included a Fine Fare supermarket, Metropolitan College of New York, Brightpoint Health, a DaVita dialysis center, Vistasite Eye Care and Boston Market, according to New York YIMBY and the New York Real Estate Journal. That is a supermarket, a college, medical tenants and restaurants under one roof, each a different occupancy. Medical tenants in particular bring requirements a store does not have, and the design professional works through them tenant by tenant.

Office and retail with a parking garage

The Hub Retail and Office Center at Third Avenue and East 156th Street opened in 2007; its architect describes 175,000 square feet of new office and retail space and a 165,000-square-foot garage renovation. Offices get smoke detection, the garage gets heat detection that exhaust will not fool.

New affordable and market-rate towers

NYC HPD describes La Central, at 556 and 600 Bergen Avenue within the Hub, as a five-building development that will bring more than 1,000 affordable apartments, with 45,000 square feet of retail, 80,000 square feet of community facility space, a YMCA, a BronxNet television studio and a GrowNYC rooftop farm; Buildings A and B, with nearly 500 units, were dedicated on June 16, 2021, and Building D, with 160 supportive and low-income units, was finished in 2019. The Jacqueline at 2980 Third Avenue and East 153rd Street is a 15-story, 69-unit building that Welcome2TheBronx called the first market-rate development in the neighborhood, with a doorman, a gym on the second floor and a 2,900-square-foot roof terrace. Multifamily building fire alarm installation in towers like these, and condominium building fire alarm installation if a future project sells its units, starts with an addressable system designed to current code. The owner's job is to keep them inspected, keep the central station account current, and file every later change.

Shelters, programs and community space

Residents on r/bronx describe the blocks around Third Avenue and 149th Street as fast food, retail and homeless shelters. Shelters and supportive housing are residential occupancies with their own rules, and a commercial floor converted to a care program may bring nursing home fire alarm installation or assisted living fire alarm installation rules. Lincoln Hospital runs its own contractors, so hospital fire alarm installation there is not work we claim.

Stores and Corridors

Business Fire Alarm Installation on Third Avenue, East 149th Street, Willis, Melrose, Bergen and Westchester Avenues

Business fire alarm installation is the core of fire alarm work at The Hub. Each corridor has its own kind of building, and the business fire detection systems that suit one block may be wrong for the next.

Third Avenue, from 149th Street north toward 156th Street

The big stores live here. Besides the former Alexander's building, Third Avenue holds furniture stores, discount chains and the Hub Retail and Office Center at its north end. Commercial fire detection systems in these buildings deal with high open sales floors, stock rooms full of cartons, freight elevators and sprinklered cellars. On the busiest block the SBS counted 5,744 pedestrians on a weekday afternoon, so devices near entrances take bumps, and strobes need to be seen from every aisle of a crowded floor.

East 149th Street between Morris Avenue and Bergen Avenue

This is the street most commuters walk, and the one where street vendors concentrate, by the SBS count. It mixes older storefront buildings, Triangle Plaza Hub, the Opera House Hotel and the approach to Lincoln Hospital. Retail store fire alarm installation on this stretch often means a small shop with a narrow cellar, a single exit to a sidewalk full of people, and apartments upstairs. Pull stations by the door and notification that reaches the back of a deep, narrow store matter more than device count.

Willis Avenue and Melrose Avenue

These two avenues meet Third Avenue and 149th Street at the bow-tie. Willis Avenue runs south into Mott Haven with storefront buildings of mixed age; Melrose Avenue runs north past newer residential buildings from the Melrose Commons plan, the community-led renewal that Nos Quedamos, founded by Yolanda Garcia, fought for and that Welcome2TheBronx describes as a 30-block plan. Here the typical fire alarm job is a ground-floor shop or community space inside a newer residential building, which means coordinating with the building's existing system rather than adding a separate one.

Bergen Avenue and Westchester Avenue

Bergen Avenue is where La Central sits, with ground-floor retail tenants such as the cafes and grocery that HPD announced in 2020. Westchester Avenue starts at the Hub and carries the Bx4 east. Tenant improvement fire alarm installation for a new cafe, gym or grocery in a new building is careful work: the base building system is new and approved, and every added device has to match its design, be filed as an amendment and be tested without disturbing apartments upstairs.

Kitchens on every block

The SBS study found the district dominated by fast-food restaurants. Commercial kitchen fire detection installation in these spaces means heat detectors rather than smoke detectors near fryers and griddles, a monitor module that reports when the hood suppression system discharges, and a decision by the designer about whether that signal is an alarm or a supervisory condition. Fire alarm kitchen hood suppression monitoring is one of the most common tie-ins we wire on this corridor.

Stores that rent, and who owns the fire alarm

With 86 percent of surveyed merchants renting, the lease decides a great deal. Usually the landlord owns the base building system and the tenant pays for devices that serve its own space, but leases vary. Before we draw anything we ask both sides for the lease clause on fire protection, the last approved fire alarm drawings and the monitoring contract, so the proposal goes to the party who will sign it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: At The Hub, Start in the Cellar and Read the Lease

On a Third Avenue storefront survey, we go downstairs before we look at the sales floor. Cellars under Hub storefronts are where stock, compressors, electric meters and old wiring live, and the 2008 fire that News 12 reported near Third Avenue and East 149th Street began in a basement. The cellar tells us whether there is detection down there at all, how cable gets from the panel to the sales floor, and whether the space has been divided among tenants since the last drawings were approved.

The second stop is paper. A building that has been a department store, a mini mall and two chain stores in less than a century usually has a Certificate of Occupancy that lags behind its use, and the FDNY will not accept a filing without a Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A that matches the drawings, or a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings when neither exists. Finding that gap during the survey saves weeks later.

The third is the neighbors. Storefront rows share walls, cellars and sometimes a sprinkler service, and the June 2024 fire near East 157th Street spread through attached businesses. A fire alarm system covers one building, but we note where it ends, what the next building's system looks like from the shared wall, and who to call next door, because that information helps responders.

Last, the street. Stores open into crowded sidewalks, so we test before opening and keep exits clear. The SBS assessment notes early closures and little nighttime activity, which makes after-hours testing easier to arrange here.

Your Questions Answered

The Hub Fire Alarm Questions From Landlords, Store Operators, Developers and Managing Agents

Old department-store buildings on Third Avenue

The old Alexander's building at 2948 Third Avenue lost its top floor in 2016 and was split between two chains. When a store building is cut down and re-let like that, does its fire alarm get redesigned or just patched?

In practice it gets redesigned on paper even if some equipment stays. Removing a floor changes the stairs, the elevators, the riser and the occupant counts, and splitting the space changes which tenant owns which device. A design professional redraws the base building system and each tenant's portion, existing devices that test well can be reused where the new design allows, and the whole package goes to the FDNY for review and a new acceptance test.

Our building's second floor has been closed to shoppers for years. What has to happen with the fire alarm before we reopen it as sales space?

Treat the floor as new work. Closed floors often have devices that were disconnected, painted over or left on a panel that has since been replaced. The engineer surveys the floor, designs coverage for the new layout and occupant load, and files it; the installer restores pathways, replaces anything that fails, and the floor is tested before customers return. If the reopening also adds escalators or changes stairs, the elevator and door interfaces are rechecked too.

The city's 2025 study lists early closings and little nighttime activity among The Hub's problems. If a store starts staying open late, does anything about its fire alarm change?

The code requirements do not change with the hours, but the practical side does. The monitoring center's call list should name someone reachable at night, the staff working late need to know what the panel's sounds and lights mean and where the exits are, and inspections or repairs that used to happen after closing have to be rescheduled. If longer hours come with a new use, such as live entertainment, that is a change of occupancy and a design question.

Theaters, the hotel and event space

The Bronx Opera House became a hotel in 2013 and an individual landmark in 2023. How does landmark status affect where fire alarm devices can go?

It adds a reviewer. Work that changes protected features of a designated landmark needs Landmarks Preservation Commission review, so the designer looks for routes and device locations that avoid decorative plaster, historic facades and visible ornament, using existing chases, ceilings and closets where possible. Code requirements do not relax because a building is historic; the craft is in meeting them quietly. Early coordination between the fire alarm designer and the preservation architect keeps both reviews moving.

We want to book live music in a former hall above our store near 149th Street. Does turning it into an event room change the fire alarm?

Almost certainly. An event room is an assembly occupancy, and the city's fire alarm rules for assembly spaces depend on occupant load, which is calculated from the floor area and how the room is used. A room that holds a crowd may need manual stations, notification sized for loud music, and in larger rooms a voice system. The change of use also has to be reflected on the Certificate of Occupancy, so the architect, the Department of Buildings filing and the FDNY fire alarm filing move together.

City Limits wrote about furniture stores near 153rd Street on Third Avenue. If we stack sofas and mattresses nearly to the ceiling, does tall stock change where smoke detectors can go?

It can. Smoke has to reach a detector, so stock piled close to the ceiling or packed between beams can delay detection, and sprinkler rules separately require clear space below sprinkler heads. The designer lays out detectors for the room as it will actually be used, and on a very high open floor may choose beam detection instead of spot detectors. Mark a maximum stacking height on the wall and keep to it, because detection designed for an open ceiling does not account for a wall of boxes.

New towers and centers

La Central puts a YMCA, a TV studio, shops and hundreds of apartments in one development on Bergen Avenue. How many separate fire alarm systems does a project like that end up with?

More than one, as a rule. Separate buildings normally get separate systems, and within a building the FDNY asks that apartment systems be filed apart from other occupancy groups, so the residential floors, the gym and studio, and the stores may each appear on their own filing. What matters for the owner is a single list of every panel, its location, its monitoring account and its inspection dates, because a five-building campus can otherwise lose track of which system watches which room.

Triangle Plaza Hub opened with a Fine Fare supermarket as its anchor. What does a supermarket need from a fire alarm that a clothing store does not?

Mostly attention to the back of the house. Refrigeration machine rooms, compressors and loading docks are hot, dusty or exposed to exhaust, so heat detection usually replaces smoke detection there, and devices inside walk-in coolers and freezers must be listed for those temperatures. Large rooftop units for refrigeration and air handling often have duct detectors that shut fans down. On the sales floor, long aisles and tall shelving shape where strobes go so they can be seen from every aisle.

Cellars, vacancies and shared walls

The 2008 fire near Third Avenue and 149th Street began in the basement of a wireless store. When a row of storefronts each has its own cellar, how do firefighters know which cellar an alarm came from?

From labels and annunciation that match the building, not from guesswork. An addressable system can name the exact cellar and device, and the panel or a remote annunciator at the entrance shows it; the monitoring center relays the same description to the FDNY. The drawings and the panel program should use the store names or addresses posted outside, and they need updating whenever a tenant changes, otherwise a signal labeled for a long-gone shop sends responders to the wrong door.

The city's 2025 study found about one in six Hub storefronts vacant. When a new tenant takes an empty store, what happens to the old fire alarm devices left on the walls?

They are tested, reused, removed or replaced according to the new tenant's design, never just left in place. A detector or pull station that no longer reports to a working panel gives a false sense of protection. The new tenant's engineer surveys what remains, the drawings show each device's fate, and anything removed is taken off the panel's program and the record drawings so the next inspection does not go looking for it.

In the June 2024 fire near East 157th Street, residents on the other side of the building were told to keep their windows shut. In a building with a store below and apartments above, how does a fire in the store reach the people upstairs?

Through whatever the approved design says, which is why mixed-use drawings deserve a close read. The store portion usually has its own detection and notification; the apartment portion follows the residential rules, where hallway detectors report to a monitoring center rather than sounding throughout. The designer decides whether an alarm in the store also sounds or signals in the residential part, and the monitoring center's call to the FDNY is what brings help either way.

The HUB Third Avenue BID runs sanitation, public safety and retail programs on the corridor. Does a business improvement district have any role in a building's fire alarm?

No direct role. The BID's own site describes services that add to the city's, such as sanitation, public safety, small business help and streetscape work, and the fire alarm stays the building owner's legal responsibility under the FDNY. The BID can still help: it knows the corridor's landlords and managers, and we let its staff know when lifts or materials will be staged on a busy sidewalk.

Equipment

Fire Alarm System Types That Fit The Hub's Stores, Mixed-Use Buildings and Towers

FAQ

The Hub Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

What is a fire area, and why does it matter when deciding whether a store needs a fire alarm?
+

A fire area is the floor space enclosed by fire-rated walls and floors, plus any space open to it. The store rules in the city's Building Code count fire areas, not storefronts, so two shops separated by a rated wall are measured separately, while two shops opened into one space are measured together. Combining storefronts can push a store over the size line that requires a system.

Does a store's mezzanine count toward the floor area that triggers a fire alarm?
+

Yes, under the 2008 text of section 907.2.7, which counted mezzanines when adding up all Group M fire areas on all floors. A mezzanine added for stock or offices can push a store over the combined-area line even when the main floor alone is below it. Show any mezzanine on the drawings from the start.

What does partial-coverage detection mean in a store without sprinklers?
+

It means automatic smoke or heat detectors in selected areas rather than everywhere. The 2008 text of section 907.2.7 called for a manual fire alarm with partial-coverage automatic smoke or heat detection, installed to NFPA 72, in a required store that has no automatic sprinkler system. The design professional chooses which rooms get coverage.

Do roll-down security gates affect where a store's pull stations and strobes can go?
+

They can. A pull station has to be reachable along the path people use to leave, and a strobe has to be seen, so neither should end up behind a gate, a gate motor or its housing. When a gate or awning is replaced, check that the installer did not cut or bury fire alarm wiring, a common cause of trouble signals at storefronts.

What is a data gathering panel on a New York City fire alarm drawing?
+

It is a remote panel that collects signals from devices in one part of a building and passes them to the main fire alarm control panel. The FDNY's tips list data gathering panels, along with the control panel, power supplies and the central office connection, among the items whose locations must appear on the floor plans. Large stores and towers often have several.

Can a store's fire alarm panel be in the cellar if customers enter on the street level?
+

Yes, if the approved design puts it there, but responders need to read the system at the door they use. That is why a cellar panel is often paired with a remote annunciator near the front entrance. The FDNY's tips ask that every panel and its location appear on the floor plans.

Should fire alarm devices be covered during a store renovation to keep out dust?
+

Only as allowed and only for as short a time as possible. Dust covers keep detectors from fouling during demolition and sanding, but a covered detector cannot sense fire, so the building owner and monitoring company need to know which devices are covered and when they are uncovered. Every cover comes off and every affected detector is checked before the space reopens.

Why does the FDNY check that the TM-1 job description matches the drawings?
+

Because the application and the drawings have to describe the same job. The FDNY's tips ask filers to make sure the job description on the TM-1 matches the scope of work shown on the drawings, and a mismatch is a common reason for an objection. If the scope grows during construction, the change goes in through a Post-Approval Amendment.

What is a walk test, and why do technicians use it before an inspection?
+

A walk test is a panel mode that lets one technician activate devices around the building and have each one logged, usually without sending signals to the monitoring center. It is a fast way to confirm every detector and pull station reports with the right label before the formal test. The monitoring center is told before and after, and the panel is returned to normal when the walk ends.

What happens to a store's fire alarm when a new suspended ceiling goes in?
+

The detectors and strobes usually have to move down to the new ceiling, and the space above it may need its own evaluation. A lower ceiling changes detector spacing, can block strobes from view, and can create a concealed space with cable and ductwork. Any relocation on a filed system goes through the design professional and is shown on revised drawings.
