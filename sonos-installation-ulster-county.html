The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Homes Need Sonos Installation

Ask what an Ulster County house needs from a Sonos system and the honest answer is usually: to fit in. This is the county of the Maverick concert hall, built in 1916 for a summer chamber festival that still describes itself as the oldest continuous one in America, of the Bearsville studios and theater that Albert Grossman built on the edge of Woodstock, and of Radio Woodstock on 100.1. Some of the people who call us here are musicians or recording engineers; many more are simply listeners with opinions. When they call about Sonos, the first question is rarely which speaker to buy. It is whether Sonos can feed the amplifier they already trust, drive the speakers they would never sell, and still let the kids stream to the kitchen.

The answer is usually yes, but only if somebody does the integration properly. A Sonos Port sends a streamed signal into an existing receiver or integrated amplifier through its line or coaxial outputs; a Sonos Amp powers passive speakers directly. Each has rules that matter in a listening room: impedance limits on the Amp, a fixed or variable output on the Port, a 48 kHz ceiling on music files, a delay on anything plugged into a line-in. We explain those rules before anything is bought, so nobody discovers them with the receiver already wired in.

Then there are the buildings. Ulster’s housing goes back centuries: the Stockade District in Kingston, where four pre-Revolutionary stone houses stand on the corners of Crown and John Streets, the stone houses of Hurley and New Paltz, the brick of the Rondout, the 19th-century residences of the Chestnut Street and Fair Street districts. Census figures compiled by Stacker put a quarter of the county’s homes at 1939 or earlier. Up in the Catskill towns the houses get younger but no easier: A-frames and cabins with ceilings that follow the roof, log walls, plank floors laid straight across exposed beams. Stone and old plaster are hard on Wi-Fi, and a ceiling that is nothing but the floor of the room above has no cavity for a speaker.

Finally, Ulster is a place people arrive at. The county Comptroller found that 36% of short-term-rental owners use mailing addresses outside Ulster, with New York City the most common. Creeks rise — Irene set flood records on the Esopus, the Stony Clove and the Rondout in 2011 — and storms take the power out. A Sonos system here has to be ready when the car pulls in on Friday night, with nobody having touched it for a fortnight. Everything below is built around those three facts: the music already in the house, the walls around it, and the weeks when the house sits empty.

  • 183,330 residents and 87,657 housing units (Census Bureau QuickFacts, July 1, 2025 estimates) — with 2.26 people per household, systems here are usually planned for couples and small families, not crowds.

  • 11.53 inches of rain at Slide Mountain during Irene, judged at least a 200-year event, with record crests on the Upper Esopus, the Stony Clove in Phoenicia and the Rondout in Rosendale (Lower Esopus Watershed Partnership).
  • 93,209 Central Hudson customers in Ulster, and more than 14,000 of them without power at the peak of the September 2026 nor’easter (Daily Freeman).

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Ulster County’s Architecture

Ulster’s houses fall into a handful of families, and each one sends the cable and the Wi-Fi a different way. We plan the speakers last; the building decides first.

18th-century Dutch stone houses

Kingston’s Stockade, Hurley’s Main Street and the New Paltz stone-house tradition. Thick rubble-stone walls, beamed ceilings and wide floorboards. Wi-Fi stops at the walls; cable travels through the cellar and up through closets or later partitions.

Rondout brick and Victorian streets

The Rondout–West Strand district on the creek and the Chestnut and Fair Street districts uptown: brick, plaster over lath, tall ceilings and decorative plasterwork that should not be cut casually. Exterior changes in these districts need the city’s certificate of appropriateness.

Catskill cabins and A-frames

Phoenicia, Shandaken, Saugerties, the Woodstock hollows: ceilings that follow the roof, knotty pine and log walls, plank decks and wood stoves. Few hollow cavities and long empty winters.

Converted barns and timber-frame studios

Open timber volumes used as studios, rehearsal rooms or living space. Big air, hard surfaces, and frequently a separate building from the house and its router.

Post-war ranches and capes

The Town of Ulster, Lloyd, Marlborough and the edges of Kingston: drywall, attics and basements, and the easiest cable routes in the county. Most standard Sonos plans work here as they would anywhere.

River-town apartments and SUNY-area rentals

Kingston and New Paltz flats and shared houses, where nothing permanent can go in without the owner’s say. Free-standing speakers and a tidy network are the usual answer.

Method notes, house by house

Stone. We don’t chase cable into old stone walls. The cellar is the highway: a cable runs along the beams below the first floor and rises in a closet, behind a later partition or beside a chimney chase. Where the house has a newer frame wing, that wing usually takes the access point and the Amp. Brackets fixed into stone carry the $65 brick-and-stone surcharge on our price list, and every fixing goes into sound stone or a mortar joint the owner agrees to.

Plaster and brick in the river towns. Old plaster is hard on wireless — Sonos lists plaster with concrete as a high obstruction — and brittle under a hole saw. Where a ceiling pair makes sense we survey with a stud finder and probe first, as Sonos’s own in-ceiling instructions direct, and the cut-out is 8.25 inches for the 6-inch model. Plaster brackets carry the $45 surcharge.

Beam ceilings and A-frames. When the ceiling is the floor of the room above, or follows a steep roof, there is often no cavity at all. Wall-mounted Era 100 or Era 300 speakers, or a pair on stands, give better sound there than forcing an in-ceiling speaker into a joist bay. Cable runs along a beam in a small timber or painted raceway, or comes up from below.

Logs and knotty pine. Log walls are solid and settle for years after building, so cable never crosses them rigidly; it travels in the floor system, the framed partitions or a surface raceway finished to match the wood. Knotty-pine panelling over studs is friendlier: a panel can sometimes be eased off, a cable fished behind it and the panel replaced, which beats any visible run in a cabin living room.

Timber volumes. Big open rooms with hard surfaces reward Trueplay tuning and careful speaker aim more than extra speakers. In a studio, we keep Sonos out of the recording chain entirely unless you want it there.

Rules that come with business music in Ulster

  • Licensed music. Spotify’s own help pages say the service is for personal, non-commercial use and can’t be played publicly from a business such as a bar, restaurant, store or salon. Sonos sells Sonos Pro to businesses, with commercially licensed music and scheduling. We set up whichever licensed service you choose.
  • Kingston’s historic districts. The city code requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission for material changes to exterior elements visible from a street in a Landmark District — the Stockade, Rondout–West Strand, Fair Street and Chestnut Street among them. Ordinary interior work, including speakers inside, is outside that review. Outdoor speakers or cable on a street-facing wall are not.
  • Woodstock’s noise law. Chapter 139 of the town code prohibits unnecessary noise between 11 p.m. and 7 a.m. and excessive sound from any sound reproduction system heard over a property line. The town’s zoning also sets a 64 dBA limit at the property line in commercial districts by day and 60 dBA at night, as Hudson Valley One reported in June 2025.
  • Electrical work. New circuits and outlets for an equipment cabinet belong to a master electrician licensed by the Ulster County Electrical Licensing Board. We do the low-voltage side and coordinate.
  • Paperwork. Certificates of insurance for landlords and building managers are free on our price list.

How we set up a shop or café system

A business system is planned around the hours it runs and the people who run it. We split the room into zones where the sound has to change — counter, seating, patio, fitting rooms — and set each zone’s level with customers in it, because an empty room with stone or brick walls is far louder than a full one. Music comes from the licensed service the business chooses, signed in under the business’s account, and schedules start and stop it so staff never need a personal phone on the system. Era 100 Pro speakers powered over a single network cable keep ceilings and walls tidy in a fit-out; in an older building with no ceiling cavity, wall-mounted speakers on an Amp do the same job.

Most Ulster shops are small, so most systems are one or two zones. The bigger decisions are about the building: a pressed or plastered ceiling that can’t be cut, a landlord who has to approve any fixing, a historic street outside the door. We take photographs and write the plan down so the landlord or the commission sees exactly what is proposed.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ulster owners actually put to us on walkthroughs, grouped the way the problems turn up: at the stereo, in the structure, out in the hollows, and behind the counter. Each answer starts with the short version.

Sonos beside the hi-fi you already own

Can a Sonos Amp drive the vintage floor-standing speakers we already own?

Usually, once we have checked two numbers on the back of each speaker or in its manual. Sonos’s Amp guide asks for speakers rated to handle at least 125 W at 8 ohms or 200 W at 4 ohms, and says never to connect anything below 4 ohms. Many classic floor-standers are 8-ohm designs and sit comfortably inside that. Some are nominal 4-ohm speakers whose impedance dips lower at certain frequencies; those we test, and if they don’t suit the Amp we suggest a Port into an amplifier built for them instead.

Two pairs of 8-ohm speakers can run in parallel on one Amp, which Sonos supports; series wiring is not. If your speakers have bi-wire terminals, we leave the factory jumpers in place and run a single good-gauge pair. Then Trueplay, where your phone supports it, tunes the speakers to the room.

Will Sonos play our 24-bit FLAC library at full resolution?

Up to a point. Sonos’s support pages list FLAC and ALAC at up to 24-bit and say Sonos plays files with sample rates up to 48 kHz; on the older S1 app, FLAC and ALAC are limited to 16-bit. AIFF and WAV are listed at 16-bit. So a 24/48 file plays as recorded. Files at 88.2, 96 or 192 kHz are a different matter: answers in the Sonos community explain that tracks above 48 kHz are streamed at CD resolution, 16-bit/44.1 kHz, instead.

If much of your library is high-sample-rate, we either point Sonos at a 48 kHz copy you make with your own software, or leave the critical listening to the system you already have and use Sonos for everything else. We tell you which before the job, not after.

A hum started when the Port was plugged into our stereo — what causes it?

Nearly always a ground loop: two pieces of equipment plugged into different outlets, joined by an RCA cable whose shield becomes the path between them. It is a long-standing complaint with Sonos’s line-level boxes — owners in the Sonos community describe the Port and the older Connect being easily affected, and the usual cures are the ones we try first: feed the Port and the amplifier from the same outlet strip, check the receiver’s ground, swap the analog connection for the Port’s coaxial digital output if your amplifier has a DAC input, and shorten or replace the interconnect.

We isolate it with the music off and the volume up, one change at a time, so you know which fix worked. If nothing in the room cures it, a ground-loop isolator on the analog line is a last resort, not a first one.

Is it worth running the Port’s digital output into the outboard DAC in our system?

If your DAC is better than the Port’s own converter, or you simply prefer its sound, yes. The Port has a coaxial digital output alongside its analog line out, so it can hand the stream to your DAC untouched and let the DAC do the conversion. It also takes one analog cable out of the chain, which removes one possible source of hum.

Two things to agree first. With a digital connection, volume normally belongs to your preamp or DAC, so we set the Port’s output to fixed. And the 48 kHz limit still applies to what Sonos sends, so the DAC improves conversion, not the resolution of the file. We listen with you on both connections before choosing.

Can a musician play a keyboard live through the Sonos speakers without hearing a lag?

Not comfortably. Sonos buffers anything that comes in through a line-in before it plays it, and owners in the Sonos community note that the app’s line-in delay settings start at 75 milliseconds rather than zero. That is fine for a turntable or a CD player, where nobody can hear the delay, but a player pressing a key and hearing the note late will notice at once.

For practice, keep the instrument on its own amplifier or monitors and use Sonos for backing tracks played from the app. If you want both through the same speakers, a small mixer feeding powered monitors does the live work, and a Port joins the house system for everything recorded.

Stone walls, beams and sloped ceilings

Our ceiling is the floorboards of the room above, carried on exposed beams — where can a ceiling speaker go?

Usually nowhere it will sound or look right. An in-ceiling speaker needs a cavity: Sonos’s 6-inch model needs 4.73 inches of depth behind an 8.25-inch hole. Where the boards are the ceiling, there is only the thickness of the floor above. Cutting a speaker into it means cutting the room upstairs too, and we don’t recommend it in an old house.

The better answers are on the walls and on furniture: a pair of Era 100s on wall brackets angled toward the seating, a Five on a sideboard, or a passive pair of your choosing on an Amp. Their cable can run along the top of a beam in a narrow, painted raceway or come up from the cellar through the floor at the wall.

In an A-frame whose living room rises to the roof peak, where should the speakers sit?

Low, and aimed at the people. Sound in a tall, steep room climbs and reflects off two sloped planes; speakers mounted high on the roofline put most of their energy into the peak. We usually place a stereo pair on the end wall or the gable side at about ear height when seated, or on stands, and let Trueplay tune them. Sonos’s ceiling guide suggests a stereo pair 6 to 10 feet apart, which a typical A-frame end wall accommodates.

If you want speakers overhead, the sloped ceiling under the loft is the one place a ceiling pair can work, because it is a flat, conventional framed section. Anything mounted on the slope itself goes into a rafter bay that has to be checked for insulation and vent channels first.

Can a network cable pass through the thick stone wall between the old house and the addition?

Yes, but rarely straight through. Drilling through thick rubble stone is slow, noisy and permanent, and the hole has to be sealed against air and insects afterward. In most houses there is an easier crossing: under the floor through the cellar, where the old foundation already has openings for pipes, or through the doorway framing where the addition joins.

Once a single cable crosses, an access point on the far side does the rest, and Sonos speakers in the old rooms connect to it rather than trying to hear a router through stone. If no crossing is possible, a pair of point-to-point wireless units set in the windows on either side can carry the network around the wall instead of through it.

Our log home has no hollow walls at all — how does speaker cable get to the right places?

From below, along the top, or in a chase that was built for it. Log walls are solid, and they settle: the inspectors’ association InterNACHI notes that a log wall can lose 3/4 inch per foot of wall height in the first two years, when most settling happens. A cable drilled through logs, or a speaker box fixed rigidly across them, can be pinched or pulled as the wall drops.

So we use the routes log builders leave: the floor system and crawl space below, the framed partitions and ceilings above, the electrical chases cut into the logs when the house went up, and window and door bucks with settling space. Where nothing exists, a surface raceway in a wood finish follows a corner or a top log. Free-standing speakers often make the most sense on the log walls themselves.

Hollows, creeks and weekend arrivals

There’s no cell signal at our place in the hollow — can we still run Sonos from our phones?

Yes. The Sonos app talks to the speakers over your home Wi-Fi, not over the cellular network, so a phone showing “No Service” still controls the system once it has joined the house network. Hudson Valley One reported in 2016 that coverage gaps follow Routes 212 and 28, where the mountains block signal, so this matters in a lot of Ulster kitchens.

What you do need is a working internet connection for streaming services and for setup, because the app signs in to your Sonos account. Local music — a library on a network drive, or a turntable through a Port or line-in — keeps playing over the home network alone. Sonos Voice Control, if you use it, is processed on the speaker itself rather than in the cloud.

Shops, cafés and venues

Can our café play Spotify through Sonos, or does a business need a licensed service?

It needs a licensed service. Spotify’s help page on public and commercial use is direct: the service is for personal, non-commercial use, and it can’t be played publicly from a business such as a bar, restaurant, store or salon. The same applies to the other personal streaming apps.

Sonos sells Sonos Pro to businesses, which streams commercially licensed music and adds scheduling and multi-location control, and other business music services work on Sonos too. We set up whichever you choose, lock the system to it so staff aren’t tempted to switch to their own phones, and leave the account in the business’s name.

Is a Sonos system loud enough to run a wedding reception or a packed gallery opening?

For background music, yes; as the main sound system for a crowd with speeches and dancing, no. Sonos makes home and small-business speakers. A barn reception or an opening night needs microphones, a mixer and speakers designed to project over a room full of people, which is a rental or a band’s own system.

Where Sonos earns its place is the rest of the day: dinner music in the courtyard, the cocktail hour on the lawn, the playlist before the band starts. We wire a line out from a Port so the event system can play from Sonos when the DJ isn’t using it, and keep the two from fighting over volume.

Can the shop’s music start on its own at opening and stop at closing?

Yes, using the app’s alarms, which Sonos describes for wake-ups but which work just as well for business hours. Each alarm sets a time, the rooms, the music, the volume, how often it repeats and how long it plays, and a system can hold up to 32 of them. A weekday alarm at opening time and a Saturday one an hour earlier covers most Main Street schedules.

Sonos Pro adds proper scheduling across locations if you run more than one shop. Either way, we set the levels with the room full rather than empty, so the morning volume isn’t too loud at 10 and too quiet at 2.

Equipment

Types of Sonos Systems We Install

We install the whole current Sonos range and the network that carries it. Here is what each piece is for in an Ulster house, with the specifications Sonos publishes.

Sonos Port

The bridge to an existing stereo. Analog line out that can be fixed, variable or pass-through, a coaxial digital out, an analog line in, a 12V trigger and two Ethernet ports. Sonos asks for at least 2 inches of clearance around it on a shelf.

Sonos Amp

125 W per channel into 8 ohms with HDMI ARC/eARC and two Ethernet ports. Drives one pair of speakers, or two pairs of 8-ohm speakers in parallel. Operating range 32°F to 104°F, so not for an unheated outbuilding in January.

Era 100 and Era 300

Stand-alone speakers for rooms with no ceiling cavity. Sonos’s Combo Adapter, listed at $39 on its site, adds a line-in and an Ethernet port. Era 300 wall mounts are for studs only.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Ulster County

We couldn’t view Google’s AI Overview for Sonos installation in Ulster County from our build environment, so we haven’t quoted it. Below are seven claims that cost guides, forums and marketing pages publish — the material answer engines tend to repeat — set against what we find in Ulster houses.

“Sonos is hi-res audio.”

Marketing for streaming speakers leans on “hi-res” without saying what it means. Sonos’s own beginner’s guide defines hi-res as at least 24 bits and 48 kHz.

That is also the ceiling. Sonos supports FLAC and ALAC at 24-bit with sample rates up to 48 kHz, and files above that are not played at their native rate. AIFF and WAV are listed at 16-bit, and the older S1 app limits FLAC and ALAC to 16-bit too. For a listener with a library of 96 kHz downloads, that is a real limit, and it is the first thing an Ulster audiophile asks us about.

It isn’t a reason to avoid Sonos; it is a reason to plan. Keep the high-rate files on the system built for them and let Sonos carry the radio, the streaming and the rest of the house.

“Plug a Port into any stereo and you’re done.”

The Port does connect to almost any amplifier with a line input. What the simple version leaves out is ground: two boxes on different outlets, joined by an RCA cable, can hum.

Sonos community threads about Port buzzing go back years, and the fixes are practical: shared power, a digital connection, a better interconnect.

The other half is volume. Leave the Port’s output variable and the app and the amp’s knob fight; fix it and the app’s slider does nothing. We set it the way you actually listen.

“Multi-room audio costs $500 to $1,000 per room.”

Angi’s whole-home audio guide puts additional zones at $500–$1,000 per room and wall or ceiling modifications at $100–$500, without naming Sonos.

In a drywall ranch that range can hold. In a stone house it describes a different job: the cost is in getting a cable across the building, not in the room. A free-standing Era 100 in a beamed room costs our $125 setup line; a wired ceiling pair in plaster is quoted at walkthrough. The national figure also says nothing about joining an existing stereo, which in Ulster is often the cheapest and best-sounding room in the house: $254 for a Port on our list.

So we price the route first. Our Hudson Valley AV page publishes its own ranges for whole-home systems, and fixed lines below are the same in every valley county.

“Use the line-in for anything — turntable, TV, instruments.”

Product pages present line-in as universal, and for recorded sources it is.

The catch is the buffer. Owners in the Sonos community point out that line-in delay settings start at 75 ms, with no zero option. A guitarist or keyboard player hears that as lag.

Records and CDs don’t care; live playing does. That is why we tell musicians to keep their instruments on their own amplification and use Sonos for everything recorded. A rehearsal room is the one place in the house where Sonos should be a playback system only.

“Just play your Spotify in the shop.”

Plenty of small-business advice treats a personal streaming account as background music.

Spotify’s own terms say it is for personal, non-commercial use and can’t be played publicly from a business. Sonos sells Sonos Pro, with commercially licensed music, precisely because businesses need a different licence.

For a Wall Street café or a Tinker Street gallery, the right service costs less than a dispute later. We set the system up on it from day one, with the business as the account holder, and schedule it so the music changes at opening and closing without anyone touching a phone.

“A mesh kit or a range extender will cover the whole house.”

Router marketing promises whole-home coverage in a box.

Sonos’s system requirements list wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power adapters as unsupported, and its interference guidance rates brick as a medium obstruction and plaster and concrete as high. Thick rubble stone is at least as hard on a signal.

Mesh can work if its nodes are joined by cable, which is the point: in an old Ulster house, the network crosses the wall on a wire, then goes wireless on the other side. Sonos also asks that a wired mesh setup connect Sonos to the primary node, and that powerline adapters stay out of it entirely.

“Without good cell coverage, smart speakers are useless.”

It is a common assumption in the hollows, where phone signal comes and goes along Route 28 and Route 212.

Sonos doesn’t use the cellular network. The app reaches the speakers over Wi-Fi, local music plays without the internet, and Sonos says its own voice control processes requests on the speaker rather than in the cloud.

What does need the internet is streaming and the first setup. With the county’s fiber build now approved for its last unserved addresses, more of those houses will have it soon.

Listening Rooms

Bringing Sonos Into a Room Built Around a Stereo

Adding Sonos to a room built around a stereo is mostly about not disturbing what already works. This is the order we follow.

  • Write down the chain. Source, phono stage, amplifier, speakers, and which outlet each plugs into. Hum problems are solved on paper first.
  • Pick the join. A spare line input on the amplifier for the Port’s analog out, or a coaxial digital input if the amplifier has its own DAC. A tape loop or an unused AUX input is fine.
  • Decide who owns the volume. Fixed output keeps the amplifier’s knob in charge; variable hands it to the app. Pass-through exists for people who want both, and we explain the trade-off.
  • Use the trigger if you have one. The Port’s 12V trigger can wake an amplifier that supports it when music starts.
  • Give the Port air. Sonos asks for at least 2 inches around it on a shelf, away from radiators, and three can share one rack unit on a third-party shelf.
  • Wire it if you can. The Port has two Ethernet ports; a cable from the router or an access point takes Wi-Fi out of the listening chain.
  • Keep the Sub away from the turntable. Bass and a stylus share a shelf badly; the Sub goes on the other side of the room.
  • Leave the critical files where they are. Anything above 48 kHz belongs on the system built to play it.

Records in a Sonos house

Vinyl and Sonos get along once the order of the chain is clear. The turntable still plays through its phono stage into your amplifier exactly as before; nothing about Sonos changes that path. What Sonos adds is the reverse direction: a phono stage’s output can also feed the Port’s analog line in, or an Era 100 through the Combo Adapter, so the record playing in the living room can be heard in the kitchen or on the porch. Because Sonos buffers a line-in, the other rooms hear the record a moment after the living room does, which nobody notices from another floor but everyone notices standing in the doorway between the two. We set the delay as low as the app allows and suggest grouping rooms that are out of earshot of each other. If the turntable is the source people use most, we label the Port’s line-in in the app with something obvious, like “Records”, so anyone in the house can find it and send the side that’s playing to the room they are in.

Two practical notes. Line-level only: a turntable without a built-in or separate phono preamp is too quiet and the wrong tone for any line input, Sonos included. And keep the Sub, and any speaker standing on the floor, away from the turntable’s stand; bass that reaches the platter through the floor is a turntable problem that Sonos can make worse.

Studios & Rehearsal Rooms

Sonos in a House With a Home Studio

A home studio and a Sonos system can share a house without sharing a signal path. That is how most of the musicians we work for want it.

  • Keep Sonos out of the recording chain. A Port’s line out can feed monitors for playback, but nothing from Sonos goes into an interface you record through.
  • Give the studio its own group. The studio speakers join the house only when you add them, so nobody upstairs starts a playlist in the middle of a take.
  • Mind the microphones. On newer voice-capable Sonos speakers the microphone switch cuts power to the mics entirely; we show you where it is.
  • Spacing from other electronics. Sonos’s interference guidance asks for at least 18 inches between a Sonos product and other electronic devices, which also keeps the Port and its power supply clear of sensitive audio gear.
  • Live playing stays on the instrument amp. Line-in delay makes Sonos the wrong path for anything you play in real time.
  • Wire the network. A cabled access point in the studio keeps Wi-Fi traffic predictable, and a separate building gets fiber or a wireless bridge rather than a weak signal from the house.

If the studio is in a converted barn or a cottage across the yard, the network to it is the bigger job. Our Hudson Valley hub goes into outbuilding links in depth; in Ulster the same rule holds — carry the network to the building on a cable or a dedicated link, then let Sonos work inside it.

One more studio habit worth keeping: the neighbours. A rehearsal room or a deck system that carries over the property line late at night is exactly what Woodstock’s noise law addresses, with its 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. window, and other towns have their own rules. Sonos’s per-room volume limit is a simple guard for the outdoor zone and the studio playback speakers, and we set it with you rather than for you.

Glossary

Sonos Terms You’ll Hear on an Ulster Job

Port. Sonos’s component that streams into an existing amplifier or receiver. It has no speaker outputs of its own.

Amp. Sonos’s amplifier for passive speakers, with HDMI for a TV and outputs for one pair, or two 8-ohm pairs in parallel.

Fixed, variable and pass-through output. The Port’s line-out modes: fixed leaves volume to your amplifier, variable lets the Sonos app control it, pass-through sends the Port’s own line-in signal through.

12V trigger. A low-voltage signal the Port sends when it plays, which can switch on an amplifier with a matching input.

Impedance. A speaker’s electrical load in ohms. The Amp should not see anything below 4 ohms.

Ground loop. A hum caused by two pieces of equipment finding different paths to ground, usually through an audio cable’s shield.

Line-in delay. The buffer Sonos adds to a line-level source before playing it, which makes it unsuitable for live instruments.

Sample rate and bit depth. How finely digital audio is sampled. Sonos plays FLAC and ALAC at up to 24-bit and up to 48 kHz.

SMB share. The file-sharing method a computer or network drive uses to offer a music library to Sonos; version 2 or 3 on the current app.

Trueplay. Sonos’s room tuning, run with a supported phone, which measures how sound reflects off walls and furnishings.

PoE+. Power over the network cable, used by the Era 100 Pro so a single cable carries both power and data.

Combo Adapter. Sonos’s accessory, listed at $39 on its site, that adds a line-in and an Ethernet port to Era 100, Era 300 and several other models.

Sonos Pro. Sonos’s subscription for businesses, with commercially licensed music, scheduling and control across locations.

Certificate of appropriateness. Kingston’s approval for visible exterior changes to a landmark or a building in a Landmark District.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Ulster Sonos Walkthroughs

Installer practice, not a customer story. We don’t publish anecdotes we can’t show you, so this is how our Ulster walkthroughs go rather than a tale from one of them.

The first ten minutes are spent at the stereo, not the router. We photograph the back panel of the amplifier, note the speakers’ impedance, and ask who uses the volume knob. Then we go to the cellar with a flashlight, because in a stone house that is where the cable will travel, and we look for the pipe openings, the later partitions and the chimney chase that make a crossing possible. Upstairs, we stand in each room with a phone and test the Wi-Fi; in the old rooms of a stone house the number often drops sharply once you step through the doorway. In A-frames and cabins we look up: if the ceiling follows the roof or is the floor above, we say early that in-ceiling speakers are out and show where wall speakers would go. In shops, we ask what music service the business pays for. Most jobs are priced from those notes the same day.

Ulster County Sonos Installation FAQ

What does it cost to connect a Sonos Port to the stereo we already have in Ulster County?
+

If the visit turns into hum-hunting on an older amplifier, that time is billed as a setup-only visit, $275 for the first hour in Ulster.

Can our Sonos play Radio Woodstock and other Hudson Valley stations?
+

Yes. Sonos says TuneIn’s complete catalog is available inside Sonos Radio, alongside providers such as iHeartRadio, and Radio Woodstock 100.1 WDST is listed on TuneIn and streams live from its own site. We search for the stations you listen to during setup and save them as favourites in each room. A station that isn’t listed can be added by its stream address if the stream is MP3, AAC or WMA. Favourites can then be dropped into an alarm, so the house wakes up to the station you choose on the mornings you are there.

Can the same Sonos app run our city system and our Ulster system as two separate homes?
+

Yes. Sonos’s support pages describe connecting the app to a second system in a different location: once both are connected, the app recognises each one automatically when your phone joins that house’s Wi-Fi. The two systems stay separate, so a speaker in Kingston never turns up in the Brooklyn app and vice versa. We connect your phone, and anyone else’s you name, before we leave.

How do you keep mice from chewing the speaker wire in a cabin that sits empty all winter?
+

By not giving them anything to chew. Pest-control and electrical guides say the same thing: rodents come in to escape the cold and go for exposed wire, and the answer is sealing holes and keeping cable covered. We run speaker and network cable in raceway or conduit through crawl spaces, basements and attics, seal the holes we make with fire-rated or rodent-resistant filler, and fit wall plates tightly. Trapping and sealing the house itself is a job for a pest-control company. When we come back for a seasonal check, we look along the runs we installed for droppings or chewed jackets before the first speaker is switched on.

Can our caretaker check that the Sonos is working before we drive up for the weekend?
+

Yes. If you want, we connect the caretaker’s phone to the system so they can open the Sonos app and confirm that every room still answers before you leave the city. We also label the router, the switch and any Amp or Port with what they feed and leave the restart order taped inside the equipment cupboard, so whoever keeps an eye on the house can bring the system back after a power cut without calling anyone.

Can Sonos play the music library stored on our network drive?
+

Yes. Sonos can stream a music library from a computer or a network-attached storage drive on your home network. The drive has to share its music folder using the SMBv2 or SMBv3 protocol; the older SMBv1 is only for the S1 app and Sonos warns of its security risks. Sonos’s system requirements cap a library at 65,000 songs. We add the share in the app and check that the formats in your library are ones Sonos lists.

Can the microphones on our Sonos speakers be switched off?
+

On the newer voice-capable speakers, yes. Sonos says its latest speakers include a switch that disconnects power to the microphones entirely when it is off. If you do want voice control, Sonos Voice Control processes requests on the speaker itself and, according to Sonos, doesn’t record or send the audio to the cloud. We set up whichever you choose, or none.

Will our amplifier turn on by itself when we press play in the Sonos app?
+

If it has a 12V trigger input, it can. The Sonos Port has a 12V trigger output that switches on with playback; connected to an amplifier or receiver with a matching trigger input, the amplifier wakes when Sonos starts and sleeps when it stops. Plenty of older and vintage amplifiers have no trigger input, in which case the amplifier is simply left on the Port’s input, or switched on by hand. We look for a trigger input on the back-panel photo you send before the visit, so the right cable comes with us.
