The Neighborhood

Why Brighton Beach Properties Need TV Installation

Brighton Beach is the strip of Brooklyn between Ocean Parkway and Corbin Place, with the Belt Parkway and Sheepshead Bay to the north and the beach and boardwalk to the south. It sits in Community Board 13 with Coney Island and Gravesend, in ZIP 11235. Most people know it as Little Odessa, and Brooklyn Magazine's 2023 guide says Russian is the language heard most on the promenade, along with Ukrainian, Georgian and some Uzbek, Polish and Armenian. That is the first thing that shapes a TV job here, and we will come back to it. The second is the buildings. Brighton Beach is not a neighborhood of brownstones or frame houses. It is a neighborhood of apartment buildings, packed in close between the elevated train and the ocean, and the wall where a family wants the TV is usually in one of them.

The oldest layer is the bungalow colonies. Forgotten NY has mapped the narrow lanes off Brighton Beach Avenue, Brighton 1st Walk, Terrace, Lane, Place and Path between Brighton 1st and 2nd Streets, the courts and walks between Oceanview and Neptune Avenues, and the wedge of Brighton 10th lanes along the old right-of-way of the train. Many of those small houses were winterized for year-round living, and many have been replaced by taller buildings. The next layer is the one that gives the neighborhood its look: the elevator apartment houses of the 1920s and 1930s on Ocean Parkway, Brightwater Court and the numbered Brighton streets, several with art deco entrances. StreetEasy lists 3130 Brighton 6th Street, for example, as a six-story elevator co-op built in 1930 with about 85 apartments. After the war came postwar co-ops such as 1311 Brightwater Avenue near the boardwalk and the apartment towers on the northern blocks, which put many more households on each lot. And most recently came Oceana, the condominium complex by Muss Development that Commercial Observer counted at 16 buildings and 927 units in 2014.

Each of those layers leaves a different wall behind the television. The 1930s co-ops usually have plaster on their interior partitions and solid masonry on the outside walls and around the elevator and stair shafts, and the plaster can sit on wood lath, metal lath or directly on block. The postwar towers often put poured concrete or block behind the living room wall, which is very strong and very hard to drill neatly. The newer condos are closer to modern framing, but some use metal studs, and a stud finder that works in one building is no help in the next. A TV installer who walks into Brighton Beach expecting drywall on wood will be wrong more often than right, and the cost of being wrong is a bracket held by anchors made for a different wall.

Then there is who lives in those buildings and what they watch. Brighton Beach has been home to a large Russian-speaking community since the Soviet Jewish emigration of the 1970s, and for decades that community has paid for television in its own language. Wikipedia records that the Russian Television Network of America, the cable channel known by its call sign WMNB, was established in Brooklyn in 1992 and signed up 12,000 subscribers between November 2000 and February 2001 alone. Today most of that viewing has moved to internet services. Kartina.TV, one of the best known, says its app runs on most Samsung and LG smart TVs, on TVs with Android TV, and on Amazon Fire TV, Apple TV and Roku. In practice that means a new TV in a Brighton Beach apartment is often not finished when it is on the wall. The family's channels have to be signed in, the remote has to make sense to the person who will use it most, and an older streaming box may have to be replaced or moved. That is part of the job we do here, and it is why our setup-only line, $195 for the first hour, exists.

Finally, there is the ocean. Brighton Beach flooded badly in Hurricane Sandy in 2012. City Limits reported basements pumped out on nearly every block, salt-soaked elevator equipment and a garage where the water rose over five feet. First Street's model puts 99 percent of Brighton Beach properties at some flood risk over the next 30 years. For a TV, the flood mostly matters in what got rebuilt afterwards: lobbies, ground-floor apartments and storefronts with newer walls, and buildings that moved equipment upward. Oceana, according to Commercial Observer, put its electrical switchgear on an elevated platform and its elevator controls on the roof. When we see fresh drywall in a ground-floor unit near the boardwalk, we ask what it covers before we drill into it.

Same-Day Repair in Brighton Beach

Need Repair in Brighton Beach? A Screen That Sags, a Bracket Loosening in Old Plaster, or a TV Left Behind by the Last Owner

The urgent TV calls from older apartment buildings tend to be the same three, and Brighton Beach's walls make each of them likely. The first is a screen that has started to tilt forward or drop on one side. In a prewar co-op that almost always means the bracket was fixed with anchors meant for drywall, gripping a skin of plaster with nothing solid behind it. Plaster is hard at the face and crumbly underneath, and every time someone pulls a full-motion arm out to watch from the kitchen, the anchor crushes a little more of it. The second is a bracket on a masonry outer wall that was screwed into the soft mortar joints rather than the brick or block, and has begun to work loose. The third is what a seller or a previous tenant leaves behind: an empty bracket, an old flat screen nobody wants, a cable poking out of a hole and a power strip taped to the wall.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back into place, and do not let anyone stand under it. If two people can lift it off safely, take the weight off the bracket and unplug it, then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, starting at $179.99 with the plaster or masonry add-on, plus the take-down. When the old holes sit exactly where the new fixings need to go, we tell you before starting whether the bracket can shift sideways to sound wall or whether the plaster needs repair first. AESS does not patch or paint walls, so if the wall itself is the problem, a crack running through the plaster or a loose section of block, we say so plainly and you can bring in the super, the board's contractor or a plasterer before we hang anything.

One more kind of repair call is common here and easy to fix: the TV works, but the family's Russian-language service does not. An old streaming box has stopped updating, the app has disappeared from an older smart TV, or the remote has been reset and nobody can find the input. That is a setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour, and most of the time we can see from a photo of the screen and the box what has happened before we come.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Brighton Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every TV call we expect from Brighton Beach. They differ in three ways that matter far more than the brand of the TV: what the wall is made of, how the screen gets from the street to your door, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

The prewar elevator co-op

Five- and six-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s, like 3130 Brighton 6th Street, built in 1930. Plaster inside, masonry outside, an elevator, a super and a board.

The Brightwater Court and Ocean Parkway apartment house

The art deco buildings on the western edge, with grander lobbies and the same plaster-and-masonry construction, often with long living rooms facing the street.

The postwar co-op or tower

Taller buildings such as the postwar co-op at 1311 Brightwater Avenue near the boardwalk and the towers on the northern blocks, where concrete and block are common.

The Oceana condominium

A 16-building complex at the eastern end, built to post-Sandy standards with equipment raised out of the flood zone, newer framing and large windows.

The lane house or small walk-up

Winterized bungalows and small buildings on the Brighton lanes, and the low walk-ups mixed in along the side streets, where the stairs are tight and the walls vary house to house.

The storefront under the El

Shops, restaurants and offices on Brighton Beach Avenue below the B and Q, many with basements, older masonry and fit-out walls added over the years.

The prewar elevator co-op: plaster partitions, masonry shell, and a board that wants to know

The six-story co-op is the building we expect most often in Brighton Beach, and it is a good building for a TV once you know its walls. The partitions between rooms are usually plaster, and in a building from around 1930 that plaster can sit on wood lath, on metal lath or straight on clay tile or block. Each takes a fixing differently. Wood lath means we look for the framing behind it, because the lath itself will not carry a screen. Metal lath confuses an ordinary stud finder, which reads metal along the whole wall. Plaster on block or tile wants a masonry anchor set into the block itself, not the plaster. The outer walls, the ones with the windows, are solid masonry. They hold a heavy screen very well, but they need a hammer drill, and in a co-op that can mean a conversation with the super about hours.

Before the visit we ask one question every co-op shareholder should ask too: does the building have an alteration policy, and does a TV bracket count? In most buildings a TV on the wall with a cord cover is treated as decorating, while opening a wall to run power inside it is treated as an alteration that needs the managing agent's approval and an insurance certificate naming the corporation and the agent. We are happy to work either way. The point is to know before the van arrives, because a super who stops a job halfway is a cost to you and a wasted trip for us. If your building wants a certificate, send us the exact names and the address the office gives you and we will get it to them before the date.

The Brightwater Court and Ocean Parkway apartment houses: long rooms and windows on the street

The apartment houses on Brightwater Court and along the southern stretch of Ocean Parkway are some of the handsomest buildings in the neighborhood, and cityneighborhoods.nyc dates many of them to the 1920s and 1930s with art deco details. Inside, they often have long living rooms with the windows at one end and the entrance at the other. That shape creates the most common TV question we get from these buildings: which wall. The long inside wall is usually a plaster partition with the hallway or the next apartment behind it. The short wall at the window end is masonry. The wall at the entrance end often has the radiator riser or a doorway in it.

Our usual advice in a long room is to put the TV on the long inside wall, opposite the sofa, rather than at the window end where the screen fights the daylight. That wall is plaster, so it carries the +$45 plaster add-on, and if it is shared with the next apartment we keep the drilling short and warn the neighbor through you. If the family wants to watch from both the sofa and the dining table, a full-motion arm lets the screen turn, but an arm puts much more leverage on the wall than a flat bracket does, and on plaster we will only use one when we can reach solid framing or block behind the plaster for every fixing point the plate offers.

The postwar co-op or tower: concrete, block and a hammer drill

In the taller postwar buildings, including the co-ops near the boardwalk on Brightwater Avenue and the towers on the northern blocks, the wall behind the TV is more likely to be poured concrete or concrete block, sometimes with a thin skim of plaster or a layer of drywall glued over it. Concrete is the strongest wall a TV can hang on and the least forgiving to drill. A bit that hits steel reinforcing has to stop, and the hole has to move, which is why the bracket position is agreed with tape before drilling and why we choose brackets with more than one row of fixing slots for these walls.

Block is different again. Hollow concrete block has a solid face and empty cells behind it, and an anchor that expands in the face alone can pull out under the weight of a large screen. We use anchors made for hollow block or we land fixings in the solid webs between cells. On our Brooklyn list, brick or stone is a +$65 add-on. Poured concrete and block are quoted from your photos on the call, because the right price depends on whether the wall is bare, skimmed or furred out with drywall, and we would rather give you the right number before booking than a guess.

The Oceana condominium: newer framing, big glass and a management office

Oceana is the newest large complex in the neighborhood, and Commercial Observer described it as built to post-Sandy standards, with electrical switchgear moved to an elevated platform and elevator controls moved to the roof. For a TV that matters less than the walls inside the apartments, which are much closer to modern construction than anything in the prewar buildings: gypsum board over framing, sometimes metal, with large windows along the outer wall. Drywall on wood studs is the case our $179.99 base price is built for. Metal studs need fasteners that bite into steel, and we confirm which you have with a scanner and a pilot hole before committing to a layout.

Condo offices typically want a contractor's insurance certificate, a working-hours window and sometimes an elevator booking, and they may have rules about opening walls that share a fire-rated assembly with the next unit or the corridor. Tell us what your office has asked for and we will have it ready. If opening the wall is not allowed, a paintable cord cover at $59 runs the cables neatly down to the console instead, and many owners never notice the difference once it is painted the wall color.

The lane house and the small walk-up: tight stairs and walls that change house to house

The bungalows on the Brighton lanes started as summer cottages, and Forgotten NY describes how they were winterized for year-round living and, one by one, replaced by bigger buildings. The ones that remain are full of surprises for a TV installer: framing that was added later, paneling over older boards, drywall over plaster, and walls that are not quite straight. We do not assume anything in these houses. The scanner, the knock and a hidden pilot hole decide the anchor, and the floors are often far enough out of level that we level the screen to true and check it by eye from the couch.

The small walk-ups on the side streets bring the other challenge: the carry. A 65-inch screen in its box is long and awkward on a narrow stair, and it goes up wrapped in moving blankets with two people. Tell us the floor and whether there is an elevator when you book, and if the box will not make a turn, we unbox it at the bottom and carry the screen alone, which is safer for a TV than forcing the box.

The storefront under the El: masonry, basements and a train overhead

Brighton Beach Avenue runs under the elevated B and Q for most of its length, and the shops along it are some of the oldest commercial spaces in the neighborhood. Behind the fit-out walls there is usually old masonry, and below the floor there is usually a basement that has flooded at least once. DNAinfo reported in 2012 that stores along the avenue reopened dark after Sandy and that at least one bookshop lost its basement stock. A TV for a shop or restaurant here goes on a wall we have checked from both sides where we can, with the power fed from a circuit the owner's electrician confirms, never from an extension cord run behind the counter.

The train is the other factor. Residents told CBS New York in 2023 that paint was falling from the structure as trains passed. Inside a shop, that matters mainly for screens near the front windows and for anything mounted on the façade, so we prefer interior walls for menu boards and sports screens, away from the doors, and we tighten every fixing on a business screen to the bracket maker's figure rather than by feel, because a wall that carries a little vibration all day rewards a bracket that was fixed properly.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Brighton Beach Living Room

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like the ones in Brighton Beach. They are our standard practice, not a story about any particular apartment or address.

Ask who watches. Before any tool comes out, the technician asks who uses this TV most and what they watch. In Brighton Beach the answer is often a grandparent who watches Russian-language news in the morning and a younger family member who watches sport and streams films at night. That one answer changes the height, the remote, the apps on the home screen and sometimes the wall.

Look at the room. Which wall faces the windows, which one is shared with the next apartment, where the radiator and its riser are, and where the outlets sit. In a building from 1930, an outlet in a metal box on the surface of the wall usually means wiring added later in conduit, and conduit is something we must not drill.

Knock, scan and drill once. A knock along the wall at screen height tells a lot about whether it is hollow or solid. The scanner confirms framing or metal, though on metal lath it will read metal everywhere. Then one small pilot hole, placed where the bracket plate will cover it, settles the question: white powder and wood means plaster on lath, white then red grit means brick, grey grit means block or concrete. If that changes the add-on from what we quoted, you hear it before we go further.

Tape, then sit. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen on the wall, and the technician asks the main viewer to sit in their usual chair. We check the height, the glare from the windows at the time of day they watch, and whether the remote will see the TV from there. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket costs a wall.

Sign in last, with the viewer. Apps and channels are set up at the end, with the person who will use them, not handed over as a list of passwords on a sticky note.

Russian and Ukrainian TV

Getting Russian-Language and Ukrainian Television Working on a New Brighton Beach TV

This is the part of a TV job that national guides never mention and that Brighton Beach households ask about first. A family buys a new screen, and the question is not only whether it will hang straight but whether Mama will still have her channels in the morning. For a long time those channels came by cable. The Russian Television Network of America, the WMNB channel founded in Brooklyn in 1992, was carried on cable systems and added a digital package with Russian World and an RTR-Planeta relay in 2003, according to its Wikipedia history. Since then most Russian-language viewing in American homes has moved to internet television: subscription services that stream live channels and archives through an app or a dedicated box.

Those services used to depend on their own set-top boxes. KartinaTV, for example, announced deals with the box makers Amino and Dune HD in the early 2010s to deliver its service. Today the same services mostly run as apps. Kartina.TV's own support page says the app is "available on most models of Samsung, LG Smart TVs, TVs supporting Android TV," and on "popular devices like Amazon Fire TV, Apple TV, and Roku." That has two practical consequences for a new TV. First, the old box may no longer be needed, which means one less device, one less remote and one less power cable behind the screen. Second, whether the app runs on the TV itself depends on the TV's platform and age, and a model that does not support it can still get the service through a small streaming device plugged into an HDMI port.

What we do on the visit

If you tell us which service you watch when you book, we check before the visit whether your new TV runs its app, or whether a streaming stick or box is the better route. On the day, after the screen is up, we install the app or connect the device, sign in with your account, and put the channels you use most at the front of the home screen. We cannot buy or create subscriptions, and we only connect services that you already pay for, but we can make sure the ones you have work properly on the new screen. If the household also keeps a cable box for English-language channels, we label the inputs so that switching between the two is one button rather than a hunt through menus.

Where the internet comes in matters too. A live channel stream is less forgiving than a film, because it cannot buffer far ahead. In many prewar co-ops the router sits in the hallway by the entry door and the TV is at the far end of a long room, behind plaster and sometimes block. If the picture already stutters on the old TV, a new screen will not fix that by itself. We look at where the router is, test the speed at the TV position, and tell you honestly whether a wired connection or a better router position would help. If a wired run is the answer, it is quoted on the call; the TV list on this page covers the mounting and the setup.

Setting it up for the person who watches

In a great many Brighton Beach homes the main viewer is an older parent or grandparent who reads Russian or Ukrainian more easily than English. The setup is where a TV job succeeds or fails for them. We change the TV's own menus to the language they prefer where the model allows it, set the TV to come on at the input they use most, remove apps nobody will touch, and show them, with their own hands on the remote, how to get from off to their channel. Then we show a second family member how to undo anything that gets changed by accident. A setup-only visit on our list is $195 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks, and if it is part of a mounting visit, the setup is done the same day before we leave.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick, Concrete or Board: What Holds a TV on a Brighton Beach Wall and What It Costs

The single biggest factor in a safe TV mount is matching the fixing to the wall. A bracket is only as strong as the four to eight points that hold it, and the right fixing for one wall can fail in another. Here is how each wall we meet in Brighton Beach is handled and priced on our Brooklyn list.

Drywall on wood studs ($179.99 base, up to 55 inches). This is the wall national guides assume, and in Brighton Beach it mostly turns up in newer condos, gut-renovated co-op units and some rebuilt ground floors. The bracket's lag bolts go into the centre of the studs. If the studs do not fall where the screen needs to be, a bracket with a wider plate or a sliding mount solves it.

Plaster (+$45). The usual interior wall in the 1920s and 1930s buildings. We find what is behind the plaster first. On wood lath, fixings go into the framing. On metal lath or plaster over block, we use masonry fixings that reach the solid material. Plaster is drilled slowly with sharp bits to avoid cracking the face, and we do not use plastic expansion plugs in it for a TV.

Brick or stone (+$65). Exterior walls of the prewar buildings, chimney breasts and some party walls. We drill into the brick itself, not the mortar, with anchors rated for solid masonry, and we check that the brick is sound before trusting it. Old brick that crumbles when drilled is rare, but when we find it we stop and tell you.

Concrete and block (quoted from photos). The common wall in postwar towers and rebuilt ground floors. Strong, but slow and loud to drill, and sometimes reinforced with steel we cannot drill through. We look at your photos and, where possible, at the building's age and type before quoting.

Metal studs (quoted from photos). Common in newer condo partitions and commercial fit-outs. They look exactly like wood-stud drywall until a screw spins. We use fasteners made to grip thin steel, and where the screen is large or on an arm, we spread the load across more than one stud.

Above a fireplace (+$130). Rare in Brighton Beach apartments, but some older units and houses have one. The add-on covers the extra height, the heat check and the work around the mantel.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common shortcut in TV mounting is also the one that is not allowed: pushing the TV's own power cord through a hole in the wall and out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not permit a flexible power cord to be run inside a wall that way, because a cord in a wall cavity can be crushed, cannot shed heat and is hidden from anyone who might later drill. In a co-op where the wall you are opening is also the wall your neighbor shares, that is a risk to more than one household.

Our in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on the Brooklyn list, solves it properly. It puts a recessed power inlet and a cable pass-through behind the TV and a matching inlet near the floor, joined by a code-compliant cable inside the wall, so the TV plugs into a real outlet behind the screen and the HDMI and network cables pass through a separate opening. It needs a wall we are allowed to open and a cavity that runs from the TV down to the outlet without a fire block or a masonry backing in the way, which is common in plaster on framing and rare in solid masonry or concrete. Where the wall cannot be opened, a paintable cord cover, $59, carries everything down the wall in a slim channel that can be painted to match. Where neither suits, the cables drop straight to a console below the TV and are tied neatly behind it.

A relative brought a TV with them from Europe. Can you hang it in our Brighton Beach apartment and make it work here?

Hanging it is usually no problem, because most TVs use the same standard bolt patterns on the back and a bracket does not care where the set was bought. Making it work needs two checks. First, power: the label on the back shows the voltage range, and many modern TVs accept anything from about 100 to 240 volts, so they only need the right plug; a set that only accepts 220 to 240 volts needs a proper converter, and we will not plug it in until that is sorted. Second, the tuner: a TV built for Europe will not pick up American broadcast channels through an antenna, but anything that comes in over HDMI, such as a streaming device or a cable box, works normally. For a household that watches internet TV anyway, that is usually enough.

Our living room on Brightwater Court is long and narrow, with the windows at one end. Which wall gives the best picture?

Our ground-floor apartment near the boardwalk was redone after Sandy, and the new drywall was put up over the old brick. What does that do to the bracket?

It changes it a lot, because the board in front is not what carries the weight. Drywall fixed over brick, often on thin furring strips, has a small gap behind it and then solid masonry. A handyman tried to mount a TV in our tower apartment and his drill kept stopping on metal inside the concrete wall. What happens if yours does the same?

It means the bit has reached the steel reinforcing in the concrete, and the right response is to stop. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Brighton Beach

These are claims that appear in generic online answers and national cost guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. They are not wrong everywhere. They are wrong often enough in Brighton Beach's buildings that they are worth correcting before you book anyone.

"Find a stud and screw the bracket into it"

The most repeated advice in TV mounting assumes a wall of drywall on wood studs. In a Brighton Beach co-op from 1930, the partition may be plaster on metal lath, plaster on clay tile or plaster on block, and the outer wall is masonry.

A stud finder on metal lath beeps along the whole wall. On plaster over block it finds nothing at all. Either way, the advice to "find the stud" leads people to drive screws into material that cannot carry a TV.

What works is reading the wall first: a knock, a deep scan and one small pilot hole behind the bracket plate. The dust tells you what the wall is, and the wall tells you the anchor.

"TV mounting costs the national average"

National guides quote a single typical price, and Angi's Brooklyn figure is an average of $280 with a typical range of $172 to $376. Those numbers blend every wall, size and building in the borough.

In Brighton Beach the price depends far more on the wall than on the neighborhood. A 55-inch screen on drywall over wood is $179.99 on our list; the same screen on plaster is $224.99 and on brick $244.99, before the bracket.

The useful question is not what the average job costs but what your wall is. Send a photo, and we will tell you which line on the list applies before you book.

"Just run the cords through the wall"

Many online answers suggest cutting two holes and pushing the TV's power cord through the wall between them. It looks tidy, and it is not permitted by the National Electrical Code, which does not allow flexible cords to be run inside walls that way.

In an apartment building the risk is shared. A crushed or overheated cord in a cavity sits next to your neighbor's apartment, and nobody who drills that wall later knows it is there.

The proper route is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list, or a paintable cord cover, $59, where the co-op or landlord does not allow the wall to be opened.

"Concrete is the easiest wall to mount on"

Concrete is the strongest wall a TV can hang on, and some answers stop there. They leave out that poured concrete in a postwar tower can hide steel reinforcing exactly where the bracket wants to go.

Hitting steel means stopping and moving the hole. Without a bracket designed with spare slots, that can push the screen off center or force a second set of holes that show.

We plan concrete jobs around that possibility: the position is agreed with tape, the bracket has room to adjust, and the job is quoted from photos so the price does not change when the drill does.

"A smart TV is ready to watch out of the box"

For a household that only watches the big American streaming services, that is mostly true. For a Brighton Beach household whose main channels come from a Russian-language internet service, it often is not.

The service's app may or may not be offered for your TV's platform, the old box may or may not still be needed, and the TV's menus may need changing to the language the main viewer reads.

"Renters and shareholders cannot mount TVs"

Some answers say flatly that renters cannot wall-mount a TV, and others say any shareholder can drill wherever they like. Both are too simple for Brighton Beach's co-ops, condos and rentals.

Most buildings allow a bracket and treat it as decorating, while opening a wall for power is usually an alteration that the managing agent or board must approve. A lease may say something different again.
