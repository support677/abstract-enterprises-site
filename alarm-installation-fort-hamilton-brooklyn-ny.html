The Neighborhood

Why Fort Hamilton Properties Need Alarm Installation

Start with the name, because it causes confusion. Fort Hamilton is first of all a working Army installation in the southwest corner of Brooklyn, surrounded by Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights. Wikipedia describes it as the only active-duty Department of Defense post in New York City, completed in 1831 with about half of the original fort still standing. Its job today is supporting the region’s Army Reserve and National Guard, and it houses the New York City Recruiting Battalion, the Military Entrance Processing Station and the North Atlantic Division headquarters of the Army Corps of Engineers. The name also belongs to the residential blocks next to the post. Wikipedia’s Bay Ridge article says the part of Bay Ridge south of 86th Street is sometimes considered a sub-neighborhood called Fort Hamilton, and that the area was once a village of that name, “named for the military installation at its center,” with Yellow Hook to the north.

The two meanings meet in one practical fact: a lot of the people tied to the post live outside the gate. The Military OneSource overview for the installation counts 573 active-duty members, 942 family members, 700 civilians and 880 Guard and Reserve members. The same overview lists the on-post housing: six historic residences on Colonels’ Row, 70 townhouses for officers and senior NCOs, 60 older high-rise apartments and 150 newer three- and four-bedroom apartments, run privately by Balfour Beatty Communities. That is about 286 homes. The Fort Hamilton housing and relocation site explains the choice in plain terms: live on post, or use the housing allowance to rent or buy off post, and contact the Housing Services Office the moment orders arrive. On the Army forum on Reddit, one soldier asking about an assignment here put it simply: right outside Fort Hamilton is Bay Ridge, and he would rather live off post.

Those households share a timetable most alarm companies never ask about. They arrive on a report date, they may leave for training or a deployment in the middle of a lease, and they move again on the next set of orders. A standard dealer package, with a 36-month monitoring agreement and equipment that technically belongs to the dealer, fits that life badly. Federal law has already noticed: the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act lets a servicemember end a home security services contract after receiving qualifying orders, with no early-termination charge. We would rather the contract question never come up, so the systems we install here are bought outright, the monitoring is month to month, and the hardware is chosen so it can come down and go into a moving box.

The other Fort Hamilton is the one that stays put. In 2013 Brownstoner summarized a New York Times profile that called the Fort Hamilton section of Bay Ridge a “naturally occurring retirement community”: leafy, stable and relatively affordable, its “low-slung character” protected by a 2005 rezoning. CityLand reported that the City Council approved that rezoning on March 23, 2005, covering 249 blocks of the Special Bay Ridge District. Many of these owners have lived in the same row house or the same Shore Road co-op for decades, and their alarm is often a panel that went in a generation ago, still dialing out over a phone line that may no longer be connected. For them the work is less about speed and more about making a dependable system that is simple to live with.

Crime here is lower than the city average, and we would not pretend otherwise. Wikipedia, drawing on NYPD figures, says the 68th Precinct, which covers Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, ranked seventh safest of 69 patrol areas for per-capita crime in 2010 and recorded 96 burglaries in 2018, with crime in every category down 88.6 percent between 1990 and 2018. But burglary south of 86th Street has followed familiar patterns. Brooklyn Paper’s 68th Precinct report of December 14, 2011, described a Marine Avenue apartment between Ridge Boulevard and 95th Street entered through a bedroom window the tenant found open, with about $17,000 in jewelry taken while the resident was out for roughly ten hours; the same report listed a Fourth Avenue apartment between 99th and 100th Streets burglarized during a day-long absence. The lesson is ordinary and useful: entry comes through a window, during an absence long enough to be noticed. An alarm’s job is to make that absence visible on your phone within seconds.

Many households here pair the alarm with a camera at the building door or shop entrance, so an alert arrives with a clip.

Three Places Called Fort Hamilton, and Which One This Page Covers

People reach this page searching for very different addresses, so here is how we use the name. BKMAG published a column in January 2014 titled “There’s No Neighborhood in Brooklyn Called ‘Fort Hamilton’,” in which Henry Stewart, a lifelong Bay Ridge resident, argued that the name is really a post office designation, pointing to the Fort Hamilton branch on Fifth Avenue and 88th Street, the Fort Hamilton branch library, the high school, and the subway map. In the same piece he quoted the local community board’s district manager saying that people she knew did call their neighborhood Fort Hamilton, and he admitted the south side of Bay Ridge feels like a different community from the north. Both views are fair, and both point at the same streets.

City Planning complicates it further. Its Dyker Heights/Ft. Hamilton rezoning, adopted by the City Council on July 25, 2007, used “Ft. Hamilton” for a strip east of the Gowanus Expressway, bounded by Seventh Avenue, the expressway, 86th Street and Poly Place. Wikipedia’s Bay Ridge article, the local post office and most real estate listings use the name for the blocks south of 86th Street between Shore Road and the expressway. And on the Army side, Fort Hamilton is the post itself, entered at the main gate where Fort Hamilton Parkway meets 101st Street.

Inherited Keypads, Silent Old Dialers and Shops That Refuse to Arm: Same-Day Alarm Repair South of 86th Street

Repair calls south of 86th Street fall into a few patterns. In rentals that turn over on orders, the problem is often a system nobody in the apartment set up: a keypad left by the last tenant that chirps for a low battery, a sensor knocked off a door frame by movers, or a code nobody knows. In long-held row houses and co-ops, the trouble is age: a panel from decades ago with a dead backup battery, or a dialer still trying to report over a copper phone line that was cancelled years ago, so the alarm sounds locally but tells nobody. On Fourth and Fifth Avenues it tends to be a store system that will not arm at closing because one door contact has drifted out of line.

Call (347) 934-8335 with the brand name printed on the keypad and the message it shows. Many fixes are a battery, a realigned contact or a reprogrammed code. When the panel itself is beyond saving, we say so and quote a replacement from the Brooklyn list: panel replacement runs $800 to $1,500, and existing door and window wiring is reused when it tests good. Service calls are billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, which is not charged on a new installation.

One habit prevents half of these calls in a rental: when you move in, ask us, or the landlord, to reset the master code and delete every old user code before you trust the system. A keypad you inherited may still answer to codes that previous tenants, cleaners or movers knew.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Fort Hamilton’s Architecture

Wikipedia’s history of Bay Ridge sketches how these blocks filled in: row houses after the subway was announced, a fivefold rise in apartment buildings by the 1920s, large old houses replaced by apartment houses by the Second World War, and, in the 1990s and 2000s, two-family houses torn down for condominiums known locally as “Fedder Homes,” after the air conditioners poking from their facades. Each layer needs a slightly different alarm.

Shore Road apartment houses

Six-story buildings facing Shore Road Park and the harbor, many from the 1930s and 1940s. Brownstoner describes 9437 Shore Road as an Art Moderne building from 1940; a broker’s page describes 9701 Shore Road as a 1935 Art Deco co-op with curved corners and oversized windows. Apartments are protected from inside; the corridor door is the perimeter.

Fourth Avenue and side-street apartment buildings

Walk-ups and elevator buildings along the R train corridor, many rented. Front doors open onto shared hallways, rear windows onto courts, yards or fire escapes. Wireless equipment that can be removed without damage is the norm.

Row houses and two-families

The low-rise blocks the 2005 rezoning was meant to protect. Attached on both sides, with a front door, a rear door to the yard, basement windows and often a rented floor. Brownstoner’s 2013 piece put row-house prices from $600,000 at the time.

Newer attached condo buildings

The six-to-twelve-unit buildings City Planning described replacing one- and two-family homes. Shared front doors, small garages or parking pads at grade, and individually owned units with their own alarms.

Detached and semi-detached houses east of the expressway

City Planning’s description of its Ft. Hamilton strip includes detached and semi-detached homes on lawns and well-kept row-house blocks. These get a full perimeter, with attention to side doors and yards.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Three- and four-story row buildings with shops on the ground floor and apartments above, plus the newer combinations of medical offices and residences that City Planning noted. The store and the apartments upstairs get separate systems.

Method notes for each building type

  • Prewar and wartime Shore Road buildings. Window frames in buildings of this age may be original steel casements or later replacement units set into deep masonry. Contacts go on the moving sash and the frame, never into the masonry; on casements that crank outward we use a contact positioned at the latch side so a window cracked for air reads differently from one swung open. Thick masonry walls slow wireless signals, so we test the signal from every sensor position before mounting anything permanently.
  • Rented apartments. Sensors are fixed with removable mounting strips where a landlord forbids screws, the panel can sit on a shelf or a small bracket, and the cellular communicator means no one has to drill for a phone or network cable. At move-out it all comes down in under an hour.
  • Row houses. Where the basement ceiling is open, a wired run along the joists reaches the first-floor doors and windows; upstairs rooms get wireless sensors so finished plaster stays untouched. Rear windows reachable from a yard, an extension roof or a fire escape all get contacts, front windows facing the street often do not.
  • Small condo buildings. Each owner’s unit alarm is independent. The board’s common-area alarm covers the garage door, the meter room and the rear door, with alerts to a short list of board members rather than a siren in the stairwell.
  • Mixed-use buildings. The store gets its own commercial panel and codes; the apartment upstairs gets its own system, even when the same family owns both. One system for both makes it impossible to arm the store at night while the family moves around upstairs.

Owners of row houses with a rented floor usually pair the alarm with a two-button intercom at the front door, so the tenant’s visitors never need the owner’s door code.

Built to Move on Orders: Owning the Alarm Instead of Renting It

For a household that moves on orders, the awkward part of a typical alarm deal is its structure: the dealer installs equipment at a low upfront price, recovers the cost through a monitoring agreement of three years or more, and keeps ownership of the hardware until the term ends. Our silo research found the same pattern from ordinary homeowners on Reddit, who described dealer plans around $50 a month on three-year terms with installation charges of $1,500 or more, and who called a five-year term at $42 a month a bad deal. For a family that can expect orders within that term, the structure adds a cancellation process, an equipment return and an argument about fees.

Congress addressed this directly. Section 3956 of the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act, title 50 of the U.S. Code, lists contracts a servicemember may terminate, and the list includes home security services, along with cell phone, internet and TV service. According to the statute, termination is available after the servicemember receives military orders to relocate for at least 90 days to a location that does not support the contract, or, in one specific case, after a stop-movement order. Notice goes to the provider in writing or electronically, with a copy of the orders. The provider may not impose an early-termination charge, must refund amounts paid in advance for any period after termination within 60 days (the billing period in which termination happens excepted), and, if the servicemember signs up again within 90 days after the relocation ends, may not charge a reinstatement fee beyond its usual installation or equipment charges. The servicemember must return any provider-owned equipment within 10 days after service is disconnected.

Diagram by AESS, summarizing 50 U.S. Code § 3956 as published by the Cornell Legal Information Institute. It covers home security services among other contracts; a legal assistance office can advise on a particular case.

One Adult at Home and One Overseas: Arming and Call Lists During a Deployment

A deployment changes an alarm’s job more than a move does. One adult runs the household, often with young children; the other may be several time zones away, with irregular access to a phone. The questions we work through with families at this stage are practical ones. Who gets the first call when a sensor trips at three in the morning? Who can let a plumber in if the spouse at home is at work? How does the person at home arm the house at night without setting it off every time a child gets up?

Our default answer is a short call list built around who can act, with the person overseas kept informed by app notifications rather than by phone calls from a monitoring station; the question-and-answer section below explains the order we suggest. App alerts carry the time, the zone name and, if a camera is linked, a short clip, so the person overseas can see what happened when they next have signal.

For arming at night we program a “stay” setting that protects every door and window while leaving interior motion detectors off, so the household can move around inside. A key fob on the bedside table can switch to full arming or trigger a panic alert without a trip to the keypad. If the family keeps a separate code for a babysitter or a dog walker, it can be limited to certain hours and deleted the day it is no longer needed. When the deployed spouse returns, nothing has to be rebuilt; the call list goes back to two adults.

A deployment checklist we leave in writing

  • Passwords. The monitoring station’s verbal password is known by everyone on the call list and nobody else.
  • Battery dates. Sensor batteries are replaced before departure, with the next due date written inside the panel door, so no chirping keypad appears in month five.
  • Empty rooms. If a room or a whole apartment will sit closed, a temperature and water sensor watches it, with alerts going to the adult in Brooklyn.

The Shore Road Apartment: Casements, Harbor Light and a Board That Wants Paperwork

Shore Road is the address south of 86th Street people picture first: six-story apartment houses on one side, Shore Road Park and the Belt Parkway on the other, and the Narrows and the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge beyond. Wikipedia counts these among the tallest buildings in Bay Ridge. Brownstoner’s August 2026 listing note on 9437 Shore Road describes a six-story, 55-unit co-op designed by the Cohn Brothers, opened as a rental in 1940 in the Art Moderne style, with faceted walls and parquet floors; a broker’s building page for 9701 Shore Road describes a six-story 1935 co-op with an Art Deco facade, curved corners and oversized windows. We name these only because their published descriptions show the building type. We have not claimed to work in either.

Three things make these apartments different to alarm. The windows: original steel casements crank outward, while replacement units in the same openings may slide or tilt, so each contact goes where that sash actually moves. The light: west-facing rooms over the park take strong afternoon sun, which changes where a motion detector can point. And the building: co-op boards usually ask for an alteration agreement or an insurance certificate and prohibit anything in the hallway, so our work stays inside the apartment door and the certificate goes to the managing agent before the visit. The questions further down this page cover the casement and sunlight details.

Forty Years on the Same Block: Updating the Alarms Long-Time Owners Already Have

The New York Times profile that Brownstoner summarized in 2013 described the Fort Hamilton section as a naturally occurring retirement community, a place where people grew old in the homes they bought. That shows up in the alarms we find. Many were installed in the 1980s and 1990s, wired well, and then left alone. Their panels may still be programmed to report over a landline. Their backup batteries may be years past their life. Their keypads may be readable only with glasses and a flashlight.

The good news is that old wiring in a row house or a co-op is often still sound. If the contacts on the doors and windows test good, a new panel and cellular communicator go on the old wiring, and the household keeps the same doors and the same habits, now reporting reliably. Panel replacement on the Brooklyn list runs $800 to $1,500. We relabel every zone in plain words, set up a keypad with large backlit buttons where it is used most, and add a key fob for anyone who prefers a button to a code.

We also ask a question many families skip: who should the system call now? A call list written decades ago may still name a neighbor who moved away, a relative’s old number or a landline that no longer exists. We update it with the owner and, if they wish, with an adult child, so an alarm on 92nd Street reaches someone who can respond today.

Owners who live alone often ask for a working buzzer and door release at the same visit, so they can see and speak to a caller before opening the vestibule door.

Moving Week: Keeping the Apartment Protected While Movers Come and Go

The most exposed days in a military family’s tour are often the last ones. The household goods move happens over several days; doors are propped open, boxes stand in the hallway, crews come and go, and the family may already be sleeping in temporary lodging. An alarm that is fully armed gets in the way, and one that is switched off protects nothing. We plan for that week when we install, because the right setup is easy if it is ready in advance.

  • Bypass by zone, not by system. During packing days, the entry door and the hall motion detector can be bypassed while windows and the bedroom stay armed, so the crew can work and an open window still reports.
  • Temporary codes with end dates. If a moving supervisor or a cleaner needs to arm or disarm, they receive a code set to expire on a specific date.
  • Last-night arming. Once the apartment is empty, the full system is armed with alerts to your phone, wherever you are sleeping.
  • Removal at the right time. We take the system down after the move-out inspection is scheduled, patch any screw holes in a rental, and pack the components with labels.
  • Monitoring end date. If you have a central station plan, set its end date for the day after the keys go back, not the day the movers come.

What a Fort Hamilton store alarm should include

  • A gate contact plus protection behind it. A contact tells you the gate moved; a glass-break detector or a contact on the door behind it tells you someone went further. Both together reduce false alarms from wind or a passer-by rattling the gate.
  • Coverage inside the room, not only at the openings. An intruder who breaks through from next door or drops in from above trips no door contact at all; motion detection over the floor and the stockroom is what notices them.
  • The back of the building. Rear doors to yards and alleys, basement hatches and roof access are the openings owners forget, and they are where entry happens when the front is well protected.
  • Codes for every employee. Each person gets a code, the system logs who disarmed it and when, and a departing employee’s code is deleted the same day.
  • A closing reminder. If the store is not armed by a set time, the owner gets an alert. Stores near the subway terminal that close late can set a later time than the rest of the avenue.
  • A silent hold-up button at the counter. For stores that handle cash or jewelry, a button that alerts the monitoring station without sounding in the store.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Fort Hamilton: Questions Answered

Orders, deployments and the off-post household

I just received PCS orders out of Fort Hamilton. Can I end my alarm monitoring contract without paying an early-termination fee?

In many cases, yes. 50 U.S.C. § 3956 lists home security services among the contracts a servicemember may terminate after receiving orders to relocate for at least 90 days to a place the contract does not serve. You send the provider written or electronic notice with a copy of the orders and the date service should end. The provider cannot charge an early-termination fee and must refund prepaid amounts covering time after termination within 60 days, apart from the billing period in which termination happens. Any equipment the provider owns has to go back within 10 days after disconnection. If a provider resists, the installation’s legal assistance office can help. Systems we install carry no long-term monitoring contract, so ending our partner plan is a phone call.

The Army may move us again in two or three years. Should we buy the alarm equipment outright or accept a leased system with a lower upfront price?

For a household on a two- or three-year clock, ownership is usually simpler. A leased system is paid off through monthly fees over the contract term, and the equipment may have to be returned when you leave. A purchased system costs more on day one, from $600 on our Brooklyn list for a basic wireless setup, but there is nothing to return, nothing to cancel except optional monitoring, and the sensors can come with you. Before you buy, we confirm in writing which parts are likely to work at the next home and which are not, such as a cellular communicator tied to a carrier that may not serve the new area.

We brought a wireless alarm from our last duty station. Can you reinstall it in our Fourth Avenue apartment and get it monitored again?

Often, yes. We start by identifying the panel and checking whether it is locked to a previous dealer’s account, because some panels sold through dealers cannot be moved to a new monitoring company. If it is free, we reset it, re-pair each sensor, update its firmware where the manufacturer allows, check that the cellular communicator connects in Brooklyn, and mount everything for the new layout. Labor-only installation of equipment you already own runs $200 to $400 on our Brooklyn list. If the panel turns out to be locked or obsolete, we tell you before any work is billed.

My spouse is deploying and will be eight hours ahead of us. Who should be first on the call list, and should the deployed spouse be on it at all?

Put the adult at home first and a local backup with a key second. The deployed spouse is better served by app notifications for every alarm and arming change than by being on the list a monitoring station calls in order, because an unanswered call overseas uses up time the station could spend reaching someone nearby. If the account is in the deployed member’s name, add the spouse as an authorized user before departure, so changes can be made without reaching someone overseas.

We live in family housing on the post. Can you install an alarm there?

Only with permission. On-post family housing is managed privately by Balfour Beatty Communities, according to the installation’s Military OneSource overview, and any changes to a home there are governed by your lease and their rules. Access to the post is also controlled. If the housing office approves a wireless system and you arrange access for our technician, we will install it inside the home with removable mounting. Without that approval, we can suggest self-contained equipment that you set up yourself and that leaves no mark.

Shore Road and small condo buildings

Our 1930s Shore Road apartment still has steel casement windows that crank outward. Can alarm contacts work on casements?

Yes, with placement that follows the way the window moves. A casement swings on side hinges, so the contact goes on the latch side of the sash, where the gap opens first, with the magnet on the frame. We use surface contacts with adhesive and small screws into the steel frame rather than anything drilled into the masonry, and with a co-op we confirm with the managing agent that nothing affects the window’s exterior. If you like a window open a crack for air, a second magnet position can let the window sit slightly open while armed. Corner windows on curved or faceted walls often need two contacts.

Our living room faces west over Shore Road Park and the afternoon sun floods the floor. Can sunlight set off a motion detector?

It can if the detector is aimed badly. A passive infrared detector senses changes in heat across its field of view, and a patch of floor warming quickly in strong sun can look like movement. We avoid this by mounting the detector so it looks along the room or toward the windows from the side, not down at the sunlit floor, by using models with temperature compensation, and by walk-testing in the afternoon. In a room with large windows, a glass-break detector covering the windows often does more than a motion detector would.

We own a unit in a small condo built where a two-family once stood, and all the owners share a garage at street level. Can the board alarm the garage door without waking every owner at two in the morning?

Yes. The common-area alarm is a separate system from each owner’s unit alarm. A heavy-duty contact goes on the garage door, and the alert goes by app and text to two or three board members rather than to a siren in the stairwell. A short delay can allow an owner arriving by car to close the door before an alert is sent, and the board sees a log of each opening. If the garage has a side door into the building, it gets its own contact so the board can see which door was used.

Stores on Fourth and Fifth Avenues

Our shop near the Fort Hamilton Triangle has glass on two streets. What changes for a corner store with two fronts?

Mostly coverage. A mid-block store has one glass front; a corner or wedge-shaped store has two, often with a side door on the second street. We place glass-break detectors so each run of glass is within range, add a contact for each gate and door, and aim interior motion detectors away from the windows so headlights and passers-by on two streets do not cause false alarms. If you install a camera, one view for each frontage means the clip that comes with an alert shows the side where it happened.

A Fifth Avenue jeweler’s security glass held up against sledgehammers. If we install laminated or multi-pane glass, do we still need glass-break detectors?

Security glass slows a break-in, as ABC7’s 2025 report showed, but it does not tell anyone that an attempt is happening. A glass-break or shock detector reports the first blows, even if the glass holds, so the monitoring station or your phone knows while the attempt is still in progress. Some acoustic glass-break detectors are tuned to the sound of ordinary glass shattering and may be less certain with laminated glass, so on security glass we often use shock sensors fixed to the frame or the pane, which respond to the impact itself. We test the chosen device on your glass before leaving.

Engine 242’s firehouse is near our store on Fifth Avenue. Will fire truck sirens and air horns set off a glass-break detector?

They should not with a properly chosen detector. Modern acoustic glass-break detectors usually require two signals in sequence: the low-frequency thump of something hitting glass, followed by the high-frequency sound of breaking. A siren or air horn alone does not provide that pattern. Older single-sound detectors can be fooled, and if your store has one that trips when trucks go by, replacing it is usually inexpensive. We walk-test with a glass-break simulator and check the event history after the first week.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Fort Hamilton

Our build environment could not open Google’s AI Overview, so the statements below are not quoted from it. They are claims we commonly see in generic answers about alarms, the kind repeated on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr cost pages and in automated summaries, with what we see on the ground here.

“Fort Hamilton is an Army base, so nearby homes are safe and do not need alarms.”

The post has its own gate and its own security, but that ends at the fence line. The blocks south of 86th Street are ordinary city streets patrolled by the 68th Precinct, the same as the rest of Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights. Being near a military installation does not change who walks down Fourth Avenue at night or whether an apartment window is left open.

Crime here is low; Wikipedia, citing city data, says the 68th Precinct ranked seventh safest of 69 patrol areas in 2010 and recorded 96 burglaries in 2018. That is low, but it is not zero, and Brooklyn Paper’s 2011 report of a Marine Avenue apartment entered through an open bedroom window, with about $17,000 in jewelry taken, happened south of 86th Street, not far from the gate.

“Military families should rent a security system rather than buy one, because they move so often.”

This sounds sensible but often gets it backward. A “rented” system is usually equipment paid off through a multi-year monitoring agreement, and the equipment may have to be returned. For a household that moves every two or three years, that structure produces exactly the cancellation and return process the family wanted to avoid.

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act includes home security services in its list of contracts a servicemember can end on orders. That protection helps, but it still requires notice, a copy of orders and, for provider-owned equipment, a return within 10 days.

An owned wireless system with optional month-to-month monitoring avoids the issue, as the section on moving with orders above explains.

“Any wireless alarm can be moved to a new house and reconnected.”

Many can, but not all. Some panels sold through dealers are locked to the dealer’s account and cannot be activated with another monitoring company. Cellular communicators work only where their carrier has the network type they use, and older units built for 3G networks no longer connect at all.

Sensors are usually the most portable part. Panels and communicators are where problems appear, and that is something to find out before buying, not after unpacking at the next duty station.

“Apartments on upper floors don’t need window sensors.”

It depends on what is outside the window. On the fifth or sixth floor of a Shore Road building, a window with nothing beneath it may need no contact. But a fire escape, a setback roof or the roof of a neighboring lower building can bring an upper window within reach.

CBS New York reported in 2013 that a burglar working elsewhere in the 68th Precinct entered nine homes by climbing fire escapes and going through windows. Height alone was not the protection.

We decide window by window from what is outside each one.

“Expect to pay somewhere between one and three thousand dollars, then a monthly monitoring bill.”

National ranges blend very different jobs: a two-sensor apartment kit, a fully wired house, a store with a gate and a hold-up button. They also often include equipment that is effectively paid for through a monitoring contract, which makes the upfront number look smaller than the true cost.

“Co-op buildings don’t allow alarm systems.”

Co-ops generally regulate how work is done, not whether an apartment can have an alarm. A board may require an alteration agreement, a certificate of insurance and limited work hours, and many prohibit anything in the hallway or on the facade.

An alarm installed entirely inside the apartment, with a cellular communicator instead of any building wiring, usually fits within those rules. We send the insurance certificate to the managing agent before the visit and follow the building’s contractor hours.

Anyone can set up their own kit at home. But in New York, a person in the business of installing alarm systems for others must hold a license from the Department of State, which requires at least 81 hours of approved training, an exam and fingerprinting.

Our landlord wants to be on the alarm call list for the apartment we rent. Should we agree?
+

That is your choice, and many tenants say no. A landlord on the call list will be phoned when the alarm trips and may be given details about your comings and goings. A middle path is to list the landlord only for water and temperature alerts, which protect the building, and keep burglary alerts to your own household and a backup contact.

Do the contract termination rights under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act also cover a military spouse?
+

In some situations. 50 U.S.C. § 3956 treats certain spouses and dependents as covered, including the spouse or dependent of a servicemember who dies in military service or suffers a catastrophic injury or illness, and a spouse or dependent who accompanies the servicemember after a stop-movement order. Others should ask the installation’s legal assistance office about their case.
