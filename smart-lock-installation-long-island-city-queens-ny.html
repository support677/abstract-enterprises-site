What we look at on a Long Island City door before we quote

The label on the hinge edge. Doors between an apartment and a tower corridor are commonly fire-rated assemblies, and a rated door carries a label — usually a small metal plate or an embossed mark on the hinge edge — showing its rating and listing. NFPA 80, the national standard for fire door assemblies, limits what may be done to a rated door in the field; its 2016 edition, as door-hardware consultants read it, permits holes larger than an inch for surface-applied hardware only where both the door manufacturer’s and the hardware manufacturer’s listings allow them. So before anything is drilled we photograph the label, check the lock’s own listing, and prefer hardware that uses the bore and strike already in the door. Where a model would need a new or enlarged hole that neither listing covers, we say no and suggest one that doesn’t.

Who decides about the corridor face. In a rental tower the landlord owns the door and the lease almost always restricts alterations; in a condo the owner usually controls the unit, but the declaration, bylaws or house rules often govern what can be seen from the shared hallway. Either way the answer is in writing somewhere, and we would rather read it with you than guess. A retrofit unit that works the existing deadbolt from inside changes nothing in the corridor and is often the first thing a management office will accept.

The closer and the air in the corridor. Tower unit doors are self-closing, as the city requires for doors onto interior corridors and stairs in multiple dwellings, and in a tall building the air moving through corridors and shafts can lean on a door as it closes. A door that swings shut but stops a whisker short of latching will defeat a motorised bolt: the lock throws, hits the strike and reports a jam. We watch the door close on its own a dozen times before we fit anything, and adjust the closer, the strike or both until it latches every time.

What is already electronic. Plenty of Long Island City apartments come with the building’s own keyless lock already on the unit door, run from the same app as the lobby. That lock belongs to the building. We never remove or re-program it without management’s written say-so; what we can do is explain your options alongside it, which are usually narrower than people hope and wider than management first suggests.

Leaving needs nothing but a hand. Whatever the outside looks like, the inside of a home door we fit has a plain thumb-turn or lever that works in one motion. HPD’s fire-safety guidance says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors, and on a twentieth-floor corridor in smoke nobody should be hunting for a code.

Compliance callouts for Long Island City business doors

  • Stair doors in tall buildings are not ordinary doors. In buildings 100 feet or taller, a 1992 Department of Buildings notice issued under the code of that era allows stair doors to be locked from the stair side except on re-entry floors, spaced no more than four storeys apart, and allows fail-safe locks on re-entry floors that release when the fire alarm operates. The current code and the building’s fire safety plan govern your floor; we confirm with the building before touching any stair door.
  • Leaving is never conditional. Anyone inside an office, studio or shop walks out by pressing a bar, turning a handle or hitting an exit button. Credentials are asked only of people coming in, and powered hardware keeps a manual release for the day the supply dies.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure is a decision, not a default. A fail-safe strike or magnetic lock lets the door open when power fails; a fail-secure strike stays locked and relies on the key. Doors on an escape route and stock-room doors usually want opposite answers, and we write the choice for each door on the drawing.
  • The frame is the landlord’s. A strike pocket is cut into metal the landlord owns, in a storefront or a suite entrance alike, so written consent comes before any cutting, and we choose hardware that can come out cleanly when the lease ends.
  • Residents’ doors follow housing law. Where offices or shops share a building with homes, the residential entrance answers to housing rules, and an owner who hands tenants fobs or app access must follow Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act. Business credentials belong on business doors, preferably on a separate platform.

A suite with two doors does fine on stand-alone smart locks. Living in a tower

The fire notice on our tower apartment door says to close the door without locking it if we escape a fire — what should auto-lock do then?

The notice is the FDNY fire safety notice that buildings post, and the instruction you are reading is for a fire inside your own apartment: get everyone out, close the door to the room that is burning, take your keys, and “close, but do not lock, the apartment door.” Closing the door holds smoke and fire inside the unit and out of the corridor and stairs everyone else will use. Leaving it unlocked saves firefighters from forcing it. A smart lock set to bolt itself thirty seconds after the door shuts will undo the second half of that instruction without anyone noticing.

There are three sensible ways to handle it, and the household should pick one deliberately. One is to leave auto-lock off and lock the door by habit, which keeps the notice’s advice intact and loses some convenience. Another is to keep auto-lock but give it a longer delay and teach everyone that, in a fire, the door gets closed and the lock gets thumbed back open from the hall with a code or the key on the way out — workable for adults, unrealistic with small children in the dark. The third is to keep auto-lock and accept that a locked door may be forced; that is a decision some people make, but it should be made, not discovered.

The same notice tells residents of fireproof buildings whose apartment is not on fire to stay inside unless conditions become dangerous, and to keep smoke out with wet towels at the door. That is another reason the inside of every door we fit opens with one turn of the hand and never asks for a code: the people inside must be able to make the call to leave instantly. Take your mechanical key or your phone with you either way — the notice says to take your keys for a reason.

Can we ask our building what its entry app keeps about us, and for how long?

Yes, and in a multiple dwelling the city has already set limits on the answer. Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, applies to owners of multiple dwellings who use smart access systems — fobs, key cards, apps, biometrics — to control entry to the building, its common areas or the apartments. It requires express consent before the owner collects reference data such as a face template, limits the data the owner may gather, bars selling it, requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless it has been anonymised, and requires robust security for whatever is kept. It also gives tenants a private right of action.

So a written question to management is reasonable: which system runs the doors, what it records each time you badge or tap in, who at the company and the vendor can see it, how long it is held, and whether there is a non-app way into your own apartment. The law separately says an owner may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to get into their dwelling unit, so the last question has a definite answer. Keep the reply; it is useful if the building changes vendors later.

Your own smart lock is a different matter. The Tenant Data Privacy Act governs owners, not tenants, and a lock you install on your own door keeps its records in your account with the lock maker. Read that company’s privacy terms before you choose one, turn off any feature you do not need, and remember that the lobby system will still record you on the way up, whatever your door does.

Standing in the corridor, our phones take ages to connect to the lock through the steel door — what’s going on?

Usually radio, not the lock. Most smart deadbolts talk to phones over Bluetooth, which is short-range and weakened by metal and concrete — and a tower corridor is mostly both. A steel or steel-skinned unit door between your phone and the lock’s radio, which sits in the interior housing on the room side, is about the worst arrangement possible. Add dozens of neighbours’ devices on every floor and the handshake that takes a second on a wooden house door can take ten.

The fixes are simple once the cause is clear. For everyday entry, use the keypad or a fingerprint reader on the outside face; neither needs the phone at all. If you like phone unlocking, choose a lock whose outside unit carries its own radio or supports tap-to-unlock at the outside face, and test it in the corridor before you commit. For remote features, a Wi-Fi bridge on the room side of the door does the long-distance work; August, for one, asks for its bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock, which in most tower layouts means an outlet in the entry hall.

What we avoid is mounting extra radio equipment on the corridor side of a rated door or in the hallway itself, which management will usually refuse and which adds nothing a keypad does not already do. We test the connection standing where you will stand, before anyone leaves.

When stolen keys opened a Court Square condo in 2025

QNS reported in June 2025 on a robbery pattern in the 108th Precinct. On the evening of Saturday, May 31, armed men robbed a man on Crane Street in the Court Square section, taking his phone and his keys. According to the report, they then went to a condo building at the corner of Jackson Avenue, “entered the residence of their second victim using the stolen keys” and took two French bulldogs. The lesson for a tower resident is about what a key means once it leaves your hand. A mechanical key works for whoever holds it, for as long as the cylinder stays the same, and re-keying takes a locksmith visit. A code can be deleted from a phone in the back of a taxi; a phone credential can be removed by a second administrator; a fob can be cancelled by management in minutes. When residents use codes or fingerprints day to day, the mechanical key can stay in a drawer at home or with someone you trust, rather than on a ring that can be taken with your wallet.

If your keys are ever taken, call the building first so the fob on that ring is cancelled, then deal with your own door: remove any phone access for the stolen handset, change your codes, and have the cylinder in your smart lock re-keyed or replaced. Make sure two people can do the first three steps, so it does not wait for one person to get home.

Offices, studios and lofts

Our company takes a whole floor in a Court Square office tower — can the stair door on our floor get a keypad on the stair side?

Possibly, but it is the building’s decision before it is yours, and the answer depends on which floor you are on. In tall buildings, stairs are part of the escape route and also the way firefighters move through the building, so the codes treat stair doors differently from office doors. A 1992 Department of Buildings notice, written under the code then in force for buildings of 100 feet or more, allowed stair doors to be locked on the stair side except on re-entry floors no more than four storeys apart, required re-entry floors to open from the stair side without a key, and allowed a fail-safe lock there only if it released when the fire alarm operated. Signs on the stair side tell people which floors they can re-enter.

The code has been rewritten since, and the building’s fire safety plan and its fire safety director are the authority on your floor today. Ask them three things: whether your floor is a designated re-entry floor; whether the stair doors are already on the building’s fire-alarm release; and whether a tenant is allowed to add anything at all to a stair door. In many towers the answer to the last question is no, and the right move is to secure the office’s own suite door instead.

Never fit a stair-side keypad that keeps people in the stair from getting back onto a re-entry floor, and never let a tenant’s lock override the building’s alarm release. We will fit hardware on a stair door only to a plan the building has approved in writing.

Our studio is one of many in a converted factory, and the landlord’s master key opens every door — can a keypad lock still take that key?

Often, yes, and it is the arrangement most loft landlords will accept. Many smart deadbolts and keypad levers are built around a replaceable key cylinder, and some accept interchangeable cores or standard cylinders that a locksmith can pin to an existing master-key system. Your studio then keeps its keypad and its code list, while the landlord’s master key — and the fire department’s access, if the building keeps keys for that — still turns the cylinder.

The practical steps are to ask the landlord which keying system the building uses and who maintains it, then choose a lock whose cylinder that locksmith can work with, and have the cylinder pinned before installation day. If the building uses a high-security or restricted keyway, the landlord’s locksmith may need to supply the cylinder; we fit the lock around it. If the lease says nothing and the landlord does not care, a stand-alone cylinder is fine, but leave the landlord a key or a code as the lease requires.

Two cautions. Taking a studio off the master system without telling anyone leaves the landlord unable to reach a leaking pipe or a fire; that is the kind of thing that turns a lock into a lease dispute. And a master key opens your door without leaving any trace in the lock’s app on most models — a keypad gives you a record of codes used, not of keys.

Most of those Long Island City entries did not involve defeating a lock; the doors were simply unlocked. That is the problem a lock that relocks itself solves best. A back door with a keypad lever or a strike that latches every time it closes, and a door-position contact that reports when it is propped, closes the gap a busy office leaves without meaning to.

The inside half of the job matters just as much: a suite door that locks behind the last person out, a schedule that unlocks the front only during reception hours, and a nightly check on the manager’s phone that every door reports closed. None of that needs a full access control system for a small office.

Older Long Island City

Hunters Point’s original doors and the Landmarks rules

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s designation report for the Hunters Point Historic District describes the houses at 21-21 to 21-29 45th Avenue as built of Westchester stone throughout, with round-headed doorways and double doors whose woodwork was then largely original; on the south side it noted that all the doors from 21-14 to 21-20 were original. Doors like those are the reason the district exists, and the reason we treat a smart lock there as a job for the inside of the door first.

The Commission’s rules on health, safety and utility equipment — the closest category to an exterior keypad — say light fixtures and intercom panels must sit within or next to the door opening, allow at most two light fixtures and one intercom panel per opening, prefer secondary facades, and limit visible conduit to about ten inches painted to match. Its permits page says ordinary repairs need no permit and that interior work needs one only when it requires a Buildings permit, affects the exterior, or sits in a designated interior landmark.

In practice that points to a lock whose outside face is small and quiet, mounted through the existing bore, with any new device kept off the stoop face. If the house already has an intercom panel at the doorway, a new keypad box beside it is exactly the kind of addition to ask the Commission’s staff about first; we provide photographs and product sheets for that conversation.

For locks, the waterfront lesson is about the ground floor. Battery smart deadbolts upstairs carry on through an outage. Lobby readers, door controllers, power supplies and the electric strikes on street doors are another matter: salt water and electronics do not mix, and a controller in a basement closet can be lost in an hour. On ground-floor and street doors near the river we keep controllers and batteries above the likely water line, choose strikes deliberately as fail-safe or fail-secure, and leave a mechanical way in that works when everything electrical is dark.

Residents of ground-floor and duplex units near the water should also keep their smart lock’s batteries fresh before storm season and know where the physical key is. A storm is the wrong time to discover that the only key went to a former roommate.

Over the rooftops on 47th Avenue

CBS New York reported in November 2013 that police were hunting a serial burglar in Long Island City who had struck five times since mid-October, most recently at 30-30 47th Avenue, and who, police said, got in from adjoining rooftops before taking jewellery and watches. It is an old report, but the pattern it describes — low buildings in a continuous row, roofs at similar heights, doors and hatches that lead down into homes — still fits plenty of the older blocks between the rail yards and the towers.

A smart lock on the front door does nothing about a roof hatch or a door from a roof landing into a top-floor apartment. Those doors need their own plan, and in a multiple dwelling the rules on roof doors and egress come first; a roof door that people may need to use to escape cannot simply be padlocked. What we can do is fit the top-floor apartment door with a deadbolt that is always thrown when the household is out, and a contact that reports when the roof-side door opens.

If your building’s roof is shared, raise it with the owner or managing agent; the street door is not the only way into a row.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Long Island City

A note on method: the research tools behind this page could not open Google’s AI Overview, so nothing here is quoted from it. Each heading below is a claim that turns up again and again in lock makers’ marketing, DIY videos, locksmith pages and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr — the material answer engines summarise — set against what we see on Long Island City doors.

“Any smart deadbolt fits any front door in about thirty minutes”

Retail how-to videos say a smart deadbolt goes in “in under 30 minutes with just a screwdriver,” and on a standard residential door with a standard bore that is fair. Plenty of Long Island City doors are not standard. Tower unit doors are often rated fire doors with their own hardware preparations, office suites use mortise locks or electrified hardware, and loft doors can be heavy steel in frames that have moved.

On a rated door, the national standard for fire door assemblies limits field preparation to what the door and hardware listings allow, so the quick job becomes reading a label and a listing before choosing a model at all.

The installation itself can still be quick. Getting to the point where it is allowed to be quick — the right model, management’s agreement, the closer adjusted so the door latches — is where the time goes.

“Renters can swap in a smart lock without drilling and nobody will know”

Social videos promote cylinder-swap and retrofit locks that need “no drilling, no wiring,” pitched at apartments. Retrofit units genuinely leave the corridor face unchanged, and they are often the right answer in a rental tower.

But “nobody will know” is the wrong frame in a building where management issues the keys, logs contractors at the desk and may already run an electronic lock on your door. Leases in new buildings commonly restrict alterations, and removing or bypassing the building’s own lock can breach the lease even if nothing is drilled.

Ask first, in writing. Most managements say yes to a retrofit on the room side; a surprising number say yes to more once they see exactly what is proposed.

“A smart lock makes your apartment secure”

It makes one door better at being locked and better at recording who opened it. Long Island City’s own reports show what else matters: the Court Square robbers in 2025 used stolen keys, the 2013 burglar came over the rooftops, and the 2026 office entries were through doors that were simply left unlocked.

Two of those three were problems a smart lock helps with — a cancellable credential, a door that relocks itself — and one was not. A roof hatch or a window is not protected by anything on the front door.

Treat the lock as one layer. The building’s entrance, your habits with keys and phones, and the other ways into your home are the rest.

“Auto-lock is always the safer setting”

Auto-lock is sold as the feature that makes forgetting impossible, and for an office back door that is exactly what you want. For a tower apartment, the FDNY’s posted fire safety notice tells residents escaping a fire in their own unit to close the door but not lock it, and to take their keys.

A short auto-lock delay quietly reverses that instruction. Nobody will think about it until the night it matters.

The safer setting is the one the household has chosen knowingly: auto-lock off with a locking habit, or auto-lock on with everyone understanding what it means for firefighters. We set it up whichever way you decide and write the choice on the handover sheet.

“Your smart lock will work with your building’s app”

Marketing for building platforms and for consumer locks both talk about integration, and some combinations do work together. Many do not: a building’s entry platform is configured by the owner and its vendor, and whether a resident’s own lock can join it is their decision, not yours.

In practice most Long Island City residents live with two systems — the building’s app or fob for the lobby, elevator and amenities, and their own lock’s app or keypad for the apartment.

That is less elegant than one app and much less fragile. When the building changes vendors, which happens, your own door keeps working.

“Historic homes can’t have smart locks”

Some articles warn owners in landmark districts away from smart locks altogether. In the Hunters Point Historic District the Commission’s concern is the exterior: its permits page says ordinary repairs need no permit and interior work needs one only if it requires a Buildings permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark.

A lock that changes the inside of an original door and uses the existing bore outside is a very different proposition from a new keypad box beside a stoop.

The sensible order is to plan the quietest possible outside face, then ask the Commission’s staff whether anything you are changing needs their review. We supply photographs and product sheets for that conversation.

“Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400”

Fixr’s January 2025 national figure is $250 to $400, averaging $300, for one Wi-Fi lock and an accessory, and a locksmith advertising from 29th Street quotes $275 to $500. Those are third-party numbers for a single residential door, not AESS prices.

We leave a one-page sheet listing the model, the label details, each credential and its end date, the auto-lock choice, and how to remove a person, plus a copy for management if they asked for one.

For New Residents

A First-Month Checklist for a Long Island City Tower Apartment

Tower moves happen to a timetable the building sets. One r/longislandcity review noted that a large building would not release keys until the day the lease began; others mention elevator bookings and contractor rules. This is the list we work through with new residents in their first few weeks, so the lock decision fits around everything else.

  • Find out what is already on the door. Is the unit lock the building’s electronic one, a plain deadbolt, or both? Who holds keys or override codes today?
  • Read the lease or house rules on alterations. Note exactly what they say about locks, hardware and the corridor face of the door, and who must approve a change.
  • Photograph the hinge edge. The fire label, if there is one, decides which locks are even possible.
  • Ask management three questions in writing. What the entry system records about you and for how long; how a cleaner, carer or visiting parent is admitted; and whether a retrofit or a bore-reusing smart deadbolt would be approved.
  • Decide on auto-lock before installation day. Read the FDNY fire safety notice on your door and agree as a household how the lock should behave.
  • Choose two administrators. Two phones, two people, so a lost phone or a business trip never leaves the door unmanaged.
  • Agree what happens at lease end. Whether the building’s original hardware goes back on, who keeps the smart lock, and that everything is reset and every user removed before you hand back the keys.

If you are moving into a condo you have bought rather than rented, add one more line: ask the board or managing agent for the building’s written rules on unit-door hardware, and keep a copy with your closing papers.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Long Island City Doors

How we approach a door in a managed building — written as our method, not as a story about a particular customer

The first look is not at the lock but at the hinge edge. If there is a label, we photograph it before anything else happens, because it decides which locks are on the table. Then we close the door from wide open and let the closer do the work, ten or twelve times, watching whether the latch drops into the strike every time or hangs a hair short.

Next comes the list of what is already electronic: the building’s lock on the door, if any; the fob that opens the lobby; the app that runs the package room. We write down which of those the resident controls, which management controls, and who to call when each one fails.

Only then do we talk about hardware, and usually the conversation is short. On a rated tower door the choice narrows to a retrofit unit or a model that uses the existing preparation; in a loft it narrows to locks that take the landlord’s cylinder; on an old row-house door it narrows to whatever leaves the outside quiet.

Last, we ask the question nobody expects: what should this door do if there is a fire in the apartment? The answer goes on the handover sheet, beside the list of people who can open it.

Our Hunters Point house is in the historic district — do we need the Landmarks Commission for a lock that changes only the inside of the door?
+

The Commission's permits page says interior work needs its permit only when the work requires a Department of Buildings permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark, and that ordinary repairs need no permit. A lock that alters only the inside of the door usually falls outside all three, but anything visible from the street can bring it back in, so ask the Commission's staff to confirm before work starts.
