The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Most Pelham Bay buildings are houses that were finished long before anyone ran a network cable, and most of them now hold more connected devices than a small office did twenty years ago. NeighborhoodScout reports that 57.0 percent of the residential real estate here was built in 1939 or earlier, a greater concentration of older homes than in 95.9 percent of American neighborhoods. City Planning's study for the 2006 rezoning found that detached homes make up roughly 72 percent of the housing stock and semi-detached homes about 17 percent, with attached houses at 6.6 percent and multifamily buildings at 4.5 percent of residential lots. ReinventNY describes the same picture from the street: detached and semi-detached one-family homes, brick two-family houses and modest postwar apartment buildings, mostly three to six stories, with Tudor-influenced and Colonial Revival details on streets such as Wilkinson, Hobart and Crosby Avenues. For a cabling crew, that means the everyday job here is not an office floor. It is a cellar, two or three floors of a house, plaster or drywall over wood framing, and very often two households who each want their own connection.

The houses came with the train. CityNeighborhoods.nyc writes that the land was part of the farm estates of Pelham Manor and that residential development was minimal until the area was annexed to the city. Wikipedia records that the IRT Pelham Line was completed to the Pelham Bay Park station on December 20, 1920, which became the line's terminal, and The Neighborhoods newsletter ties the neighborhood's growth to that extension. Despite the name, Pelham Bay was never part of the old Town of Pelham. The building boom that followed produced the brick and frame houses that still line the side streets, and each of them has absorbed every generation of communication wiring since: a telephone pair stapled along the cellar joists, a coax splitter from the first cable franchise, a phone jack in the kitchen that no one has used in years, and finally a provider's modem placed wherever the installer found an outlet near the point where the line came in. In a lot of Pelham Bay houses that point is a front room, a back bedroom or the cellar, and the Wi-Fi has been fighting brick and plaster from there ever since.

The bigger buildings sit at the edges by design. The rezoning, which the City Council adopted on March 22, 2006, mapped most of the inner blocks to R5A, a district that allows only one- and two-family detached homes, and kept the R6 and R7-1 districts along Westchester, Crosby and Buhre Avenues. The Neighborhoods notes that zoning has kept multi-story buildings mainly at the neighborhood's edges. That is where the co-ops are. Listings describe 1870 Pelham Parkway South as a 57-unit elevator building from 1960, 1910 Pelham Parkway South as a six-story, 52-unit elevator co-op from 1964, and 1874 Pelham Parkway South as a six-story, 95-unit building from 1970. Pelham Bay Towers at 3121 Middletown Road is described as a 12-story building completed in 1963 with 157 units and two elevators, and there is a condominium at 1725 Edison Avenue. Buildings of that era usually have concrete floors and masonry or block walls, which leave no hollow path between floors, and they come with a board, a managing agent and an alteration process that has to approve every hole.

The provider picture explains why so many calls start with the same complaint. A Bronx internet guide from BuildingHealthX says parts of Pelham Bay, along with Riverdale, Throggs Neck and Morris Park, have the densest installed Fios base in the borough, but it also warns that a provider's address checker can show fiber available for buildings where fiber reached the basement and was never pulled up the risers. The same guide says the landlord or building management has to grant Verizon access to install the conduit, risers and in-unit boxes inside a building. Verizon itself lists Pelham Bay among the Bronx neighborhoods where Fios is widely available. HighSpeedInternet.com lists Optimum fiber plans in the Bronx up to 8,000 Mbps and describes Fios running down the east side of the borough through Pelham Bay and Edgewater Park. ISPReports gives Verizon fiber 99.9 percent availability in Middletown-Pelham Bay, yet one resident review on the same site complains that 5G home internet was not an option because of very poor cell signal at home, and that Verizon began running Fios to the neighborhood and then stopped. All of those statements can be true at once. The service is often at the curb or in the cellar. What is missing is the cable from there to where people actually use it.

The neighborhood is also adding buildings that need cabling planned from the start. The Bronx Times reported permits for an eight-story residential building with 36 units at 1609 Crosby Avenue and a four-story building with 13 residences on a vacant lot at 1536 Gillespie Avenue. Marketproof summarized a filing dated March 27, 2026 for a ten-story, 83-unit building at 2925 Westchester Avenue that would replace a low-rise built in 1932. Norwood News reported applications in 2021 for a 17-story residential building at 3250 Westchester Avenue, and New York YIMBY has reported a nine-story mixed-use building at 2135 Colonial Avenue. A new building at 3217 Westchester Avenue, steps from the Pelham Bay Park station, listed security cameras, intercommunication devices and an elevator among its amenities when its 11-unit lottery opened in January 2026. Each of those needs a telecom room, a riser, a drop to every unit and dedicated cable for the building's own systems, and every one of them is far cheaper to plan on drawings than to fix after the walls close.

Fiber in the Cellar, Nothing Upstairs: Closing the Riser Gap in Pelham Bay's Postwar Co-ops

The co-ops along Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road are where the gap between "available" and "installed" shows most clearly. A provider can bring a fiber cable to the cellar, mount its equipment on a basement wall, and correctly report the building as served, while the shareholders on the fourth floor still have no way to receive that service unless a riser carries it up to them. The BuildingHealthX guide describes exactly this pattern in the Bronx, and it adds that inside an apartment building the provider needs management's permission to install conduit, risers and in-unit boxes. Why a 1960s building is harder than its age suggests

People sometimes assume a postwar building will be easier to cable than a prewar one. In Pelham Bay's co-ops, the opposite is often true. Buildings of this era were usually built with concrete floor slabs and block or masonry walls between apartments and around stairs, which means there is no hollow cavity to fish a cable through from one floor to the next. Each crossing is either an existing sleeve or shaft, or a new hole cored through concrete. Coring a slab is not a casual decision: it needs the board's approval, sometimes an engineer's review, a check for anything embedded in the slab, and a listed firestop seal afterward. So the first question in a co-op is always the same: what vertical paths already exist? Telephone closets stacked floor over floor, a sleeved riser that once carried the building's phone pairs, a utility chase beside the stair, or a shaft the building no longer uses. If one of those exists and has room, the job is mostly horizontal work along corridor ceilings. If none does, the board has a decision to make, and we give it the options in writing.

What the provider will do, and what it usually won't

When a provider wires an apartment building, it builds what it needs for its own service: a cable into the cellar, its equipment, and possibly a riser and boxes in units that sign up. That work is valuable, but it belongs to the provider's system and is designed around its own equipment. It does not cable the management office, the laundry room, the lobby cameras or the boiler controller, and it does not leave the building a documented plant that another provider could use. A building-owned riser is a different asset. It is designed for the building, owned by the building, labeled and recorded for the building, and any provider the board admits can use it to reach apartments. We are happy to coordinate with a provider's installers in the same building; we simply recommend that the board decide in advance which pathways the building keeps for itself.

Corridors, ceilings and the one route residents see every day

In a six-story co-op, the horizontal runs along each corridor often add up to more cable than the riser itself. Where a corridor has a dropped ceiling, cable rides on supports above it and drops into each apartment over its entry door. Where the corridor has a plaster or concrete ceiling, the options are a painted surface raceway tucked into the corner where wall meets ceiling, or a run inside an existing soffit if one exists. Raceway is the route residents see every day, so we show the board a sample length and its exact path on one floor before committing the building to it. Inside each apartment, the cable usually enters over the entry door or through the hall closet and ends at one jack where the shareholder's router will sit, with the shareholder free to extend from there with their own contractor if the board allows.

The paper a board will want before anything is drilled

Co-op boards are right to ask for documentation. A useful package contains a floor-by-floor description of every penetration and how it will be sealed, the route of the riser and the corridor runs, the location and size of any new cabinet, the hours the crew will work and how residents will be notified, the insurance certificate the managing agent requires, and a statement of who will own the finished cable. We prepare that package as part of the drop plan, so the board reviews one document instead of a series of emails.

Two-Family Houses

Two Households, One Cellar: Cabling Pelham Bay's Brick Two- and Three-Family Houses

The brick two-family is the signature Pelham Bay property, and its listings read almost the same from block to block. Homes.com shows a Pilgrim Avenue two-family with two three-bedroom, one-bath apartments over a finished walk-out basement, and a Mayflower Avenue building with five apartments. Another listing describes a detached three-family brick home on an oversized 50 by 100 foot lot at 1738 Hobart Avenue, and another a brick semi-detached duplex on the Hutchinson River Parkway. For cabling, these houses share a layout that matters more than their facades: one cellar where every utility enters, two or three households stacked above it, and often a finished basement that a previous owner closed up without thinking about cable.

Start where the providers come in

In a two-family it is common to find two providers, one per household, entering at different points: one through the front wall near the meter bank, one through the rear near the yard. Before we design anything, we find each entry, follow each provider's cable to its modem, and photograph what we find. That tells us where a shared cabinet could sit within reach of both entries, or whether each household's equipment should stay near its own entry. Moving where a provider enters the house is the provider's call, not ours, but placing the household equipment sensibly after that entry is ours, and it usually solves the problem of a modem on a windowsill.

One cabinet with two networks, or two cabinets

There is no single right answer. A lockable wall cabinet in the cellar can hold both households' equipment on separate shelves with separate patch panels, so each household's cable lands in its own section and can never be patched into the other's by accident. That works well when the owner lives in the house and holds the key. Where the upstairs apartment is rented and the owner would rather not be called every time the tenant's router needs a restart, the tenant's equipment can live inside the tenant's own apartment, with only the cable from the provider entry passing through the cellar to reach it. Either way, each household has its own provider, its own router and its own private network, and neither can see the other's devices.

Getting from the cellar to the second-floor apartment

The vertical route in a Pelham Bay two-family usually exists, but it has to be found. Interior partitions in houses of this age are often wood-framed, and a cable can sometimes be fished from the cellar ceiling up an interior wall into a first-floor room, then on to the floor above through a closet stacked directly over another closet. Where the house was built with masonry partitions or the closets do not line up, a run beside existing plumbing or heating pipes, or a short length of painted raceway inside a closet, may be cleaner. The owner sees every proposed opening on paper first. Where cable passes between floors through framing, the gap around it is sealed so the house does not gain a new open path between stories.

The finished basement: tiles, soffits and the boiler room

Finished basements are the most common surprise in Pelham Bay houses. A previous owner may have closed the ceiling with drywall, boxed in pipes with soffits and built a partition around the boiler and meters. We look for the existing access points first: the boiler room, which is rarely finished, the soffits, which are hollow by nature, and any dropped-tile section. If the plan needs a new opening in a finished ceiling, it is small, marked on the plan and placed where patching will be easiest. We never run data cable along a flue or across a boiler, and we keep it well clear of gas piping.

When three households share a house

A three-family, like the detached house listed on Hobart Avenue, follows the same logic with one more route. Each apartment gets its own run from the provider entry or the shared cabinet, and the plan shows clearly which cable serves which unit. The cellar cabinet, if there is one, stays in a space the owner controls so the owner never needs a tenant's permission to reach it. Spare runs toward the front and back walls are usually worth adding while the cellar is open, because cameras and an entry panel tend to follow within a few years.

A new building on an old lot: do not trust what was there

When a ten-story building replaces a low-rise built in 1932, as the Westchester Avenue filing describes, the old building's service connections tell you little about what the new one needs. The old building may have had a single telephone cable and a coax drop entering wherever was convenient in 1932 or later. The new one needs a planned service entrance with spare conduit to the street, a telecom room close to it, a riser with spare sleeves at every floor, and a path to the roof. We start from the new drawings and the provider's requirements, not from whatever is found in the demolition.

Next to the expressway and the el: where the entrance goes

A building beside a major road or an elevated line has one frontage where digging, conduit and outside equipment are hardest. The entrance for providers usually belongs on the quieter side of the lot, where a sidewalk can be opened with the fewest complications and where the conduit is least likely to be disturbed by future roadwork. For a large building we also recommend a second, separate entrance pathway, so a second provider, or the first provider's backup route, can come in without sharing the same hole in the foundation. The architect and civil engineer decide the final locations; our job is to make sure the drawings show them.

Stores downstairs, apartments upstairs

Mixed-use buildings such as the one reported on Colonial Avenue put commercial tenants and residents in the same envelope with very different needs. We plan the commercial spaces with their own pathway from the telecom room, their own space for equipment, and conduit stubbed into each shell so a future tenant can be cabled without opening finished residential corridors. The residential riser stays residential. Neither side should ever need a key to the other's room.

The building's own devices, on their own wire

The lottery listing for 3217 Westchester Avenue names cameras and intercommunication devices among the amenities, and almost every new building now includes something similar: an entry panel, cameras in the lobby and halls, an elevator interface, a laundry room system, sometimes a package locker. Those belong on a separate switch in a locked room, on battery backup, with their own internet service, so that a tenant's provider change or an unpaid bill never touches the front door. We draw them on the same plan as the apartment drops, with their own ports and labels.

What to set in concrete before the pour

Spare pathway is cheap while the building is being formed and expensive afterward. On every new building we propose spare sleeves at each riser floor, an empty conduit from the telecom room to the roof, another to the street entrance, and pull strings left wherever a future cable is likely. The concrete crew sets them from our marked drawings; the general contractor and architect approve the locations.

Detached houses: the cellar ceiling does most of the work

In a detached Pelham Bay house, the cellar ceiling is the main road. From a cabinet near the provider entry, cable travels along the joists to a point under each room that needs a jack, then up through the bottom plate of an interior wall into a box at a sensible height. Exterior walls are harder in older houses because they may have bracing, insulation added later, or masonry, so we favor interior walls where furniture already stands. Upper floors are reached through a closet stacked over a closet, through the wall behind a stair, or through an attic or knee-wall space if the house has one and it can be reached safely. The plan lists every room, the wall each jack goes on, and how the cable gets there, so the owner can move a jack on paper before anyone drills.

Semi-detached pairs: work from the free side

A semi-detached house gives us three walls that belong entirely to the owner and one that is shared. We plan every route on the owner's side of the building, keep penetrations well away from the party wall, and avoid fastening anything to it. In the cellar, where the party wall usually continues as a foundation wall, the cabinet goes on a wall that is the owner's alone. If the provider's cable happens to enter near the shared wall, we pick it up there and carry it to a better location on the owner's side.

Two- and three-family houses: whose space is whose

The technical work in a two-family is not difficult; the planning around people is what matters. Every route is checked against who controls the space it passes through. Shared cellars are fine if the owner controls them. A finished basement rented as an apartment, or a cellar area included in a tenant's lease, is not a place for the owner's cabinet or for another household's cable. We note on the plan which household each run serves and which spaces it crosses, so there is never a question later about why a tenant's connection depends on a room they cannot enter.

Postwar walk-ups: small buildings, real constraints

A seven-unit walk-up like the one listed on Crosby Avenue is close in size to a large house but has the rules of an apartment building: one stair everyone uses, apartments occupied on every floor, and a cellar that serves the whole building. Brick and plaster partitions leave little room to fish, so the vertical route is usually a stacked set of closets, a disused chase, or a painted raceway in an inside corner of the stair. We show the owner the exact route on each landing before committing to it, and we seal each floor crossing the same day the cable goes through.

The 1960s co-ops: concrete changes the conversation

The elevator co-ops at the neighborhood's edges are the buildings where a cabling plan is most likely to need an engineer's eye and a board vote. Concrete slabs are strong and quiet, but they hide whatever was embedded in them, and coring through one should never be guesswork. Our first step is to map every existing vertical route, including old telephone closets, sleeves and shafts, and to measure how much room remains in each. Only if nothing usable exists do we propose new cores, and then each one is located, described and sealed on paper before the board is asked to approve it. The elevator hoistway is never a route for our cable; it belongs to the elevator system and its contractor.

Avenue buildings: two worlds stacked

The older two- and three-story buildings on Westchester Avenue, like the 1927 building listed at 3211, put a store's working network under a few apartments. The store's equipment belongs in the store's own space, ideally in a small locked cabinet away from the counter and any heat. The apartments' cable belongs on its own route. Where both share a cellar, we agree with the owner in writing which spaces each party may use before quoting.

Field Notes: The First Ten Minutes in a Pelham Bay Cellar

We don't publish stories about particular customers' houses unless we can document them, so this is a routine rather than an anecdote. It is how a technician starts the walk-through of a brick two-family or a detached house in Pelham Bay before anyone talks about jacks.

Fiber, providers and what is inside the building

The Fios availability map lists our Pelham Parkway South co-op as covered, yet none of our neighbors has fiber. How can both be true?

Availability maps usually describe whether a provider's network reaches the address, not whether it reaches each apartment. The BuildingHealthX guide to Bronx providers warns that a checker can show fiber available for buildings where the cable reached the basement but was never pulled up the risers, and that management has to grant access for the provider to install conduit, risers and in-unit boxes. So the first step is to look in the cellar: is there provider equipment there, and does anything leave it heading upstairs? If fiber is in the basement and nothing goes up, the gap is inside the building. The board can either negotiate an access agreement for the provider to build its own riser, or build a building-owned riser that any admitted provider can use. We can map what exists and price the second option so the board can compare.

Our provider's multi-gig router has one fast port and three ordinary ones. What decides whether the rooms upstairs actually see the speed we pay for?

Several links in a chain, and the slowest one wins. HighSpeedInternet.com lists Optimum plans in the Bronx up to 8,000 Mbps, but a plan that fast only reaches a room if the router port, the switch after it, the cable to the room, the jack and the device's own network port all support that speed. Many provider routers have one multi-gigabit port and several ordinary gigabit ports, and many laptops and televisions have gigabit ports at most. A good plan puts a switch with multi-gigabit ports on the router's fast port, runs properly certified cable to the rooms that can use it, and leaves ordinary gigabit for the rooms that cannot. We check the provider's equipment and the devices before recommending anything, so you do not pay for speed a television can never use.

Our co-op's lobby camera recorder, laundry machines and management office each sit on their own small internet account. Can they share one building network?

Usually, and it often makes things simpler. Separate accounts tend to appear one at a time as each vendor arrives, and the result is several modems in the basement, several bills and nobody sure which box serves what. A single building network can carry all of those systems on one internet service the co-op controls, with each system on its own segment so the laundry vendor's equipment cannot see the camera recorder and neither can see the office computers. Cable from a central cabinet reaches each location. Some vendors require their own connection by contract, so we ask the managing agent for those contracts before proposing to consolidate anything, and we keep any vendor that must stay separate on its own port and its own line.

Co-ops, condos and who pays

Shareholders in our 1960s co-op ask for wired jacks one apartment at a time. Should the board keep approving them case by case, or plan one riser for everybody?

Case-by-case approvals tend to produce a building with several partial routes, each built differently by a different contractor, each crossing the same concrete floors in new places. After a few years nobody knows which hole carries which cable. A single planned riser, built once with spare capacity, lets each future request become a short branch from a known route rather than a new penetration. It also gives the board one set of records. The trade-off is cost up front. A reasonable middle path is to build the riser and the cabinet as a building project and let each shareholder decide whether to take a branch to their apartment now or later. We can price both approaches in one document so the board can see the difference.

If a riser goes in, should the co-op pay for each apartment's cable, or should each shareholder?

That is the board's decision and often depends on the proprietary lease and house rules, which we do not interpret. What we can do is split the drop plan so the decision is easy to make. The building portion covers the cabinet, the riser, the corridor runs and every common-area connection. The apartment portion covers the cable from the corridor into each unit and the jack inside it, priced per apartment. Some boards pay for everything as a capital improvement; others pay for the building portion and offer the apartment portion to shareholders at the per-unit price. Either way, the written plan shows exactly what each party is paying for and who owns the result.

We own a unit in a condominium on Edison Avenue. Who controls the corridor our cable would have to cross, and who do we ask?

In a condominium, the space inside your unit is generally yours and the corridors, shafts and basement are usually common elements controlled by the condominium's board through its managing agent, according to the condominium's own declaration and bylaws. Inside your unit, you can typically arrange cabling with less involvement. Anything that leaves the unit, passes through a corridor ceiling or uses a shared shaft needs the board's consent. Start with the managing agent: ask for the alteration rules and any insurance requirements for contractors. We will prepare a short description of the route and the openings for you to submit, and we will not touch common space until written consent arrives.

New buildings and buildings that may change

Our planned 17-story building sits beside the Bruckner Expressway. Should it have more than one way for providers to enter?

For a building that size, we recommend it. Norwood News reported applications for a 17-story residential building at 3250 Westchester Avenue, and a building of that height with that many households depends entirely on the path between the street and its telecom room. Two entrance conduits, placed on different sides of the lot where possible, let a second provider arrive without sharing the first provider's hole in the foundation, and give the building a backup route if roadwork, a utility repair or damage ever affects one of them. They also keep the building free to choose providers later. The entrances should be on the sides of the lot where opening a sidewalk is least complicated. The architect and civil engineer set the final locations; we make sure the drawings show both and that each leads to the same telecom room.

Our two-story building on Westchester Avenue may be sold to a developer in a few years, the way the 1932 low-rise at 2925 is being replaced. Is it worth cabling now?

It depends on how long you expect to own it and what the tenants need in the meantime. If the stores and apartments are struggling with unreliable connections today, a modest plan that fixes the cabinet, runs tested cable to the spots that matter and cleans up the cellar can pay for itself in fewer service calls and happier tenants within a short time. What we would not recommend is building expensive pathway, such as new riser sleeves or exterior conduit, in a building likely to be demolished. We can write a two-tier plan: the work worth doing for the next few years, and the work that only makes sense for a long-term owner, priced separately so you can decide.

Our general contractor wants to substitute a cheaper cable than the one on the drawings. What should the developer check before agreeing?

Ask for the substitute's exact product data and compare four things with what was specified: the category rating, the jacket rating for the spaces it will pass through, whether it is listed by a recognized testing laboratory, and whether the jacks and panels on the drawings are rated to work with it as a tested system. A cable that is cheaper because it uses lower-grade conductors, or because its jacket is not rated for the space, can fail certification or fail inspection. Require that every run be certified on a cable analyzer after installation, with the results delivered, whatever cable is used. If the substitute is genuinely equivalent and the results pass, the saving is real. If it is not, the test will show it before the walls close, which is the only good time to find out.

Houses, shops and practical matters

We are opening a business near the Pelham Bay Park station whose license comes with security conditions. How does the cable plan fit around them?

The plan starts from the conditions, so we ask for them in writing before we design anything. Licenses for regulated products sometimes specify camera coverage of certain rooms and entrances, how footage is stored and for how long, and who may enter storage and equipment rooms. Each required camera position needs its own tested run back to a recorder in a locked room, the recorder and network gear need battery backup, and the room itself may need its own entry control. We cable for all of it and document every run against the list of requirements, so an inspector can see that each condition has a wire behind it. The camera and access control equipment is installed under New York's Article 6-D licensing, and our quote shows which parts are data cabling and which are licensed security work.

We run a seasonal tax and bookkeeping practice from the ground floor of our two-family. Is business-grade cabling worth it for a few busy months a year?

For a practice that handles clients' financial information, the cabling decision is less about speed than about separation and reliability. The busy months are exactly when a dropped connection or a shared network causes the most trouble. A modest plan gives each work computer and the printer a wired drop, puts the practice on its own router and network separate from the household's, and locks the equipment in a small cabinet. Any client Wi-Fi becomes a guest network that cannot reach the work computers. The cable stays useful in the off-season for the household, and if the practice ever moves, the runs simply become extra connections for the house.

Our co-op board president keeps the only key to the basement network room. Who should hold access once the new system is in?

One key holder is a risk in both directions: if that person is away, nobody can restart equipment, and if the key is copied informally, nobody knows who can get in. A practical arrangement gives keys to the super, the managing agent and one board officer, records who holds each, and keeps a spare in the managing agent's office. Providers' technicians and contractors are let in by the super, not given their own keys. If the room holds the camera recorder, consider an electronic lock on its door that records entries. We leave a printed instruction sheet inside the cabinet door so whoever opens it knows what is inside and whom to call.

Two wired rooms in a detached house on Wilkinson Avenue and a riser through a co-op on Pelham Parkway South go through the same eight steps. Find where each provider's line ends, at the curb, on the front wall, in the cellar or up a riser, so the plan starts from the real handoff point.

Cellar ceiling, stair, closets, chases and every floor crossing, with existing cables traced by tone and probe and finished surfaces noted.

Every drop with its cable, route, termination, firestopping and price, plus the cabinet, switch and backup power, in one document.

6. Pull, seal and terminate

Cable along the approved routes, each penetration sealed the same day, and jacks and panels terminated to one standard.

7. Certify every run

Copper tested on a cable analyzer and fiber strands checked; anything that fails is fixed and retested before the crew leaves.

8. Hand over the records and walk the closet

Port map, test results and penetration photos as one file, both ends of every run labeled, and a walk through the cabinet with the owner, super or agent.

Pelham Bay Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Can we reach you by text or WhatsApp instead of a phone call?
+

Yes. Many Pelham Bay owners and supers prefer to send photos of the cellar, the provider's box and the rooms by message rather than describe them on the phone. If you would rather talk it through, call and we will book the visit during the same conversation.

Will you deal directly with our co-op's managing agent?
+

Yes, once the shareholder or board tells us who the agent is. Most managing agents want to receive the insurance certificate, the scope of work and the schedule straight from the contractor, and it saves the shareholder from relaying technical questions back and forth. We copy the shareholder or board contact on everything we send, so nobody is left out of the conversation, and we never treat the agent's approval as permission to enter an apartment without the resident's agreement.

Can you work alongside the electrician our building already uses?
+

Yes, and in many jobs it is necessary. If the cabinet, a recorder or an access point needs a new outlet or circuit, the building's licensed electrician does that work. We give the electrician the exact locations and power needs from our plan, agree on dates so the outlet is ready before our equipment arrives, and keep our data cable separated from their power wiring as the code requires. The quote states clearly which work is ours and which is the electrician's.

When the provider swaps our modem, does anything in the house need redoing?
+

Usually not. A well-built plan connects the provider's modem to the household's own router or switch with a single short cord in the cabinet, so a new modem simply replaces the old one in the same spot and the cable to every room stays exactly as it was. If the new modem also acts as a router, it may need to be set to pass the connection through to your router, which the provider or a setup visit can handle. The house wiring itself does not care which modem sits at the start of it.

How many people usually come on a house job in Pelham Bay?
+

Most house jobs need two technicians, because fishing cable from a cellar into an upstairs wall goes much faster and more cleanly with one person feeding and one person receiving. A larger building or a new-construction prewire may need more people on certain days. The drop plan tells you how many people to expect and for how long, so you can plan around it and let a tenant or neighbor know in advance.

Do you test the provider's own fiber or coax line too?
+

No. The provider's line up to its modem or network box belongs to the provider, and only the provider can test, repair or move it. We test everything after that point: every cable we install and any existing house cable you ask us to evaluate. If our tests show the house wiring is sound but service is still poor, that points to the provider's side, and we will tell you so plainly, with our test results, so you can raise it with them.

What happens if the co-op board rejects part of the plan after we have signed?
+

Nothing is drilled until the board approves, so a rejection changes the plan rather than wasting work. We revise the affected routes, for example a corridor raceway the board does not want, propose alternatives and reprice only the lines that changed. If no acceptable route exists for part of the job, that part comes off the plan and the price. The rest can go ahead as approved.

How much noise reaches the apartments below while you drill?
+

Drilling through wood framing is brief and quiet compared with coring concrete, which is loud and carries through a building's structure. In a co-op, we schedule any coring for hours the managing agent approves, tell the agent how long each core takes, and suggest that residents on the floors above and below receive notice. Most of a cabling day is quiet work: pulling cable, terminating jacks and testing. The noisy minutes are few and can be planned.

Will you put the basement ceiling tiles and any panels back the way you found them?
+

Yes. In a finished basement with a dropped ceiling, we lift only the tiles we need, set them aside carefully and replace them at the end of the day. Access panels on soffits or utility walls go back the same way. If a tile is already cracked or crumbling when we lift it, we show you before replacing it, so there is no question later about how it got that way. Any drywall openings agreed in the plan are left neat and ready for patching.
