The Neighborhood

Why Morrisania's Towers, Shared Campuses and Third Avenue Stores Need Fire Alarms Designed Around Who Is Inside

Morrisania sits in the south-central Bronx, in Community District 3, which it shares with Crotona Park East. Wikipedia draws it from the Cross Bronx Expressway on the north to East 163rd Street on the south, with Crotona and Prospect Avenues on the east and Webster Avenue on the west, and names Third Avenue as its main street; CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws a tighter outline. The neighborhood counted 37,865 residents in the 2010 census, up from 29,797 ten years earlier, according to Wikipedia, and most of that growth went into large buildings.

In the late 1960s and 1970s, arson and disinvestment destroyed about 40 percent of Morrisania's housing and more than half the population left, according to the newsletter The Neighborhoods. What replaced the losses was not a return to small tenements but public housing towers, rehabilitated walk-ups and, since 1990, subsidized apartment buildings on empty lots; city cultural planning documents count about 8,500 new homes built and another 9,000 preserved since then.

So the typical Morrisania fire alarm protects a lot of people at once. Wikipedia lists 20 New York City Housing Authority developments in the neighborhood. Forest Houses, between East 163rd and East 166th Streets and Trinity and Tinton Avenues, has 15 buildings of 9, 10 and 14 stories and 1,350 apartments, completed in November 1956. Webster Houses has five 21-story buildings. Morris I and II together have 17 buildings of 16 and 20 stories, McKinley Houses five of 16. And Morrisania Air Rights, completed in 1981, has three buildings of 19, 23 and 29 stories, 843 apartments in all, built on steel trusses over the Metro-North tracks that run about 20 feet below Park Avenue.

Recent fires show why. On November 13, 2025, a fire on the sixth floor of a Forest Houses building seriously injured two firefighters and hurt three other people, one of them a minor; afterward every elevator door carried an "unplanned elevator outage" sign, News 12 reported. "Many tenants on that floor had complained to housing about the condition," one resident told News 12, describing a smell from the apartment. Two weeks later, on Thanksgiving night, a fire on the sixth floor of a high-rise on Washington Avenue sent one resident to Lincoln Hospital (ABC7 New York). On February 20, 2026, close to 80 firefighters answered a 6 a.m. call for a burning balcony on a high-rise on East 169th Street, where two people reportedly had minor injuries (News 12 Bronx). None of these reports blamed a fire alarm; they show how often Morrisania's fires happen in buildings where the alarm, the elevators and the stairs have to work together.

The second pattern is sharing. The Morris High School building at 1110 Boston Road opened in 1904 as the city's first co-educational high school, built for about 2,500 students, according to Lehman College's Bronx architecture survey. After Morris High was phased out from 2002, the building reopened as the Morris Campus, and Wikipedia lists four small high schools inside it today. Its vaulted auditorium, Duncan Hall, seats 1,200. One building, several principals and one fire alarm: whoever answers that panel speaks for all of them.

The third pattern is new construction. On September 29, 2026, the city announced the groundbreaking of Sol on Park, an 18-story building with 229 homes for older New Yorkers on the Morris Houses campus, more than 8,000 square feet of community space, on-site health services and a new space for the Morris Houses Resident Association, with move-ins planned for early 2029 (NYC Mayor's Office). At the smaller end, Marketproof recorded permits filed on February 19, 2026 for six-story, 66-foot buildings at 1217 Washington Avenue and 458 East 168th Street.

This page covers commercial and code-required fire alarm systems only: the building systems in apartment buildings, mixed-use buildings, schools, stores, churches and new construction. Smoke alarms inside individual one- and two-family homes follow separate rules and are not part of this service.

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Morrisania's Building Types, From Walk-Ups to 29-Story Towers

Commercial fire alarm systems are required by a building's use and size, not by its ZIP code. These are the building types that bring Morrisania owners and managers to a fire alarm design, with the point that matters most in each.

Postwar public housing towers

Forest, Webster, Morris, McKinley and the other NYCHA developments were built between the 1950s and 1980s, long before today's high-rise rules. NYCHA owns and maintains them, and it replaces their fire alarms through its own capital program; its fact sheet on fire alarm replacements lists new panels, communicators, strobes, magnetic door holders, pull stations and smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors, all to a system approved by the FDNY and the Department of Buildings. The same fact sheet tells residents to expect "significant noise and debris," that "noise disruptions due to alarm testing may occur," and that construction alone runs 24 to 30 months after 6 to 12 months each for planning, design and procurement (NYCHA). That work belongs to NYCHA and its contractors, but it is the best public picture of what replacing a fire alarm in an occupied tower involves.

Towers over the railroad

The three Morrisania Air Rights buildings, designed by The Eggers Partnership, sit on steel trusses developed at MIT so that trains could keep running while the buildings went up over the open cut (Wikipedia). The fire alarm code is the same as for any tower; the practical differences are vibration and spaces near the railroad that can't be entered without coordination.

Privately owned high-rises

Privately owned and nonprofit-owned elevator buildings are designed and maintained by their owners. In New York City, a building is a high-rise when an occupied floor is more than 75 feet above the lowest level of fire department vehicle access (NYC Building Code section 403). Section 403 lists automatic smoke detection, a fire alarm, emergency voice/alarm communication and a fire command center among the features of a high-rise built under the current code, with the FDNY's 2019 bulletin on command center locations applying to residential buildings taller than 125 feet; an older one keeps the system it was approved with until it is altered. Knowing which of those you have is the first question of any upgrade.

The Morris High School building

Designed by Charles B. J. Snyder in Collegiate Gothic, with an eight-story entrance tower, the building was restored in 1997 and now serves several schools (Lehman College; Wikipedia). The NYC Building Code requires a fire alarm in every school building regardless of size, so the question here is not whether, but how a single system serves four schools, a 1,200-seat auditorium and a 1955 cafeteria and gymnasium addition, inside a landmark district where the exterior can't change without Landmarks Preservation Commission review.

Six-story apartment buildings with stores below

This is the bread and butter of Morrisania's private stock: prewar walk-ups and elevator buildings, and their newer six-story replacements of about 66 feet, often with a store or office on the ground floor. Under the NYC Building Code, as summarized by NY Engineers, an R-2 apartment building needs a fire alarm when dwelling units sit three or more stories above the lowest exit discharge or the building has 16 or more units, so nearly every one of these buildings has a system. The design work is in the ground floor: whose panel the store's devices report to, and who gets the trouble call.

Churches, halls and the Carnegie library

Trinity Episcopal Church of Morrisania, begun in 1874, is the oldest building in the Morris High School Historic District (Historic Districts Council), and the Morrisania Library on East 169th Street is a 1908 Carnegie branch (6tocelebrate). Rooms like these reach a fire alarm requirement by occupant load, which the NY Engineers summary puts at 300 people for assembly spaces.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the People Before You Count the Devices

Our technicians start every tall-building job in neighborhoods like Morrisania with a question that has nothing to do with wire: how many people are in this building at 3 a.m., and how many of them can't use the stairs quickly? A replacement in a 20-story building means hundreds of residents living with a half-old, half-new system for weeks, so the plan has to say what protects each floor every night.

The second habit is to find the person who will actually answer the panel. In a shared school building, a mixed-use building with a day program on the ground floor, or a tower with a lobby desk that is staffed only part of the day, the answer is often unclear until someone writes it down. An annunciator is only useful in front of someone who knows what it means.

The third is to treat the elevators as part of the fire alarm. In a building where the elevators are already out of service, the stairs carry everyone, and the notification design and the instructions to residents have to reflect that. These are working habits, described in general terms; we don't discuss specific clients' buildings on this page.

Your Questions Answered

Morrisania Fire Alarm Questions From Tower Managers, Schools, Store Owners and Developers

Towers and residents

NYCHA's fact sheet gives fire alarm replacements 24 to 30 months of construction. What should Forest Houses or Webster Houses residents expect while the work goes on in their building?

Noise, some dust, short planned outages and visits to common areas, spread over a long stretch. NYCHA's fact sheet says contractors put up plastic barriers to limit dust, that residents get advance notice of outages, that some areas may be blocked off for safety, that alarm testing can be noisy and that asbestos abatement may be needed. At the end, property management and residents are trained on the new system and the pull stations. The work is NYCHA's own project, so questions about a building's schedule go to property management; outside companies can't do or speed up that work.

After the November 2025 Forest Houses fire, the elevators had outage signs on every door. What does a tower's fire alarm do with the elevators, and what if they're already out of service?

In a building with elevator recall, smoke detectors in the elevator lobbies, machine room and hoistway tell the fire alarm to send the cars to the designated floor and keep them there for firefighters. That only works with elevators in service. When the elevators are down for repair, recall has nothing to recall, and residents and firefighters depend on the stairs. Notification still has to reach every floor, and the building's emergency plan has to tell residents what to do without elevators.

The February 2026 fire on East 169th Street started on a high-rise balcony. Should a building put fire alarm devices out on its balconies?

Usually not. Smoke detectors are made for indoor air and give false alarms in wind, rain and cold, so open balconies are rarely where a building's detection goes. What protects the building is what happens once fire reaches the inside: detection in corridors and common rooms, notification that reaches every apartment, and any sprinkler or balcony protection the building's code requires.

Morrisania Air Rights stands on steel trusses over the Metro-North tracks. Who has to be involved when fire alarm work in a building like that reaches spaces next to the railroad?

The building owner, its design professional and the railroad. The code requirements for the fire alarm are the same as for any tower of its height and use, but cable routes, devices or access paths that would enter railroad property or space over live tracks need the railroad's coordination and its own safety rules. The design professional plans those routes before filing so the installer never has to improvise near the tracks. Steady train vibration also argues for checking terminations at every inspection, not just testing devices.

Schools and shared buildings

In a building shared by four high schools, like the Morris Campus, which school's office gets the fire alarm annunciator?

The annunciator goes where the FDNY and the design professional want firefighters to read it, usually at the main entrance they will use, not in any one school's office. Each school's office can be given its own remote display where the design allows, so staff on every floor know where the alarm is. What matters most is that every school has named staff who can read the display and that one person speaks for the building when firefighters arrive.

Duncan Hall, the vaulted auditorium in the Morris High School building, seats 1,200. How do horns, strobes and speakers work in a big historic room like that?

Large, tall rooms with hard surfaces echo, and a voice message can blur. The design professional sizes notification for the room's volume and seating: strobes placed so they can be seen from the seats and the balcony, horns or speakers spread out rather than concentrated, and voice intelligibility checked where speakers are used. In a historic room, devices are placed where they won't damage plaster, wood or decorative finishes.

The Morris High School building has an eight-story entrance tower above a five-story school. Do the rooms inside a tower like that need fire alarm coverage?

If they are used, yes. Occupied rooms in a tower, such as offices, storage or mechanical space, are part of the building and are covered as the design requires, with detection suited to the room and notification people inside can hear and see. Rooms that are closed and unused may need less, but the design professional decides from how they are actually used. Narrow tower stairs also make it worth thinking about how a technician will reach every device for testing.

New and taller buildings

The tallest Morrisania Air Rights building has 29 stories. Is radio coverage for firefighters part of the fire alarm in a tower that tall?

It is a related system, not the fire alarm itself. Concrete and steel can block firefighters' radios, and the NYC Building Code's high-rise section lists emergency responder radio coverage alongside the fire alarm. An in-building auxiliary radio communication system needs its own FDNY permit to operate. New towers plan for it in the drawings, and a tower getting a new fire alarm should ask the design professional whether radio coverage is part of the scope or a separate project.

Sol on Park will put a health and wellness center and more than 8,000 square feet of community space under 18 floors of senior apartments. How does a fire alarm handle a public health space at the base of a residential tower?

The building has one fire alarm, but the base floors are designed for their own uses. A health space with patients, a community room with events and a resident association office each get detection and notification suited to the people using them, all reporting to the tower's panel and command center. The design professional works out the occupancy of each space and how the voice system addresses it, and the operators of those spaces need to know how alarms reach them and who answers.

The six-story buildings filed at 1217 Washington Avenue and 458 East 168th Street are 66 feet tall with a store below. What fire alarm does a building like that need?

A building like that is an R-2 apartment building with units three or more stories above the lowest exit, so it needs a building fire alarm, but at 66 feet it isn't a high-rise and won't need voice evacuation or a fire command center for height alone. Expect manual stations at exits where required, smoke detection in corridors and other spaces the code lists, notification in common areas and apartments as designed, sprinkler monitoring, and a central station connection.

Forest Houses' land now includes an eight-story privately owned building built on property NYCHA sold. When a private building stands on a NYCHA campus, whose rules govern its fire alarm?

The same city rules as any other private building. Its owner files with the FDNY, hires the installer, holds the central station account and maintains the system; NYCHA's capital program covers only NYCHA's own buildings. Where the two share a driveway, fire lane or utility path, the drawings should show where the private building's devices and cable stop, so nobody on either side assumes the other is responsible.

If the fire alarm panel in a landmarked public building like the Morrisania Library fails, can it be replaced before anything goes to the Landmarks Preservation Commission?

The fire protection comes first. A failed panel is reported to the FDNY, a fire watch is kept, and the replacement is filed with the FDNY as a modification of the approved system. Landmarks review applies when the work touches what the Commission regulates: new conduit or devices on a façade clearly would, and whether an interior swap needs any Commission sign-off is something the design professional settles with the Commission at the start, without holding up the fire watch or the FDNY filing.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Morrisania

What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is the building-wide network of smoke and heat detectors, manual stations, horns, strobes and speakers, wired to a fire alarm control panel that watches every floor, warns the people inside, runs other building equipment and reports to a central station that calls the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? A detector senses smoke or heat, or someone pulls a station; the panel confirms it, then within seconds sounds the notification, sends the elevators home, releases door holders, shuts down fans and transmits the alarm. Commercial fire detection systems come in a few kinds, and these are the ones that fit Morrisania's buildings.

Addressable

What is an addressable fire alarm system? Each device has its own address, so the panel reports "14th floor, east corridor" in a tower or "Room 108" in a school. Addressable fire alarm installation, addressable fire alarm system installation and addressable fire alarm panel installation are standard for new buildings and full replacements.

Conventional

What is a conventional fire alarm system? Devices are wired in zones, and the panel only knows which zone is in alarm. Conventional fire alarm installation, conventional fire alarm system installation and conventional fire alarm panel installation still suit a single store or a small hall with a handful of devices.

The difference

What is the difference between addressable and conventional fire alarms? An addressable system names the exact device and warns when a detector is dirty; a conventional system names a zone. In a tower, that saves firefighters a floor-by-floor search.

Voice evacuation

Fire alarm voice evacuation installation and fire alarm emergency voice communication for towers and large assembly rooms, with fire alarm speaker installation and fire alarm speaker strobe installation so messages can tell one floor to leave and others to stay.

Intelligent detection

Intelligent fire alarm installation and an intelligent fire detection system use detectors that compensate for slow drift and report when they need cleaning, which helps in garages, compactor areas and dusty cellars where ordinary detectors trip.

Air sampling

Aspirating smoke detection installation, an air sampling smoke detection system, VESDA installation and VESDA service for tall auditoriums, server and telecom rooms, and spaces where spot detectors can't be reached for testing. Data center fire detection installation usually starts with this choice.

Devices

Fire Alarm Devices in Morrisania Buildings and Where Each One Belongs

Fire alarm equipment installation in Morrisania ranges from a tower's twentieth-floor corridor to a garage bay on Third Avenue. Fire detection device installation and fire alarm initiating device installation cover the devices that tell the panel something is wrong; notification appliances tell people.

Smoke detectors

Commercial smoke detector installation covers corridors, elevator lobbies, stairs where required, electrical and telecom rooms, and classrooms and program rooms where the design calls for them. Addressable smoke detector installation and photoelectric smoke detector installation are the usual choice. Commercial smoke detector replacement, commercial smoke detector testing and commercial smoke detector repair are routine, and smoke detector circuit troubleshooting on an older conventional loop often comes down to one corroded splice in a damp cellar. The FDNY's design tips call for detectors to sit at least 36 inches from supply diffusers and return openings, which matters in renovated lobbies where new ductwork landed beside old detector locations.

Duct detectors

Duct smoke detector installation, duct smoke detector testing and duct smoke detector replacement apply to air handlers in schools, new mid-rises and the rooftop units of larger stores. The FDNY expects fans moving more than 2,000 cubic feet per minute to shut down on alarm, and a duct detector is usually how that happens.

Heat detectors

Commercial heat detector installation, fixed temperature heat detector installation and rate of rise heat detector installation go where smoke detectors would false-alarm: garage bays, school and takeout kitchens, boiler rooms, laundry rooms, trash rooms and dusty storage. Heat detector replacement is common after a kitchen or garage renovation. Commercial kitchen fire detection installation is usually paired with monitoring of the hood suppression system.

High ceilings and open rooms

What should the lobby staff in a Morrisania apartment tower do in the first five minutes after the fire alarm panel goes into alarm?
+

Can a fire alarm replacement in an occupied Morrisania high-rise be done without moving residents out?
+

Yes, and that is how it is normally done. The old system stays in service while new devices go in, and each floor or zone is switched to the new panel and tested before crews move on. Expect short power outages with advance notice, noise and some dust held behind plastic barriers.

Why does a tall building's fire alarm need extra power supplies on its upper floors?
+

Horns, strobes and speakers draw more power than the panel can send up a long riser, and voltage drops over distance. Remote notification power supplies, each with its own batteries, are placed on upper floors to drive the devices there, and the design professional's voltage drop and battery calculations show where they go. Each one is another cabinet to inspect and another set of batteries to replace on schedule.

Does the compactor room in a Morrisania apartment tower need fire alarm devices?
+

Usually the design covers it, because compactor and trash rooms hold a lot of fuel and are visited rarely. Dust, odors and heat make ordinary smoke detectors a poor fit there, so heat detection is common, and the chute's own sprinkler protection is monitored where the building has it. The design professional decides from the building's code and layout.

What do the different sounds from a tower's fire alarm mean: a steady alarm, a voice message and a trouble beep?
+

A horn pattern or a voice message is an alarm: follow the instructions, and leave if told to. A short repeating beep or chirp from the panel or annunciator is a trouble signal, which means part of the system needs service, not that there is a fire; it is for staff and the technician, not residents. During announced tests, the building should say how a real alarm will be announced.

Can a school building's class-change bells and the fire alarm horns be the same system?
+

No. The fire alarm is a separate, supervised system with its own sounds and its own approval, and its signal has to be distinct from ordinary bells so students and staff know it means leave now. The fire alarm can be designed to silence a separate paging system when an alarm starts.

Will residents hear the fire alarm all day during the FDNY acceptance test in an occupied building?
+

They will hear it in bursts. Each device and each notification zone has to be shown working, so alarms sound floor by floor through the test, with quiet stretches in between. Post notices in the lobby and elevators beforehand with the test hours and how a real alarm would be announced.

+

If your store's devices report to the building's system, yes: they are part of the approved system being replaced, and new devices go in your space as the drawings show. Expect a visit to install them, a short period when the old and new systems overlap, and testing with the rest of the building. If your store has its own separate system, the building's replacement shouldn't touch it, but the drawings will show any connection between the two.

Do the stair doors in a Morrisania tower unlock when the fire alarm goes off?
+

In a high-rise built under the current NYC Building Code, stair doors that are locked from the stair side have to unlock on a signal from the fire command center, so people in the stairs can get back onto a floor. Older towers follow the rules they were approved under. The fire alarm provides the signal, and the acceptance test proves the doors release.

Is a tower's fire pump connected to the fire alarm?
+

Where a building has a fire pump, the fire alarm normally supervises it: it reports when the pump is running, when it loses power and when its controller has a problem, so a pump failure doesn't go unnoticed. Those signals are wired through monitor modules and tested with the sprinkler contractor. The design professional lists them on the drawings.

Why does our tower's fire alarm drawing set include a riser diagram, and should we keep a copy?
+

The riser diagram shows how the panel connects to every floor: which circuits run up which shafts, where the power supplies and modules sit, and how each floor ties in. When a trouble appears on the 15th floor, it is the first page a technician looks at. Keep a copy with the approved drawings at the panel or fire command station, and update it whenever the system changes.
