The Neighborhood

Why Midwood Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Midwood's front doors were mostly hung between the 1920s and the 1950s, and a great many of them still carry the entry hardware of that era, patched and extended ever since. The neighborhood was farmland called Midwout, "middle woods," settled by the Dutch in 1652, and it stayed rural until it was annexed to the City of Brooklyn in the 1890s. The Brighton Line cut through it in 1878, trolleys reached Coney Island Avenue in 1894, and then, as the Midwood Development Corporation puts it, it became more developed in the 1920s, when large middle-class housing tracts and apartment buildings were built. CityNeighborhoods.nyc calls the 1920s through the 1940s Midwood's golden age of growth. That timing matters for a buzzer, because it means most of the neighborhood's multiple dwellings were built before 1968, the line New York law draws for which apartment buildings must have an intercom from the start.

What stands on the blocks today is a mix that changes every few streets. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes freestanding Victorian homes on East 19th Street, limestone rowhouses along Ocean Avenue, red-brick two-families on East 13th through 16th Streets, and six-story prewar apartment houses on Kings Highway and Avenue I. Ocean Parkway and Ocean Avenue add the larger elevator co-ops and rentals, some of them from the late 1930s and some post-war. Brownstoner's listing for 1717 Avenue N describes a 1937 Art Deco building by the architects Kavy & Kavovitt with a circular window in its entrance door; its listing for 1600 Ocean Parkway, at Avenue P, notes a 1939 certificate of occupancy, an elevator, etched front doors and "an intercom phone for visitors." Corcoran lists 1119 Ocean Parkway as a 1951 six-story co-op of 84 units with a doorman and a live-in super. Each of those buildings has a different entry system behind the same idea: a stranger presses a button, someone upstairs answers, and the door lets one person in and then locks again.

The last part of that sentence is the one that fails most quietly. A buzzer can ring perfectly while the street door behind it no longer swings closed, or closes but doesn't latch, or latches but releases for anyone who leans on it. In May 2018 CBS New York reported that a man "followed the 81-year-old victim into the building where he lives and attacked him" at East 10th Street and Foster Avenue in Midwood. The report doesn't say how the door was opened, and we don't guess. But it describes the exact moment an entry system exists to protect, and in a building where the lobby door drifts open for a few seconds after every resident walks through, that moment is unguarded no matter how well the buzzer rings. That is why a Midwood buzzer repair here always includes the closer, the latch and the strike, not just the button and the handset.

Midwood is also, in the words of one neighborhood guide, a predominantly Orthodox Jewish community, with yeshivas and synagogues on nearly every block. That shapes how front doors are used for a full day every week, and it shapes what residents ask of an entry system: a street door that works with a key as well as a buzzer, a keypad or fob that doesn't become the only way in, and a door that still locks when nobody is pressing anything. We cover that in its own section below. We don't give religious rulings, and we don't claim any device is approved for Shabbat; we repair and adjust the hardware so that whatever the building and its residents decide, the door does what it is supposed to do.

Midwood facts that shape a buzzer repair

  • Built mostly before the 1968 line: the 1920s–1940s were Midwood's main building era (CityNeighborhoods.nyc), and the Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide ties the automatic intercom requirement to multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments.
  • Elevator buildings with intercoms already in place: 1600 Ocean Parkway (1939 certificate of occupancy) is listed with "an intercom phone for visitors"; 1119 Ocean Parkway is a 1951 co-op of 84 units with a doorman (Brownstoner, Corcoran).
  • Two-families by the block: red-brick two-families run along East 13th through 16th Streets, each with two households sharing one street door (CityNeighborhoods.nyc).
  • A landmark district on the northern edge: the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District on March 18, 2008, covering about 250 houses between Foster Avenue, Avenue H, Ocean Avenue and the subway cut; some sources count it as Flatbush.
  • A tailgating case on record: CBS New York reported in May 2018 that a suspect followed an 81-year-old man into his own building at East 10th Street and Foster Avenue and attacked him.
  • Population: 52,835 at the 2020 Census (Wikipedia), living mostly in walk-ups, elevator buildings and one- and two-family houses.

The Lobby

Art Deco Lobbies, Heavy Doors and Old Risers: Repairing Intercoms in Midwood's Elevator Buildings

The six-story apartment houses on Ocean Parkway, Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway, Avenue I and Avenue N are the buildings most people picture when they think of a Brooklyn intercom. A street door, sometimes a second vestibule door, a panel of names or a keypad on the wall between them, a riser of thin paired wire climbing through the building, and a handset or a call box in every apartment. In Midwood, many of these buildings went up in the late 1930s, and their entrances were designed as showpieces: Brownstoner notes the circular window in the Art Deco entrance door at 1717 Avenue N, and the high-style Art Deco niche and etched front doors at 1600 Ocean Parkway. The doors are heavy, the frames are original, and the intercom has usually been replaced at least once inside the same wall openings.

Why a prewar riser is usually worth keeping

The copper inside a 1930s or 1950s riser is rarely what failed. Brick Underground's 2019 intercom guide quotes Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith saying "It's all in the wire connections," and reports that problems typically sit at the lobby base, the apartment station, the door strike or the power supply, with "the need for a total system overhaul" unlikely. That matches what a Midwood elevator building usually shows us: a corroded terminal block in the cellar, a splice in a hallway junction box that a painter or plumber disturbed, a handset whose contacts have worn, a supply that has been running hot for twenty years. Each of those is a component repair. A larger example from Park Slope shows why owners should protect the wire: when the 148-unit Park Vanderbilt co-op replaced its audio-only intercom with video in 2015, Security Sales reported that it reused its existing cable everywhere except two units and saved about $20,000 doing so. A Midwood co-op from the same era has the same incentive to keep its riser.

How we work a dead apartment line

When one apartment, or one vertical line of apartments, stops ringing or talking, the question is whether the fault is in the apartment, in the riser between floors, or at the cellar block where that line lands. We test at the lobby panel first, then at the cellar terminal for that apartment's pair, then at the apartment itself. If the signal leaves the panel and reaches the cellar block but not the apartment, the riser or a hallway splice is the suspect, and a tone tracer narrows it to a floor. If every apartment on one line is dead, the common point is almost always a junction or block, not five separate failures. We label each pair as we go, because many Midwood buildings have had their apartments renumbered or their panels replaced without anyone updating the cellar.

The door is half the system

In these lobbies the street door does a lot of work. It is heavy, often wood and glass or bronze-framed, and it carries an electric strike that has to release a latch under the door's weight and then let the door pull itself shut and catch again. When residents report that "the buzzer doesn't work," we often find a strike that hums and holds because the door has sagged on its hinges and the latch is binding on the keeper, or a closer that has lost its latch speed so the door stops an inch short of the frame. Neither is an intercom fault, and replacing the panel fixes neither. We test the release with the door closing on its own, adjust the strike and keeper to the door as it hangs, and set the closer so the door finishes its swing and latches. If the hinges or frame need a carpenter or locksmith, the quote says so.

Keys, Keypads and the Weekly Day of Rest

Keeping a Midwood Street Door Locked When Residents Can't Use the Buzzer

In much of Brooklyn an entry system is judged by one question: does it let the right person in when they press a button? In Midwood there is a second question, asked every Friday afternoon in a lot of buildings: how do residents who keep Shabbat get in and out for twenty-five hours without using it? Many observant households don't operate electric devices from Friday evening to Saturday night, and that includes pressing a call button, keying a code or presenting a fob. A building's answer to that question decides how its door is used, and how its door is used decides which parts wear out.

What the record shows when the answer is "leave it open"

The easiest answer, and the worst for security, is to leave the street door unlocked or wedged for the day. CBS New York reported in April 2016 on a building on President Street in Crown Heights whose front door "was left unlocked for the Sabbath"; residents told the station that observant Jews in the building don't use electronics, and surveillance video showed a man following a young woman into the lobby, where he grabbed her phone and cash. That was not Midwood, and we cite it only because it describes the risk plainly. A door that is propped every week also wears its hardware in a particular way. A closer held open against its own spring loses its adjustment. A latch that is taped or blocked leaves residue in the strike. A door that slams when the wedge is kicked out each Saturday night loosens its hinges and its keeper. When a Midwood building calls us about a strike that "stopped catching," we ask how the door is used on the weekend, because the answer often explains the fault.

Keypads, fobs and the key that still has to work

The opposite problem appears when a building upgrades. A board replaces keys with a keypad or fobs, and a resident who can't use either on Shabbat is suddenly locked out one day a week. Habitat Magazine reported in July 2024 on a co-op shareholder "forbidden from using electric devices on the Sabbath" whose building had installed an "electronic keypad beside the front door that requires residents to enter a code to open the door." The lawyers it quoted were blunt; Andrew Lieb told the magazine that if the co-op flatly rejected the request, the shareholder could sue for discrimination "because they are effectively making the building unavailable to observant Jews," and the New York Times column Habitat cited said a request for a key to a side door should generally be honored unless that entrance poses a credible danger. In 2015, VIN News reported that Orthodox residents of LeFrak City in Queens had sued over new key fobs that provide entry "through an electronic signal." We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice. The hardware point is simple, though: an electric strike on a street door does not have to remove the key. A fail-secure strike stays locked when its power is off, but the lockset it sits beside can still be opened with a mechanical key, so a building can keep a buzzer, a keypad or a fob for most of the week and a key path for residents who need one.

What we do, and what we leave to others

On a Midwood repair we make sure the mechanical path works as well as the electric one: the cylinder turns freely, the latch retracts fully with the key, the strike's keeper doesn't drag on a latch that is being withdrawn by hand, and the closer brings the door all the way shut whichever way it was opened. Some buildings and synagogues use timers or specialist controllers on their doors and elevators. We don't install or certify Shabbat devices, and we don't tell any household or board what is permitted; that is for them and their rabbi. If a controller like that is already on a door we are repairing, we identify it, leave it as we found it, and tell you if the strike or supply we are replacing affects it. If the building wants to change how its door behaves on Shabbat, that becomes a separate written proposal, not something we alter on a repair visit.

The Two-Family Blocks

Two Bells, One Vestibule: Buzzer Repair in Midwood's Brick Two-Family Houses

Walk down East 13th, 14th, 15th or 16th Street between the lettered avenues and the pattern repeats: attached and semi-detached red-brick houses, a short stoop or a few steps, a street door with two buttons beside it, and inside, a small vestibule with one door to the ground-floor apartment and a stair to the upper one. The owner usually lives on one floor and rents the other, or two generations of a family share the house. The bell system is simple, and it was simple when it was installed: two buttons, one transformer in the cellar, a chime or buzzer on each floor, and, in many houses, a release button upstairs wired to an electric strike on the street door so the upper household can let a visitor in without walking down.

Crossed bells and the cellar splice

The commonest two-family complaint is not silence but confusion. The tenant's visitor presses the top button and the owner's chime rings, or both ring, or one button works only when the other is held. Nine times in ten the cause is in the cellar or behind the button plate, where the two bell circuits share a common wire from the transformer and split toward each floor. A splice that was remade during a boiler job, a plate that was reinstalled with its wires swapped, or a short between two thin conductors where they pass through a joist will all produce crossed or ghost rings. We disconnect each floor's run at the transformer, test it alone, and then rebuild the split on a labeled terminal strip, so that each button reaches only its own household.

The upstairs release that stopped releasing

The second complaint is the release. A strike on a two-family's street door often dates from whenever someone decided the upstairs household shouldn't have to come down every time the bell rang, and it may share the bell transformer even though a strike draws much more current than a chime. Over the years the transformer weakens, the strike gets a little less power than it needs, and the door starts releasing only if the visitor pushes at the right moment. We measure the transformer while the strike is energized, check the strike's rating against what feeds it, and look at the door's alignment, because a sagging brick-house door can bind on its keeper as badly as a lobby door can. The repair may be a properly rated transformer, a separate supply for the strike, or a keeper adjustment, and the written quote tells you which before anything is bought.

Who owns a two-family's buzzer

A legal two-family is not a multiple dwelling, so the intercom rules in the Attorney General's guide that apply to apartment buildings don't apply in the same way. That doesn't make the tenant's bell unimportant; it makes it a matter between owner and tenant, governed by the lease. We repair it for whoever is responsible, and when a tenant calls us directly we ask that the owner approve the visit, because the transformer, the cellar wiring and the street door belong to the house, not to one apartment. If a house has been divided into more apartments than its certificate of occupancy allows, that is a question for the owner and the Department of Buildings, not for a buzzer technician, and we say so rather than wiring around it.

The Detached Houses

Porch Buttons, Chimes and House Intercoms in Midwood's Victorian, Tudor and Colonial Homes

North of Avenue J, and especially around Midwood Park and Fiske Terrace near Foster Avenue and Avenue H, Midwood is a neighborhood of detached houses with porches. Their history is unusually well recorded. CityLand reports that T.B. Ackerson cleared the land for Fiske Terrace in 1905, laid out streets, sewers and water lines, and built over 150 single-family homes within eighteen months, in series of ten from stock plans; the Flatbush Gardener blog notes that about 75 percent of the houses in the Midwood Park part of the district were built by the John R. Corbin Company. Further south, Brownstoner has written about narrow 1920s and 1930s Tudors, among them a 15-foot-wide house on East 28th Street from 1932 with four floors of living space and a garage. A house like that has a door, a button and a chime, often a back or side door with its own button, and sometimes a mid-century house intercom with stations on several floors.

The chime that hums and the button that sticks

For a detached house the repair is usually a short chain: the button at the porch, the wire through the frame, the transformer in the cellar and the chime in the hall. When the chime hums without ringing, its plunger is often stuck or the transformer is weak; when the bell keeps sounding, the button has usually stuck closed, which can cook the transformer if it is left that way. When a video doorbell has replaced the porch button, the old transformer may not be able to keep the camera powered and still ring the chime. We find the transformer, read its label, measure it with everything on the circuit working, and replace the part that can't keep up. We don't do line-voltage electrical work; if the transformer has to move to a new box or circuit, an electrician's part is named in the quote.

Repairs inside the historic district

On March 18, 2008, the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, about 250 houses bounded by Foster Avenue, Avenue H, Ocean Avenue and the subway line. Its permit guidebook says ordinary repair and maintenance doesn't need a permit, and that staff can approve intercom panels within or adjacent to a door opening, with a limit of one panel per door opening and no drilling through cast iron or decorative surfaces. For an owner in the district, that means a like-for-like repair of a button, chime or porch station can simply go ahead. A new or larger panel on the facade, or a station on a porch post where there wasn't one, is a change the owner may need to clear with the Commission first, and we tell you that before ordering a part.

Who Owns the Repair

The 1968 Line, Co-op Boards and Rental Buildings: Who Answers for a Midwood Buzzer

Because Midwood was built mostly before 1968, its owners and tenants often read about New York's intercom law and come away unsure whether it applies to them. The New York Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide puts it this way: multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that can buzz the door open. Older multiple dwellings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request it, and the guide notes that the landlord may recover the cost. ButterflyMX's 2026 summary of the same statute, Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, reads it the same way.

That leaves many Midwood prewar elevator buildings on the "if requested" side of the line, but in practice nearly all of them already have an intercom, many for decades. Habitat Magazine, writing in February 2024 about a co-op shareholder who lost his intercom after a downstairs renovation, said that the city's Housing Maintenance Code also requires a functioning intercom, and quoted the attorney Nancy Kourland: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." HPD writes buzzer faults as violations of the Housing Maintenance Code, using wording like "properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system." Brick Underground's 2014 guide for tenants advises starting with a written request, because "formal written requests tend to get more efficient responses than texts or phone calls," and notes that tenants in rent-stabilized buildings can also file a reduction-of-services complaint with the state housing agency. None of this is legal advice, and we don't represent tenants or owners in disputes.

What it means for a repair is practical. In a rental building, we work for the owner or managing agent, apartment by apartment, and leave a dated record of what was tested and fixed that the owner can use with HPD if a violation is open. In a co-op or condo, we work for the board or managing agent; if a shareholder's renovation damaged the riser, our record shows where the break was, which is the evidence a board needs when it decides who pays. In a two-family or a house, the owner decides. And at the door itself, the rule that matters most is the one about the door locking: HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C condition, which is why we never leave a building's street door repaired at the buzzer but failing at the closer.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Midwood's Architecture

Midwood's housing was built in waves: Ackerson's and Corbin's detached houses around Fiske Terrace and Midwood Park after 1905, the brick two-families and rows of the 1920s, the Art Deco elevator buildings of the late 1930s, and the post-war co-ops and newer infill since. Each wave left a different kind of entry system, and that, more than the brand on the panel, decides how a repair goes.

The Art Deco Elevator Building

Late-1930s six-story buildings like 1717 Avenue N and 1600 Ocean Parkway: ornamental lobbies, heavy original doors, a riser through the core and an intercom replaced at least once inside the original openings.

The Post-War Co-op

Larger 1950s co-ops on Ocean Parkway, such as the 84-unit building at 1119 Ocean Parkway: elevators, a doorman or live-in super, a lobby panel that may ring a desk, and a board that approves every repair.

The Prewar Walk-Up

Smaller apartment houses of four to six stories along the lettered avenues and side streets: a street door, a vestibule, one riser, a cellar supply and tenants on every floor.

The Brick Two-Family

Attached and semi-detached houses on East 13th through 16th Streets: two buttons, one transformer, a split in the cellar and often an upstairs release wired to a strike on the street door.

The Detached Porch House

Victorians, Colonials and Tudors in Midwood Park, Fiske Terrace and along East 19th Street and beyond: porch buttons, chimes, side doors, and sometimes a house intercom on several floors.

The Mixed-Use Avenue Building

Stores at street level with apartments above on Avenue J, Avenue M, Kings Highway and Coney Island Avenue: a residents' door beside the shopfront and a staff door at the back.

How the building type changes the repair

The Art Deco elevator building is a riser and door problem. Its cable is old but generally sound, and its faults collect at the cellar block, at hallway splices and at the street door's strike and closer. Rules and limits at Midwood's commercial and institutional doors

  • Residents' doors in mixed-use buildings: the apartments above a store are a multiple dwelling, so the Attorney General's guide on self-locking entrance doors and intercoms applies to their door, and HPD writes buzzer violations there under the Housing Maintenance Code.
  • Self-closing doors: HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C violation, so a residents'-door repair always includes the closer and the latch.
  • Egress first: at any door people leave through, whether a school entrance, a shul's side door or a store's rear exit, the release must never prevent someone inside from getting out. With an electric strike, the inside lever always opens the door; we explain fail-safe and fail-secure behavior before replacing one.
  • Magnetic locks: a maglock holds the door itself, so it has to release for anyone leaving; the building code ties maglocks on egress doors to specific release conditions, and we don't install one where a strike is the right answer.
  • Line voltage: new circuits and work on the line-voltage side of a supply need a licensed electrician and, where required, a Department of Buildings permit; we name that work separately and coordinate.
  • Reach: a call button or keypad used by the public should sit within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range, with 48 inches as the high forward reach, which matters when an old panel is replaced at a school or office door.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Midwood: Questions Answered

Elevator buildings and their street doors

Our late-1930s building on Ocean Parkway still has its original etched glass and metal-framed street door. Can a new electric strike go in without cutting into the old frame?

Usually, yes, because the frame was cut for a strike or latch keeper long ago and a replacement can often use that same pocket. Strikes are made in many body sizes and faceplate lengths, and the first job is to measure the existing cutout, the latch's position and the frame's depth, then choose a strike that fits what is already there. Where the old pocket is slightly too small, a strike with a shorter body or an adjustable keeper often avoids enlarging it. If no available strike fits without cutting, we tell the board before ordering, with a photo of the frame and the options, so the building decides whether a small, careful enlargement is acceptable. We don't touch the glass, and if the door itself needs rehanging, that is named as a carpenter's or locksmith's part of the job.

Since the strike on our street door was replaced, a key still turns in the lock but the door won't open unless someone buzzes it. How can a new strike stop the key from working?

The key and the strike are meant to be two independent ways of letting the latch go. When the key turns, the lockset pulls the latch back into the door; when the strike is powered, its keeper swings out of the latch's way. If the new strike's keeper is set too tight against the latch, or the strike is mounted a little high or low, the latch can't fully withdraw past it, so the key turns but the door stays caught. A strike of the wrong function or the wrong latch type can do the same. In a Midwood building where some residents rely on the key on Shabbat, that matters a great deal. We check the keeper's position against the latch with the key turned, shim or reset the strike if it is off, and confirm that the strike matches the lockset. When it is set right, the key opens the door cleanly with the strike unpowered.

Our lobby door takes so long to close that people we don't recognize walk in behind residents. Can it be made to shut faster without being a hazard for older neighbors?

Yes, with care. A door closer has separate adjustments for how fast the door swings through most of its arc and how fast it finishes the last few inches, the latch speed. A slow, weak closer leaves a gap of several seconds after every entry, which is exactly when someone can follow a resident in, the situation CBS New York described at East 10th Street and Foster Avenue in 2018. Making the whole swing fast would make the door heavy and dangerous for older residents. The better adjustment is usually a moderate main swing and a firm, reliable latch speed, so the door doesn't slam into people but does finish closing and catch every time. If the closer is worn out and won't hold an adjustment, we replace it and say so in the quote. We set it, test it with the strike, and leave the building a note of the settings.

A whole column of names on our post-war co-op's lobby panel stopped working at once, and the board is talking about buying a new panel. Does it need to be replaced?

Not necessarily. On many panels the buttons are wired in groups, and a column or row that fails together usually points to the one thing that group shares: a connector behind the faceplate, a ribbon or harness, a common wire to the cellar, or one section of a terminal block. Those are repairable. We open the panel, test the group's common connection, and trace it to the cellar block before anyone prices a replacement. If the panel's electronics have genuinely failed and parts are no longer made, we tell the board that too, and quote a panel repair or faceplate and a replacement side by side so the decision is based on what we found, not on a guess.

Two-families and houses

In our two-family on one of the East streets, the upstairs release opens the street door fine, except when the downstairs chime is ringing at the same moment. Then nothing happens. Why?

Because both are drawing on the same small transformer, and it can't carry them together. A chime draws a little current; an electric strike draws much more. When a transformer that was sized for two chimes is also asked to pull a strike, it can manage one load at a time but sags below what the strike needs when the chime is energized too. That is a classic sign of an undersized or aging transformer. We measure its output with the strike alone, then with the chime and strike together, and compare the readings with the strike's rating. The usual fix is a transformer with more capacity for the whole circuit, or a separate supply for the strike so the chime and the door stop competing. Either way, the price is in writing before anything is bought, and any line-voltage connection is an electrician's job.

Our narrow Tudor has four floors and the chime is in the front hall. Nobody hears the bell from the top floor. Can a second chime be added on the same circuit?

Often it can, but adding a chime is an addition, not a repair, so it gets its own line in the quote. Whether the existing transformer can ring two chimes depends on its rating and on what else it feeds, such as a video doorbell or a side-door button. We check the rating and measure the circuit under load, then look at how a wire could reach the top floor in a house that narrow, through an existing chase, a closet stack or alongside other cable, without opening finished walls. If the transformer is too small for a second chime, the quote says so and includes a properly rated one. In some houses, a wireless chime paired to the front-door button is the simpler answer for an upper floor, and we will tell you when that is the case.

Stores, schools and the people who ring

Our store on Avenue J keeps its stock in the cellar, and boxes have ended up stacked against the residents' intercom power supply. It has been dropping out. Can the supply just be moved?

It can be moved, but it shouldn't just be pushed somewhere else. An intercom supply gives off heat and needs air around it, and one buried behind cartons can overheat and shut down or fail early. It also needs to stay where the building's owner and the next technician can reach it. Moving it means extending its low-voltage wiring and, on the line-voltage side, a properly mounted outlet or a connection by an electrician. We first test whether the supply itself has been damaged, then agree a new location with the building owner, not only with the store, because the supply belongs to the residents' system. The quote separates the low-voltage relocation from any electrician's work.

Our yeshiva buzzes parents in at the main entrance at drop-off and pickup, and a few times the door hasn't latched after a crowd came through. What's going on?

Usually the door hardware is losing a fight with the crowd. When dozens of people pass through in a few minutes, the door is pushed, held and let go repeatedly; a closer that has lost its latch speed, a hinge that has worn, or a strike whose keeper has been knocked slightly out of line will all leave the door resting against the frame instead of catching. We check the closer's settings, the hinges and the strike's alignment, and test the door at full swing and from a half-open position the way it is actually used. Whatever we adjust, the door must still open freely from inside for everyone leaving the building, so a school entrance's release is always arranged so that the inside lever or push bar opens the door no matter what the buzzer does.

The exterminator and the gas meter reader both say nobody answers when they ring the tenants' bells in our building. The tenants say they never hear a ring. Who's right?

Possibly both. A tenant review of a building near Midwood complains that the exterminator "rarely rings the tenants bells," so this frustration runs in both directions. If the panel's buttons for some apartments have failed, or the pairs for those apartments are open in the riser, visitors press and hear nothing while the tenant upstairs hears nothing either, and each side assumes the other isn't trying. We test every button on the panel against its apartment, which takes an extra person upstairs or a handset tester at the cellar block, and list which apartments ring and which don't. That list ends the argument and tells the owner exactly which stations or pairs need repair.
