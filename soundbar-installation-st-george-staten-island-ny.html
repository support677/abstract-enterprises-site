The Neighborhood

Why St. George Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Most of Staten Island is a borough of houses with driveways. St. George is not. It is the island's civic centre and its front door: Borough Hall stands on Richmond Terrace with the borough's courthouses close by, the ferry and the Staten Island Railway terminate here, and nearly every local bus route connects at the terminal at the foot of the hill. Real estate guides describe the neighbourhood as roughly 0.41 square miles in the 10301 zip code, one of the most densely built parts of the borough, with a younger population than most of the island. That density is the first thing that changes a soundbar job. A soundbar here goes into a rented apartment as often as an owned house, and the person paying for it is frequently someone who will move within a few years.

The second thing is age. The St. George Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on July 19, 1994, protects houses that the Historic Districts Council dates from the 1830s to the 1930s, set into the steep topography above the shore. HDC's summary is that the neighbourhood only flourished after the 1880s, when the ferry terminal and the Staten Island Rapid Transit lines arrived, and that most of the houses still standing are Colonial Revival, Queen Anne and Shingle Style. In soundbar terms that means wood lath and plaster, tall parlor walls broken up by windows, pocket doors and fireplaces, framing that does not sit where a modern stud finder expects it, and an owner who does not want a single unnecessary hole.

Walk down the hill and the building stock changes again. Two- and three-family houses line the streets climbing south from Richmond Terrace. Pre-war elevator buildings such as the Ambassador Apartments on Daniel Low Terrace hold flats with plaster walls, a managing agent and building rules. The two towers of Castleton Park on St. Marks Place, among the tallest buildings on Staten Island, contain 454 apartments. On the water, Bay Street Landing turned century-old grain, coffee and dry-goods warehouses into loft co-ops and condos in the 1980s, keeping raw concrete ceilings and exposed columns in at least one of its buildings. And the newest homes are rentals such as Lighthouse Point, a 115-unit building of studios and one- and two-bedrooms on New York Harbor beside the terminal.

That spread is why a St. George soundbar job is never a copy of the last one. The technician who mounted a bar on a drywall partition in a new waterfront rental at ten in the morning can be fishing a cable through century-old plaster on the hill by two, and working out how to hang a bar on a concrete wall in a loft with no hollow cavity at all by five. Each needs a different anchor, a different way to hide the wires and, often, a different conversation with whoever owns or manages the building.

The people who live here describe it the same way. On Reddit's r/statenisland, residents answering a newcomer called St. George fairly walkable and pleasant, with a lot of old-style houses that have charm and character, and pointed out that it is the closest you can live to the ferry if you commute into Manhattan. Another thread called it the best neighbourhood on the island for someone starting a Manhattan job, citing an active community with neighbourhood events. Those are the households we meet: commuters who want the visit after work or on a weekend, renters who need the install to leave little behind, and owners of old houses who care more about the plaster than the price.

And St. George is about to change again. In September 2026 the Staten Island Advance reported a draft city plan for up to 2,500 apartments in seven towers on the waterfront around Empire Outlets and the former New York Wheel site, with construction proposed to start by 2029. Smaller projects nearby, including a 14-storey, 117-unit building the City Council approved in August 2026, would add hundreds more. Whatever happens to those plans, more of the neighbourhood's TVs will hang on new-build partitions in the years ahead, and the plaster houses on the hill will still be there.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for St. George's Architecture

Six kinds of home share these few hundred acres, and each one asks a soundbar for something different. Here is how we approach each, starting at the top of the hill and working down to the water.

Historic district houses on the hill

Queen Anne, Shingle Style and Colonial Revival houses between Carroll Place and St. Marks Place, most of them from the decades after the 1880s. Expect wood lath and plaster, often two layers of paint and wallpaper over it, parlors with fireplaces, bay windows and pocket doors, and framing that may be balloon-built, with studs running from the foundation to the roof.

The bracket goes into framing found by test-drilling rather than trusting a scanner, the screws are sized to pull the bracket tight without crushing the plaster keys, and cables either drop inside an open stud bay or run in a cover painted to match the trim.

Fort Hill and the two- and three-family streets

Just south of Hamilton Avenue the houses are similar in age and style to the designated district but outside its boundary, and two- and three-family houses are common on the streets climbing south from Richmond Terrace. Partitions added during those conversions can be anything from plaster on wood lath to drywall to old fibreboard.

We ask who owns the wall before we drill. In a divided house the owner may live upstairs and the TV may be on the wall shared with the stair hall that serves the other units, which is a reason to use a cover instead of opening the wall.

Pre-war elevator co-ops and rentals

Pre-war elevator buildings, such as the Ambassador Apartments on Daniel Low Terrace, typically put flats behind plaster on masonry or clay block, under a managing agent and, for co-ops, an alteration agreement. Paperwork is normal here: proof of insurance, permitted working hours, sometimes a request to keep dust off the corridor.

We send the certificate of insurance free, keep the job inside the unit, and choose a bracket that needs four holes instead of eight when the wall is solid masonry.

The St. Marks Place towers

Castleton Park's two towers hold 454 apartments, a $91 million CAMBA Housing Ventures partnership with Stellar Management aimed at keeping them affordable. Large managed buildings have their own rules for contractors, and many leases expect the tenant to undo anything they add.

For tower renters we favour the methods that leave least behind: a bar on the console, a TV-attached soundbar bracket that uses the TV mount's holes, and a paintable cover rather than in-wall work.

Bay Street Landing lofts

The warehouses were built for cargo, and at least one building kept its raw concrete ceilings and exposed support columns when it became condos. Concrete and brick do not have a hollow cavity to hide a cable in, and the TV often has to stand where the furniture works rather than against a wall.

Anchors here are sleeve or wedge types set with a hammer drill, priced with the concrete add-on, and wiring runs in surface raceway painted to the wall or under a console.

New waterfront rentals

Lighthouse Point and the buildings planned near it belong to the type of new construction that is typically a concrete or steel frame with drywall partitions, often on metal studs. These are the easiest walls in St. George to mount on, but they come with a lease, a management office and sometimes a building-supplied Wi-Fi network.

We use toggle-style anchors rated for the bar, which is light, and leave the walls looking the way the management office expects at move-out.

Two things on that table are worth explaining. First, we treat old plaster as a material to protect, not just a surface to get through. Lime plaster on wood lath grips the lath with small keys of plaster pushed through the gaps; drill too fast or overtighten a screw and those keys snap, and a dinner-plate-sized patch comes loose. Pre-drilling with a sharp masonry bit at low speed and stopping the screw as soon as the bracket is snug avoids most of that. Second, metal studs in new buildings are thin, so we do not screw a bracket into them as if they were wood; a toggle anchor through the drywall carries a soundbar comfortably, because even the big bars are light.

Weight is the reason soundbars are forgiving. According to Sonos, the Arc Ultra is about 46.4 inches wide and weighs about 13 pounds, and the Beam Gen 2 is about 25.6 inches wide at roughly 6.4 pounds. Sanus rates its universal soundbar bracket for most bars up to 20 pounds. Those numbers are well inside what a properly anchored bracket holds in plaster, drywall or concrete; the work is in finding something solid and keeping the wall intact, not in fighting the load.

Commercial Soundbars

Business Soundbar Installation in St. George

St. George is where Staten Island goes to work, see a judge, catch a boat and shop for outlet bargains. The businesses that need sound under a screen here are not the strip-mall dentists of the South Shore; they are offices that orbit the civic centre, shops and restaurants that live on ferry foot traffic, and the shared rooms of large residential buildings.

Richmond Terrace civic centre

Borough Hall, completed in 1906 to a Carrère & Hastings design and still in its original use, anchors a stretch of government buildings and the professional offices that serve them. Meeting rooms with a screen for video calls and reception TVs for visitors are the usual requests; quiet, clear speech matters more than bass.

Empire Outlets and the terminal

Empire Outlets at 55 Richmond Terrace opened on May 15, 2019 beside the St. George Terminal and bills itself as the city's only outlet shopping destination. Retail screens need sound that carries a few metres and no further, mounted where shoppers and trolleys cannot knock it.

Bay Street

Bay Street runs from the terminal past Lighthouse Point and Bay Street Landing toward Tompkinsville and Stapleton. The Bay Street corridor rezoning studied a 20-block, 45-acre area across Tompkinsville, Stapleton and St. George, and restaurants, bars and cafés along it are the typical TV-and-soundbar customers.

Ballpark and harbor-front venues

SIUH Community Park, the former Richmond County Bank Ballpark at 75 Richmond Terrace and home of the Staten Island FerryHawks, draws game-night crowds to the waterfront. Bars and function rooms that fill on those nights want commentary to carry over a crowd, which is where a bar's dialogue mode earns its place.

Building lounges and community rooms

Towers and new rentals often have a lounge, a fitness room or a community room with a TV. These are shared spaces, so the bar is set with a sensible maximum volume, the remote is labelled, and the building manager gets a one-page note of what was set and why.

Museums, schools and civic groups

The National Lighthouse Museum occupies a building of the former lighthouse depot complex that dates back to 1862, a short walk from the ferry, and Curtis High School stands on Hamilton Avenue. Institutions like these sometimes need a soundbar for a single exhibit or meeting room rather than a full sound system; we tell you honestly which one you need.

Rules that apply to business screens

Projection into walkways. The 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2) limit objects mounted on a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to a 4-inch projection into a circulation path. A TV with a soundbar under it, on a reception wall beside a corridor, has to be either above 80 inches at its lowest edge or shallow enough to stay within that 4 inches. We measure this before drilling, because moving a bracket later leaves holes in a finished wall.

Sound that reaches homes. St. George puts restaurants, bars and shops underneath and beside apartments more than anywhere else on the island. The city's noise code guide sets limits on commercial music heard inside a nearby home — 42 decibels, and between 10 PM and 7 AM no more than 7 decibels above the ambient level — and treats low-frequency bass separately. A soundbar in a café is rarely the problem; a subwoofer on the floor against a party wall can be. We put the sub where its bass stays in the room and set a volume ceiling staff cannot easily override.

Power and certificates. Business tenants in managed buildings usually need a certificate of insurance before any contractor works on site. That certificate is free from us. Any new outlet or circuit is an electrician's work, not ours, and we tell you that at quote stage rather than on the day.

Most St. George businesses that call about a soundbar also ask about cameras or the network. If the shop needs both, our commercial security camera installers on Staten Island and the Staten Island network installation team can plan the cabling at the same time, so the screen, the bar and the cameras share one tidy route.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in St. George: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up in St. George and almost nowhere else on Staten Island, grouped by the kind of home that raises them.

Old Houses on the Hill

Our parlor fireplace still has its original mirrored overmantel — where do the TV and soundbar go so it stays untouched?

Not over the fireplace. A carved overmantel with its mirror is usually fixed to the chimney breast with a few hidden screws or cleats, and the mirror glass is old enough that drilling anywhere near it is a risk nobody should take. Nor is it a good height for a screen: the top of a Victorian overmantel can sit well above eye level, and a TV hung there puts the picture at a neck-craning angle and the soundbar far above the ears it is meant to reach.

The answer is almost always another wall. In a front parlor with a bay window, the long wall opposite the fireplace or the wall beside the entrance often works, as long as it is not the one holding the pocket doors (see the next question). If no wall suits, the TV goes on a low console or a stand, and the soundbar sits on top in front of it for $79 with no holes at all. Some owners prefer that in a restored room, because the whole system can be moved out of the way when the room is used for guests.

If you are set on the chimney breast, the only arrangement we would consider leaves the overmantel in place and uses the plain plaster to one side of it, with the TV on a short articulating arm and the bar on a TV-attached bracket. We look at that in person first.

The longest wall in our Victorian parlor hides a pair of pocket doors — can the soundbar and its cables use that wall?

Usually not where the doors slide. A pocket door disappears into a cavity inside the wall, and that cavity has no studs in it for the length of the door. A bracket screwed in there finds nothing behind the plaster, and a screw that is long enough to grip will scrape or jam the door the next time someone closes it. A cable fished through the same cavity has the same problem: the door rubs it every time it moves.

What we do instead is find where the pocket ends. The framing on the far side of the pocket, beyond where the doors stop when they are fully open, is ordinary wall and can carry a bracket. We check that boundary by opening the doors fully, measuring how far they travel, and test-drilling past that point. If the TV has to be centred over the pocket for the room to work, the TV goes on a stand or a console in front of the wall and the bar sits on that, which keeps the doors working and the plaster whole.

Our house is balloon-framed — can the soundbar cables drop down an open stud bay from the attic or come up from the cellar?

Sometimes, and it is one of the few advantages an old house gives you. In balloon framing the studs run in one piece from the foundation sill to the roof plate, so a bay between two studs can be an open chute from cellar to attic. That is how electricians wired many of these houses in the first place. If the bay behind the TV is clear, a fish rod pushed up from the cellar or dropped from the attic can carry HDMI and the in-wall power kit to the spot behind the screen without opening the plaster except for two small plates.

Three things can stop it. Many old houses have had fire blocking added at the floor lines during renovations, which closes the chute. Some bays carry old knob-and-tube wiring or gas pipe that we will not disturb. And a parlor wall on the floor above the cellar is easy, but a second-floor bedroom wall means passing a floor in between. We confirm what we can from the cellar and attic before quoting the in-wall kit at $169, and if the bay is blocked, the fallback is a paintable cord cover for $59.

Balloon framing also means the stud bays connect to the attic, so a hole in the wall can draw air. We seal around the plates we install so the wall does not start whistling in winter.

Can the rear speakers go high on the wall without cutting into the plaster cornice?

Yes. A cornice or crown moulding in an old parlor is run plaster or fixed wood, and it does not need to be touched. Rear surround speakers for most soundbars are small, and the brackets that suit them are fixed with one or two screws, so they can sit on the plain wall a hand's width below the cornice, high enough to keep them out of the way and angled down toward the seats.

The real question in a tall Victorian room is whether high is right at all. Rear speakers for most systems sound best a little above ear height when seated, not at the ceiling. In a parlor where the ceiling is well above the doors, mounting a rear speaker just under the cornice puts it so high that the surround effect floats over your head. We usually set them at about the height of the top of the door casing, or on floor stands behind the sofa, which avoids the plaster entirely.

The other check is power. Many “wireless” rear speakers still need an outlet each, and old parlors rarely have outlets where rears want to go. We tell you before the visit whether your rears need power and where it would come from.

Our TV sits on an antique sideboard — can the cables reach the soundbar without drilling it?

Yes, and we would not drill an antique anyway. A modern media console has holes and grommets built in; a sideboard or a dresser from the 1900s does not, and its back panel is often thin, original and part of what makes it valuable. The cables can almost always go another way.

The usual route is behind the piece: the sideboard stands a little off the wall, the power and HDMI run down the wall behind it in a cover painted to match, and they come up at the back edge of the top where the TV and bar sit. If the sideboard sits flat against the wall, felt pads on its back feet give it enough clearance without anyone noticing. A short, flat HDMI cable lies under the bar where it cannot be seen from the sofa.

The thing to watch is weight and heat. An antique top is not always as solid as it looks, so we check that it carries the TV evenly, and we keep the cable box and any streaming boxes out of closed drawers where they cannot breathe.

Landmarks, Landlords and Divided Houses

Does a speaker or cable fixed to the outside of a landmarked St. George house need a Landmarks permit?

Very possibly, and that is a different matter from anything inside. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's published guidance says owners in a historic district must get a permit before work that affects the exterior of a designated property, while interior work needs one only when it requires a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. A soundbar on a parlor wall is interior work that does not touch the outside. A speaker screwed to a porch post, a cable stapled up a clapboard wall or a hole drilled through the exterior to feed a porch speaker is exterior work on a landmarked building.

The Commission's staff say they can tell an owner whether a permit is needed for a particular job, and that is the right place to ask before anything goes on the outside of a district house. Our practice is simple: we do not drill through the exterior wall or fix anything to the facade of a house in the district unless the owner has confirmed with the Commission first.

For music on a porch there are easier answers. A battery-powered portable speaker that pairs with the soundbar or your phone, kept indoors when not in use, needs nothing fixed to the house at all.

Our apartment is in a subdivided old house and the TV wall is fibreboard, not plaster — what will hold a bracket?

Not the fibreboard itself. Old fibre wallboard of the kind used to divide houses into apartments in the mid-twentieth century is soft and crumbly compared with plaster or modern drywall. An anchor set into it alone can pull out with surprisingly little force, and a soundbar that is bumped while someone dusts is enough to do it.

So the bracket has to reach the framing behind. Partitions like these are usually built on wood studs, which a scanner can find through the board; we confirm each one with a thin test hole before the bracket goes up. If the studs do not fall where the bar needs to be, a painted plywood backer board fixed across two studs ($85) gives the bracket a solid base. In a rented apartment we ask whether you have the owner's agreement first, because the partition belongs to the house.

If the wall turns out to be something stranger, such as fibreboard over old plaster, we tell you on the spot and price it by the published line for whatever is behind it before anything is drilled.

Living on Ferry Time

Can the TV and soundbar switch on by themselves on a timer for the morning news before the boat?

Usually yes, and the soundbar follows the TV rather than needing a timer of its own. Many TVs have an “on timer” or wake-up setting in their system menu that turns the set on at a chosen time and tunes it to a chosen input or channel. When the soundbar is connected to the TV's eARC or ARC socket with HDMI-CEC enabled, it wakes with the TV and plays the sound. We set the volume the TV wakes at, too, so the news comes on at a level that suits seven in the morning rather than last night's film.

Two things decide whether it works. The TV has to offer the timer at all; not every model does, and some only wake to the built-in tuner rather than a cable box. And the cable box or streamer has to be on, or set to wake with the TV, for its channel to appear. We test the whole sequence before we leave by setting the timer a couple of minutes ahead and watching it fire.

Neighbours and Bass

Keeping the Bass in Your Own Home

St. George packs people closer together than anywhere else on Staten Island, and a subwoofer does not respect a lease line. The soundbar itself is rarely the issue; its high and middle frequencies stay mostly in the room. The low notes from a subwoofer travel through floors and walls as vibration, and how far they go depends on how the building is put together. That is different on the hill, in the pre-war buildings, in the towers and in the lofts.

Divided houses on the hill

When an old one-family house was split into two or three apartments, the floors between them usually stayed what they always were: wood joists with boards on top and plaster on the ceiling below. That kind of floor carries a subwoofer's thump straight down. In these houses we keep the sub off the floor boards where we can, sitting it on a dense pad or a solid piece of furniture rather than directly on the boards, place it away from the wall shared with the stair hall, and set the bar's bass level a notch below the factory default. Night mode, which most bars offer, compresses the loud moments so dialogue stays clear without the explosions shaking the ceiling below.

Pre-war masonry buildings

Plaster on brick or clay block blocks airborne sound well, but the floors and the steam pipes running between flats can still carry low notes. Keeping the subwoofer out of corners helps more than people expect, because a corner makes bass louder in the room and louder in the structure. A bar with a built-in woofer and no separate sub is often enough in a one-bedroom flat in these buildings.

Towers and new rentals

Concrete slabs in a tower stop most airborne sound, and neighbours there tend to hear bass only when the sub is on the floor right against a shared wall. The fix is simple placement: a metre or so away from the party wall, facing into the room. In new buildings with drywall-on-metal-stud partitions between units, bass travels sideways more easily than through the slab, so the shared wall is the one to keep the sub away from.

Lofts

Converted warehouses were built to carry heavy loads, and their concrete floors rarely pass bass downstairs the way wood floors do. The challenge in a loft is the opposite: a big, hard room makes everything echo. A sub there can usually be turned up rather than down; the work goes into placing the bar so dialogue cuts through the reflections, and into adding rears if the sofa is far from the TV.

Rooms With a View

Harbor Windows, Parlor Bays and Where the Sofa Goes

In St. George the best wall for the TV is often decided by the view. On the waterfront, living rooms in the newer buildings are laid out around windows facing the harbor and the Manhattan skyline. On the hill, the parlors of the old houses have bay windows on the street side and a fireplace on the inside wall. Either way, the TV usually goes on the wall that is left over, and the soundbar's job is to make that choice sound right.

The rule that matters most is that the bar should face the seats squarely. A soundbar throws its dialogue channel straight ahead and bounces its surround effects off the side walls. If the sofa sits at an angle to the TV so that everyone can also see the water, the dialogue arrives off-centre and the side reflections bounce unevenly, one wall plaster and the other glass. Where the room allows, we suggest turning the sofa to face the TV and keeping a chair by the window for the view. Where it does not, we set the bar's centre channel a little louder and run the room calibration from the actual seat so the bar compensates.

Glass and bare floors make a room bright and echoey; plaster, books and a rug make it calmer. In a new rental with floor-to-ceiling windows, curtains or blinds drawn while watching do more for the sound than any setting, and a rug in front of the TV stops the floor reflecting dialogue back at the screen. In an old parlor with heavy curtains, rugs and upholstered furniture, the opposite can happen and the bar sounds a little muffled until its treble is lifted slightly.

The waterfront is also never completely quiet. A 2026 neighbourhood guide notes higher ambient noise for homes close to the ferry and bus terminal, and a Community Board 1 agenda in 2022 listed ferry horns at the Ferry Maintenance Facility during the night among the issues residents raised. In those apartments we lean on the bar's dialogue or speech mode rather than overall volume, so voices stay clear over the background without the whole system getting louder.

Bay windows deserve a note of their own. A three-sided bay behind the sofa is a poor place for rear speakers, because the angled glass scatters their sound. Rears work better on stands either side of the sofa, slightly behind the listeners, or on the plain walls beside the bay. And if the TV must go in the bay itself, the bar goes on a console in front, not on brackets screwed to the window frames.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in St. George

Search for soundbar installation and you get confident summaries built from national booking sites and manufacturer blogs. Most of what they say is fine for a new house with drywall. Here is where it falls short in a neighbourhood of 1890s plaster, converted warehouses and managed towers.

“Soundbar mounting is a quick job: assess, mount, position, clean up.”

One national booking platform's Staten Island soundbar-mounting listing describes the service as assessing the location, securing the bracket, mounting and positioning the bar, and cleaning up. That is an accurate list for a modern wall, and it is the job we do in a new rental on Bay Street.

It misses the step that takes longest on the hill: finding out what the wall is. A Queen Anne parlor can be lath and plaster over balloon framing, a chimney breast can hide a working flue, and a divided house can have fibreboard partitions. Each changes the anchor, and getting it wrong cracks plaster that is expensive to repair.

So in St. George “assess” means a test hole and a look at the dust, not a glance. It adds minutes, not money, and it is the reason old walls come out of our visits intact.

“Hiding the wires costs $75 to $300.”

Angi's cost guide puts concealed TV wiring in that range nationally. As a national average it is reasonable, and our own in-wall kit at $169 sits inside it.

What the range cannot tell you is whether in-wall work is possible at all. In a Bay Street loft there is no cavity to hide a cable in; in a tower rental the lease may forbid opening the wall; in a balloon-framed house the bay may be open from cellar to attic or blocked at every floor.

That is why we quote in-wall concealment only after the route is confirmed, and why a $59 paintable cover is the honest answer for much of St. George. The price follows the building, not a national average.

“You need eARC to get Dolby Atmos.”

Some product guides state it flatly. eARC does carry Atmos at full quality, and when both the TV and bar have it we use it.

But owners in Sonos's own community forum point out that eARC is not required for Atmos: many TVs pass Atmos over ordinary ARC inside a Dolby Digital Plus stream, which is how most streaming services deliver it anyway. Plenty of St. George renters have a TV a few years old with ARC only.

So before anyone tells you to replace a working TV, we check what the TV and the services you watch actually output. Often the answer is a settings change, not a purchase.

“Atmos bars bounce sound off any ceiling.”

Polk Audio's Atmos setup guide is more careful: up-firing drivers want a flat, rigid, reflective ceiling, roughly 7.5 to 12 feet high, and acoustic tiles defeat them.

St. George has every kind of ceiling. A parlor ceiling on the hill can be taller than that range; a loft at Bay Street Landing may be raw concrete with beams and ducts breaking up the surface; a tower apartment's ceiling is usually flat and in range.

We look up before we recommend an Atmos bar. In a room where the ceiling will not cooperate, a bar without up-firing drivers plus rear speakers often sounds better and costs less.

“The best height for a soundbar is just below the TV.”

That line appears on booking-platform pages, and for a TV hung at a normal height it is right. Dolby's own guidance is to put the bar below the TV and near ear level, and Yamaha puts seated ear height at around 42.5 inches.

The trouble in old houses is that the TV is often not at a normal height. Where an overmantel, a radiator or a window seat pushes the screen up the wall, “just below the TV” can still be well above the ears of anyone on the sofa.

In those rooms the bar is often better on the console below, angled up slightly, even when the TV is on the wall. We show you both before drilling.

“Wireless rear speakers need no wiring.”

The box says wireless, and the audio link is. Power is another matter: Crutchfield's listing for Samsung's HW-Q990D notes that each rear speaker needs its own AC outlet, and the subwoofer needs one too.

In a new rental that is rarely a problem. In a Victorian parlor with two outlets, both on the fireplace wall, it can be the deciding factor, because an extension cord across a doorway is a trip hazard and a fire risk.

We count outlets at quote stage. If the rears cannot be powered sensibly, we say so and suggest a bar that does not need them.

“Any internet connection is fine for streaming to a soundbar.”

For the TV's own sound over HDMI, the internet does not matter at all. For streaming, Netflix recommends about 15 megabits per second for 4K, and app-controlled bars need a network they can join and see.

In St. George the snag is not speed but the network itself. Large buildings sometimes supply Wi-Fi through a managed system with a sign-in page, and some older bars, such as Sonos's Beam Gen 2, reach the router only over the 2.4 GHz band or Sonos's own wireless mesh, and 2.4 GHz is crowded in a dense block.

We check the network the bar will live on, and where it cannot join cleanly, we set it up so the TV sound works perfectly and tell you exactly what to ask the building for.

St. George Soundbar Installation FAQ

How can I tell whether our house is inside the St. George Historic District?
+

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the district on July 19, 1994. As the Historic Districts Council describes it, the district runs from the south side of Carroll Place to the north side of St. Marks Place, and from Nicholas Street to the west side of Westervelt Avenue. Fort Hill, just south of Hamilton Avenue, is outside it; residents have asked for an extension there. To check a single address, use the LPC's online map of designated properties. For a soundbar that stays indoors, the answer rarely changes what we do.

Our Queen Anne house has a rounded turret room. Can a TV and soundbar go on a curved wall?
+

Usually not on the curve itself. A flat bracket touches a curved wall at two points and leaves the TV rocking, and most turret walls are plaster on closely spaced framing, so there is little to anchor into. The better spots are the flat wall the turret opens from, or a low console inside the bay, where the bar sits on top for $79 and nothing is drilled. If you want the TV in the turret anyway, we look at it in person first.

Can we try the soundbar at a couple of heights before anything is drilled?
+

Yes, and on an old wall we prefer it. The technician holds the bar, or a strip of painter's tape the same length, at two or three heights under the TV while you sit where you normally watch. You choose; we mark the centre and level line, check what is behind the plaster there, and only then drill. It adds a few minutes, not a charge.

Our living room has a wall of old mirror tiles where the TV would go. Can you mount through them?
+

We do not drill mirror glass; it cracks too easily and the glued tiles can come off with the bracket. The choices are to have the tiles taken down by a glazier or the building first, then mount on the wall underneath at the normal price for whatever that wall is, or to leave the mirror alone and set the bar on a console under a TV on its stand, which is $79 for the bar.

Our shop has an old pressed-tin wall. Can a soundbar bracket go through it?
+

Often, yes. Pressed tin is a thin sheet nailed over furring or plaster, so the bracket's screws have to reach the framing or masonry behind it, not the tin. We find that structure first, drill the tin cleanly so the pattern doesn't dent, and price the job by the published add-on for whatever is behind it, such as plaster at $45 or brick at $65.

Our building supplies the Wi-Fi and we don't have the router login. Can the soundbar's streaming still be set up?
+

Yes for the TV sound itself, because HDMI eARC or ARC does not use the network at all. Streaming music and app control need the bar to join a Wi-Fi network, which works on most building networks with a normal password. Some managed networks show a sign-in page or keep devices from seeing each other, which can stop the app finding the bar; if so, we set it up over Bluetooth or through the TV, and tell you what to ask building management for.
