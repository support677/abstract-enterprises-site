The County

In Westchester, Co-ops Outsell Condos, and Their Front Doors Show the Strain

Most people outside the county picture Westchester as single-family houses on wooded lots. Boards know better. In the twelve months through October 2026, OneKey MLS counted 1,581 co-op closings in Westchester County against 1,250 condominium closings, at a twelve-month median co-op price of $240,000. That is roughly 130 co-op apartments changing hands every month. For building security for co-ops, the number that matters most is not the price but the turnover. Every one of those sales is a fob to cancel, a directory name to change, an intercom station that a new shareholder will test on the first evening, and sometimes a set of keys that never came back from the seller.

The listings show where that stock sits. On the day we wrote this page a public listing site carried 466 co-ops for sale across Westchester County, and the first page alone ran from Yonkers ZIP codes 10701, 10703, 10704 and 10710 through Mount Vernon, Eastchester, Bronxville, New Rochelle, White Plains, Rye, Tarrytown, Croton-on-Hudson, Peekskill, Briarcliff Manor, Mount Kisco and North Salem. The features the sellers chose to mention tell a security contractor a lot: Mount Vernon listings that advertised a gate and "security," Yonkers listings that leaned on parking, a pool or being near the train, and elevator buildings in Yonkers, New Rochelle and Peekskill. That is a county of garden complexes with lots and gates, mid-century towers with garages and elevators, and older buildings within walking distance of Metro-North, sometimes all on the same managing agent's list.

Some of the stock is old enough to have its own historians. A local history published in February 2026 describes Garth Road in Eastchester, just south of the Scarsdale Harlem Line station, as a street of 13 residential buildings, "nearly all cooperatively-owned," built in waves between 1926 and 1966. Buildings like that carry nearly a century of wiring decisions in their walls: a cloth-covered riser from the first intercom, a second system added in the 1970s, a camera cable someone pulled through a dumbwaiter shaft. At the other end of the scale, Habitat described one Yonkers complex in 2022 as the largest co-op in the county: 778 units in five buildings, with no doormen. A co-op that size runs its entire front-door security through intercom panels and locks, which is exactly why what happened inside its panels, covered further down this page, is worth every board's attention.

Westchester co-ops also have a support network most areas lack. The Westchester Cooperative and Condominium Advisory Council, formed in 1979 and part of the Building and Realty Institute, was described in The Cooperator as representing more than 400 co-ops and condos in Westchester and the Mid-Hudson region. Boards here compare notes, share vendor experiences and ask hard questions of contractors. We write this page for that kind of reader: a treasurer who wants the number before the meeting, a president who wants to know what the law actually says outside New York City, and a managing agent who needs one co-op security contractor for buildings in three different municipalities.

Westchester County facts that shape a co-op security job

  • 1,581 co-op sales in twelve months: through October 2026, compared with 1,250 condo sales and 4,544 single-family sales; co-op median $240,000, days on market 56 (OneKey MLS, Westchester County, October 2026). High turnover means credential lists go stale quickly unless someone owns them.
  • The Multiple Residence Law, not the Multiple Dwelling Law: the state's Multiple Residence Law applies to "all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population and to all towns and villages" (MRL §3). Yonkers, the county's largest city, counted 211,569 people in the 2020 Census (NYS Comptroller), so even Yonkers falls under the MRL. The New York City intercom rules most online articles quote do not govern a Westchester building.
  • A county fair-housing clock for co-op applications: since August 1, 2021, Westchester's law gives co-op boards deadlines to acknowledge an application and to approve or reject a complete one within 60 days, plus fair-housing training for board members (Westchester County Human Rights Commission). It doesn't regulate security equipment, but it does set the pace at which new shareholders arrive and need credentials.
  • Annual alarm permits in Yonkers: the city's alarm chapter requires a permit to be renewed every year, gives a 30-day grace period for a newly installed system, and treats the first two false alarms in a calendar year as warnings; more than four in a year may lead to suspension or revocation (City of Yonkers Code, alarm systems).
  • Heat as well as storms: Con Edison said Westchester "experienced the greatest impact" during the early-July 2026 heat event, with the largest outages in Greenburgh, Yonkers, Elmsford, Dobbs Ferry and Rye (Con Edison, July 5, 2026).
  • A licensed trade: installing or servicing security and fire alarm systems as a business requires a New York Department of State license under General Business Law §69-m. Ours is NYS #12000287431.

Who usually picks up the phone

In Westchester the first call rarely comes from a shareholder. It comes from a managing agent who handles a dozen co-ops between Yonkers and White Plains, from a super who has replaced the same door release three times this year, or from a board member who chairs the building committee and has been asked to "look into the intercom." Self-managed co-ops are common too, especially smaller garden complexes and walk-ups where the treasurer also handles the contracts. Each of them needs something slightly different from us. The agent wants a proposal format that matches the other bids on the desk. The super wants to know which parts to keep in the basement. The board member wants a plain explanation of the options and a number that will survive the board meeting. We write proposals for all three at once.

Intercoms

Co-op Intercom Upgrades in Westchester: Replace, Repair or Phase It

The intercom is where nearly every Westchester co-op security conversation begins, because it is the system shareholders touch every day and the one that fails in the most visible way. A visitor stands outside pressing a button that does nothing; a delivery driver leaves a notice instead of a package; an elderly shareholder walks down four flights to let in a home aide. By the time the board asks for a co-op intercom upgrade, the building has usually spent several years paying for repairs on a system whose parts are getting hard to find.

The first decision is whether to replace at all. A lobby panel that is twenty-five or thirty years old can often be kept running for a while with a new power supply, a rebuilt release circuit or a refurbished amplifier, and for a co-op that has a facade or boiler project already eating the reserve, that may be the right answer for a year or two. What changes the answer is the riser. If testing shows the old pairs are sound, a replacement can reuse them, and the project becomes a new lobby panel plus new apartment stations or phone calling, with no walls opened. If the riser has failed in several places, the board is choosing between re-cabling, a wireless or app-based apartment end, or a mix. Trade reporting on intercom replacement makes the same point: one installer interviewed by The Cooperator advised boards to "retain the existing infrastructure with the exception of the panel" where they can, because full rewiring is "expensive and highly disruptive" (The Cooperator, Nov. 2025).

The path we recommend for most Westchester boards is a written scope first and bids second. The scope lists every entrance, the number of apartments and floors, which apartments have been combined, what is installed now, what works and what does not, and what the board wants shareholders to be able to do: answer on a wall station, on a phone, or both; open the door from an app; see the visitor. With that scope in hand, two or three co-op intercom installation bids can be compared line by line instead of as three totals that describe three different systems. Our board guide to co-op intercom upgrades walks through writing that scope, comparing bids and notifying shareholders. For the equipment itself, our Westchester intercom installation page lays out the county's ranges: a video intercom for a four- to eight-unit building runs $1,200–$3,500, a multi-unit building system with a tenant directory $1,200–$5,000, and IP systems with smartphone access for larger buildings $2,500–$8,000 and up.

How the building type changes a coop intercom project

Prewar elevator buildings near the stations. The buildings along Garth Road, in Bronxville and around the older village centers often have handsome lobbies the shareholders want left alone: marble or terrazzo floors, wood paneling, a brass mail wall. The panel location is usually fixed by the original vestibule, and the job becomes finding a panel that fits the existing opening or a faceplate that covers the old cut. Cable routes are the hard part; the original riser may run through a shaft that has been closed for decades, so we test before we promise anything.

Postwar towers and mid-rises. The larger buildings in Yonkers, New Rochelle and White Plains tend to have one main lobby, a garage entrance, a service door and sometimes a second lobby on another street. Their intercoms were often installed in one go when the building went up or converted, so every apartment has the same station model, which makes a phased replacement easier. The decision here is usually how many entry points get full video panels and how many get a reader and a simple call button.

Garden complexes. Hartsdale, Mount Vernon, parts of Yonkers and the Sound Shore have co-ops spread over several two- and three-story buildings with a stair hall at each entrance. Each stair hall may serve six to twelve apartments, so the project is many small panels rather than one large one, and the cable between buildings matters as much as the cable inside them.

What shareholders get at the apartment end

There are three main choices, and most Westchester co-ops end up mixing them. A wall station in each apartment, audio or video, is the most familiar and the easiest for older shareholders; it needs working wiring to every apartment. Phone calling, where the lobby panel rings a shareholder's mobile or landline, avoids the riser entirely but depends on the panel's connection and the shareholder's phone. An app adds video and remote door release but asks every shareholder to install and keep using it. We price all three on the proposal so the board can choose apartment by apartment if it wants to, and we flag which apartments' wiring tested poorly so the board knows where a wall station would cost more.

What the law says, briefly

Online articles about co-op intercoms are almost all written for New York City, where the Multiple Dwelling Law sets intercom requirements for certain buildings. That law applies to cities of 325,000 people or more. Westchester buildings fall under the Multiple Residence Law instead (MRL §3), along with the state building and fire code as enforced by the city, town or village. We don't give legal advice, and a board with a question about its obligations to shareholders should ask the co-op's attorney; what we can do is make sure the system we install works, is documented, and keeps the door locking and releasing the way the board intends.

Panel Audit

What Is Wired Into Your Entry Panel? A Yonkers Story Every Board Should Know

In early 2022 Habitat reported on a problem at the county's largest co-op, the 778-unit Yonkers complex mentioned above. The managing agent's intercom company opened a misbehaving panel and found a device nobody on the board had approved: an Amazon Key for Business unit, installed inside the intercom so delivery drivers could open the door without buzzing anyone. According to the article, the installer was a local vendor, and the service agreements had been signed by people with no authority to sign for the co-op. A similar device turned up at another building the same agent managed; the super there caught an installer at work, a police report was filed and the box was removed. Amazon ended its relationship with that vendor and offered to reimburse the co-ops for the intercom company's time (Habitat, Feb. 17, 2022). The agent's comment to Habitat is the lesson in four words: "At least ask permission."

Nobody was hurt and the devices came out. But the story shows something that applies to every cooperative apartment building in the county. An entry panel is a small computer and a set of relays wired to the lock on the most important door in the building. Anything connected to those relays can open that door. Over twenty or thirty years, a busy panel collects connections: a key switch for the fire department, a relay for a delivery service, a link to a long-gone alarm company, a second power supply someone added when the first one ran hot, a camera tapped off the panel's video output. Some are harmless and useful. Some are not documented anywhere. A board that can't list what is connected to its front door doesn't fully control it.

What a co-op panel audit covers

  • Every wire on the door release circuit: where it goes, what can energize it, and whether that device belongs to the co-op, a vendor or nobody.
  • Every network connection: for IP and cloud panels, which accounts can log in, which integrations are switched on, and who holds the administrator password.
  • Every power supply in the chain: panel, strike or maglock, amplifier, reader controller, each one tagged and its breaker or outlet noted.
  • Every credential type that can open the door: keys, codes, fobs, app accounts, delivery services, fire service override.
  • A plain written list the board files with its building records, with a photo of each connection, and a recommendation on anything that should come off.

We don't remove anything on the audit visit without the board's say-so. If a device belongs to a delivery service or another vendor, the board decides whether to keep it, ask for a proper agreement or have it taken out. If a connection is part of the fire alarm or a fire service override, it stays, and we tell the board who maintains it. An audit like this is often done on the $250 service call for a single-entrance building; a complex with many panels gets a written price for the whole survey first.

Repairs

Door Buzzer and Intercom Repair for Westchester Cooperative Buildings

Most of our Westchester co-op calls are repairs, and most repairs fall into a short list of faults. The door won't release from the apartments, or releases from some and not others. A floor or a line of apartments stopped ringing. The lobby speaker is dead or howls. The panel reboots, freezes or shows an error. The strike buzzes but the door stays shut, usually because the door has dropped on its hinges or the closer has been adjusted. Each of those has a handful of usual causes, and a technician who has seen the same panel models in dozens of buildings can usually narrow it down in the first half hour.

A co-op door buzzer repair starts with the $250 service call. On that visit we test the panel, the power supplies, the release circuit and the riser at the points we can reach, and either fix it then or give the board a firm price for the part and the labor. Our Westchester door buzzer repair page publishes the component ranges we use on co-op repairs in the county, and the table below repeats them. For video panels, app-based systems and intercoms that need a full diagnostic rather than a buzzer fix, our Westchester intercom repair page goes deeper; where its examples differ from the ranges here, the written price after the visit is the one that counts.

Faults we see again and again in the county

Releases that fail after a heat wave. Power supplies and transformers in a closet or a boiler-room corner run hot all summer. When a long heat spell is followed by voltage dips, the weakest supply gives out first. The symptom is often a release that works in the morning and fails by evening.

Upper floors that go quiet. In tall buildings, a single bad splice or a corroded terminal block in the riser can cut off everything above it. The fix may be one junction box, which is why we trace before we quote a re-cable.

Doors that won't latch. A surprising number of "intercom" calls are door problems: a sagging door, a worn closer, a strike plate that has shifted a few millimeters. Electronics can't open a door that is bound against its frame, and they can't lock one that never closes. We adjust what we can on the visit and tell the board when a door or frame needs a carpenter or locksmith.

Two systems fighting over one lock. Where a building added fobs years after the intercom, the reader controller and the panel sometimes share the strike without proper isolation. Each works on its own; together they cause intermittent faults. Separating them properly is usually a small job.

Buildings that want a simple upgrade rather than a repair, say a new button panel or a basic video buzzer at a walk-up, can see our door buzzer installation page for Westchester County.

Access Control

Key Fobs and Access Control for Westchester Co-ops With High Turnover

With roughly 1,600 co-op sales a year in the county, the hardest part of co-op access control in Westchester is not the hardware. It is the list. Every sale, sublet approval, new aide, new porter and change of managing agent adds or removes someone who can open the building's doors. Keys can't keep up with that: nobody knows how many copies of the side-door key exist, and changing a cylinder means handing new keys to everyone. Fobs and phone credentials solve the problem only if someone actually maintains the list.

A well-run fob system for a Westchester co-op has three things. First, one credential list for every shared door: lobby, side and service doors, garage, laundry, storage, bike room, roof and any package room. A shareholder's fob is shut off once and stops working everywhere. Second, a clear owner: usually the managing agent, sometimes the super, with board members able to see the list but not necessarily change it. Third, a routine tied to the co-op's own paperwork. When the board approves a sale, the buyer's fobs are issued at the closing and the seller's are turned off the same day. When the county's fair-housing clock is running on an application, nothing changes in the access system until the board has actually approved the buyer.

Our Westchester access control installation page covers hardware in detail and publishes the county ranges: $1,500 to $2,500 for a single-door keypad or fob reader, $4,000 to $15,000 for a multi-door system, and $15,000 to $40,000 and up for a full building with elevator restriction. For a co-op, the proposal breaks that down door by door, so the board can start with the lobby and the garage and add laundry and storage rooms later.

Mag lock or electric strike, and keeping egress free

Most Westchester co-op doors take an electric strike, which works with the existing lockset and leaves the door mechanically lockable if power fails. Glass storefront-style lobby doors and some double doors take a magnetic lock instead. Either way, the door must always open freely from the inside, and a maglock needs a proper request-to-exit device and a release tied to the fire alarm where the building has one. Those details are set by the state code and checked by the local building department, so we design to them and show the board where each piece goes.

Privacy rules: what applies here and what doesn't

Boards that read about New York City sometimes ask whether the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act governs their fob system. That law is a city law, New York City Local Law 63 of 2021, written for "smart access buildings" within the five boroughs (Local Law 63 of 2021). It doesn't extend to Yonkers or Scarsdale. That doesn't mean anything goes. A sensible co-op still decides in writing how long door logs are kept, who can see them and what happens to a former shareholder's data, and many attorneys recommend just that. We set the system up to match whatever retention and access policy the board adopts.

Elevators

Elevator Access Control in Westchester's Elevator Co-ops

Westchester has a large share of elevator co-ops: the towers in Yonkers and New Rochelle, the mid-rises around White Plains and Hartsdale, the older elevator buildings near the Harlem Line stations. In those buildings, the lobby door is only the first line. A visitor who gets past it, or a tailgater who follows a shareholder in, can ride to any floor. A reader in the elevator car closes that gap: shareholders tap a fob to reach their own floor or every residential floor, visitors buzzed in from the lobby are sent to the floor that let them in, and the garage and lobby stops stay open to everyone.

Elevator work is always a joint job. We install the reader and the controller and wire them to the car; the building's elevator maintenance company makes the connections inside the elevator controller and signs off on the work. Our elevator access control installation page for Westchester starts at $1,875 per car for a Level 1 card, fob, code or intercom reader and $3,125 per car for Level 2 biometric readers; the elevator company's charges are billed separately by that company. For a co-op with two or three cars, Level 1 in every car is the usual first step, and it uses the same fobs as the lobby.

A few points come up in nearly every Westchester elevator project. Older cars may need an elevator company upgrade before any reader can be connected, and the board should get that price before committing. Fire service operation always overrides the reader, so firefighters are never locked out. Movers, contractors and aides can be given time-limited credentials for a single day or a set schedule. And the board should decide in advance whether the reader applies around the clock or only overnight; some co-ops leave the car open during the day and restrict it after 10 p.m.

Cameras

Co-op Security Cameras in Westchester: The Short Version

Cameras have their own pages in our series, so this section stays brief. In a Westchester co-op, the first cameras usually go in four places: the lobby or vestibule, aimed so a face is seen as someone comes through the inner door; the mail and package area, from above, so hands are visible; the garage ramp or lot entrance; and any side or service door that gets propped. Garden complexes add the walks between buildings and the lot; towers add the garage levels, the laundry room and sometimes the elevator lobby on each floor. A dedicated co-op camera page for the county is being prepared. Until then, our apartment building security camera installation page for Westchester covers multifamily layouts in detail and publishes an installed range of $625 to $1,875 per camera in the county.

Three board decisions matter more than the camera model. Who can view live and recorded video, and from where? How long footage is kept before it is overwritten? And what happens when the police, a shareholder or an insurer asks for a clip? The Cooperator has noted that a co-op's governing documents typically bar owners from changing common elements without board approval, and that recording audio can raise issues under the state's eavesdropping law (The Cooperator). We install video-only cameras in common areas, point them away from apartment doors and windows, and leave the board with a short written note of what each camera sees, so the camera policy can be written against the actual system.

Packages

Packages, Vestibules and Lobbies Without a Doorman

Package theft from co-op lobbies is a real Westchester problem, not a national headline borrowed for a sales pitch. In late 2024 Yonkers police arrested three people after reports of suspicious activity on Bronx River Road; officers found several open Amazon packages with the contents removed, taken from the lobby of a building nearby, and the three were charged with petit larceny, possession of stolen property and possession of burglary tools (ABC7 New York). A police lieutenant's point to reporters was simple: "The sooner we get the information, the sooner we can react."

The numbers have since moved in the right direction. In June 2026, News 12 reported that package thefts had fallen in several Westchester departments: Yonkers police counted a 50% decrease at the First Precinct, and White Plains had about 13 reports so far that year against 18 in the same period of 2025. White Plains' deputy commissioner of public safety still told the station, "Cameras are still good" (News 12 Westchester, June 9, 2026). For a co-op, a falling citywide count doesn't help much if the building's own vestibule is the soft spot, and every board that has had to tell a shareholder "we don't know who took it" understands why.

What actually works in a cooperative building lobby

  • Move packages behind a second locked door. If drivers leave boxes in the outer vestibule, anyone who walks in can take them. A fob-controlled package room, or a delivery code that opens only the inner door during set hours, keeps them behind a lock.
  • Know which credential opened the door. A delivery code or a driver credential that is separate from shareholder fobs means every delivery entry is logged and can be turned off without affecting residents.
  • Put the camera where the package sits. One camera covering the shelf or the locker bank, mounted high and looking down, records hands and faces at the moment it matters.
  • Keep the doors closing. A propped inner door or a closer that never quite latches undoes everything else, so we check both on every package-area job.
Power

Heat Waves, Storms and Con Edison: Keeping a Westchester Co-op's Doors Working

Westchester co-op boards tend to think about power in winter, when storms bring trees down on lines. The summer of 2026 was a reminder that heat matters too. During the early-July heat event, Con Edison said Westchester "experienced the greatest impact," naming Greenburgh, Yonkers, Elmsford, Dobbs Ferry and Rye as having the greatest outages (Con Edison, July 5, 2026). A month earlier, on June 7, a storm cut power to more than 5,000 Westchester customers in places including Bronxville, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle, Scarsdale, White Plains and Yonkers (Con Edison).

An entry system is only as good as its power. When the lights go out, a co-op's front door does one of two things: it stays locked and the intercom goes dark, so shareholders need a key; or, with some maglock setups, it unlocks. Neither is acceptable for hours at a time in a building full of people. Brownouts and voltage dips during a heat wave are often worse for electronics than a clean outage, because supplies and panels brown out, reboot and sometimes corrupt their settings.

What we put in so the doors keep working

  • Battery backup sized for the system: a UPS or battery-backed power supply on the panel, the release and the reader controller, sized to carry them through the outages the building actually gets.
  • Power supplies that aren't cooking: moved out of boiler rooms and sealed closets where we can, or vented and replaced with units rated for the heat.
  • Surge protection where cable leaves the building, such as a panel at a detached garage or a camera on a light pole.
  • Settings saved off the device: directory, codes and credentials backed up, so a panel that loses its memory in a brownout is restored in minutes instead of being reprogrammed from scratch.
  • A known fail state for every door: written down, agreed with the board and matched to the building code, so the super knows what the door will do before the next outage instead of during it.
Scope

Cabling, Fire Alarm, Alarm Permits and What We Leave to Other Trades

Low-voltage cable and the building's small network

Modern co-op security equipment runs on network cable. A video panel, an IP camera, a fob controller and an elevator reader all want Cat6 and a switch, and many want power over the same cable. In older Westchester buildings the cable is often the biggest unknown in the project: the riser may be eighty years old, the conduit full, and the only practical path a shaft or a closet stack. We survey routes before pricing, reuse what tests well, and run new cable where it doesn't. The building's security equipment then lives in one labeled cabinet with its own switch, kept apart from the shareholders' internet. For larger cabling scopes, our structured cabling installation page for Westchester County covers drops, racks and certification.

Fire alarm work, within our scope

Fire alarm systems in co-op buildings are life-safety systems, and in Westchester they are reviewed by whichever city, town or village the building is in, under the New York State Uniform Code. Yonkers, for example, has its own fire code chapter that refers to "the New York Uniform Code" and places enforcement with the city's Fire Commissioner (Yonkers Fire Code). Fire alarm projects start with drawings, go through local review and end with an acceptance test, and they are priced per building after that process is mapped out; we don't publish fire alarm prices. Our fire alarm installation page for Westchester County explains that path. On a security project, our job is to make sure maglocks and access-controlled doors release when the fire alarm calls for it, and that nothing we install interferes with the fire alarm system.

Burglar alarms and city permits

Some co-ops want a monitored alarm on the management office, a storage room or a roof door. Where they do, the municipality's alarm rules apply. Yonkers requires an alarm permit renewed every year, gives a new system a 30-day grace period, issues written warnings for the first two false alarms in a calendar year and may suspend or revoke a permit after more than four (City of Yonkers Code). Other Westchester municipalities have their own versions. We set the board up with the permit paperwork and design the system to keep false alarms down: door contacts that are properly aligned, entry delays long enough for the super, and no motion sensors where heating vents or hanging signs will trip them. Our alarm installation page for Westchester County has the details, including the monitoring options we offer.

Electrical work belongs to the building's electrician

We handle the low-voltage side: panels, readers, strikes, maglocks, cameras, cable and power supplies plugged into existing outlets or circuits. If the project needs a new high-voltage circuit, a dedicated outlet in the equipment closet or a hardwired connection, that is work for a licensed electrician. Westchester County requires plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade license (Westchester County Consumer Protection), and we coordinate with the co-op's electrician or recommend that the board bring one in.

Virtual doormen and "managed security"

We don't run a virtual doorman, a remote concierge or a managed security subscription. We install and maintain the intercom, cameras and access control that the building's staff, or a virtual-doorman provider the board hires, will use, and we can connect new equipment to a provider the co-op already has. Boards should be wary of any contract that ties the hardware to a monthly service they can't leave.

Board Process

Getting a Westchester Co-op Security Project From Proposal to Install

The technical side of a co-op security project is usually the easy part. The harder part is moving it through a board, a managing agent and a few hundred shareholders without stalling. Here is how the projects we see in Westchester actually move.

Approval and the business judgment rule

Security decisions are board decisions. New York's highest court held in 1990 that, so long as a co-op board "acts for the purposes of the cooperative, within the scope of its authority and in good faith," courts will not substitute their judgment for the board's (Levandusky v. One Fifth Ave. Apt. Corp.). That standard applies statewide, including in Westchester. In practice it means the board should be able to show a reasoned process: a survey, comparable bids, a vote. We supply the paperwork that makes that process easy to document.

Requests for proposals and comparing bids

Many Westchester co-ops put security work out to bid, and the managing agent often runs the process. A good RFP describes the building and what the board wants, not a brand. When bids come back, the board should check that each one includes the same entrances, apartment stations or app licenses, door hardware, power, cable, programming, training and warranty. Habitat once described a co-op board member who wrote the intercom RFP himself and scored five sealed proposals before choosing an installer that reused the building's existing copper (Habitat). That level of structure is exactly what makes bids comparable. We respond to RFPs and board bid requests, and our proposals are itemized so each line can be matched against the others.

Certificates of insurance and alteration agreements

Managing agents will ask for our certificate of insurance naming the co-op and the agent. We issue it once the job is awarded, not with the quote. Work inside apartments, such as replacing wall stations, is normally covered by the co-op's own scope rather than a shareholder's alteration agreement, because the intercom is building equipment; if the board's rules say otherwise, we follow them.

Telling shareholders

Shareholders accept a new system far more easily when they know what is coming. We give the board a notice it can adapt: the dates, what will be off and for how long, whether anyone needs to be home for a station swap, how fobs or app invitations will be handed out, and who to call with questions. For large buildings we schedule apartment work floor by floor and post the schedule in the lobby and the elevator.

Compliance notes for Westchester co-op security work

  • State security and fire alarm installer license under GBL §69-m (ours: NYS #12000287431).
  • Multiple Residence Law and the state Uniform Code, as enforced by the city, town or village building department.
  • Municipal alarm permits where a monitored alarm is installed (Yonkers requires annual renewal).
  • Elevator connections made by the elevator maintenance company.
  • New high-voltage circuits by a county-licensed electrician.
  • No New York City rules (Multiple Dwelling Law intercom sections, Tenant Data Privacy Act) unless the co-op's attorney advises otherwise.
Your Questions Answered

Westchester Co-op Boards Ask, We Answer

These questions come from boards, agents and supers in the county. Costs, fobs, elevators and cameras are covered in their own sections, so they aren't repeated here.

Who controls the front door

Someone installed a delivery device inside our intercom panel without board approval. How do we find out what else is connected to our entry system?

Book a panel audit. We open the panel, the power supplies and any controllers tied to the door, trace every wire on the release circuit and every network connection, and photograph each one. The result is a written list of what is connected, who it appears to belong to and whether it can open the door. Anything the board didn't approve is flagged with a recommendation. We don't pull devices on the spot unless the board tells us to, because some, like a fire service override, have to stay.

After the audit, the board should keep the list with its building records and update it whenever anything changes. The managing agent should know that no vendor, delivery company or contractor connects anything to the door without written approval. And if the panel is cloud-managed, the board should change the administrator password and remove any user accounts that don't belong to the co-op or its agent.

A delivery company is offering to add keyless access to our lobby door for free. What should the board ask before saying yes?

Ask who signs the agreement and on whose authority, who owns the device, what exactly it can do (open which doors, at what hours, for which drivers), whether every entry is logged and who can see that log, how it is powered, and how the co-op can switch it off or have it removed. Ask, too, whether it connects to the panel's release circuit or to a separate relay; a separate, clearly labeled relay is much easier to manage. The Yonkers case Habitat reported shows what happens when those questions are skipped.

Many boards decide that a time-limited delivery code or a separate package room is simpler. If the board does go ahead, we can install the device on its own labeled relay, with its own power, so the rest of the entry system doesn't depend on it.

Old buildings and old lists

Our 1920s co-op near the Scarsdale station has an original lobby the shareholders love. Can a new entry panel fit in without looking out of place?

Usually, yes. Most panel makers offer flush-mount or recessed versions and several finishes, and the panel can often go into the existing opening with a custom faceplate that covers the old cutout. Where the original directory is a framed brass board, some buildings keep it as decoration and mount a slim video panel beside it. Readers can be small enough to sit in a door frame or behind a decorative plate.

We bring finish samples and photos to the board, and we mock up the panel's size and position with tape on the wall before anything is ordered, so shareholders can see it in place. The wiring for a prewar lobby is the bigger question, and we answer it by testing the existing riser first.

How often should a large co-op reconcile its fob list against the shareholder roster?

At least once a year, and after every change of managing agent or super. In a building with frequent sales, quarterly is better. The reconciliation is simple: export the list of active credentials, compare it with the stock ledger and the list of approved occupants, staff and vendors, and turn off anything that doesn't match. Credentials that haven't been used in six months deserve a second look.

Good systems make this easy by labeling each credential with the apartment and the person, not just a number. If your current list is a spreadsheet of fob numbers with no names, the first reconciliation takes longer, but every one after it is quick.

We're changing managing agents. What has to be handed over so the new firm can run the intercom, fobs and cameras?

Administrator logins for the intercom panel or its cloud account, the access control software, the camera recorder and any remote viewing app; the current credential list; the directory; the equipment map or drawings; warranty and service records; and the contact for whoever maintains each system. The outgoing agent's personal accounts should be removed and new accounts created in the co-op's name.

If nobody can find some of those logins, it is usually possible to reset them on site, but it takes a technician and sometimes the manufacturer's support line. We can do the handover on a single visit, change the passwords with the board and the new agent present, and leave a one-page summary so the next transition is easier.

Daily life with the system

During a summer heat wave our lobby panel kept rebooting. What was going on?

Almost always power. Voltage dips during a heat event can drop a panel's supply below what it needs, so the panel restarts, sometimes over and over. Supplies mounted in hot closets or boiler rooms make it worse, because they are already running near their limit. Occasionally the panel itself overheats if it sits in direct afternoon sun behind glass.

The fix is usually a battery-backed power supply or UPS that holds the panel steady through dips, plus relocating or venting the supply. We also back up the panel's settings, so if it does lose its memory, the directory and codes come back in minutes.

Can the board find out which apartment buzzed a visitor in on a particular night?

On most modern panels, yes. Video and IP panels keep a call log that records which apartment was called, whether it answered and whether it released the door, with the time. Some also store a snapshot of the visitor. Older audio systems keep no record at all.

Whether and how the board looks at that log is a policy question. Many co-ops limit access to the managing agent and the board president and look only after a specific incident. We set the log retention to whatever the board decides and show the agent how to pull a single entry.

A shareholder wants to mount a camera in their own window looking over the parking lot. Does the board have a say?

That depends on the co-op's proprietary lease, house rules and the board's attorney, not on us. Generally, anything attached to the building's exterior is a board matter, and a camera recording common areas raises privacy questions that a building-wide camera system is set up to handle. A camera sitting inside an apartment and pointed out of a window is a grayer area.

If the shareholder's concern is the parking lot, the simplest answer is often a building camera that covers it, with footage the board controls. We can price that as a small add-on to the existing system.

Myth Check

What AI and Generic Articles Get Wrong About Westchester Co-op Security

Boards increasingly ask an AI assistant or skim a national article before calling a contractor. Here are six claims we hear repeated that don't hold up for Westchester co-ops, with what the sources actually say.

1. "Co-ops are a Manhattan thing; Westchester is condos and houses."

OneKey MLS counted 1,581 co-op sales in Westchester in the twelve months through October 2026, more than the 1,250 condo sales (OneKey MLS). Co-ops are a large share of the county's apartment market, and their security needs are their own.

2. "New York's Multiple Dwelling Law sets your intercom requirements."

The Multiple Dwelling Law covers cities of 325,000 or more. Westchester's cities, towns and villages fall under the Multiple Residence Law (MRL §3). The co-op's attorney is the right person to say what that means for a specific building.

3. "Your fob system must follow the Tenant Data Privacy Act."

That act is New York City Local Law 63 of 2021 and applies within the city (Local Law 63). A Westchester board should still adopt its own written policy on logs and data, but it isn't bound by the city law.

4. "Anything inside the intercom panel belongs to the intercom."

Habitat's 2022 report on the county's largest co-op found delivery-access devices installed inside intercoms under agreements signed by people with no authority to sign (Habitat). Panels collect third-party connections over the years, and boards should know what each one is.

5. "Package theft is out of control everywhere, so cameras alone are the answer."

News 12 reported falling package-theft counts in Yonkers and White Plains in 2026 (News 12). Cameras help, as the White Plains official said, but in a co-op the bigger fix is getting packages behind a second locked door.

6. "A building intercom costs $1,200 to $8,500."

That is a national consumer range (HomeAdvisor), mostly describing houses. A co-op's cost depends on entrances, apartments, the riser and the apartment end. Our Westchester pages publish the county's ranges, and a co-op gets an itemized price after a free survey.

Field Notes

What We Check on a First Visit to a Westchester Co-op

Whether we are there for a $250 repair or a free survey, the first visit to a Westchester co-op follows the same route through the building. These notes describe our crews' general practice, not any particular building.

  • The front door, closed and open. Before touching the panel, we watch the door close on its own. Does it latch? Does the strike line up? Does the closer slam or drift? A door that doesn't latch makes every other fix pointless.
  • The panel, from both sides. We call a few apartments, release the door from each, and look at the panel's back box: how many cables come in, whether any don't belong, and whether the box is wet, rusty or full of old splices.
  • The power. We find every supply feeding the panel, the release and the readers, read its output under load, and note where it lives and how hot that space gets.
  • The riser. At the points we can reach, usually the basement terminal and a junction on an upper floor, we test pairs for continuity and shorts. On a survey we test enough to say which apartments can keep wall stations.
  • Every other door. Side, service, garage, laundry, storage and roof doors: what locks them now, who has keys, and whether any are propped.
  • The mail and package area. Where packages land, whether that spot is behind a locked door, and what a camera there would see.
  • The equipment room. Recorder, switch, controllers, labels or the lack of them, and whether the room itself is locked.
  • The paperwork. Who holds the logins, whether there are drawings, and what the last contractor left behind.

At the end of the visit, the super or agent gets a short verbal summary, and the board gets a written one: what we found, what we fixed, and what we recommend next with prices. Nothing is replaced without approval beyond the repair we were called for.

FAQ

Westchester County Co-op Security Systems FAQ

Does the Westchester County fair-housing timeline for co-op applications affect when a new buyer gets fobs?
+

Indirectly. The county's law sets deadlines for the board to acknowledge an application and decide on a complete one within 60 days, but it says nothing about entry systems. What it does is make closings more predictable. The sensible routine is to issue the buyer's fobs or app access at the closing, never before board approval, and turn off the seller's credentials the same day. We set the system up so the managing agent can do both in a few minutes from the admin screen.

How many false alarms can a Yonkers co-op have before the city can act on its alarm permit?
+

Under the Yonkers alarm chapter, the first two false alarm notifications in a calendar year bring a written warning with no charge, later ones are dealt with under a separate section of the chapter, and more than four in a calendar year may lead to suspension or revocation of the permit. The permit also has to be renewed every year. A co-op with a monitored alarm on its office or storage rooms should keep the permit current and fix the cause of any false alarm right away.

Our lobby panel is too loud for the apartments right above the vestibule. Can its volume change at night?
+

On many video and IP panels, yes. Speaker and ringback volume can be set separately, and some panels allow a quieter night schedule. On older audio panels the volume is a single adjustment on the amplifier, so we can turn it down, but it stays down all day. Moving the speaker grille or adding a small hood that points the sound toward the visitor also helps. We check the setting on the service call before suggesting new equipment.

Should the trash-room door on each floor of our co-op tower be put on fobs?
+

Usually not. Trash and compactor rooms on residential floors are used by everyone on that floor, often several times a day, and adding a reader means more hardware to fail and more complaints. Those doors may also be fire-rated with closers and hardware the code expects to stay as they are. The co-op's money is better spent on the doors that let people into the building, the garage and the shared rooms in the basement.

Can old door contacts from a disconnected alarm system be reused to warn us when a door is held open?
+

Often they can. A door contact is a simple switch, and if it is still mounted, aligned and its cable tests good, it can be connected to the access control system or the camera recorder to send an alert when a door is held or forced. We test each contact and its wiring before reusing it. Contacts that are part of the fire alarm or a monitored system stay with that system and aren't repurposed.

Why does one vestibule camera so rarely show both the visitor's face and the package?
+

Because the two need different angles. A face is best caught at head height looking toward the door as someone comes in, while a package on a shelf or the floor is best seen from above. A single camera high in a corner often sees the tops of heads and a blurry shelf. Two inexpensive cameras, one at each angle, usually do far better than one expensive camera trying to do both.

Can shareholders get a phone alert when a package is left in the vestibule?
+

Some systems can do something close to it. A camera with object or motion detection can send an alert when something appears in a defined zone, and some package rooms and locker systems notify the recipient directly. Alerts for every motion in a busy lobby quickly become noise, though, so we usually set alerts for the package area only and send them to the super or the agent rather than to every shareholder.

Can a co-op reuse the coax cable from an old lobby camera system for new cameras?
+

Sometimes. Many newer cameras can run over existing coax using adapters or hybrid recorders, which saves opening walls in a finished lobby. Whether it works depends on the cable's condition and length, which we test. Where the coax is damaged or too long, we run new Cat6, often in the same pathway once the old cable is pulled. The proposal shows which runs we can reuse.

How do board members view the cameras from home without making the recorder easy to hack?
+

Give each viewer a separate account with view-only rights, use strong passwords and two-step login where the system offers it, keep the recorder's firmware up to date, and reach it through the manufacturer's secure app rather than by opening ports on the building's router. We set that up, keep the cameras on their own network, and leave the administrator account with the managing agent rather than with an individual board member.

Is a video intercom with a built-in camera enough, or does the lobby still need a separate camera?
+

For seeing who is at the door, the panel's camera is usually enough. It doesn't see what happens after the door opens: who walks in behind the visitor, what is taken from the mail area, or whether the inner door is propped. That is why most co-ops add at least one camera inside the vestibule or lobby. The panel camera answers who rang; the lobby camera answers who came in.

Can the intercom open the garage door for a visitor who's parking?
+

Yes, if the garage door has an operator that accepts a dry-contact input, which most do. A call box at the ramp can ring a shareholder's phone or apartment station, and the shareholder presses a key to open the gate or overhead door. The same box can take fobs for residents. We wire it to the door operator's input; any changes inside the operator itself are done by the garage door company.

Our laundry room machines take a card. Can the laundry room door use the same card as the machines?
+

Rarely. Laundry cards are issued by the laundry vendor and work only with its machines, while the door uses the co-op's access control system. The practical answer is to put the laundry room door on the building's fob, so the same fob opens the lobby and the laundry room, and leave the laundry card for paying for machines. If the vendor offers a phone app instead of cards, that still stays separate from the door.
