Norwood was built fast and built tall for its time. Josiah Briggs laid out the streets in 1889 on land that had been the Varian family's dairy farm, and over the next few decades the blocks between Mosholu Parkway, Van Cortlandt Park and the Bronx River filled with five- and six-story apartment houses in Art Deco, Tudor Revival and neo-Renaissance dress. NeighborhoodScout puts 94.8 percent of the neighborhood's real estate in apartment buildings and 60.2 percent of its housing in buildings from 1939 or earlier. In August 2026 two of those buildings made the news within fifteen days of each other. On August 5 an explosion and five-alarm fire tore through a 27-apartment building at 374 East 209th Street, where residents later said they had smelled gas. On August 20 a four-alarm fire at 3435 DeKalb Avenue, which officials believe had been burning for some time before anyone noticed, ran from the top floor into the cockloft, and residents told Norwood News they heard no alarm on their own floors. Those two fires describe what a fire alarm installer in Norwood is really being asked to do: put working smoke, carbon monoxide and now natural gas detection in every apartment of an old building, make sure the warning reaches each floor and not just one, and look hard at the century-old space above the top-floor ceilings. This page explains how we approach each of those jobs, what the city's rules ask of Norwood owners, where a filing with the FDNY comes into it, and what the work costs at our standard Bronx rate.

The Neighborhood

Why Norwood Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Walk up Bainbridge Avenue from Mosholu Parkway toward Gun Hill Road and almost every building you pass is older than anyone living in it. That age is the first reason fire detection matters here. A building put up in 1924, like the 54-apartment walk-up at 3225 Bainbridge Avenue, has lived through every phase of home fire protection the city has had: no detectors at all, then battery units that tenants could open, then hardwired alarms in some apartments, then the current rule that every residential smoke detector must run on a sealed ten-year battery or be hardwired into the unit's electrical system. Most Norwood buildings carry pieces of several of those eras at once, apartment by apartment, depending on when each unit last turned over. The ceilings and walls have been patched, painted and rewired so many times that nobody can say from the hallway what is up there. The roof space above the top floor is often the oldest part of the building, untouched since it was framed. And the people inside have changed too: the neighborhood that was known from the 1970s through the 1990s as Little Belfast, for its Irish families around Bainbridge Avenue and East 204th Street, is now home to a far wider mix of households, many of them working shifts at Montefiore, the neighborhood's largest employer and landowner since 1913.

The second reason is what happened in August 2026. At 3435 DeKalb Avenue, between Kossuth Avenue and East 208th Street, fire broke out on the top floor just before six in the morning and, according to fire officials quoted by ABC7, had probably been burning for quite some time before residents noticed. Some residents told Norwood News they heard no fire alarm and woke only because a relative smelled smoke, a neighbor knocked or firefighters arrived. One fifth-floor resident heard a faint alarm on the floor above and nothing on their own. A fourth-floor resident said a detector was supposed to be in front of their door and was not there. HPD said its records showed no smoke detector violations at the building. Two weeks earlier, at 374 East 209th Street, between Decatur Avenue and Parkside Place, an explosion and five-alarm fire destroyed a five-story building, the Department of Buildings ordered it vacated over structural stability, and Norwood News later reported three people dead and fourteen injured. Residents said they had smelled gas; FDNY call logs showed no gas leak reports at that building from January through August.

Neither of those reports tells us why any particular device did or did not sound, and we make no claim about either building beyond what the news said. What they show is the pattern a fire alarm plan in Norwood has to answer. Fires that start in the early morning, in an old building, high up, can grow for a long time before anyone is awake to notice. A warning that reaches one floor and not the next leaves people asleep below. A missing device outside an apartment door is invisible from the city's records. And smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, however good, say nothing about gas, which is why HPD told owners in its November 2024 bulletin that from 2025 they will also have to install natural gas detectors, and suggested switching now to combined devices that include gas detection.

The third reason is the commercial strip. Norwood's shops sit along Bainbridge Avenue, East 204th Street, Gun Hill Road, Jerome Avenue and Webster Avenue, many of them under apartments and many in one-story buildings that share walls and roofs with their neighbors. The block of Bainbridge Avenue south of East 206th Street has burned twice: a 2009 fire and then a four-alarm commercial fire in October 2023 that destroyed several small businesses, the second to hit the same bakery. The lot at 3109 to 3123 Bainbridge Avenue is still empty, and Foodtown LLC has filed plans for multi-story apartments there. A shop's fire alarm on a block like that protects more than one business, and it protects the families who may be asleep above the shop next door.

The prewar walk-up: many households, one stair and a gas range in every kitchen

A Norwood walk-up of fifty or seventy apartments is, for fire alarm purposes, a stack of separate homes joined by a stair. The legal duty inside each apartment belongs to the owner: smoke detecting devices that are hardwired or run on a sealed ten-year battery, carbon monoxide alarms where the rules call for them, and now natural gas detectors under HPD's newer requirement. What makes the walk-up difficult is not any one device but the count. Every apartment has to be entered, every bedroom checked, every existing alarm read for its date and power type, and every gap recorded. We plan these buildings line by line, so all the A apartments on every floor are done on one visit and the B line on the next, which keeps the super's job simple and lets tenants know exactly which day is theirs. Where a building already has hall devices on a panel, we treat those as a separate job with separate rules. Where it has nothing in the halls, we explain what an owner can add, what would make it a filed fire alarm system, and what that filing involves, before any price is written. In a walk-up the stair is the only way down for most people, so whatever goes in has to make sure a fire on the second floor is noticed by the family on the sixth before the stair fills.

The prewar elevator building: cellars, shafts and a building system that has outlived its paperwork

The larger prewar buildings, the ones with an elevator and a bigger cellar, are where we most often find a building fire alarm system that works partly and is understood by nobody. There may be a panel by the cellar door, hall detectors on some floors, a pull station in the lobby and a bell that has not rung in years. The original installer may be long gone, the drawings lost and the zone labels describing apartments that were renumbered decades ago. Before we price anything in a building like this we read the panel and its event history if it keeps one, walk every floor matching devices to zones, test a sample of devices, and look at the cellar spaces where fire grows unseen: the meter room, the boiler room, the storage area and the elevator machine space. Then we sort what we found into three lists. Devices and parts that can be repaired or replaced like for like, which ASM Integrators' summary of the rules treats as a repair. Changes that alter the design, such as moving or replacing the control panel with different equipment, which need new plans filed with the FDNY. And gaps that the owner should discuss with a registered design professional. The owner gets all three lists in writing, so the building's money goes first to the things that protect people now.

The co-op: a board for the halls, shareholders for the kitchens

Norwood has a good number of prewar co-ops, many of them converted with help from Mosholu Preservation Corporation, which Montefiore founded in 1981 to buy and renovate apartment buildings near its campus. In these buildings fire detection splits along the lines of the proprietary lease. The board looks after whatever is in the public halls, stairs, lobby and cellar. The alarms inside each apartment may be maintained by the corporation or left to each shareholder, depending on the building's documents. When the HPD gas detector rule arrived, many boards asked us the same thing: who buys and installs them? We do not interpret leases, but we can make either answer work. For a board that takes the job on, we survey every apartment and price one building-wide program, with a list of each unit's devices, models and replacement dates that the board can keep in its files. For a board that leaves it to shareholders, we offer the same standard to each household and send the board a confirmation for each apartment we complete, so the corporation knows which units are done and which still need attention. Either way, the hall system is handled separately, under the board's authority, and anything that changes the design goes through the design professional.

The new Webster Avenue building: a modern system and a learning curve

The buildings rising along Webster Avenue since the rezoning came with modern fire alarm systems, designed and filed as part of construction. Some include supportive and senior apartments, which changes who needs to hear an alarm and how fast they can respond. Our role in these buildings is rarely to install a new system. More often an owner or manager asks us to help them understand the one they have: what each panel message means, which monitoring company holds the account and who is on its call list, where the device list and as-built drawings are kept, which detectors inside apartments are the household alarms the owner must maintain and which belong to the building system. For buildings with many older or disabled residents, we also look at the in-apartment alarms with the residents in mind, including devices with strobes or lower-frequency tones for people who are hard of hearing. When the elevators in a tall building stop working, as News 12 reported at two Webster Avenue buildings, residents who cannot manage stairs depend even more on an early warning and on staff who know where they live. We help managers keep that list current alongside the fire alarm records.

The three-family house and the storefront: smaller buildings, the same three gases

Norwood's three-family private houses and its one-story and mixed-use storefronts are a smaller share of the neighborhood, but they come up on every few calls. A three-family house with tenants is a rental building, so the owner's duty covers the tenants' apartments: smoke alarms of the sealed or hardwired type, carbon monoxide alarms where required, and gas detection where there is gas service. We plan the house as one building, with linked alarms so a fire in the cellar is heard on the top floor. Storefronts are different. A store with apartments above needs a plan that connects the two, so a fire in the shop at night reaches the families upstairs. A store in a row of one-story shops needs to consider the roof it shares with its neighbors, the lesson Bainbridge Avenue has learned twice. Whether a store needs a filed fire alarm system depends on what it is and how big it is, which a registered design professional determines. Many need linked detection and a monitored signal rather than a filed system, and we price those from our small-business list.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Norwood Walk-Up From the Gas Meters to the Roof Hatch

Begin where the gas comes in. On a Norwood survey the technician goes down before going up. The cellar meter room tells us how many apartments have gas service, which matters now that gas detectors are part of the owner's duty, and whether anyone has noticed a smell there. The boiler and water heater get a look for carbon monoxide planning, and the storage spaces get noted for what is in them.

Then every landing, on the way up. On each floor we note the hall detectors, if any, whether they are wired or simply battery units stuck to the ceiling, the stair doors and whether they close, and anything stored or charging on the landings. In a single-stair building, that stair is the whole escape plan.

Sample apartments, top floor first. With the super, we look inside a few units, always including the top floor, and read every alarm's label for its type and date. Top-floor tenants are asked about smells, stains or heat on their ceilings.

Finish above the ceiling. At the bulkhead or hatch, a light and a camera on a pole show what the cockloft holds: whether it is open from end to end, whether it is packed with blown-in insulation like the DeKalb Avenue building, whether old cable or junction boxes lie in it, and whether it continues into the building next door. Those photos go to the owner whether or not we get the job. Only after all that does anyone start a price.

Compliance notes for Norwood buildings and businesses

  • Apartment detectors are sealed or hardwired. Brick Underground's summary of city rules says all residential smoke detectors must run on a ten-year sealed, non-removable battery or be hardwired into the unit's electrical system.
  • Ten days to file the certificate. The Habitat Group notes that within 10 days of installing or replacing detectors, an owner must file a Certificate of Satisfactory Installation with HPD. We hand over the device details the owner needs to do that on time.
  • Gas detectors are now part of the owner's duty. HPD's November 2024 owner bulletin said that starting in 2025 owners will also have to install natural gas detectors, and suggested combined devices that include gas detection.
  • Subsidized buildings follow NSPIRE. HPD's inspections page says that in buildings under NSPIRE, detectors must be hardwired or use sealed ten-year batteries, with at least one working alarm on every level, one outside every sleeping area and one inside each bedroom.
  • Building systems start with approved plans. The FDNY's fire code material says design and installation documents for fire alarm systems must be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. We install, label and pre-test; the design belongs to a registered architect or engineer.
  • A repair is not a redesign. ASM Integrators' summary draws the line: replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel with something different needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Voice comes with height. SPARC's summary of the DOB bulletin on existing buildings says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. Norwood's five- and six-story walk-ups are far below that; a design professional should answer the question for the tallest buildings.
  • Small buildings may not need a manual system. UpCodes' reading of the 2022 Building Code says a manual fire alarm system is not required in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has direct exit outside. Household alarms are still required in every unit.
  • Landmarks are individual, not district-wide. We found no designated historic district in Norwood. Landmarks Preservation Commission review applies at individually designated sites, none of which are ordinary apartment buildings.
DeKalb Avenue, August 2026

A Faint Alarm Upstairs, Nothing on the Fifth Floor: What the DeKalb Avenue Fire Says About Floor-to-Floor Warning

The fire at 3435 DeKalb Avenue on the morning of August 20, 2026 has stayed with many Norwood owners because of what residents said afterward rather than how big it was. The building is one of three structures in a multi-story residential complex. The fire began on the top floor, spread into the cockloft, and went to four alarms; Norwood News reported four people injured. Officials believed it had been burning for quite some time before residents noticed. Residents described waking to a relative who smelled smoke, a neighbor at the door or firefighters in the hall. One person on the fifth floor heard a faint alarm on the floor above and nothing on their own. A resident on the fourth floor said, "Actually, I'm supposed to have a smoke detector in front of my door and there's not one there. So, there was no alarm going off in the building." We do not know what detection the building had, how it was arranged or whether any of it was working that morning, and we draw no conclusion about the owner. We use the residents' words because they describe, in plain language, the gap a fire alarm plan is supposed to close.

An apartment alarm warns an apartment. The sealed or hardwired smoke alarm inside a unit is designed to wake the people in that unit. It is the most important device in the building, and it is required in every apartment. But it is a household device. It does not sound in the hall, it does not sound on the floor below, and it does not send a signal anywhere. A fire on the sixth floor will set off the alarms in the apartment where it started, and perhaps in apartments where smoke enters soon after, while families a floor or two down may hear nothing at all until the smoke reaches them. A faint sound from upstairs is often exactly that: one apartment's alarm, heard through a ceiling.

Warning between floors is a building decision. For a whole building to hear a fire, something has to carry the signal from where the fire is to where the people are. In a building fire alarm system, devices in the halls and stairs report to a panel, and the panel sounds notification on every floor. That kind of system, new or changed, needs plans from a registered design professional and FDNY approval. In some smaller buildings, owners choose to link apartment alarms within a unit and add monitored detection in common spaces, but linking alarms across many separate apartments raises its own problems, because one household's burnt toast then wakes the whole building. The right answer depends on the building's size and occupancy, and we explain the options and their filing consequences before any money is spent.

Fires that start before dawn need detectors that are actually there. A fire at six in the morning finds most people asleep. That is when a missing detector, a detector with a dead or removed battery, or one past its replacement date matters most. The practical work in an older Norwood building is not glamorous: an apartment-by-apartment count, every missing or expired alarm replaced with a sealed or hardwired one, every hall device checked against the panel, and a list kept that shows the date of each. HPD's records will show a violation only after someone reports a problem and an inspector writes it up. A building's own list shows the truth every day.

The cockloft and the blown-in insulation. The FDNY chief at the scene told CBS New York, "The building had blown-in insulation, which tends to hold the heat and smoke in... The wood in that cockloft is over 100 years old and very dried out." That description fits a lot of Norwood. Many buildings had insulation blown into their roof spaces over the years to save on heating, and the framing above many top floors is original. Insulation is not a fire alarm problem by itself, but it changes how a fire up there behaves and what a detector would need to sense it. We look at that space on every survey and tell the owner what we see, and where detection in it is part of a building system, the design professional chooses the device and location.

What else slowed the response. News 12 reported that two cars were parked at hydrants, so crews had to run hoses around them. That is not something a fire alarm fixes, and we mention it only because it is a reminder that every minute of early warning is precious when the minutes after the call are not always under anyone's control.

East 209th Street, August 2026

Smoke, Carbon Monoxide and Now Gas: Why Norwood Owners Are Adding a Third Kind of Detector

On August 5, 2026, an explosion and five-alarm fire struck 374 East 209th Street, between Decatur Avenue and Parkside Place, a five-story building that the FDNY said held 27 apartments. The Department of Buildings issued a full vacate order over the building's structural stability. Residents told reporters they had smelled gas before the explosion; ABC7 reported that FDNY call logs showed no gas leak reports at that building from January through August. Council Member Eric Dinowitz said, "A lot of the buildings in the Bronx are older. This building that was on fire was not fireproof." The cause is a matter for investigators, and we say nothing about it here. What the reports make plain is that a smell people noticed and nobody reported is a warning that went nowhere.

Smoke alarms and CO alarms do not sense natural gas. A smoke alarm reacts to particles of smoke. A carbon monoxide alarm reacts to carbon monoxide, the product of incomplete burning in a boiler, water heater, oven or range. Natural gas is a different thing altogether: the fuel itself, escaping before it burns. A building can have perfect smoke and CO protection and no device at all that would notice a gas leak in a kitchen. That gap is exactly what HPD addressed in its November 2024 bulletin, telling owners that starting in 2025 they will also have to install natural gas detectors and that they may want to switch now to combined devices that include gas detection.

What that means in a prewar Norwood building. Most of Norwood's older apartment houses were built with gas ranges in every kitchen, fed from meters in the cellar. That makes the gas detector requirement a per-apartment job much like the smoke alarm requirement: every unit with gas service entered, a device placed where its maker specifies for natural gas, its date recorded, and the owner's records updated. Because the same visit already brings us into each kitchen and bedroom, it is usually sensible to look at all three kinds of protection together, replacing smoke and CO alarms that are past their dates and adding gas detection at the same time, rather than knocking on every door three separate times. Combined units can reduce the number of devices on a wall, though a separate gas detector is sometimes the better choice where the right height for gas and the right location for smoke do not coincide.

A detector only helps if someone acts on it. The East 209th Street reports are a reminder that warning is a chain: something has to notice, and then a person has to do something. A gas detector sounding in a kitchen tells that household to leave and call 911 from outside. It does not call anyone itself. In our handover sheets for tenants we spell that out in plain words, along with the difference between the three sounds: smoke, carbon monoxide and gas. For owners, we suggest a simple written rule posted by the mailboxes: if you smell gas anywhere in the building, leave and call 911 and the utility from outside, then tell the super. A smell that ten people noticed and nobody reported should never happen in a building that has that rule on the wall.

Older construction and how fire moves. Many Norwood buildings were not built to today's fire-resistance standards, as the council member said. In a non-fireproof building, wood floors and partitions let fire and smoke move faster between floors than in concrete construction, which brings us back to the theme of the DeKalb Avenue section: the people a floor or two away from a fire need their own warning, and they need it early.

The Paper Trail

Certificates, Recalls and Records: Keeping Proof That Every Norwood Apartment Is Protected

Fire protection in an apartment building lives as much on paper as on the ceiling. After the DeKalb Avenue fire, HPD said its records showed no smoke detector violations at the building, while a resident said a detector was missing outside their door. Both statements can be true at the same time, because city records reflect inspections and complaints, not a live count of every device. An owner who wants to know the real state of a building needs records of their own, and the city expects owners to create some of them.

The Certificate of Satisfactory Installation. When an owner installs or replaces smoke detectors, a Certificate of Satisfactory Installation has to be filed with HPD within ten days, as The Habitat Group's summary explains. In a large walk-up where alarms are replaced in several rounds, that deadline arrives again and again, and it is easy to miss. We make it simpler: at the end of each visit the owner gets a list of every apartment we completed, every device we installed, its type, its location and its date, in a form that makes the certificate straightforward to prepare. The owner files it; we provide everything needed to do so on time.

Recalls are real, and they reach Norwood. Norwood News reported a recall of 11,000 units of three-pack smoke detectors of the LSHOME brand, sold on Amazon from February 2024 through December 2025, because they could fail to warn during a fire. A landlord or tenant who bought a pack like that to fill a gap did the right thing for the wrong device. Part of every survey is reading the brand and model of each alarm, and when we find a recalled unit we replace it and note it on the record. Owners who keep a model list can check new recalls against it in minutes instead of entering every apartment again.

The types that are allowed. City rules allow only sealed ten-year battery alarms or hardwired alarms in residential units. Older alarms with an openable battery door are the ones whose batteries get borrowed for a remote or a toy, and once that happens the city's records cannot tell. Replacing them with sealed units removes that problem permanently.

What a good record looks like. For each apartment: the unit number, each device's room and position, its type (smoke, CO, gas or combined), its power (sealed battery or hardwired), its brand and model, its install date and its replacement date. For the building system, if there is one: each zone or address with a plain description, the panel model, the standby battery date and the monitoring account. Kept current, a record like that answers an inspector, an insurer, a buyer and a worried tenant with the same sheet of paper.

Hundred-year-old cocklofts

The space above our top floor was filled with blown-in insulation years ago. If heat detection goes up there, won't the insulation bury it or keep the heat away from it?

It can, which is why a detector in an insulated roof space has to be placed with the insulation in mind rather than dropped onto it. Loose-fill insulation settles, drifts and gets disturbed whenever someone crawls through, and a heat detector lying in or under it will not sense much. Detectors belong on the underside of the roof framing or deck, above the level of the insulation, where hot gases from a fire collect first. The FDNY chief at DeKalb Avenue said blown-in insulation tends to hold heat and smoke in, which is another way of saying a fire can smolder inside it before much heat reaches the open space above. That makes the type of device a real decision. Heat detectors are usually chosen for roof spaces because dust, cold and summer heat make smoke detectors unreliable there, but their rating has to suit the temperatures that space actually reaches under a summer roof. When the detection is part of a building fire alarm system, the registered design professional sets the type, spacing and location, and adding devices to an existing system may be something the FDNY needs to see. Our part is the survey that informs that design: photos of the space, the depth and condition of the insulation, the framing, the access points for testing, and anything else up there, such as old wiring or junction boxes, that the owner should know about. We also make sure every opening we create between the top floor and the roof space is sealed with fire-rated material before we leave.

The cockloft over our building runs straight into the one next door. Should roof-space detection on our side report to anyone in the neighboring building?

Gas, the HPD rule and the East 209th Street explosion

HPD says owners now have to put in natural gas detectors. In a prewar Norwood walk-up, which apartments and which rooms actually get one?

Start with where the gas goes. HPD's November 2024 bulletin told owners that starting in 2025 they will also have to install natural gas detectors, so the duty follows gas service, and in most of Norwood's older buildings that means the kitchens, because gas ranges were original equipment. Our survey begins in the cellar meter room, where the number of meters and their labels give a first count of which apartments are on gas. Then we confirm it inside each unit, because over the decades some tenants or owners have switched to electric cooking and some meters serve spaces other than apartments. For each apartment that has gas, the device goes where its manufacturer specifies for natural gas, in the room with the gas appliance or close to it, and away from places where cooking fumes or cleaning sprays would cause false readings. Bedrooms do not get gas detectors as a rule; they get smoke alarms and, where required, carbon monoxide alarms. The details of exactly which units and spaces the rule covers are in HPD's own guidance, and an owner should read that guidance or ask their counsel if a building has unusual arrangements, such as a single meter feeding several apartments or gas used only for a central boiler. What we deliver is a unit-by-unit count of gas service, a device placed and dated in each apartment that has it, and a record the owner can show. Common spaces such as the meter room and boiler room are not apartments, but some owners choose to add gas detection there too; we discuss that as an option, not a requirement.

Our managing agent wants to replace old smoke alarms and add gas detectors in the same round of apartment visits. Is that sensible, or should they be separate jobs?

For most Norwood buildings, doing it together is the better plan, as long as the scope is written clearly. The hardest part of any in-apartment program is access: arranging entry to fifty or seventy homes, giving notice, returning for units where nobody answered, and keeping the paperwork straight. Doing that once instead of twice saves the tenants disruption and the owner money, because the most expensive part of the job is often the time spent on the stairs, not the devices. On a combined visit we read every existing smoke and CO alarm for its type and date, replace anything that is the wrong type, past its date, recalled or missing, add any alarms the bedroom layout requires, and install the gas detector in the kitchen where there is gas service. Combined devices that include smoke, CO and gas detection, which HPD's bulletin mentions, can reduce the number of devices on the walls, but they are not always the best fit: the ideal mounting position for a smoke alarm is not always the ideal position for a gas sensor, and a combined device has to be replaced all at once when any part of it reaches end of life. We recommend combined units where the room allows a single position that suits all three functions, and separate devices where it does not. Two cautions. Each round of smoke detector replacements starts HPD's ten-day clock for the Certificate of Satisfactory Installation, so the owner should plan the filing around the visit schedule. And the quote should list smoke, CO and gas devices separately per apartment, so the owner can see what each part costs and adjust if needed.

Tenants tell our super they sometimes smell gas in the halls, but nobody ever calls it in. What should a building put in place?

Treat every report as real and make it impossible for a smell to stay a rumor. The reports from East 209th Street, where residents said they smelled gas before the explosion and FDNY logs showed no gas leak calls at that building in the months before, describe exactly the situation an owner should want to prevent. The first part is procedure, and it costs nothing. Post a clear notice by the mailboxes and in the laundry room, in the languages your tenants read: if you smell gas, do not switch lights on or off, leave the building, and call 911 and the gas utility from outside; then tell the super. Make sure the super does the same and does not go looking for the leak with a lighter or a flashlight switch. The second part is detection. Inside apartments, the gas detectors HPD now requires will catch a leak at its most common source, the kitchen. In common areas, a gas detector is not a legal requirement in most halls, but an owner who hears repeated reports can choose to place one in the cellar meter room, where leaks from fittings and meters show up first, and arrange for it to alert the super and not just beep in an empty room. The third part is follow-up: every report gets written down with the date, time and location, and a pattern of reports from one floor or one line of apartments goes to the gas utility for investigation. A fire alarm installer can provide the devices and the alerts. The utility, not us, is who finds and fixes a leak.

What the records can and cannot tell you

HPD's site shows no smoke detector violations for our building. Can we take that to mean every apartment has a working detector?

No, and the DeKalb Avenue reports are the clearest illustration of why. HPD said its records showed no smoke detector violations at that building, and a resident said the detector that was supposed to be outside their door was not there. A violation appears in HPD's records when an inspector visits, usually because of a tenant complaint, and writes it up. If nobody complains, or the inspector comes about heat or a leak and does not look at the ceiling, or the apartment with the missing device is never entered, the record stays clean. A clean record means no one has documented a problem; it does not mean no problem exists. The only way to know is to look: every apartment entered, every bedroom and hall checked, every device read for type and date, and every gap fixed and recorded. That is the core of our first visit to a building whose owner wants certainty. The result is a list of every unit with a status, such as complete, device missing, wrong type, past its date or recalled, and a price to bring the whole building into line. The owner then has a record that reflects reality and can be updated each time an apartment turns over. It also helps with the ten-day Certificate of Satisfactory Installation, since each completed round produces the details the certificate needs. Clean city records are worth having. A building's own list, kept current, is worth more.

Norwood News asked which agency oversees fire alarm testing. For our own building, who actually checks that the alarms are tested?

It depends on which alarms you mean, which is why the question is so often confusing. Inside apartments, smoke, carbon monoxide and gas detectors are the owner's responsibility under the Housing Maintenance Code, and HPD is the agency that inspects and writes violations for them, mostly in response to complaints. HPD also receives the Certificate of Satisfactory Installation an owner files after installing or replacing detectors. A building fire alarm system, meaning a panel with devices in halls, stairs and service rooms, falls under the FDNY's fire code. Its design and installation documents go to the FDNY for review and approval, and the FDNY inspects a filed system once it is complete. Once a system is in service, the owner is responsible for keeping it tested and maintained and for keeping the records, and the FDNY can ask to see them. The Department of Buildings comes into the picture for construction work and for the building as a whole, as the vacate order at East 209th Street showed, but it is not the agency that tests your alarms. In practice no agency comes by on a schedule to press every test button in your building; testing happens because the owner arranges it. What we provide is that testing, done on a schedule the owner sets, with a written report for each visit: which devices were tested, which passed, what was replaced and what needs attention. Kept in a binder at the panel or with the managing agent, those reports are what an inspector, an insurer or a buyer will ask for.

How will I know the person at my door is really your technician?
+

Can you still do the work if our building's elevator is out of service?
+

What should I do if I find one of the recalled LSHOME smoke detectors in my apartment or building?
+

Do not take it down until a working replacement is ready to go up, so nobody spends a night without any alarm. Replace it with a sealed ten-year or hardwired alarm, keep the recalled unit or its packaging for the manufacturer's recall process, and note the change on the building's device list. Owners can send us a photo of the label and we will confirm whether it matches the recall Norwood News reported.

Where on the wall does a natural gas detector go, and does it need its own outlet?
+

Natural gas is lighter than air, so gas detectors are usually mounted high, but the exact height and distance from the range come from the manufacturer's instructions for the specific model, and we follow them. Some models plug into an outlet, some are hardwired and some run on sealed batteries. In older Norwood kitchens with few outlets, we choose a model that suits the power available rather than running new wiring.

Our building's panel is decades old. Can you tell whether parts are still available for it?
+

Usually, yes. A clear photo of the panel's door label and the inside of the cabinet tells us the maker and model, and from that we can say whether replacement modules, detectors and batteries are still made. If they are, a repair is often the cheaper path. If they are not, we explain whether a replacement on the existing wiring is possible or whether a change in design would need new plans filed with the FDNY.

Our prewar ceilings are cracked plaster. Will a detector stay up on them?
