The Neighborhood

Why DUMBO Properties Need Security Camera Repair

DUMBO is small, about a tenth of a square mile between the two bridges, with 4,975 residents counted in 2020 and one ZIP code, 11201. What makes camera repair here different is not its size but its buildings. Nearly everything on these blocks was built as a factory or warehouse between the 1850s and the 1920s, and the National Register's DUMBO Industrial District includes some of the earliest large reinforced-concrete factories in the country. Those buildings were never planned around cameras. Today one of them may hold a condo association, a managing agent, a dozen office tenants and a restaurant at street level, and each of those groups may have bought cameras at a different time from a different company. When something stops working, the first job is finding out whose equipment it is, where its cable goes and which recorder it reports to.

The conversions explain a lot of what we find. The Clocktower Building at 1 Main Street was built in 1914 for the box maker Robert Gair and became 124 condominiums in 1998, with a 24-hour concierge and three freight elevators. 70 Washington Street, from 1910 and 1911, was converted into 259 homes in 2005. Office buildings such as those grouped as Dumbo Heights, 81 Prospect, 117 Adams, 77 Sands, 77 Prospect and 175 Pearl, hold design and technology companies whose own IT people run the floors they lease. In a building like that, camera cable shares ceiling space with data, sprinkler and electrical lines, and runs across concrete decks that nobody wants to drill again. Most camera faults we see in these buildings are found where one system meets another: at the switch, at the patch panel, or at a splice above someone else's ceiling.

Power and water are the second reason. In June 2025, Con Edison asked about 68,000 customers in Dumbo, Vinegar Hill, Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn and several neighboring areas to cut back on electricity while crews fixed equipment problems, and said, "Con Edison has reduced voltage by 8 percent in the area to protect equipment and maintain service as crews make repairs." A lower voltage is not an outage, but it is hard on a camera system: a power box that was already working near its limit can drop cameras one by one, and a recorder can restart in the middle of writing. Water is the other threat, because so many recorders live in cellars. In September 2019 a 24-inch water main from 1905 broke under Water Street between Pearl and Jay Streets during the street reconstruction, and Doreen Gallo of the DUMBO Neighborhood Alliance told the Brooklyn Eagle, "The buildings along the street were flooded with at least six inches of water." At the Lassen & Hennigs deli at 177 Water Street, the manager said, "Our entire basement looked like Les Miserables — all of Les Miserables," and in 2012 DNAinfo described DUMBO businesses and homes left under multiple feet of water by Hurricane Sandy.

The third reason is what the video is needed for. The DUMBO thefts that reached the news in 2025 and 2026 were shop thefts in business hours and quiet thefts of cars and materials, and in each kind the camera is often the only account of what happened. In July 2025, News 12 Brooklyn reported that the Alo Yoga shop on Front Street lost about $380 of shirts one afternoon and about $6,300 of merchandise the next day. On October 1, 2025, a thief at DUMBO Market on Front Street was confronted by an employee, showed a firearm and rode off on a bicycle toward Washington Street. Brooklyn Paper's 84th Precinct reports describe two luxury cars driven out of the Front Street Garage while police reviewed the parking lot's security footage, and a thief who cut a gate lock at a construction site at 29 Jay Street, took about $20,000 of copper wire and was "caught on surveillance camera fleeing the site in a vehicle." A recording helps only if the camera was working, the recorder was saving and the clock was right. Repair here is about making sure all three are true before the day you need them.

Finally, the street itself has changed around the cameras. The city finished a $108 million reconstruction of DUMBO's cobblestone streets in December 2025, work that began in April 2019 and rebuilt 25 blocks with new sewers and water mains. Washington Street, with its view of the Manhattan Bridge, now draws crowds that residents described to News 12 in June 2026 as the "Times Square of Brooklyn," and the trains on the Manhattan Bridge pass overhead, by one 2025 neighborhood petition's count every five minutes at peak hours. A DUMBO storefront or lobby camera has to cope with a busy frame from morning to night and a building that hums every few minutes.

Security Camera Repair for DUMBO's Architecture

Seven kinds of property cover nearly every DUMBO camera call. What sets them apart for a repair is who controls the recorder, how the cable gets from the camera to the head end through concrete, brick or timber, and how many other systems share the same closet.

The loft condo with a front desk

Converted factories run by a condo board and a managing agent, with a concierge desk, freight elevators, a mailroom and a building recorder that the desk watches but does not own.

The rental loft building

Converted warehouses with a landlord, a super and dozens of renters, where the building cameras cover the doors and halls and residents add doorbells and indoor cameras of their own.

The office loft leased floor by floor

Old industrial buildings rented to design, media and technology companies, each with its own IT, its own switch closet and sometimes its own cameras alongside the landlord's.

The ground-floor shop or restaurant

Storefronts on Front, Water, Washington and Main Streets with a counter camera, a door camera and a recorder in the back room or the cellar, under offices or apartments.

The gallery, studio or event room

Open spaces whose walls, lighting and furniture change from show to show, with cameras that must follow the layout and protect valuable work.

The newer apartment building

Post-conversion residential buildings with network cameras, card-access doors and a building management company, usually wired with modern cable but run by several vendors.

The garage and the job site

Parking garages under or beside loft buildings, and fenced construction sites with temporary cameras, where the footage matters most after something has already left.

The loft condo with a front desk: the desk watches, the board decides

In a building like the Clocktower, with 124 condos, a 24-hour concierge and three freight elevators, the camera system is a shared asset run by a board through a managing agent. The front desk sees the live pictures; the recorder may sit in a management office, a cellar room or a closet behind the mailroom. Before a visit we confirm in writing who the managing agent has named as the contact, who holds the recorder's administrator login, and which cameras are building property as opposed to a resident's own doorbell or a shop's system. The faults we find most in these buildings are a monitor at the desk that has lost one recorder after another was added, freight elevator cameras whose travelling cable has worn through, and mailroom cameras that were placed for the room as it was before the package shelving went in. Ask the managing agent for any drawings or vendor invoices before we arrive; they save time.

The rental loft building: the landlord's cameras and everyone else's

A rental loft has a simpler chain of command, the landlord or the management company, but a messier camera picture. The building's own cameras cover the street door, the stairs, the halls and sometimes the roof. Then residents add video doorbells at their unit doors and indoor cameras pointed at their own space, and some of those see the hallway too. When the building system fails, the landlord pays, and when a resident's own device fails, the resident does. We sort out which is which on the first visit and keep the two apart, because a resident's camera on the building's network, or the building's camera on a resident's Wi-Fi, is a fault waiting to happen. Brick Underground has noted that no city law requires a building to have working cameras, but a landlord who knows thefts are happening may be expected to take reasonable steps, and attorney Jeffrey Reich told the site that "Fixing the security camera would certainly be a reasonable action for the landlord to take in this instance."

The office loft leased floor by floor: the IT closet is not yours alone

Office lofts are where camera systems and computer networks collide. A company leasing a floor on Jay Street or up the hill on Prospect Street usually has an IT person or an outside IT firm, a firewall, switches and Wi-Fi access points, and a handful of network cameras that someone added along the way. Those cameras often draw power from the same PoE switch as the access points, sit on the same network as laptops and phones, and depend on a router whose settings the IT firm changes without telling anyone about the cameras. The common failures follow from that: cameras that reboot after new access points are plugged in, cameras that disappear from the app after a firewall update, and a recorder that nobody can reach because its address changed. We work with your IT people rather than around them, tell them exactly which ports and addresses the camera system needs, and leave the change written down so the next network change does not undo the repair.

The ground-floor shop or restaurant: one counter, one cellar, many hours open

Shops in DUMBO sit at the bottom of buildings that are mostly offices or homes, and their camera systems are small but worked hard. The usual layout is a camera over the door, one or two over the counter or the register, one on the stock room, and a recorder on a shelf in the back or down a stair in the cellar. A shop camera has the hardest picture in the neighborhood to get right: a bright street through a glass front, crowds passing all day, and display lighting that changes with each season's stock. The thefts at Alo Yoga and DUMBO Market on Front Street in 2025 both happened in daylight with staff present, so the counter and door pictures need to identify a face at the moment someone walks out, not just show that someone was there. We check that on every shop repair by playing back a recorded clip of a person at the door, not just glancing at the live view.

The gallery, studio or event room: the layout moves, the cameras do not

DUMBO has long been home to artists, galleries, photographers, makers and performance spaces, from the loft conversions of the late 1970s to the theater at St. Ann's Warehouse. Spaces like these change their walls, lighting and furniture for every show or shoot, and a camera placed for one layout can end up looking at the back of a temporary wall for the next. The repairs we see in these rooms are cameras knocked during an install, cables pulled loose when a wall went up, and recorders unplugged to free an outlet for lights. The longer fix is usually placement: cameras high on permanent structure, aimed at doors and storage rather than at the art itself, with cable run where install crews will not reach it. Where valuable work sits overnight, the storage room and the loading door matter more than the gallery floor.

The newer apartment building: modern cable, many vendors

The residential buildings put up in and around DUMBO since the loft conversions usually have the best wiring in the neighborhood: network cameras on structured cable, a proper telecom room and a battery backup. Their weakness is the number of vendors. One company did the cameras, another the card readers, a third the intercom and a fourth the internet service, and each assumes the others are responsible when something stops talking. A typical call is a lobby camera that still records but no longer shows on the intercom screens, or cameras that went offline the day the internet provider swapped its equipment. We start with the building's own documents, test each link in turn, and hand the managing agent a short note of what was wrong and whose equipment it was in, so the next call goes to the right company first.

The garage and the job site: the footage is the whole point

Parking garages and construction sites are where DUMBO footage has mattered most in the precinct reports. In Brooklyn Paper's account of the Front Street Garage theft, two cars worth a combined $155,000 left the garage one morning and police were reviewing the lot's security footage; at the 29 Jay Street site, the thief who cut the gate lock and took about $20,000 of copper wire was caught on camera leaving in a vehicle. In both settings the camera does its job only if it covers the way in and out, holds enough days of video for the theft to be found, and keeps recording at night when a site's temporary power may be switched off. Garage cameras fail from exhaust grime, clipped mounts and long runs back to an office; site cameras fail from weather, from being moved as the work moves, and from power shut off at the end of the day. We repair both, and say plainly what a temporary setup can catch.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Tracing a DUMBO Loft Building's Cameras From the Front Desk Down to the Cellar

These notes set out how our technicians work through a camera system in a shared loft building, the most common kind of DUMBO call. They describe standard practice, not a story about any particular address.

Start where people watch. The technician begins at the front desk monitor or the management office screen, because that is where the complaint came from. Which tiles are black, which are frozen, which show the wrong place, and what time does the recorder think it is? A photo of the screen goes on the job record before anything is touched, along with the recorder's drive status.

Find every recorder. Loft buildings often have more than one. The building system may sit in a cellar room, an older recorder may still run the freight elevator and loading door, and a shop or office tenant may have its own box on the same cable paths. We list each recorder, its brand and its channel count, and which cameras each one actually sees, because a camera blamed on the building's recorder sometimes belongs to someone else's.

Read the power before the cameras. At the head end, the technician checks the outlet, the battery backup and its self-test, the PoE switch's power budget and port lights, and any central power box for coax cameras. After a hot week of reduced voltage, a backup with a worn battery or a switch near its power limit is a more likely culprit than any camera.

Follow the run, not the label. Where labels exist they are checked, not trusted. A tone probe and a cable tester confirm which run reaches which camera, and where a run tests open, the tester shows roughly how far along it the break is, so only that stretch of ceiling or shaft needs opening, with the building's say-so.

Check the camera last, and at night. Only after power, network and cable test good is a camera judged dead. Then the replacement is set to match the recorder, checked live, checked in playback, and checked again after dark if the problem was a night picture.

Leave the building a map. Before leaving, every traced run is labeled at both ends, the recorder's clock is set to update itself, failure alerts go to the person the managing agent names, and a short list of what each recorder controls goes on the job record for the next visit.

Fault by Fault

Low Voltage, Shared Networks, Water or the Street: How DUMBO Camera Systems Break and What Each Repair Involves

Four causes account for most of what we find behind DUMBO camera calls. Each leaves recognizable signs, and spotting them early is what keeps a repair to the part that failed instead of a new system nobody needed.

Low voltage: the outage that never quite happened

The June 2025 voltage reduction is a good example of a power event that does not look like one. The lights stay on, a little dimmer; the refrigerator keeps running; and a camera system that had no margin starts to misbehave. Power boxes for coax cameras deliver less than their rating when the line voltage drops, so the cameras at the end of the longest runs, which already receive the least power, flicker or drop first. PoE switches that were close to their power budget may cut ports to protect themselves. A recorder whose power supply is ageing may restart while it is writing, and a drive that restarts mid-write too many times starts to report errors.

The signs are a pattern rather than a single failure: the same two or three cameras dropping on hot afternoons, a recorder log full of restarts at similar times, or a switch whose fan runs flat out. The repair is usually modest. We measure what the power box and switch are delivering, replace a tired supply or battery, move one or two cameras to a port or supply with headroom, and check the drive's health. Power supply repair is $150 to $400 on our list, and a drive replacement $200 to $450 by capacity.

What we do not do is electrical work. If the outlet itself is weak, a circuit trips, or the building's wiring is at fault, you need a licensed electrician, and we tell you what we measured so they start in the right place. Once the power is sound, we finish the camera side.

Shared networks: someone else's change takes your cameras down

In an office loft, the camera system rarely fails on its own schedule. It fails the week the IT firm swaps the firewall, the day the internet provider replaces its router, or the morning after new Wi-Fi access points go into the closet. The cameras may still be recording perfectly well while the phone app shows them all offline, or a group of cameras may vanish because the switch can no longer power them alongside the new equipment.

We sort these out by testing from the recorder outward: does it see each camera on its own network, does it reach the internet, and does the app reach the recorder? That tells us within minutes whether the fault is the cameras, the switch or the route out of the building. The fix is usually a setting, a port, a power budget or a separate network segment for the cameras, and most of it is configuration that the service call covers.

The lasting part is the note we leave for your IT people. A short list of the camera system's addresses, ports and power needs, kept with the network documents, is what stops the next change from repeating the same outage.

Water: the cellar a block from the river

Cellars are where DUMBO buildings put the things nobody wants to see, which is why so many recorders, switches and power boxes end up there. They are also where water goes. The 2019 Water Street main break put at least six inches of water into basements along the street in a single morning, and Sandy did far worse in 2012. Water does not have to reach the recorder to harm it: damp air in a cellar corrodes connectors and power supplies over years, and a drive in a humid room fails sooner than one in a dry closet.

A recorder that has stood in water should be treated as suspect even if it turns on, because corrosion keeps working after it dries. If the footage on it matters, we take the drive out first and try to read it in a clean recorder before deciding anything about the box. Then the head end goes on a shelf or wall mount well off the floor, or better, to a room upstairs, with the battery backup beside it. Moving a recorder is often a short job when spare cable or an existing path allows it, and a priced one when a new path has to be agreed with the building.

Outdoors, the same logic applies to cameras at street level along Water Street and the blocks near the park. Their cable entries and connector boxes are resealed on any repair, because the cable is the path water follows into a wall.

The street: crowds, trains and a frame that never empties

The last cause is the street. A camera facing Washington Street, the Archway or a busy stretch of Front Street sees people almost all day, so a recorder set to save on motion saves almost continuously, and the drive fills and overwrites faster than the owner expects. A storefront door camera looks from a dim interior at a bright street and turns faces into silhouettes. And in buildings close to the Manhattan Bridge, trains pass every few minutes at peak hours, so a mount that was slightly loose keeps working looser, and a picture that wobbles every few minutes sets off motion alerts and blurs the frames that matter.

These are repairs of settings and mounting more than of parts. We set detection zones to the doorway rather than the whole sidewalk, adjust exposure so a person at the door is lit, confirm how many days the drive actually holds at the real level of activity, and tighten or replace mounts so the picture stays still. Where a camera simply faces the wrong way for what it is meant to catch, we say so and price moving it.

What a DUMBO shop owner needs from a camera repair

For a shop on Front or Washington Street, the camera system has three jobs: show a clear face at the door, show the hands at the counter, and keep enough days of video that a theft found at the end of the week is still on the drive. Almost every shop repair we do here comes back to one of those. Door cameras are the hardest, because they look out of a dim shop into a bright street and the street never empties. The fix is usually a camera with better handling of bright backgrounds, aimed at the inside of the door frame rather than the sidewalk, with a detection zone limited to the doorway so the recorder is not filling its drive with passers-by. We set it, then test it the only way that counts: someone walks out of the door while we watch the recording, not the live view.

Counter cameras fail differently. Display lights get moved, a new shelf goes up, a camera is knocked during restocking, and the picture slowly stops covering the register. That is a settings and mounting repair, covered by the service call when no part is needed. Recorders behind the counter fail from heat, dust and being switched off with the lights at closing, so we give them their own protected outlet and battery backup.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in DUMBO: Questions Answered

These are the situations condo boards, managing agents, landlords, office managers, shopkeepers and contractors describe when they call us about cameras in DUMBO. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down the page.

Building systems in converted lofts

Our lobby has one round fisheye camera on the ceiling, and every clip looks like it was filmed through a peephole. Can the picture be flattened, or does the camera have to go?

Often it can be flattened. A fisheye camera records a single circular image, and the flat views you expect are produced afterwards by software that knows the lens, a step called dewarping. If your recorder or app does not support that particular camera's lens, you see the raw circle. The repair is usually to enable dewarping on a recorder or viewing app that supports the camera, or to replace the recorder with one that does. Where a lobby needs a clear face at the door and a clear view of the mailroom, two ordinary cameras sometimes serve better than one fisheye, and if that is our honest view we price both options so the board can choose.

Our building's video intercom panels upstairs were supposed to show the lobby camera, but residents only see a blank screen. Is that a camera repair or an intercom repair?

It can be either, and the first test tells which. Most systems send the camera's picture to the intercom as a network video stream, either from the camera itself or through the recorder. If the camera records normally and plays back on the recorder, the camera is fine and the problem is the stream: a changed address, a changed password, or an intercom update that dropped the setting. If the camera is dark on the recorder too, it is a camera fault first. We check both ends on one visit, and because we repair intercoms as well as cameras, you do not need two companies pointing at each other.

Several residents have Ring or Nest doorbells at their loft doors. Can those feed the building's recorder so management has one place to look?

Usually not. Consumer doorbells like Ring and Nest are built to record to their own company's cloud and app, and they generally do not offer the kind of standard video stream a building recorder needs. They also belong to the residents, along with their footage. If the board wants every unit door covered on the building system, the reliable route is building-owned cameras or door stations wired to the building recorder, with clear rules on who may view them. We can repair the building system and advise on that, and residents keep their own devices for their own use.

Office networks and suites

Since our IT firm added Wi-Fi access points to the closet, two of our hallway cameras restart several times a day. Could the new equipment be the cause?

Very likely. Network cameras and access points both draw power from the PoE switch through their data cables, and every switch has a total power budget. Add a few access points and the switch may not have enough left for every camera, so it cuts power to some ports and the cameras on them reboot, often at busy times when the access points draw the most. We read the switch's power figures on site. The fix is to give the cameras ports with guaranteed power, move them to their own switch, or replace the switch with one that has a larger budget. A PoE port swap runs $150 to $350 on our list, and we tell your IT firm what changed.

Our IT consultant says our camera recorder can be reached from the internet through an open port. Can that be closed without losing phone viewing?

Yes, in most cases. Older installations often reached phones by opening a port on the office router straight to the recorder, which leaves the recorder's login page exposed to anyone who finds it. The usual repair is to close that port and switch phone viewing to the manufacturer's own secure connection service, or to a private network connection your IT firm manages, then update the recorder's software and replace any default or shared passwords with individual logins. We do the camera side and agree the router side with your IT people, so phone viewing works again before we leave.

Our startup is leaving its DUMBO suite when the lease ends. What should happen to the recorder, the logins and the footage before we hand back the keys?

First check your lease for who owns the cameras: some suites came with the landlord's equipment, and some companies added their own. Export any footage you are required or likely to need, then remove the company's phone and cloud accounts from the system so former staff and the next tenant cannot reach each other's video. If the equipment stays, reset it with the landlord's agreement and hand over the new administrator login. If it leaves with you, we take it down, cap the cables neatly and note which runs remain for the landlord. Doing this before the last week saves a scramble on move-out day.

Cuts, temporary walls and job sites

A renovation in the suite next door cut one of our camera cables somewhere above the shared ceiling. Can the break be found without opening the whole ceiling?

Usually, yes. A cable tester can measure roughly how far along the run the break is, and a tone probe follows the cable through the ceiling from our side, so the search narrows to a few tiles or one access panel instead of a whole corridor. Then the damaged section is replaced or joined properly at a junction box, and the run is tested end to end. Getting into the neighbor's space, or a common ceiling, needs the landlord's or managing agent's approval, so we arrange that before the visit. Who pays for damage caused by another tenant's contractor is between you, the landlord and that tenant; we give you a written note of what we found.

We are the general contractor on a DUMBO site, and the fence cameras stop recording when the site power goes off at night. Can they be kept running?

Yes, with the right power arrangement. Temporary site cameras usually stop at night because they share a circuit with the tools and lights that are switched off at the end of the shift. The fix is a separate circuit that stays on, which your electrician provides, or a battery backup in a weatherproof enclosure sized to carry the cameras and recorder through the night. We also check that the recorder or the cameras' own storage is saving, that remote viewing works over the site's connection, and that the cameras still cover the gate after the last phase of work moved them. The 2025 copper theft at 29 Jay Street was caught on camera leaving the site; the aim is to make sure yours would be too.

Our gallery builds new temporary walls for every exhibition, and each time a camera ends up facing the back of a partition. Is there a better way to place them?

Yes. Cameras that move with each show will always fall behind it. The better plan is to mount them high on permanent structure, columns, beams or the perimeter walls, and aim them at the things that do not change: the entrance, the loading door, the storage room and the main circulation path. Wider-angle or adjustable-zoom cameras placed that way can see over temporary walls, and the cable stays where install crews will not knock it. When we repair a gallery system, we suggest positions like that and price any moves, so the next installation does not undo the repair.

The bridge, the street and the crowds

Trains on the Manhattan Bridge shake our building every few minutes. Can that vibration wear out the hard drive in our recorder?

It is rarely the main cause, but it does not help. Surveillance hard drives are built to write around the clock and cope with ordinary building movement. In our experience heat, unstable power and age kill far more recorder drives than vibration does. Still, in buildings close to the bridge we set the recorder on a solid shelf or wall mount rather than on top of other equipment, keep it away from the wall that carries the most rumble, and check the drive's health report on every visit. If the report shows reallocated sectors or read errors, the drive is replaced before it fails, at $200 to $450 by capacity.

Our lobby camera sees a long stretch of the Washington Street sidewalk, full of visitors all day. Should its view be narrowed to our own entrance?

For most buildings, yes. A camera that sees half a block records thousands of strangers a day, fills its drive faster, sends constant alerts and still shows each person too small to recognize at your door. Narrowing the view to the entrance, the vestibule and the step outside gives a larger, clearer picture of everyone who actually comes in. Most recorders can also black out part of the frame permanently with a privacy mask, which helps where a camera cannot avoid seeing a neighbor's window or doorway. Whether and how your building may record the public sidewalk is a question for your own lawyer; the practical case for a tighter view is strong either way.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in DUMBO

These claims appear in AI-generated answers and in national guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr and from camera makers' blogs. Each is paraphrased, then checked against what repairs actually involve in DUMBO's loft buildings, offices and shops.

"Most camera problems are a dirty lens, a bad cable or a weak Wi-Fi signal"

Reolink's repair guide lists blurry images, cameras that will not power on, unstable video feeds and failed night vision as the common problems, and suggests cleaning, a multimeter, a Wi-Fi extender and splicing a cut wire as fixes. For a single camera on a house, that is a fair start.

In a DUMBO loft building, the camera is rarely alone. It shares a switch with an office's access points, a ceiling with other tenants' cable and a power circuit with a whole closet of equipment. Many of the faults we find are at those meeting points, and no amount of cleaning or splicing at the camera end will reach them.

What a reliable diagnosis needs is the whole chain tested in order, from the outlet and backup through the switch and recorder to the cable and camera. That takes a visit, which is why our repairs start with a service call rather than a guess.

"Camera repair in Brooklyn costs about $1,400"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average for surveillance camera installation or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and a full span of $160 to $3,500. Those figures mix installations and repairs together.

A repair on its own usually costs far less. On our Brooklyn list, most single faults are $150 to $700, and only a recorder replacement, a rewire or several cameras at once reach $700 to $1,500. A full rewire starts at $1,500.

The number that matters is the written price after the diagnosis, which tells you what failed, and the $150 service call is credited against it if you go ahead.

"If the lights stayed on, the power did not hurt your cameras"

Advice about outages tends to focus on full blackouts. The June 2025 event in northwest Brooklyn was different: Con Edison reduced voltage by 8 percent across Dumbo and the neighboring areas while it repaired equipment, and most homes kept their lights.

Camera systems with little margin do notice. Power boxes put out less, PoE switches near their budget drop ports, and ageing recorder power supplies restart. The damage may surface days later as a drive error or a camera that drops every hot afternoon.

If your cameras became unreliable after a hot spell with no outage, tell us. It points us to the power side of the system first.

"Wireless cameras are the easy fix for an old building"

Guides often recommend replacing a troublesome wired camera with a Wi-Fi one to avoid running cable. In a timber-and-brick warehouse or a concrete factory loft, that advice runs into the walls.

Concrete floors and thick masonry block Wi-Fi badly, and the router a Wi-Fi camera depends on often belongs to a tenant or an office whose network changes without warning. The result is a camera that works on installation day and drops out whenever the signal or the router changes.

In most DUMBO buildings the existing cable is the most valuable part of the system. We repair it and keep using it, and only suggest wireless where a run genuinely cannot be made.

"Building owners have to keep the cameras working"

AI answers sometimes say landlords are required to maintain security cameras. Brick Underground has noted that no city law requires buildings to have working cameras at all.

The picture is more nuanced. Attorney Jeffrey Reich told Brick Underground that "A landlord that is on notice of criminal activity at a property may be obligated to take reasonable safety precautions," and that fixing a dead camera would be a reasonable step. Condo boards and commercial landlords also have their own rules and leases.

In practice, a DUMBO repair is approved by whoever controls the recorder: the board, the managing agent, the landlord or the business. We are not lawyers, and we work with whoever that is.

"Anyone can install or fix cameras without a license"

Some answers say no license is needed to work on security cameras in New York. That is true for an owner working on their own system, and for an employee doing it on the owner's behalf.

For a company you hire, it is not. The New York Department of State licenses businesses that install, service or maintain security systems, and its rules include closed-circuit television used to detect intrusion, theft or movement.

Ask any company working in your building for its license number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and our certificate of insurance can go to your managing agent before the visit.

"Landmark districts only care about storefronts and windows"

It is easy to assume a camera is too small to interest the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Most of DUMBO is in the historic district designated in 2007, and the Commission's guidebook has specific rules for security cameras.

It asks for cameras that are small, well spaced and painted to blend, fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork rather than through cast iron or decorative surfaces, with about ten inches of visible conduit at most. Repairing existing safety equipment needs no permit.

That is why we repair in place where we can, and keep any replacement on the same mount or within those rules.

FAQ

DUMBO Security Camera Repair FAQ

Should a building post signs telling visitors that the cameras are recording?
+

Many managers do, as a deterrent. Habitat magazine described a Brooklyn and Queens property manager putting signs in vestibules to warn thieves the building is monitored, and Midboro Management's president told Brick Underground that cameras and signage are a great deterrent. A sign works best when the cameras behind it are actually recording, which is what the repair confirms.

Our loft's cameras show on the Control4 or Savant touchscreens. Will they still appear there after the repair?
+

They should. Smart-home systems pull a stream from each camera or from the recorder, so a repair that changes a camera, an address or a password can break that link. We note the settings before we start, reconnect the streams afterwards and check each touchscreen before we leave.

The camera at the far end of our loft floor runs through a small box halfway along the cable. Is that normal, and can the box fail?
+

It is normal on long runs. Network cable is rated for about 100 meters, so installers add a PoE extender to reach further, and those small boxes do fail, especially when they sit in a hot ceiling or lose power in a brownout. We test it, replace it if needed, and note where it is hidden.

Our camera's picture has slowly gone soft although the lens is clean. Can it be refocused instead of replaced?
+

Often, yes. Many cameras have an adjustable zoom lens, and building movement, heat or a knock can shift the focus over time. Motorized models refocus from the recorder; manual ones are adjusted at the camera. If refocusing does not restore a sharp picture, the sensor or lens has failed and we price a replacement.

Can each employee's camera login be removed on the day they leave the company?
+

Yes, if everyone has their own login rather than one shared password. During the repair we set up individual accounts with the right permissions and show the office manager how to remove one in a minute, so a departing employee loses access the same day.

The app our cameras used has been discontinued. Can the cameras still be viewed?
+

Usually. When a brand retires its app or connection service, the cameras and recorder normally keep recording on site. We can often move viewing to a supported app, standard viewing software or a new recorder that accepts the existing cameras, and we tell you which before any part is ordered.

Will repairing one camera reset the settings for the rest of the system?
+

No. A camera is replaced or repaired on its own channel, and the other cameras, their schedules and alerts are left as they are. If the recorder itself has to be replaced, we save its settings first where it allows and rebuild the rest, then check every channel before we leave.

The speaker on the camera at our side gate stopped working. Is that part of a camera repair?
+

Yes. Cameras with two-way talk have a speaker and microphone in the housing, and they fail from water, a loose internal plug or a setting turned off in the app. We check the setting first, then the hardware, and price a replacement camera only if the audio parts have failed.

Our cameras record to software on an old office computer that keeps crashing. Can that be fixed, or should it move to a recorder?
+

Both are possible. We can repair or update the software if the computer is sound, but an ageing office PC running around the clock is a weak point. A dedicated recorder is often the steadier choice, and we price both so you can compare them.

Residents want one of our roof cameras shared as a live view of the bridge. Is that safe to set up?
+

It can be, if it is kept apart from the security system. Sharing a building camera directly can expose the recorder to the internet. The safer route is a separate camera with its own sharing service, aimed at the view and not at doors or windows, so the security cameras stay private.

Do you repair the cameras inside a building's package room or mailroom?
+
