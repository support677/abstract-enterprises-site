The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Hill Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Hudson Hill is small, steep and unlike anywhere else in the borough, and those three facts decide most of what a camera system here has to do. Wikipedia gives its edges as the Henry Hudson Parkway on the east, the Hudson River on the west, West 246th Street on the south and West 254th Street on the north, and notes that it is also called Riverdale Estates. It sits in Bronx Community District 8 and is patrolled, like the rest of Riverdale, by the 50th Precinct. The land falls away from the parkway toward the river, so a house that faces Independence Avenue or Palisade Avenue at street level can have its garden, its garage or a gate at the far end of a long, steep lot, a full story or more below the front door. A camera plan that only looks at the front step covers the part of the property everybody can already see.

The local worry that comes up again and again is cars. In a July 24, 2026 story on auto and catalytic-converter thefts, the Riverdale Press quoted an NYPD spokesperson saying that even when there is video, it often is not very valuable, because crews wear masks and gloves, finish a theft within minutes and keep their exposure to cameras short. A 2022 story in the same paper quoted a resident describing how easy it is to work along the parkway service road, steal rims and tires, load them into a car and hop straight onto the highway; a resident living off Independence Avenue with no secure garage said she parks in well-lit spots instead. A 2025 story from just across the parkway noted that much of Fieldston Road is poorly lit, and that the outdoor cameras at one apartment building there were aimed so badly that they caught only the driveway, not the street where the damage happened. More broadly, the Riverdale Press reported that felony assaults in the 50th Precinct in 2024 reached their highest mark since 1993. We are not ranking any block, and none of these reports describes every street. What they show is that the curb, the service road and the edges of a lot are where things go wrong, and that a camera is only as useful as the spot it is aimed at.

Landmark rules are the second thing that shapes the job. The Riverdale Nature Preservancy describes the Riverdale Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on October 16, 1990, as starting at the corner of West 252nd Street and Independence Avenue and running north to West 254th Street and west to the edge of Riverdale Park. Google Arts & Culture describes it as about fifteen acres of steeply sloping land with thirty-four buildings overlooking the river and the Palisades. Outside the district, several buildings are landmarks in their own right, including Alderbrook House at 4715 Independence Avenue (designated December 14, 2010), the Greyston Gatehouse at 4695 Independence Avenue (March 22, 2011) and the Spaulding House, the coachman's residence (July 28, 1981). The LPC Permit Guidebook gives security cameras their own section, alongside window bars, lighting and intercom panels, and points to the Commission's rules under which staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria. When a camera goes up first and paperwork follows, it can end in a hearing: in a 2015 Fort Greene case about legalizing cameras installed without a permit, the Historic Districts Council called them sore thumbs on a well-preserved façade and suggested black cameras tucked into the cornice instead.

The third thing is privacy, and it matters more on a quiet residential hill than on a shopping street. A 2026 opinion piece in the Riverdale Press noted that a Ring camera now sits beside many Riverdale front doors, and worried that because these cameras so often record sidewalks and neighboring properties, people are filmed without knowing it. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera aimed into their private yard. In a co-op, the hallways and grounds are common areas the board controls, and a shareholder cannot put hardware there alone. And the person doing the work matters too: New York requires businesses that install security systems, including cameras, to hold a state license, and the MBFAA licensing course warns that unlicensed installers can be fined up to $10,000. We design every Hudson Hill system around those three limits from the first visit.

Hudson Hill facts that shape a camera plan

  • A steep lot, west toward the river: the ground drops from the parkway to the Hudson, so garages, garden gates and outbuildings often sit far below and far away from the front door and the router.
  • Two markets on one hill: roughly 225 to 237 one-family lots, and 485 apartments in five complexes on Henry Hudson Parkway West, which together hold about 68 percent of the area's homes.
  • Landmark lots: the Riverdale Historic District covers about fifteen acres and thirty-four buildings, and the LPC Permit Guidebook has a dedicated section on security cameras.
  • Cars at the curb: local reporting describes tire, rim and catalytic-converter thefts, crews working the parkway service road, and residents without garages parking under lights.
  • A quiet, residential grid: the LPC called the area strictly residential with no commercial center, so most cameras here watch homes, drives and grounds, not shop counters.
  • Few entry complaints: city data showed about 150 HPD complaints inside the lines since 2020, mostly heat and water, and none for intercoms or buzzers, so building cameras here are usually about grounds and garages, not broken front doors.

What Hudson Hill owners often add next. Owners who start with cameras on the driveway and the curb often ask a few weeks later about an alarm on the carriage house or detached garage, or a video intercom at a gate down the lot. Co-op boards that start with the garage tend to come back for the parking area and the paths between buildings. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port at the recorder for that next step, so a trench or an attic only has to be opened once.

Start from where the car sleeps

Ask a Hudson Hill homeowner what they want to protect and the answer is usually the car, the tires and the catalytic converter before it is the house. So we start there. Where is the car at three in the morning: in a garage at the bottom of the drive, in the drive itself, at the curb on Independence or Palisade Avenue, or on the parkway service road because the house has no garage at all? Each answer moves the camera. A garage needs a view of its door and its approach. A car in the drive needs a camera set back on the house so the whole vehicle and anyone crouched beside it stays in frame. A car at the curb needs a camera aimed along your own frontage, high enough to see over a hedge and lit well enough to show a figure at night. We also tell owners plainly what the police told the Riverdale Press: crews in masks and gloves who finish in minutes are hard to identify from any camera. What a well-placed camera still gives you is the time, the direction they came and went, the vehicle they used and a record your insurer will accept, plus an alert that can bring you to the window while it is happening.

Map the lot by distance and drop, not by room

In a city row house, a camera plan is drawn on a floor plan. On a Hudson Hill lot it is drawn on the ground. We walk from the curb to the front door, from the house to the garage, down the steps or the path to the lower garden, and across to any cottage, pool house or carriage house, measuring each distance and noting how far the ground drops. Those numbers decide whether a camera can be wired straight back to the recorder, needs a small switch partway down, or should run through conduit buried along an existing path. They also decide where the recorder goes. In many houses here the best spot is a dry, locked corner of the basement on the downhill side, closer to the outbuildings and well away from the front door, so that someone who gets in cannot simply carry it off. Wi-Fi rarely survives the trip from a router inside thick masonry to a camera a hundred feet down a slope, so we only use wireless where a short hop is genuinely all that is needed.

Decide who sees what before the first camera goes up

A family house may need views for the owners, adult children who check in from elsewhere, a caretaker or gardener who comes on set days, and sometimes a property manager when the house sits empty for a season. A co-op needs separate access for the board, the managing agent, the superintendent and any security staff, and nothing for shareholders unless the board decides to share a view. We set those accounts before we leave, each with its own login and limits: the caretaker might see the gate and garage but not the inside of the house, the agent might see every common area, and only the owner or the board holds the main administrator account that can change settings or delete footage. Alerts follow the same logic. The lower gate, the garage and the carriage house alert at night; the front walk records all the time without buzzing anyone's phone. We write the list down, so the next person to manage the house or the building can read it.

Old villas: thick walls, protected faces and a long way to the street

The surviving villas were built for a summer colony, not for cars, and the camera work has to respect both their walls and their landmark status. Stone and stucco walls can be very thick, so we look first for a route that already exists: a basement window, a former fixture box, an old pipe opening, a gap at the eaves. A single planned opening on a rear or plain wall can carry several camera cables, sealed afterward so water and pests stay out. Exterior positions go on plain surfaces away from carved stone, trim and cornices, in a finish chosen with the owner, and on any district or landmarked building they are written up for LPC staff review before anyone climbs a ladder. Because these houses usually sit well back from the road, a camera at the front door alone shows a long, empty lawn. The curb and the gate are covered by a camera placed nearer them, often on an outbuilding or a gatepost the owner controls, with a narrow frame that stops at the property line.

Carriage houses on the alley: the door nobody else watches

ARC Riverdale describes Sycamore Avenue as the district's old carriage alley, lined with carriage houses, stables and a cottage built or altered in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Many of those buildings now hold cars, tools, guest rooms or offices, and their doors open onto the alley, not onto the owner's lawn. That makes the alley door one of the most important doors on the property and one of the least watched. We frame a camera tight on that door from the eave above or just inside, so it shows whoever opens it and leaves the rest of the alley, and the houses across it, out of the picture. Cable runs back to the main house through buried conduit along an existing path where the owner agrees, so the carriage-house camera appears in the same app as the rest. Where a carriage house sits inside the historic district, its exterior counts just as much as the main house's under the landmark rules.

Colonial Revival houses: attics, basements and garages that help

The houses built as the estates were divided up in the 1930s and 1950s are often the easiest on the hill to wire, because most of them have a basement, an attic and a garage, and many were built with plain eaves and wood trim. Cable for the front and side cameras usually drops from the attic to a camera under the eave, and cable for the back and garage usually runs along the basement joists and out through one sealed opening. Where these houses have later additions, we check what each part of the house is made of before choosing a mount, because the original wall and the addition rarely match. These houses also tend to sit closer to the street than the villas, which brings the neighbors closer too: side cameras are aimed down the length of the side yard, not across it into the house next door, and front cameras are set to the frontage and the curb.

Parkway co-ops: the board's recorder, the garage and the service doors

The elevator co-ops on Henry Hudson Parkway West were built between 1952 and 1961, nine to thirteen stories tall, with lobbies, service entrances and parking. In these buildings, cameras in common areas belong to the corporation and are decided by the board, usually with the managing agent handling the work. Our job is to give them a plan that covers the places shareholders actually worry about: the main entrance and lobby framed for faces, the service and delivery doors, the garage ramp and parking rows, and the doors from the garage into the building. Cable travels through risers, service spaces and garage ceilings to a locked room only the people the board names can open, never through the elevator shaft. Garages bring their own problem, headlights shining straight into a lens, so cameras there are placed to look along the rows rather than into oncoming cars. The garden co-op at 4601 is a different build altogether: 35 low buildings spread across one site, where the work is mostly about paths, parking and getting cable between buildings, which we cover in the questions below.

Co-op garages and parking: where boards spend first

When a parkway co-op board asks where camera money does the most good, the honest answer on this hill is usually the garage and the parking areas, because that is where the local reporting puts the trouble: cars, tires, rims and converters. A garage plan starts at the vehicle entrance, with a camera placed to show each car as it comes in and goes out, angled so headlights pass across the frame rather than straight into it. The rows follow, each camera looking along a row from one end so anyone working between cars stays in view, then the doors that lead from the garage into the building and any pedestrian gate onto the service road. Outdoor parking needs the light checked after dark, because a camera aimed at an unlit corner of a lot records nothing useful. Footage stays on the board's recorder with access limited to the people the board names, and we write down the retention period so the board can tell shareholders how long a clip will exist before it is overwritten.

Building and restoration sites on the hill

Hudson Hill keeps changing in small ways: a villa restored, a 1950s house rebuilt, a new house on one of the vacant lots. Building sites here are unusual for the city, often set back on a sloping lot behind a gate, with materials, tools and machines left overnight in a place no neighbor can see. A temporary camera on a pole or on the existing structure, aimed at the gate, the material store and the access drive, with alerts outside working hours, does most of the work. Where the site has no permanent power or internet yet, we can plan cameras that run from the builder's temporary power and a cellular connection, then move or replace them with the permanent system once the house is closed in. On a landmark lot, even temporary equipment on the existing building should be checked against the permit for the work.

Rules and limits for building and institutional cameras in Hudson Hill

  • The board decides common areas. In a co-op, hallways, lobbies, garages and grounds are controlled by the board; a shareholder cannot add hardware there on their own.
  • Landmark exteriors need approval. Exterior cameras on Riverdale Historic District buildings and individual landmarks are planned to the LPC Permit Guidebook's camera criteria and submitted before work begins.
  • Private places stay off camera. No camera looks into an apartment, a private patio, a restroom or any room where people expect privacy, and we test every view for reflections.
  • Neighbors' yards stay out of frame. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets residents sue over cameras aimed into their private yards, so side and rear views are masked at the property line.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is mounted on a self-closing door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it latching.
  • Openings stay rated. Every hole through a rated wall or floor in a co-op building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Audio stays off. We set building cameras to record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York licenses businesses that install security systems, including cameras, through the Department of State; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which boards and managing agents usually ask to see first.

Security Camera Installation in Hudson Hill: Questions Answered

Landmark lots and the look of a camera

Our house sits just outside the Riverdale Historic District line, but the house next door is inside it. Does the Landmarks Preservation Commission have any say over our cameras?

Not over a house that is neither inside the district nor an individual landmark; the Commission's permits apply to designated property. The district boundary here is specific, starting at West 252nd Street and Independence Avenue and running north to West 254th Street and west to Riverdale Park, so we check your exact lot before planning. If your house is outside, the decisions are yours, though we still keep views off the neighboring house, since a camera aimed into a protected neighbor's yard raises privacy problems of its own. If any part of the lot, such as an outbuilding, falls inside, we plan that part to the Commission's camera rules.

Should a camera on a stone or stucco villa be black, white or painted to match the wall?

It depends where it sits, and on a landmark building the finish is part of what staff review. In the 2015 Fort Greene case, the Historic Districts Council's advice was to use black cameras tucked into the cornice so they would not stand out against a well-preserved façade. On a pale stucco wall a small, light camera can disappear better; in a shadowed soffit or under dark trim, black often does. We choose the smallest camera that still does the job, place it where the eye does not land first, and show the owner the finish and size before anything is ordered, so the choice is made once.

Steep lots, outbuildings and cars

Our lot drops steeply toward the river. How does cable reach a camera at the bottom of the garden?

Usually by following a route that already exists: along the drive, beside a path or stair, or next to an existing pipe or utility run, inside conduit buried just under the surface. We measure the distance first, because a camera cable can carry power and picture only so far; on very long runs a weatherproof switch partway down, powered from the house, keeps every camera within range. Where trenching is not welcome, a short, well-supported run along a wall or fence can work. A battery camera with a wireless link is the last resort, used only where the signal tests strong at the bottom of the slope.

Our carriage house faces the old alley on Sycamore Avenue, not our lawn. What should a camera there actually watch?

The door onto the alley, framed tightly enough that it shows whoever opens it and little else. That door is the one few people see, because it faces away from the main house. A camera under the eave above it, or just inside if the building allows, gives a clear face as someone approaches. We mask the rest of the alley and the buildings across it, wire the camera back to the main recorder so it sits in the same app as every other view, and set alerts for the hours the carriage house should be empty. If the carriage house is in the historic district, its exterior position goes into the same LPC plan as the main house.

Our car lives on the street because the house has no garage. Can a camera on the house cover it?

Often, if we plan for where you actually park. A resident off Independence Avenue told the Riverdale Press she parks in well-lit spots for exactly this reason, and light matters as much as the camera. We place a camera on the front of the house high enough to see over hedges and walls, aim it along your own frontage where you park, and check it at night to see whether street lighting is enough or whether the camera needs its own illumination. Many owners find the useful result is an alert when someone stops beside the car after midnight, which brings them to the window while it is happening.

Police say theft crews wear masks and finish in minutes. Is a camera aimed at the parkway service road still worth having?

Yes, as long as you know what it can and cannot give you. The NYPD spokesperson quoted in July 2026 was right that a masked person is hard to identify. A camera still records the time, how many people came, which way they arrived and left, and the vehicle they used, which is what investigators and insurers ask for first. Set with a narrow alert zone on your own frontage, it can also warn you while a theft is under way. We aim service-road cameras along the frontage, not across the traffic, so headlights do not wash out the picture and passing cars do not trigger alerts.

A building nearby put up outdoor cameras that only ever showed its own driveway. How do we make sure ours see the street?

By deciding what each camera must show before choosing where it goes. The Riverdale Press reported in 2025 that a Johnson Avenue building's cameras caught the driveway but not the street, where the damage was happening. We stand at the spot we need covered, the curb, the gate or the parked car, then work backward to a mount that can see it at a useful size. A test picture on a phone at the mounting spot, by day and after dark, settles it before any hole is drilled. If one camera cannot do both the drive and the street well, we use two rather than one that does neither.

Can the cameras tell us the moment a motion light comes on by the garage?

Yes. The Riverdale Press reported a Labor Day weekend case in Riverdale where a family saw a garage motion light come on, opened their security footage on a phone, and the 50th Precinct later made an arrest. A camera can do the same job without anyone happening to look outside: we draw an alert zone around the garage door and approach, set it for the hours the garage should be quiet, and send the alert, with a short clip, to the phones you choose. Where a light is already there, we aim the camera so the light helps the picture instead of blinding it.

Parkway co-ops and condos

In one of the parkway elevator co-ops, which cameras does the board pay for, and which can a shareholder add on their own?

Anything in a common area, the lobby, hallways, elevators, garage and grounds, belongs to the corporation and is the board's decision and budget; a shareholder cannot add hardware there alone. Inside the apartment, a shareholder can usually use a camera that sits on a shelf and is not fixed to walls, subject to the house rules. Anything on the apartment door, a balcony or a terrace depends on the proprietary lease and house rules, so the shareholder should ask the managing agent in writing first. We work with either side, and we will not mount anything in a common area without written approval from the board or agent.

Our garden co-op has dozens of two-story buildings on one site. How do cameras on far buildings reach one recorder?

The 1951 complex at 4601 Henry Hudson Parkway West spreads 52 apartments across 35 two-story buildings, so cable between buildings is the main design question. Where the buildings already share underground utility paths, we use them; where they do not, conduit buried along existing walkways or short runs along building walls the co-op controls carry the cable. Small switches in a few building basements gather nearby cameras so no single run is too long, and everything ends on one recorder in a locked room the board chooses. Cameras watch paths, parking and the busiest doors, aimed away from ground-floor windows and private patios.

Can a co-op board point its grounds cameras straight at individual shareholders' doors?

That is the question a New York co-op shareholder put to Justia's lawyer panel in April 2026, and boards should get their own legal advice on it. A short written policy saying what is recorded, how long it is kept and who can view it helps shareholders trust the system.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Hudson Hill

"Hudson Hill is the part of Riverdale around Riverdale Avenue."

Some descriptions online, and even some internal notes we have seen, place Hudson Hill along Riverdale Avenue in the West 230s. That puts it in the wrong place entirely.

Wikipedia's definition is narrow and specific: west of the Henry Hudson Parkway, east of the Hudson River, from West 246th Street to West 254th Street. That is a residential slope of estates, Colonial Revival houses and parkway co-ops, with no shopping street at all.

For camera work the difference is everything. A Riverdale Avenue job is a storefront and an apartment lobby; a Hudson Hill job is a long drive, a curb, a carriage house on an alley or a co-op garage. We plan for the hill as it really is.

"A security camera will catch the car thieves."

Quick answers often treat a camera as a way to identify whoever touches your car. The NYPD told the Riverdale Press in July 2026 that theft crews here wear masks and gloves, work fast and limit their exposure to cameras.

So a camera will rarely produce a face that names someone. What it does produce is the time, the number of people, the direction of travel, the vehicle, and an alert while it is happening, provided it is aimed at the right spot and the spot is lit.

That is how we set curb and service-road cameras: narrow alert zones on your own frontage, low-light performance checked after dark, and honest expectations written into the plan.

"Any camera in a historic district has to go to a public hearing."

Owners in the Riverdale Historic District are sometimes told that a single camera means months before the full Commission. The LPC Permit Guidebook says otherwise: it has its own section on installing security cameras and points to rules under which staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria.

The catch is meeting those criteria, which cover where cameras go and how they look. A plan that ignores them, or a camera installed first and legalized later, can end up in front of the Commission, as the 2015 Fort Greene case did.

We read the guidebook section against the actual walls before proposing positions, and we prepare the plan so the owner can submit it cleanly. We do not promise any approval timeline, because the Commission sets it.

"Hudson Hill is all mansions, so every job is an estate job."

Directory sites and vendor copy often picture the whole area as grand estates. The handful of surviving villas are real, but they are the exception by number.

City lot records show roughly 225 to 237 one-family lots, most of them ordinary-sized for this hill, and 485 apartments in five complexes on Henry Hudson Parkway West, which hold about 68 percent of the area's homes.

That changes who we talk to. Many Hudson Hill camera jobs are for co-op boards and managing agents, or for a shareholder asking what they may put inside their own apartment, not for an estate manager.

"Wireless cameras are the simple answer for any house."

AI answers lean on battery and Wi-Fi cameras because they need no cable. On a small lot with the router a few feet from the front door, that can be true.

On a Hudson Hill lot, the router may be inside thick masonry and the garage or gate a long way down a slope. Signal fades, batteries drain faster in the cold, and the camera that matters most is the one that drops out. Yelp reviews from around the Bronx include owners who swapped a long-running wired system for wireless and had trouble ever since.

We wire the cameras that cover the far end of the lot, and use wireless only where a short, strong hop is all that is needed and tests well on site.

"Anyone handy can put up cameras; no license is needed."

Several quick answers say camera installation needs no special credentials. New York's licensing rules count security cameras among the systems an installing business must be licensed for, and the MBFAA course warns of fines up to $10,000 for unlicensed work.

A homeowner fitting their own doorbell is a different matter from a company running a system through a house or a co-op building for pay. Boards and managing agents usually ask for the license and insurance before anything else.

AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, and we show it, and our insurance, before work begins.

"Pointing a camera at the street is always fine."

Many answers say it is legal to record anything visible from your property. Recording a public street from your own house is generally allowed, but that is not the whole story on a quiet residential hill.

The Riverdale Press ran a 2026 opinion piece about neighbors being filmed by doorbell cameras without their knowledge, and New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets residents sue over cameras aimed into their private yards.

So we frame street cameras on your frontage and the curb, mask neighboring yards and windows, keep audio off, and check every view with the owner before we leave. A camera that respects the neighbors is one the neighbors do not complain about.

FAQ

Hudson Hill Security Camera Installation FAQ

We don't want to be the house that films the whole block. How tightly can a front camera be framed?
+

Quite tightly. A camera with an adjustable lens can be zoomed so it covers your door, your walk and your stretch of curb and little else, and most recorders let you black out parts of the picture permanently, so a neighbor's porch, windows or yard never appear in any recording. We set those masks during installation and check them with you on a phone before we leave. A narrower view also gives a larger, clearer picture of what matters, so you lose nothing useful by leaving the rest of the street out.

A neighbor replaced an old wired system with wireless cameras and has had trouble ever since. What usually goes wrong?
+

The usual problems are signal, batteries and distance. Thick masonry and a long lot weaken Wi-Fi, so the farthest cameras drop off first, and battery cameras that wake up often in a busy spot or in cold weather need charging more than expected. Cloud-only cameras also stop recording when the internet goes down. If your house already has working camera cable in the walls, it is often worth keeping it and fitting new cameras to it, with an adapter if the old cable is coax. We test old runs before deciding, and use wireless only where it holds up on site.

Our house is one of the 1950s houses built when the estates were divided. Is there usually a hidden path for camera cable?
+

Usually, yes. Houses from that period most often have an accessible attic over the second floor and a basement with open joists, which together reach almost every eave and door. Cable for front and side cameras drops from the attic to a camera under the eave, and cable for the back door and garage runs along the basement ceiling and out through one sealed opening. Later additions or finished basements can block parts of that route, which is why we look in the attic and basement before quoting, not after.

Our gate faces the Henry Hudson Parkway. Will the traffic set off motion alerts all day and night?
+

Not if the camera is set up properly. We draw the alert zone only around your gate, drive and frontage, so movement on the parkway beyond it is ignored, and where the camera supports it we set alerts for people and vehicles stopping inside that zone rather than for any motion at all. We also aim the camera along the frontage rather than straight across the lanes, so headlights do not wash out the picture at night. After a few days of use we can adjust the zones if anything still triggers too often.

Our caretaker comes on set days and lives elsewhere. Can he see the gate and garage cameras but nothing inside the house?
+

Yes. Most recorders let us create a separate account for each person and choose exactly which cameras it can see, whether it can play back recordings, and whether it can export clips. A caretaker can have live view of the gate, the garage and the grounds, with no access to indoor cameras and no ability to change settings or delete footage. If he stops working for you, his account can be switched off in a minute from the owner's phone, without changing anyone else's login.

Our property has an old stone wall and gate posts along the street. Can a camera watch the gate without being fixed to the stonework?
+

Usually, and on a historic wall that is often the better choice. A camera on the house, an outbuilding or a separate post set inside the wall, with a longer lens, can watch the gate opening without a single hole in the old stone. Where the wall and posts are part of a district or landmarked property, avoiding them also keeps the landmark plan simpler. If the only good view really does need a mount on the wall, we look for an existing joint or fixture location and include it in the plan for approval first.

In our co-op's garage, headlights shine straight at the camera. Which camera copes with that?
+

Placement matters more than the model. A camera that looks straight at the ramp will be blinded by every car, so we place entrance cameras to look across the path of cars as they enter, and row cameras to look along the rows from one end. On top of that, cameras with strong backlight and wide dynamic range handling keep detail in the dark corners while a bright light passes through the frame. We check the garage views with cars actually moving before we call the job done.

Weekend visitors to the gardens park all along our block. Will their cars block our street camera?
+

They can block a low camera aimed at the curb. Mounting the camera higher on the house, looking down along your frontage, lets it see over parked cars to your own car and the sidewalk. If your car is often parked a little way down the block on busy days, a second, narrower camera aimed toward that stretch can help. We look at the view on a busy weekend photo if you can send one, and set the camera so the busy days and the quiet nights both give a useful picture.

The railroad runs along the river below our house. Will passing trains set off the rear camera?
+

They will if the camera is aimed out over the river with motion alerts covering the whole view. We aim rear cameras at your own garden, path and lower gate, and draw the alert zone tightly around those areas, so trains, boats and lights on the far shore fall outside it. If you want a wide view of the river for its own sake, we can record it without alerts. Strong lights moving far off can still affect night exposure, so we check the rear view after dark before handover.

Does the Landmarks Preservation Commission treat a video doorbell on a district house the same way it treats a camera?
+

The LPC Permit Guidebook covers security cameras and intercom panels in the same health and safety chapter, so a video doorbell on the exterior of a district house should be treated as something to plan and check, not as an exception. The safest path is to include the doorbell in the same plan as any cameras, with its size, finish and exact position shown. On many houses a doorbell can sit within an existing door surround or replace an old bell button, which keeps changes to the façade to a minimum.

Would cloud-only cameras be simpler than a recorder for a house with eight or ten cameras?
+

For a few cameras, cloud storage can be simple. For eight or ten, a recorder in the house usually makes more sense: it keeps recording when the internet goes down, it does not use your upload bandwidth all day, and it has no monthly fee per camera. You can still view and export clips from your phone anywhere. If you want a copy off the property as well, we can set the recorder or selected cameras to send important clips to cloud storage, so you get both without paying to stream every camera all the time.
